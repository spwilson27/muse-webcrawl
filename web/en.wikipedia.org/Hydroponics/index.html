Hydroponics is a type of horticulture and a subset of hydroculture which involves growing plants, usually crops or medicinal plants, without soil, by using water-based mineral nutrient solutions in an artificial environment. Terrestrial or aquatic plants may grow freely with their roots exposed to the nutritious liquid, or the roots may be mechanically supported by an inert medium such as perlite, gravel, or other substrates.

History

The earliest records of soilless growing include the Hanging Gardens of Babylon and the floating gardens (chinampas) of the Aztecs, though these were not strictly hydroponic. In the 17th century John Woodward grew plants in water cultures, and in the 1860s Julius von Sachs and Wilhelm Knop developed standard nutrient solutions. The term "hydroponics" was coined by William Frederick Gericke of the University of California in the 1930s, who promoted the method for commercial production. Hydroponic installations supplied fresh vegetables to Allied troops in the Pacific during the Second World War and later featured at Disney's EPCOT Center and in NASA research on life-support systems.

Techniques

Nutrient film technique (NFT)

A shallow stream of nutrient solution is continuously recirculated past the bare roots of plants in watertight channels. NFT is widely used for lettuce and herbs.

Deep water culture (DWC)

Plants are suspended in net pots above a reservoir of oxygenated nutrient solution, with roots submerged and aerated by air pumps and air stones.

Other systems

Ebb and flow (flood and drain) periodically floods a grow tray; drip systems deliver solution to each plant; aeroponics suspends plants in air and mists the roots; wick systems passively draw solution through a wick. Aquaponics combines hydroponics with aquaculture, using fish waste as the nutrient source.

Advantages and disadvantages

Hydroponics allows precise control of nutrients, faster growth rates (often 30–50% faster than soil), water savings of up to 90% through recirculation, no weeds, and production in places without arable soil, including deserts and cities. Disadvantages include higher initial cost, dependence on electricity, the need to monitor pH and electrical conductivity, and the risk of rapid spread of water-borne diseases such as Pythium root rot.

Nutrients

Essential elements supplied in solution include the macronutrients nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, calcium, magnesium and sulfur, and micronutrients such as iron, manganese, zinc, copper, boron, molybdenum and chlorine. Solutions are usually maintained at pH 5.5–6.5.
