Cheese

Cheese is a dairy product produced in a wide range of flavours, textures, and forms by coagulation of the milk protein casein. It comprises proteins and fat from milk, usually the milk of cows, buffalo, goats, or sheep. During production, the milk is usually acidified and either the enzymes of rennet or bacterial enzymes with similar activity are added to cause the casein to coagulate. The solid curds are then separated from the liquid whey and pressed into finished cheese. Most cheeses melt at cooking temperature.

Over a thousand types of cheese exist and are currently produced in various countries. Their styles, textures and flavours depend on the origin of the milk (including the animal's diet), whether they have been pasteurised, the butterfat content, the bacteria and mould, the processing, and how long they have been aged. Herbs, spices, or wood smoke may be used as flavouring agents. The yellow to red colour of many cheeses is produced by adding annatto. Some cheeses are categorised by the texture of their rind, such as soft cheese, semi-soft cheese, semi-firm cheese, and hard cheese.

Cheese is valued for its portability, long life, and high content of fat, protein, calcium, and phosphorus. It is more compact and has a longer shelf life than milk, although how long a cheese will keep depends on the type. Hard cheeses, such as Parmesan, last longer than soft cheeses, such as Brie or goat's milk cheese. The long storage life of some cheeses, especially when encased in a protective rind, allows selling when markets are favourable.

History

Cheese is an ancient food whose origins predate recorded history. There is no conclusive evidence indicating where cheesemaking originated, whether in Europe, Central Asia or the Middle East, but the practice had spread within Europe prior to Roman times and, according to Pliny the Elder, had become a sophisticated enterprise by the time the Roman Empire came into being. Proposed dates for the origin of cheesemaking range from around 8000 BCE, when sheep were first domesticated, to around 3000 BCE. Evidence for cheese production includes perforated pottery sieves found in Europe dating to around 5500 BCE, and residues of milk fats identified on pottery from Poland and Croatia.

The earliest evidence of cheesemaking in the geological record dates to 1650 BCE and was found in China's Tarim Basin. In ancient Egypt, cheese was made and tomb murals depict its production. The Romans industrialised cheesemaking, producing hard cheeses in quantities suitable for long-distance transport and military rations; large cheese presses and curing houses were characteristic of Roman estates. After the fall of Rome, monastic communities across Europe preserved and refined cheesemaking techniques, and many now-famous varieties trace their origins to monastery production.

Production

Cheesemaking begins with milk, which may be raw or pasteurised. Starter cultures of bacteria convert milk sugars into lactic acid, lowering the pH. Rennet — traditionally extracted from the stomach lining of young calves, and now often produced microbially — is added to coagulate the casein into a gel. The curd is cut to release whey, then cooked, drained, salted, and shaped. Many cheeses are then aged (ripened) under controlled temperature and humidity for periods ranging from a few days to several years, during which bacteria and moulds develop characteristic flavours and textures.

Global cheese production in recent years has exceeded 20 million tonnes annually, with the United States the largest producer, followed by Germany and France. Cow's milk accounts for the great majority of world output, though goat, sheep, and buffalo milk cheeses hold important regional and culinary roles.

Types

Cheeses are commonly classified by texture and moisture content:

  • Fresh cheeses — cottage cheese, ricotta, fresh mozzarella, and queso fresco; soft, high-moisture, and not aged.
  • Soft-ripened cheeses — Brie and Camembert, characterised by a bloomy white rind of Penicillium mould.
  • Semi-soft cheeses — Havarti, Munster, and washed-rind varieties such as Limburger.
  • Blue cheeses — Roquefort, Gorgonzola, and Stilton, veined with Penicillium roqueforti.
  • Firm and hard cheeses — Cheddar, Gouda, Emmental, Gruyère, and Parmigiano-Reggiano.
  • Processed cheese — cheese products made by melting and emulsifying natural cheeses with additional ingredients.

Culinary uses

Cheese is a staple ingredient in cuisines worldwide. It is eaten on its own, served with bread, crackers, and fruit, and used in cooking: melted on pizza and sandwiches, grated over pasta, baked into sauces such as fondue and mornay, and incorporated into dishes from macaroni and cheese to quesadillas and cheesecake. Melting behaviour varies with moisture and fat content and with pH; high-moisture, moderately acidic cheeses such as mozzarella stretch and melt readily, while aged low-moisture cheeses brown and crisp.

Nutrition and health

Cheese is a concentrated source of protein, calcium, phosphorus, and vitamins A and B12, but it is also high in saturated fat and sodium, and calorie-dense. Lactose content falls as cheese ages, so hard aged cheeses contain only trace amounts and are often tolerated by people with lactose intolerance. Some cheeses, particularly aged and fermented varieties, contain appreciable levels of biogenic amines such as tyramine and histamine.

Etymology

The word cheese comes from the Latin caseus, through Old English ċīese. Related words appear across Indo-European languages, including German Käse, Dutch kaas, and, via a different Latin derivative (formaticum, cheese made in a mould), French fromage and Italian formaggio.
