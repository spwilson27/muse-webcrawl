Hypercorrection

In sociolinguistics, hypercorrection is a non-standard language use that results from the over-application of a perceived rule of language-usage prescription. A speaker who hypercorrects applies a rule in an inappropriate context, aiming at a prestige form but overshooting the target. The effect is often attributed to speakers who are anxious to use "correct" language, particularly second-language learners and speakers of stigmatised varieties striving toward a prestige dialect.

Examples

Classic English examples include "between you and I" (over-applying the rule against "me" in subject position), pronouncing the silent t in "often" after learning that some silent letters should be sounded, and using "whom" wherever an object pronoun occurs, even where "who" is correct. The "h" added by some speakers in words like "haitch" for the letter H is another instance.

Linguistic analysis

William Labov's studies of New York City speech showed that lower-middle-class speakers hypercorrected, using more prestige forms in careful speech than even upper-middle-class speakers — a crossover pattern that became evidence for linguistic insecurity and for the social stratification of language change. Hypercorrection can spread through a language community and eventually become accepted usage.
