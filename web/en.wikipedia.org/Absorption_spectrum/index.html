Absorption spectrum

An absorption spectrum is the spectrum of electromagnetic radiation transmitted through a substance, showing dark lines or bands at wavelengths where the substance absorbs energy. Atoms and molecules absorb photons whose energies match the differences between their quantised energy levels, so the positions of the absorption features form a characteristic "fingerprint" of the absorbing material. The absorption spectrum is the counterpart of the emission spectrum, whose bright lines appear at the same wavelengths.

History

In 1802 William Hyde Wollaston noticed dark lines in the solar spectrum; Joseph von Fraunhofer mapped hundreds of them from 1814 and catalogued the strongest, which still bear his name and letter designations (such as the sodium D lines). Around 1860 Gustav Kirchhoff and Robert Bunsen showed that each chemical element produces a unique spectral pattern and that the Fraunhofer lines arise because cooler gases in the Sun's atmosphere absorb light at the same wavelengths they emit when heated — founding the science of spectroscopy and revealing the composition of the Sun and stars.

Applications

Absorption spectroscopy is used across science: in astronomy, to determine the composition, temperature, density and motion of stars, galaxies and exoplanet atmospheres; in chemistry, through UV–visible and infrared spectrophotometry (governed quantitatively by the Beer–Lambert law), to identify compounds and measure concentrations; and in Earth observation, where features such as the oxygen A-band and chlorophyll's red edge reveal atmospheric and surface properties. In stellar spectra, absorption lines dominate because the hot interior's continuous light passes through the cooler outer atmosphere.

Relation to theory

Kirchhoff's laws of spectroscopy state that a hot dense body produces a continuous spectrum, a hot dilute gas an emission-line spectrum, and a continuous source viewed through a cooler dilute gas an absorption-line spectrum. Quantum mechanics explains line positions via transitions between discrete energy levels of electrons, nuclei and molecular vibrations and rotations.
