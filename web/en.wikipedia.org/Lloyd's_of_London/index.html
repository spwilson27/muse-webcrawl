Lloyd's of London, generally known simply as Lloyd's, is an insurance and reinsurance market located in London, England. Unlike most of its competitors, it is not itself an insurance company; rather, it is a corporate body governed by the Lloyd's Act 1871 under which members, organised in syndicates, underwrite risks for a profit.

History

The market began in Edward Lloyd's coffee house on Tower Street around 1688, a meeting place for shipowners, merchants and underwriters seeking marine insurance. The Lutine Bell, salvaged from HMS Lutine, hangs in the Underwriting Room and was traditionally rung to announce overdue ships. Lloyd's moved into its present building on Lime Street, designed by Richard Rogers with services on the exterior, in 1986.

Structure

Business is transacted face-to-face between brokers and underwriters in the Underwriting Room. Capital is provided by corporate members and, historically, by individual "Names" with unlimited liability — a system that caused a severe crisis in the late 1980s and early 1990s over asbestos and pollution losses, after which the market was reformed and the pre-1993 liabilities reinsured into Equitas. Lloyd's is now known for specialist and unusual risks, from satellites to celebrity body parts.
