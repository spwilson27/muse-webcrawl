Coat of arms of Germany

The coat of arms of Germany, known as the Bundesschild, displays a black eagle with red beak, tongue, and talons on a golden shield. The eagle is one of the oldest state emblems in the world, and the German federal eagle continues a heraldic tradition reaching back to the Holy Roman Empire.

History

The eagle was the emblem of the Holy Roman emperors, ultimately derived from the aquila of the Roman legions; by the fifteenth century the imperial eagle was depicted double-headed. After 1806 the successor states used various eagles, and the revolutionaries of 1848 adopted a black eagle on gold for the Frankfurt Parliament. The Weimar Republic standardized a single-headed eagle in 1919, in a design associated with Karl-Tobias Schwab. The Federal Republic of Germany adopted the arms by proclamation of President Theodor Heuss in 1950, restoring the Weimar form; the version designed by Sigmund von Weech in 1928 is used on the presidential standard and other state emblems. East Germany instead used a socialist emblem of a hammer and compass within a wreath of grain until reunification in 1990.

Forms and use

The federal eagle appears in several stylized variants on flags, seals, passports, and government buildings. The especially plump rendering on the wall of the Bundestag plenary chamber in Berlin, nicknamed the "fette Henne" ("fat hen"), is the best known. The arms themselves may be used only by federal authorities, though the eagle motif in general heraldic form is widespread.
