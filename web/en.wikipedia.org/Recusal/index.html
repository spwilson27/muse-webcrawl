Recusal

Recusal is the withdrawal of a judge, prosecutor, government official, or juror from a case or decision because of a potential conflict of interest or lack of impartiality. A recused judge takes no part in the proceedings, and another judge is assigned in their place.

Recusal preserves both actual and apparent impartiality in adjudication.

Grounds

Common grounds include a financial interest in the outcome, a personal or family relationship with a party or lawyer, prior involvement in the matter, or statements suggesting prejudgement. In the United States, 28 U.S.C. § 455 requires a federal judge to disqualify themselves "in any proceeding in which his impartiality might reasonably be questioned", as well as in specified situations such as personal bias, prior service as a lawyer in the matter, or a financial interest.

Procedure

Recusal may occur voluntarily (sua sponte) or on the motion of a party. Standards differ on whether the deciding factor is actual bias or the appearance of bias; the maxim that justice must "be seen to be done" underpins the appearance-based approach used in many common law systems. In Caperton v. A. T. Massey Coal Co. (2009), the U.S. Supreme Court held that due process required a state supreme court justice to recuse himself from a case involving a major campaign benefactor, because the probability of bias was constitutionally intolerable.

Beyond the judiciary

The term is also used for legislators, executive officials, and members of administrative bodies who abstain from decisions in which they have a conflict, and for jurors removed for cause during selection.
