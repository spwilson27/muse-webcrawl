Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar

Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar (19 October 1910 – 21 August 1995) was an Indian-American theoretical physicist who made significant contributions to the scientific knowledge about the structure and evolution of stars. He was awarded the 1983 Nobel Prize in Physics with William A. Fowler for theoretical studies of the physical processes of importance to the structure and evolution of the stars. His mathematical treatment of stellar evolution yielded many of the current theoretical models of the later evolutionary stages of massive stars and black holes. Many concepts, institutions and inventions, including the Chandrasekhar limit and the Chandra X-ray Observatory, are named after him.

The Chandrasekhar limit

On his voyage from India to Cambridge in 1930, the nineteen-year-old Chandrasekhar worked out that a white dwarf star above a certain mass — now known as the Chandrasekhar limit, about 1.4 times the mass of the Sun (1.44 M☉) — cannot support itself against gravity by electron degeneracy pressure and must collapse further. His result was publicly attacked by Sir Arthur Eddington at a Royal Astronomical Society meeting in 1935, a humiliation that contributed to Chandrasekhar's decision to leave Cambridge for the University of Chicago in 1937. Decades later the result was vindicated as the foundation for understanding supernovae, neutron stars and black holes.

Career and later work

Chandrasekhar spent his career at the University of Chicago and Yerkes Observatory, working systematically through successive fields — stellar structure, radiative transfer (his 1950 monograph Radiative Transfer), hydrodynamic and hydromagnetic stability, and finally the mathematical theory of black holes, summarised in The Mathematical Theory of Black Holes (1983). He edited the Astrophysical Journal from 1952 to 1971, transforming it into a leading journal. NASA's Chandra X-ray Observatory, launched in 1999, is named in his honour.
