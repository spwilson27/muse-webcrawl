Iambic pentameter

Iambic pentameter

FootIamb (unstressed–stressed)

Iambic pentameter is a type of metric line used in traditional English poetry and verse drama. It describes a particular rhythm: a line of verse with five metrical feet, each consisting of one short (or unstressed) syllable followed by one long (or stressed) syllable, for example:

Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?
(da-DUM da-DUM da-DUM da-DUM da-DUM)

The word "pentameter" comes from the Greek penta- (five) and metron (measure); "iambic" refers to the iamb, a foot of one weak beat followed by one strong beat. Iambic pentameter is the predominant metre in English dramatic and narrative poetry, close in rhythm to natural English speech.

History

Geoffrey Chaucer introduced the ten-syllable line into English poetry in the 14th century, influenced by French and Italian models. In the 16th century the Earl of Surrey developed unrhymed iambic pentameter — blank verse — for his translation of the Aeneid, and Christopher Marlowe and William Shakespeare made it the standard metre of English drama. John Milton used blank verse for the epic Paradise Lost (1667). The rhymed variant in paired lines is the heroic couplet, perfected by Dryden and Pope. Later poets from Wordsworth and Keats to Robert Frost and Derek Walcott continued to write in the metre.

Forms

  • Blank verse — unrhymed iambic pentameter, used in drama and epic.
  • Heroic couplet — pairs of rhyming pentameter lines.
  • Sonnet — fourteen pentameter lines in the Petrarchan or Shakespearean schemes.

Variation

Skilled poets vary the strict pattern with substitutions (trochees, spondees, pyrrhics), feminine endings (an extra weak syllable), and headless lines, to avoid monotony and mimic speech. Shakespeare's lines frequently invert the first foot: "Now is the winter of our discontent" begins with a trochaic substitution.
