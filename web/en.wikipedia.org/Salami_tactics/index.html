Salami slicing tactics

Salami slicing tactics, also known as salami slicing, salami tactics, or salami-slice strategy, is the practice of using a series of many small actions to produce a much larger result that would be difficult or unlawful to perform all at once. The term originates with Hungarian communist leader Mátyás Rákosi, who described how he eliminated the non-Communist opposition in post-war Hungary "cutting them off like slices of salami" (Hungarian: szalámitaktika).

Political use

Rákosi's Hungarian Working People's Party destroyed rival parties piecemeal between 1945 and 1949, demanding a coalition partner's leaders be surrendered one at a time as "fascists" or collaborators until the opposition was gutted. The concept has since been applied to legislative manoeuvres, territorial expansion, and institutional takeovers in which each increment is too small to provoke a decisive response, even though the cumulative effect is large.

Other uses

In finance, "salami slicing" describes fraud schemes in which tiny amounts are skimmed from very large numbers of transactions. In geopolitics, the phrase is used in analysis of incremental faits accomplis, for example gradual militarisation of disputed maritime features. In science publishing, "salami publication" means dividing one study's results into the smallest publishable units to inflate publication counts.
