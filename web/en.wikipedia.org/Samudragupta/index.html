Samudragupta

Samudragupta (r. c. 335 – c. 375 CE) was the second ruler of the Gupta Empire of ancient India, succeeding his father Chandragupta I. Through extensive military campaigns he transformed the Gupta kingdom into an empire dominating much of the Indian subcontinent, and his reign is regarded as the beginning of the "Golden Age" of classical India.

Campaigns

The principal source for his reign is the Allahabad Pillar inscription, a Sanskrit panegyric (prashasti) composed by his courtier Harishena. It describes Samudragupta uprooting numerous kings of northern India (Aryavarta) and annexing their territories, while the kings of the south (Dakshinapatha) were defeated, captured and released as tributaries. Frontier kingdoms such as Samatata, Kamarupa and Nepal acknowledged his suzerainty, and the inscription claims the homage even of the Kushanas, the Shakas and the ruler of Sri Lanka.

Coinage and culture

Samudragupta issued an extensive gold coinage of high purity. His coins portray him in varied roles — archer, battle-axe bearer, slayer of a tiger, and playing the veena — reflecting both martial power and personal accomplishment in music and poetry; tradition celebrated him as Kaviraja, "king of poets". He is said to have performed the Ashvamedha (horse sacrifice) to mark his imperial status. His policy combined conquest in the north with pragmatic tributary arrangements elsewhere, laying the foundation for his successor Chandragupta II's empire.
