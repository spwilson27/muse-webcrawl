Space weather

Space weather
DomainConditions in near-Earth space driven by solar activity
DriversSolar wind, coronal mass ejections, solar flares, energetic particles
EffectsGeomagnetic storms, auroras, satellite damage, radio blackouts, power-grid currents
ForecastingNOAA Space Weather Prediction Center; ESA Space Weather Service

Space weather is the variable conditions in the space environment between the Sun and Earth, driven by the solar wind, solar flares and coronal mass ejections (CMEs). Just as terrestrial weather affects life on the surface, space weather affects satellites, radio communications, navigation signals, power grids and astronauts. Severe events — geomagnetic storms — can disable spacecraft, degrade GPS accuracy, and induce currents in pipelines and transmission lines.

A coronal mass ejection, the main driver of severe geomagnetic storms.

Causes

The Sun continuously emits the solar wind, a stream of charged particles. Disturbances arise when CMEs — billions of tonnes of magnetised plasma — are hurled outward and strike Earth's magnetosphere, or when fast solar-wind streams from coronal holes arrive. Solar flares add bursts of X-rays and energetic particles that ionise the upper atmosphere, causing radio blackouts on the sunlit side of Earth.

Effects and notable events

The Carrington Event of 1859, the strongest recorded storm, set telegraph lines sparking and produced auroras visible in the tropics; a comparable storm today could cause widespread technological damage. In March 1989 a geomagnetic storm collapsed Quebec's power grid, leaving six million people without electricity. In February 2022 a modest storm increased atmospheric drag enough to doom about 40 newly launched Starlink satellites.

Forecasting

Sentinel spacecraft at the L1 point (DSCOVR, ACE, and ESA's planned Vigil mission) sample the solar wind roughly an hour before it reaches Earth, giving forecasters short warning. Ground and space observatories track active regions to predict flares and CMEs days in advance.
