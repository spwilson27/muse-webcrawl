Carl Rogers

Carl Ransom Rogers (8 January 1902 – 4 February 1987) was an American psychologist and one of the founders of humanistic psychology. He developed person-centered therapy (originally client-centered therapy), which transformed psychotherapy by emphasising the client's own capacity for growth within a relationship of empathy, genuineness, and unconditional positive regard.

Life

Rogers was born in Oak Park, Illinois, studied agriculture and then theology before switching to psychology at Columbia University, earning his PhD in 1931. He worked with children in Rochester, New York, taught at Ohio State University and the University of Chicago — where he established a counseling center — and later at the University of Wisconsin and in La Jolla, California.

Person-centered therapy

In works such as Counseling and Psychotherapy (1942), Client-Centered Therapy (1951), and On Becoming a Person (1961), Rogers argued that three conditions in the therapist foster personality change: congruence (genuineness), unconditional positive regard, and accurate empathic understanding. Rather than directing or interpreting, the therapist reflects the client's feelings, trusting the client's "actualizing tendency". Rogers pioneered empirical psychotherapy research, recording and analysing sessions, and was among the first to subject therapy to scientific study.

Influence

Rogers' ideas reshaped counseling, education (student-centered learning), and conflict resolution; late in life he facilitated encounter groups between Protestants and Catholics in Northern Ireland and other divided communities, and was nominated for the Nobel Peace Prize shortly before his death in 1987.
