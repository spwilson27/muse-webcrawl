Morris dance

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Morris dancing is a form of English folk dance usually accompanied by music. It is based on rhythmic stepping and the execution of choreographed figures by a group of dancers, usually wearing bell pads on their shins and sometimes dancing with implements such as sticks, swords or handkerchiefs. A fool or animal character may also feature.

History

The earliest known written reference to Morris dancing in England dates from 1448, when the Goldsmiths' Company in London paid "Morys dauncers" seven shillings. The name may derive from "Moorish dance" (morisco), reflecting Renaissance European court entertainments, though its actual origins are uncertain. By the 17th century Morris was widespread at church ales and festivals; it declined with industrialisation but was collected and revived from 1899, when Cecil Sharp encountered the Headington Quarry dancers in Oxfordshire.

Styles

The main regional traditions are Cotswold Morris, danced with handkerchiefs or sticks by six dancers; North West Morris, a processional style from Lancashire and Cheshire danced in clogs; Border Morris from the Welsh border counties, traditionally with blackened or painted faces (now often replaced by other disguise) and rag jackets; Longsword dance from Yorkshire; and Rapper sword dance from Northumberland and County Durham. Molly dancing from East Anglia is a related form.

Revival and organisation

The Morris Ring (founded 1934), the Morris Federation and Open Morris coordinate hundreds of sides in the United Kingdom and abroad, including teams in North America, Australia and continental Europe. Women's and mixed sides, once controversial, are now common.
