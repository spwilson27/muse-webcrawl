Hippie

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Hippies at an outdoor festival in the late 1960s

A hippie (also spelled hippy) is a member of the counterculture of the 1960s, an anti-authoritarian subculture that began in the United States in the mid-1960s and spread worldwide. Hippies rejected middle-class materialism and conventional social norms, espousing peace, love, personal freedom, communal living and artistic expression, and were closely associated with psychedelic rock, folk music, Eastern spirituality and opposition to the Vietnam War.

Origins

The hippie movement grew out of the Beat Generation of the 1950s and the folk and civil-rights movements. Its epicentre was San Francisco's Haight-Ashbury district, where the 1967 "Summer of Love" drew tens of thousands of young people. Landmark events included the Human Be-In (January 1967), the Monterey Pop Festival (1967) and Woodstock (1969).

Culture

Hippie style favoured long hair, colourful and ethnic clothing, tie-dye, flowers and symbols of peace. Many experimented with psychedelic drugs such as LSD, explored Eastern religions and astrology, lived in communes, and travelled overland along the "hippie trail" to places such as Kathmandu and Goa. Music by artists including The Beatles, Jefferson Airplane, Janis Joplin and The Grateful Dead formed the movement's soundtrack.

Legacy

Although the movement faded in the 1970s, hippie values left a lasting imprint on Western culture — environmentalism, organic food, sexual liberalisation, alternative medicine, world music and the ethos of later movements from punk to the New Age and modern festival culture.
