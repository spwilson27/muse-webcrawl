Māori (Māori: [ˈmaːɔɾi]) are the indigenous Polynesian people of mainland New Zealand (Aotearoa). Māori originated with settlers from East Polynesia, who arrived in New Zealand in several waves of canoe (waka) voyages roughly between 1320 and 1350. Over several centuries in isolation, these settlers developed their own distinctive culture, language, mythology, crafts and performing arts, organised around tribal groups (iwi) and sub-tribes (hapū), led by chiefs (rangatira).

Early contact with Europeans began with Abel Tasman in 1642 and intensified after James Cook's voyages from 1769. Trade in muskets transformed intertribal warfare during the Musket Wars (c. 1807–1842). In 1840, representatives of the British Crown and many Māori chiefs signed the Treaty of Waitangi (Te Tiriti o Waitangi), New Zealand's founding document. Colonisation, the New Zealand Wars of the 1860s, land confiscations, and introduced diseases caused severe population decline and land loss through the 19th century.

History

Polynesian settlement of New Zealand, dated by radiocarbon evidence to around 1280–1350 CE, was the final major chapter of Polynesian voyaging across the Pacific. Settlers brought kūmara (sweet potato), taro, dogs (kurī) and the Pacific rat (kiore), hunted the flightless moa to extinction, and built fortified villages (pā). By the time of European arrival the population is estimated at around 100,000. After near-extinction fears in the late 19th century (population fell to about 42,000 in 1896), the Māori population recovered strongly in the 20th century, alongside urbanisation and a cultural and language renaissance from the 1970s onward.

Culture

Māori culture includes whakapapa (genealogy), oral traditions of migration from Hawaiki, carving (whakairo), weaving (raranga), tattooing (tā moko), the haka war dance, and kapa haka performance. The marae (meeting ground) with its carved meeting house (wharenui) remains the centre of community life. Te reo Māori, an Eastern Polynesian language, became an official language of New Zealand in 1987; immersion education (kōhanga reo, kura kaupapa) has driven a language revival, though the language remains endangered.

Contemporary issues

Since 1975 the Waitangi Tribunal has inquired into breaches of the Treaty, and the Crown has negotiated settlements including apologies, financial redress and return of assets (notably the Waikato-Tainui settlement of 1995 and the Ngāi Tahu settlement of 1998). Māori have dedicated seats in Parliament (established 1867). Despite progress, Māori continue to experience significant disparities in health, income, education and incarceration rates, which remain central topics in New Zealand public life.
