Fraser Canyon Gold Rush

Miners in the Fraser Canyon, 1858
Date1858–1859 (peak)
LocationFraser River, British Columbia
CauseDiscovery of gold in the Fraser River gravel bars
OutcomeCreation of the Colony of British Columbia (1858)

The Fraser Canyon Gold Rush, also known as the Fraser River Gold Rush, began in 1858 when news reached San Francisco that gold had been found along the Fraser River in what is now British Columbia. Within months, some 25,000–30,000 miners — most of them Americans fresh from the California goldfields — flooded into the region, transforming it and leading directly to the founding of the Colony of British Columbia.

Discovery and rush

Gold was found by prospectors working the gravel bars of the Fraser around the winter of 1857–58, following earlier finds shipped to the San Francisco mint by the Hudson's Bay Company. Miners travelled by steamer to Victoria and then upriver to diggings at Hill's Bar, Yale, Boston Bar and Lytton. The influx overwhelmed the Hudson's Bay Company's loose administration of the mainland and alarmed Governor James Douglas, who feared American annexation.

Consequences

In response, Britain proclaimed the Colony of British Columbia in November 1858, with Douglas as governor and New Westminster as its planned capital. Tensions between miners and the Indigenous Nlaka'pamux people flared into the Fraser Canyon War of 1858, which ended in negotiated peace treaties. The rush declined as the easily worked bars gave out, but many miners moved on to the richer Cariboo Gold Rush of the 1860s, and the colony's roads, towns and institutions — including the Cariboo Road — were lasting products of the gold era.
