Eukaryote

The eukaryotes constitute the domain of Eukarya, organisms whose cells have a nucleus enclosed by a nuclear envelope. They are distinguished from the prokaryotes — bacteria and archaea — by this membrane-bound nucleus and by numerous other internal structures called organelles. Eukaryotes include all animals, plants, fungi, and the diverse unicellular protists; they range in size from microscopic single cells to the largest organisms on Earth.

Cell structure

Eukaryotic cells are typically much larger than prokaryotic cells and compartmentalize their biochemistry into organelles: the nucleus stores the genome on linear chromosomes; mitochondria generate most of the cell's chemical energy (ATP); in plants and algae, chloroplasts carry out photosynthesis; the endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus process proteins and lipids; and a dynamic cytoskeleton of actin filaments and microtubules maintains shape and enables movement.

Origin

Eukaryotes arose through endosymbiosis: an archaeal host cell engulfed an alphaproteobacterium that became the mitochondrion, roughly 1.6–2.1 billion years ago. Chloroplasts later derived from engulfed cyanobacteria. The last eukaryotic common ancestor (LECA) already possessed a nucleus, mitochondria, sexual reproduction (meiosis), and a cytoskeleton.

Diversity

Modern classification groups eukaryotes into supergroups such as Opisthokonta (animals and fungi), Archaeplastida (plants and algae), SAR (stramenopiles, alveolates, rhizarians), and Excavata. Multicellularity evolved independently many times, in animals, plants, fungi, and several algal lineages.

Reproduction

Eukaryotes reproduce asexually by mitosis and sexually by meiosis followed by cell fusion, producing genetic diversity through recombination. Their life cycles may be haploid-dominant, diploid-dominant, or alternate between generations.
