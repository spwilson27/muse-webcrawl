Milankovitch cycles

Milankovitch cycles describe the collective effects of changes in the Earth's movements on its climate over thousands of years. The theory was worked out by Serbian geophysicist and astronomer Milutin Milanković in the 1920s–1940s, building on earlier work by James Croll and others. The cycles arise from variations in orbital eccentricity, axial tilt (obliquity), and axial precession, which together alter the distribution of solar radiation received by the Earth and help pace the ice ages.

The three cycles

Eccentricity

The shape of Earth's orbit varies from nearly circular to mildly elliptical (eccentricity 0.0005 to 0.06) with dominant periods around 100,000 and 413,000 years. Eccentricity chiefly modulates the amplitude of the precession effect and slightly changes total annual insolation.

Obliquity

The tilt of Earth's axis relative to its orbital plane oscillates between about 22.1° and 24.5° over ~41,000 years. Greater tilt increases seasonal contrast at high latitudes — a key control on ice-sheet growth, since cool summers allow winter snow to persist.

Precession

The direction of the axis and of the orbit's long axis rotate with combined periods near 19,000–23,000 years, determining whether a hemisphere's summer occurs near perihelion (closest approach to the Sun) or aphelion, and thus the intensity of its seasons.

Evidence

Milanković's theory was largely vindicated in 1976 when Hays, Imbrie, and Shackleton analysed deep-sea sediment cores and found climate oscillations at periods matching the orbital cycles. Ice cores from Antarctica and Greenland later confirmed the pacing. Ice ages of the past ~1 million years have followed roughly the 100,000-year eccentricity cycle, while earlier Pleistocene glaciations followed the 41,000-year obliquity rhythm — the "Mid-Pleistocene Transition" remains an active research problem.
