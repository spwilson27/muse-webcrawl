Voyager Golden Record

The Voyager Golden Records are two phonograph records, one on each of the Voyager spacecraft launched in 1977. The records contain sounds and images selected to portray the diversity of life and culture on Earth, and are intended for any intelligent extraterrestrial life form who may find them. They function as a kind of time capsule and message in a bottle, carrying greetings in 55 languages, music from many cultures, natural and human-made sounds, and 115 images encoded in analogue form.

Background

The record was produced by NASA with a committee chaired by astronomer Carl Sagan of Cornell University. Sagan had previously helped design the plaque carried by Pioneer 10 and 11. The committee, including creative director Ann Druyan, worked under tight deadlines to assemble the contents in 1977. The records themselves are 12-inch gold-plated copper discs, housed in gold-plated aluminum covers electroplated with uranium-238—whose decay can be used to date the record's age—together with a cartridge, stylus, and pictorial instructions for playback at 16⅔ revolutions per minute.

Contents

Greetings and sounds

The record opens with greetings in 55 languages, from ancient Akkadian to modern Wu Chinese, followed by United Nations greetings, whale greetings, and a 90-minute essay of "The Sounds of Earth": thunder, wind, surf, birdsong, a baby's cry, a kiss, Morse code, a train, an F-111 flyby, and Chuck Berry's "Johnny B. Goode" among the musical selections.

Music

The 90-minute music selection includes Bach's Brandenburg Concerto No. 2 and "Well-Tempered Clavier", Beethoven's Fifth Symphony and String Quartet No. 13 (the Cavatina), Mozart's The Magic Flute, Stravinsky's The Rite of Spring, gamelan music from Java, a Navajo night chant, Indian raga performed by Kesarbai Kerkar, Blind Willie Johnson's "Dark Was the Night", Louis Armstrong, and Chuck Berry.

Images

The 115 images include scientific diagrams and photographs of landscapes, architecture, food, animals, and human beings in everyday activities, along with definitions of basic mathematics and physics intended to establish a shared frame of reference.

Journey

The two records are aboard Voyager 1 and Voyager 2, now in interstellar space. In about 40,000 years, Voyager 1 will pass within 1.6 light-years of the star Gliese 445. Because the records were designed to last a billion years, they may outlive both the spacecraft and, conceivably, the Earth itself.
