Kontor

A Kontor (from Middle Low German kontor, "office," ultimately from Latin computare) was a fortified foreign trading post of the Hanseatic League. The Kontore were the League's principal bases abroad — self-governing merchant enclaves with their own warehouses, churches, courts, and elected aldermen.

The four great Kontore

Four Kontore stood above the rest. In Novgorod, the Peterhof was the eastern terminus of Baltic trade, exchanging western cloth and metalware for Russian furs, wax, and hemp. At Bergen in Norway, the German merchants of Bryggen dominated the stockfish trade for centuries. In London, the Steelyard (Stalhof) on the Thames enjoyed wide privileges until its merchants were expelled in 1598. At Bruges, the kontor organised trade with Flanders and the Mediterranean until the harbour silted and business shifted to Antwerp.

Organisation and life

Each Kontor was governed by elected aldermen (Oldermänner) and bound its residents by strict statutes: merchants and apprentices lived communally, were usually forbidden to marry locally, and submitted to the Kontor's own jurisdiction. Smaller establishments, often called factories or branches, existed in dozens of ports from Lisbon to Reval.

Decline

As national states consolidated and Dutch and English competition grew, the Kontore lost their privileges; Novgorod's was closed by Ivan III in 1494, London's in 1598, and Bergen's German office was dissolved in 1754, the last to survive.
