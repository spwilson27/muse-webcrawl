Renaissance music

Renaissance music is the music of the European Renaissance, conventionally dated from about 1400 to 1600. It is characterized by the flowering of vocal polyphony — the weaving together of several independent melodic lines — in both sacred genres (Mass, motet) and secular ones (madrigal, chanson, frottola, villancico), and by a growing emphasis on smooth consonance, text expression, and the bass line as a foundation for harmony.

Overview

The period begins with the Franco-Flemish (Netherlandish) composers who dominated European music for over a century — Guillaume Du Fay, Johannes Ockeghem, Josquin des Prez, Orlande de Lassus — and who carried the polyphonic style to the courts and chapels of Italy, France, Spain, and the Empire. The invention of music printing, beginning with Ottaviano Petrucci's Harmonice Musices Odhecaton (1501), spread music widely and made amateur performance a domestic pastime.

Characteristics

Renaissance counterpoint treats consonance as the norm, with dissonance carefully prepared and resolved, generally by step. Imitative polyphony — voices entering successively with the same melodic idea — became a central technique, especially in the motet and the parody Mass of the later sixteenth century. Modes, inherited from chant theory, gradually gave way toward the major–minor tonal system at the end of the period.

Sacred music

The Mass Ordinary and the Latin motet were the prestige genres. Giovanni Pierluigi da Palestrina in Rome, William Byrd and Thomas Tallis in England, and Tomás Luis de Victoria in Spain represent the High Renaissance style. The Protestant Reformation produced new forms: Luther's German chorales, the metrical psalms of the Calvinist tradition, and the English anthem; the Counter-Reformation's Council of Trent debates about intelligibility of text became associated (partly legendarily) with Palestrina's Missa Papae Marcelli.

Secular and instrumental music

The Italian madrigal — set by composers from Adrian Willaert and Cipriano de Rore to Luca Marenzio, Carlo Gesualdo, and Claudio Monteverdi — became the era's most expressive and experimental genre, using chromaticism and word-painting. Instrumental music grew in importance: dance collections, intabulations for lute and vihuela, keyboard fantasias and toccatas (notably in Venice under Andrea and Giovanni Gabrieli), and consorts of viols. In Venice, polychoral writing for multiple choirs exploited the architecture of St Mark's Basilica.

Transition to the Baroque

Around 1600, the Florentine Camerata's experiments with accompanied solo song (monody) and the new continuo practice produced opera — Monteverdi's L'Orfeo (1607) is the first opera still regularly performed — inaugurating the Baroque era.
