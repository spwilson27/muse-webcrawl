A mockumentary — a portmanteau of mock and documentary — is a type of film or television programme depicting fictional events but presented as a documentary, typically for comic or satirical effect. Mockumentaries borrow the grammar of non-fiction film — talking-head interviews, fly-on-the-wall camerawork, voice-over narration, archival "footage", and lower-third captions — and apply it to invented subjects, producing humour from the friction between the sober form and the absurd content.

History

Precursors include Orson Welles's notorious 1938 radio "news bulletin" of The War of the Worlds and Luis Buñuel's sardonic pseudo-ethnographic film Las Hurdes (1933). Peter Watkins's The War Game (1965), a fictional nuclear attack on Britain rendered as a BBC documentary, was so convincing it was withheld from broadcast for twenty years. Woody Allen's Take the Money and Run (1969) and Zelig (1983), the Beatles film A Hard Day's Night (1964), and the Rutles' All You Need Is Cash (1978) established the comic form.

The genre's landmark is This Is Spinal Tap (1984), Rob Reiner's largely improvised portrait of a fading heavy-metal band, which set the template: a fictional documentary crew following deluded subjects whose self-image far exceeds their reality. Christopher Guest, a Spinal Tap co-creator, extended the style in Waiting for Guffman (1996), Best in Show (2000), and A Mighty Wind (2003).

Television

From 2001, the British sitcom The Office, created by Ricky Gervais and Stephen Merchant, popularised the mockumentary sitcom: single-camera, no laugh track, characters confessing to an unseen crew. Its American adaptation and successors such as Parks and Recreation, Modern Family, and Abbott Elementary made the style a mainstream comedy standard. Sacha Baron Cohen's Borat (2006) added a confrontational variant, placing a fictional character among real, unsuspecting people.

Characteristics

  • Documentary devices used ironically: expert interviews, reconstructions, "archive" material.
  • Comedy arising from dramatic irony — the audience sees what the subject cannot.
  • Frequent improvisation and handheld, naturalistic photography.
  • Satire of institutions, celebrity, media, and fandom as much as of the subjects themselves.
