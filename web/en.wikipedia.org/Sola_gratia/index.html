Sola gratia


Martin Luther, whose theology centred on salvation by grace alone

Sola gratia (Latin for "by grace alone") is one of the five solae of the Protestant Reformation, expressing the belief that salvation comes by the grace of God alone, not through any merit or good works of the sinner. It is a foundational doctrine of Lutheran, Reformed, and many other Protestant traditions, in contrast, as the Reformers saw it, with the late medieval Catholic view that grace and human cooperation—including the sacramental system and works of merit—together effect salvation.

Doctrine

Proponents argue from passages such as Ephesians 2:8–9 ("For by grace you have been saved through faith... not a result of works, so that no one may boast") that grace is an entirely free, unmerited gift. In Reformed theology, grace is commonly described as irresistible for the elect, while Lutheran theology teaches that grace can be resisted but that conversion is wholly God's work (monergism). The doctrine is closely linked to sola fide (faith alone): faith itself is regarded as a gift of grace rather than a meritorious human achievement.

History

The phrase crystallised in Reformation debates of the 16th century, though its roots are traced by Protestants to Augustine of Hippo's anti-Pelagian writings. The Council of Trent (1545–1563) rejected the Reformers' formulations while itself teaching that justification is initiated by grace. In 1999, the Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification, signed by the Lutheran World Federation and the Catholic Church, announced a consensus that salvation is by grace through faith, narrowing (without eliminating) historic differences.

The five solae

Sola gratia is grouped with sola scriptura (Scripture alone), sola fide (faith alone), solus Christus (Christ alone), and soli Deo gloria (glory to God alone) as summary slogans of Reformation theology, though the canonical list of five took shape in later Protestant scholarship.
