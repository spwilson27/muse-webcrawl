Actinopterygii

KingdomAnimalia
PhylumChordata
ClassActinopterygii
Known species~33,000 (over half of all vertebrates)

Actinopterygii, the ray-finned fishes, is a class of bony fish (Osteichthyes). Their fins are webs of skin supported by bony or horny spines called rays, in contrast to the fleshy, lobed fins of the Sarcopterygii (lobe-finned fishes). Ray-finned fishes dominate the world's waters, comprising more than 99% of the over 33,000 described species of fish and nearly half of all vertebrate species.

Anatomy

Characteristic features include a swim bladder used for buoyancy (derived from an ancestral lung), ganoid or cycloid/ctenoid scales (reduced or lost in some groups), a homocercal tail in most modern forms, and jaws with a mobile premaxilla in the advanced teleosts. Most are covered in scales and breathe through gills protected by an operculum.

Classification and evolution

The class includes the Chondrostei (sturgeons and paddlefish), the Holostei (gars and bowfin), and the overwhelming majority in the Teleostei, with over 40 orders including percomorphs, cypriniforms (carps and minnows) and salmoniforms. Ray-finned fishes first appear in the fossil record in the Late Silurian, about 425 million years ago, and radiated massively after the end-Permian extinction and again in the Cenozoic.

Importance

Teleosts include most of the world's commercial food fish — tuna, cod, salmon, herring and anchovies — and popular aquarium fish, and serve as key model organisms in biology, such as the zebrafish (Danio rerio).
