Jacques Anquetil (8 January 1934 – 18 November 1987) was a French road racing cyclist and the first cyclist to win the Tour de France five times (1957 and 1961–1964). Nicknamed "Maître Jacques" and "Monsieur Chrono", he was a supreme time-trialist whose elegant, aerodynamic style set new standards, and his rivalry with the crowd favourite Raymond Poulidor defined French cycling in the 1960s.

Career

Anquetil turned professional in 1953 and immediately won the Grand Prix des Nations time trial, which he won nine times in all. He took his first Tour de France in 1957 at the age of 23, then, after a gap, won four consecutive Tours from 1961 to 1964. He also won the Giro d'Italia twice (1960, 1964) and the Vuelta a España in 1963, making him the first rider to win all three Grand Tours. In 1965 he performed the remarkable feat of winning the eight-day Dauphiné Libéré and then, hours after finishing, starting and winning the 560 km Bordeaux–Paris race behind motor-paced dernys.

Style and rivalry

Where the romantic Poulidor attacked in the mountains and won the public's heart, Anquetil was cool, calculating and frankly professional: he raced for money, managed his effort meticulously, and built his victories in time trials. He was candid about the era's use of amphetamines, famously remarking that one could not win the Tour "on mineral water alone". In 1967 he set an hour record of 47.493 km that stood for years, though it was not ratified at the time because he refused a post-race doping control.

Later life

Anquetil retired in 1969 and became a farmer, broadcaster and newspaper columnist. He died of stomach cancer in 1987, aged 53, and remains one of the most admired — and debated — champions in French sporting history.
