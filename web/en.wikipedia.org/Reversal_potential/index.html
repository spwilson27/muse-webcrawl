Reversal potential

The reversal potential of an ion is the membrane potential at which the direction of ionic current through an open ion channel reverses. At a membrane potential below (more negative than) the reversal potential, ions flow in one direction; above it, they flow in the opposite direction, because the electrochemical driving force changes sign. For a channel permeable to a single ion species, the reversal potential equals that ion's equilibrium potential, given by the Nernst equation.

In electrophysiology, the reversal potential is measured experimentally by recording the current through a channel (or synaptic current) at a series of holding potentials and finding the voltage at which the current is zero. It is a key diagnostic for identifying which ions a channel carries.

Nernst equation

For a single ion, the equilibrium (reversal) potential is

E = (RT / zF) ln([ion]out / [ion]in)

where R is the gas constant, T the absolute temperature, z the ion's valence, F the Faraday constant, and the terms in brackets the concentrations outside and inside the cell. At body temperature, typical values in a mammalian neuron are approximately −90 mV for potassium (EK), +60 mV for sodium (ENa), and around −65 mV for chloride (ECl), reflecting their concentration gradients across the membrane.

Multi-ion channels

Many channels are permeable to more than one ion. Their reversal potential lies between the equilibrium potentials of the permeant ions, weighted by their relative permeabilities, as described by the Goldman–Hodgkin–Katz voltage equation. For example, the nicotinic acetylcholine receptor at the neuromuscular junction passes both Na+ and K+ readily, so its reversal potential is near 0 mV: opening these channels at rest therefore drives the membrane toward 0 mV, producing the depolarizing end-plate potential. Measuring how a channel's reversal potential shifts when ion concentrations are changed allows its permeability ratios to be calculated.

Synaptic reversal potentials

Synaptic currents reverse at characteristic potentials that reveal the sign of the synapse. AMPA-receptor-mediated excitatory currents reverse near 0 mV, hence they are inward (depolarizing) at resting potential. GABAA-receptor currents are carried mainly by chloride and reverse near ECl; depending on the cell's chloride gradient they may be hyperpolarizing, shunting, or even slightly depolarizing while still inhibitory in effect. NMDA receptor currents also reverse near 0 mV but are additionally gated by voltage-dependent magnesium block. The concept is central to voltage-clamp analysis of synaptic transmission.
