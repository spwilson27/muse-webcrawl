Prince Albert of Saxe-Coburg and Gotha (26 August 1819 – 14 December 1861) was the husband and consort of Queen Victoria. Born in the Duchy of Saxe-Coburg and Gotha to a family connected to many of Europe's ruling monarchs, he married his first cousin Victoria in 1840 and became her closest adviser and an influential figure in British public life.

Marriage and role

Although initially given no formal role and denied access to state papers, Albert gradually became the Queen's private secretary in all but name and a central figure in the administration of the royal household. The couple had nine children, whose marriages into Europe's royal houses earned Victoria the nickname "grandmother of Europe". In 1857 Victoria formally granted him the title Prince Consort.

Public work

Albert promoted education, science, industry and social reform. He was the driving force behind the Great Exhibition of 1851, whose profits were used to purchase the South Kensington estate that became home to the Victoria and Albert Museum, the Science Museum, the Natural History Museum and Imperial College — the area nicknamed "Albertopolis". He served as Chancellor of the University of Cambridge, modernised the royal estates at Osborne and Balmoral, and supported causes including the abolition of slavery. His intervention in the Trent Affair in 1861 helped avert war between Britain and the United States.

Death and legacy

Worn down by overwork and chronic illness, Albert died at Windsor Castle on 14 December 1861, probably of typhoid fever, aged 42. Victoria entered deep mourning, wearing black for the rest of her reign. Memorials across the empire include the Albert Memorial and the Royal Albert Hall in London.
