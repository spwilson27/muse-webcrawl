Yttrium is a chemical element with the symbol Y and atomic number 39. It is a silvery-metallic transition metal chemically similar to the lanthanides, and is often classified among the "rare-earth elements". Yttrium is almost always found combined with lanthanides in rare-earth minerals and is never found in nature as a free element.

History

The element is named after the village of Ytterby in Sweden, near which the mineral gadolinite was found. In 1794 the Finnish chemist Johan Gadolin isolated a new earth, "yttria", from the mineral; the metal itself was first prepared in impure form by Friedrich Wöhler in 1828. Ytterby also lent its name to ytterbium, terbium, and erbium.

Properties and compounds

Yttrium is relatively stable in air, forms the oxide Y2O3, and has a single naturally occurring isotope, 89Y. Its chemistry is dominated by the +3 oxidation state. Because its ionic radius lies among those of the heavy lanthanides, yttrium behaves in nature like a heavy rare earth despite its much lower atomic mass.

Applications

Yttrium compounds have many high-technology uses: yttrium aluminium garnet (YAG) is the host crystal of Nd:YAG lasers; yttrium iron garnet (YIG) is used in microwave filters and radar; yttria-stabilised zirconia is a tough ceramic used in thermal barrier coatings and oxygen sensors; and the yttrium-barium-copper-oxide compound YBa2Cu3O7 (YBCO), discovered in 1987, was the first superconductor to operate above the boiling point of liquid nitrogen. Yttrium phosphors historically produced the red colour in cathode-ray tube displays and are used in LEDs.
