Dirichlet's theorem on arithmetic progressions

In number theory, Dirichlet's theorem, also called the Dirichlet prime number theorem, states that for any two positive coprime integers a and d, there are infinitely many primes of the form a + nd, where n is also a positive integer. In other words, there are infinitely many primes that are congruent to a modulo d.

History

The result was conjectured by Euler and Legendre and proved by Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet in 1837, in a paper that founded analytic number theory. Dirichlet's proof combined analysis and algebra, introducing characters modulo d and the L-functions now named after him.

Sketch of proof

The proof studies the Dirichlet L-series L(s, χ) for all characters χ modulo d and shows that the product of these series diverges at s = 1 in a way that forces infinitely many primes in each coprime residue class. The crucial hardest step is proving that L(1, χ) ≠ 0 for non-principal characters — now known to be intimately connected with the possible existence of a Siegel zero.

Generalisations

Dirichlet's theorem is generalised by the Chebotarev density theorem in algebraic number theory and by the Green–Tao theorem in the additive direction. Quantitative forms estimate the number of such primes up to x, approximately x / (φ(d) log x), uniformly in d for small moduli (Siegel–Walfisz theorem).
