Letterboxing

Letterboxing (hobby)
OriginDartmoor, England, 1854
EquipmentCompass, clues or guidebook, personal rubber stamp, ink pad, logbook
Related activitiesGeocaching, orienteering, hiking

Letterboxing is an outdoor hobby that combines hiking, orienteering, and puzzle-solving: participants hide small weatherproof boxes in public outdoor places and distribute clues to their locations, while seekers who find a box leave their mark in its logbook with a personal rubber stamp and record the box's stamp in their own. The term is also used in film and television for the practice of displaying a wide image within a narrower frame using mattes (see letterboxing (filming)); this article concerns the hobby.

History

The hobby traces to 1854, when Dartmoor guide James Perrott left a bottle for visiting cards at Cranmere Pool in the north of Dartmoor, England. Walkers began leaving self-addressed postcards in the box, to be carried back and posted by the next visitor — hence "letterbox." By the twentieth century there were dozens of boxes on the moor; today Dartmoor alone holds hundreds, catalogued in an annually updated guide, and the pastime has spread worldwide.

How it works

A letterbox contains at minimum a logbook and a unique, often hand-carved, rubber stamp. Clues may be simple compass bearings from a landmark, or elaborate riddles, maps, and puzzles. Finders imprint the box's stamp in their own notebook and leave their "signature stamp" in the logbook; boxes are then re-hidden exactly as found. Traditional etiquette forbids moving boxes and discourages spoiling clue locations publicly.

Relation to geocaching

Letterboxing predates GPS-based geocaching by nearly 150 years and relies on clues and compass work rather than coordinates, though hybrid "geocaching letterbox" caches exist. North American letterboxing was popularised after a 1998 Smithsonian magazine article, and online clue repositories now serve a large community of "boxers."
