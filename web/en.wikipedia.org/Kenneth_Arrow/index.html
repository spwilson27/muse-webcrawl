Kenneth Joseph Arrow (23 August 1921 – 21 February 2017) was an American economist, mathematician, writer, and political theorist, regarded as one of the most influential economists of the 20th century. He won the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in 1972 with John Hicks — at 51, still among the youngest recipients — for pioneering contributions to general economic equilibrium theory and welfare theory.

Impossibility theorem

Arrow's doctoral dissertation, Social Choice and Individual Values (1951), proved his famous impossibility theorem: no rank-order voting system can convert individual preferences into a consistent collective ordering while satisfying a short list of seemingly modest fairness conditions (unrestricted domain, non-dictatorship, Pareto efficiency, and independence of irrelevant alternatives). The result founded modern social choice theory and reshaped political science and welfare economics.

General equilibrium and welfare

Working with Gérard Debreu, Arrow provided the first rigorous proof of the existence of a competitive equilibrium (the Arrow–Debreu model, 1954) and clarified the conditions under which markets are efficient — the fundamental theorems of welfare economics — as well as the many ways real markets can fail, notably through externalities and missing markets.

Other contributions

Arrow's 1963 paper "Uncertainty and the Welfare Economics of Medical Care" founded the field of health economics, analysing how uncertainty and asymmetric information pervade health care. He made major contributions to endogenous growth theory (learning-by-doing, 1962), the economics of information and discrimination, inventory theory, and risk bearing. Late in life he wrote prominently on climate change economics, arguing for strong early action on emissions abatement.

Life and honours

The son of Romanian-Jewish immigrants, Arrow studied at City College and Columbia, served as a weather officer in the U.S. Army Air Forces during the Second World War, and spent most of his career at Stanford and Harvard. He received the John Bates Clark Medal (1957) and the National Medal of Science (2004), and supervised or mentored several Nobel laureates. He died at his home in Palo Alto in 2017, aged 95.
