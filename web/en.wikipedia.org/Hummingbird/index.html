Hummingbird

Hummingbirds are small birds of the family Trochilidae, native to the Americas. They are known for their ability to hover in mid-air by rapidly flapping their wings — 12 to 80 times per second depending on the species — and are the only birds that can fly backwards. Their English name comes from the humming sound created by their beating wings.

Biology

Hummingbirds are among the smallest warm-blooded vertebrates; the bee hummingbird of Cuba, about 5–6 cm long and under 2 grams in weight, is the smallest bird in the world. They have the highest mass-specific metabolic rate of any homeothermic animal, with heart rates exceeding 1,200 beats per minute in some species; to conserve energy at night or when food is scarce they enter torpor. Many species show brilliant iridescent plumage produced by structural coloration in the feathers.

Feeding

Hummingbirds feed mainly on floral nectar, which supplies the energy for their flight, and also catch insects and spiders for protein. Their long bills and extensible, forked tongues are adapted to particular flowers, and hummingbirds are important pollinators of many New World plants. An individual may visit hundreds of flowers a day and can consume more than its body weight in nectar.

Distribution and species

More than 360 species are recognised, distributed from Alaska to Tierra del Fuego, with the greatest diversity in the tropical Andes of Colombia and Ecuador. Some North American species, such as the ruby-throated hummingbird, are long-distance migrants, crossing the Gulf of Mexico on migration.
