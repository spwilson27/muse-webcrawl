German Expressionism

German Expressionism was a modernist movement in painting, literature, theatre, architecture, and especially cinema that emerged in Germany in the early twentieth century. Rejecting naturalism, Expressionist artists sought to convey inner emotional experience through distortion, exaggeration, and vivid, non-naturalistic colour and form.

The jagged sets of The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari (1920) epitomise Expressionist film design.

Painting

The movement began with the artist groups Die Brücke (The Bridge, founded in Dresden in 1905 by Ernst Ludwig Kirchner and others) and Der Blaue Reiter (The Blue Rider, founded in Munich in 1911 by Wassily Kandinsky and Franz Marc). Their work used violent brushwork and clashing colour to express anxiety about the modern world, an anxiety deepened by the First World War, in which several of the artists were killed.

Cinema

Expressionist cinema flourished in Weimar Germany between 1919 and the late 1920s. Films such as Robert Wiene's The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari (1920), F. W. Murnau's Nosferatu (1922), and Fritz Lang's Metropolis (1927) used painted shadows, tilted sets, and stylised acting to externalise psychological states. Many of its techniques and personnel — driven into exile by the Nazi regime, which condemned Expressionism as "degenerate art" — profoundly influenced film noir and horror cinema in Hollywood.
