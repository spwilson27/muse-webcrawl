Rayleigh–Taylor instability

The Rayleigh–Taylor instability, or RT instability (after Lord Rayleigh and G. I. Taylor), is an instability of an interface between two fluids of different densities which occurs when the lighter fluid is pushing the heavier fluid. Examples include water suspended above oil in the Earth's gravitational field, mushroom clouds such as those from volcanic eruptions and atmospheric nuclear explosions, supernova explosions in which expanding core gas is accelerated into denser shell gas, and instabilities in plasma fusion reactors.

Mechanism

Rayleigh–Taylor instability occurs when a denser fluid rests on top of a lighter fluid in a gravitational field, or equivalently when a lighter fluid accelerates a heavier one. Any small perturbation of the interface grows: bubbles of light fluid rise into the heavy fluid while spikes of heavy fluid fall into the light fluid, eventually forming the characteristic mushroom-shaped structures.

Growth rate

For two inviscid fluids, linear stability analysis gives a growth rate n proportional to the square root of g k A, where g is the acceleration, k is the perturbation wavenumber, and A is the Atwood number, (ρheavy − ρlight)/(ρheavy + ρlight). Surface tension and viscosity act to suppress growth at short wavelengths.

Applications

The instability is important in astrophysics (supernova remnant structure), inertial confinement fusion, geophysics (salt dome formation and mantle dynamics), and engineering. Closely related phenomena include the Richtmyer–Meshkov instability (shock-driven) and the Kelvin–Helmholtz instability (shear-driven).
