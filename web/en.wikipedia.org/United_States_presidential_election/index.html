United States presidential election

The election of the president and vice president of the United States is an indirect election in which citizens cast ballots for a slate of electors of the Electoral College, who in turn directly elect the president and vice president. The process is regulated by a combination of federal and state laws. Presidential elections occur quadrennially — every four years — on Election Day, the Tuesday after the first Monday in November.

Process

Candidates are nominated through primary elections and caucuses held by the political parties, followed by national nominating conventions. In the general election, each state receives electoral votes equal to its number of senators and representatives; there are 538 electors in total, and 270 votes are needed to win. Nearly all states award all their electors to the plurality winner of their popular vote; Maine and Nebraska use a congressional-district method. Electors meet in their state capitals in December to cast the official ballots, which Congress counts in January.

History

The system was established by Article II of the Constitution and modified by the Twelfth Amendment (1804), after the election of 1800 produced a tie. Five presidents have won the Electoral College while losing the national popular vote, most recently in 2000 and 2016, fueling recurring debate about reform. The first election, in 1788–89, unanimously chose George Washington. Terms begin at noon on January 20 following the election (Inauguration Day), a date fixed by the Twentieth Amendment.

Eligibility

Under the Constitution, a candidate must be a natural-born citizen of the United States, at least 35 years old, and a resident of the United States for 14 years. The Twenty-second Amendment limits presidents to two elected terms.
