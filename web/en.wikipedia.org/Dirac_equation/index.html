In particle physics, the Dirac equation is a relativistic wave equation derived by British physicist Paul Dirac in 1928. In its free form, it describes all spin-½ massive particles, such as electrons and quarks, for which parity is a symmetry. It is consistent with both the principles of quantum mechanics and the theory of special relativity, and was the first theory to account fully for special relativity in the context of quantum mechanics.

(iħγμ∂μ − mc)ψ = 0

Predictions

The equation demands the existence of negative-energy solutions, which Dirac interpreted in 1931 as predicting a new particle with the mass of the electron but opposite charge — the positron, the first predicted antimatter particle, discovered by Carl Anderson in 1932. The equation also naturally explains electron spin and yields the correct fine structure of the hydrogen spectrum. Its solutions are four-component spinors.

Significance

The Dirac equation is regarded as one of the most beautiful in physics; it has been called the relativistic counterpart of the Schrödinger equation and forms the foundation of quantum field theory and quantum electrodynamics. The equation is inscribed on Dirac's commemorative plaque in Westminster Abbey in the compact form (i∂̸ − m)ψ = 0.
