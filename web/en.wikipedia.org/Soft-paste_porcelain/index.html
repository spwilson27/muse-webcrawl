Soft-paste porcelain

Soft-paste porcelain is a type of ceramic material developed in Europe in the sixteenth to eighteenth centuries in attempts to reproduce Chinese hard-paste (true) porcelain. It is fired at lower temperatures than hard-paste porcelain — roughly 1,100–1,200 °C — and its body is less vitrified, somewhat porous, and prone to slumping in the kiln.

Composition

Recipes typically combined white clay with a glassy frit (ground glass, quartz, or calcined flint) and sometimes soapstone or bone ash. Early successes included Medici porcelain in Florence (1575–87) and the wares of Rouen, Saint-Cloud, Chantilly, Mennecy, and Vincennes–Sèvres in France, and Chelsea, Bow, and Worcester in England.

Properties

Soft-paste bodies have a warm, creamy appearance and take enamel colours beautifully, but they are softer, chip more easily, and can be cut with a file; a knife steel will scratch them. The discovery of kaolin deposits in Europe and experiments such as bone china largely displaced soft-paste porcelain by the late eighteenth century.
