Quantum chemistry

Quantum chemistry, also called molecular quantum mechanics, is a branch of physical chemistry focused on the application of quantum mechanics to chemical systems, particularly towards the calculation of electronic contributions to physical and chemical properties of molecules, materials, and solutions. Quantum chemists use the Schrödinger equation to describe the behaviour of electrons in atoms and molecules, and thereby explain and predict chemical bonding, reaction mechanisms, spectroscopy and reactivity.

History

The field began in 1927 when Walter Heitler and Fritz London successfully calculated the ground state of the hydrogen molecule — the first application of quantum mechanics to a chemical bond. Shortly afterwards, Robert Mulliken and Friedrich Hund developed molecular orbital theory, while Linus Pauling and John Slater developed valence bond theory. The two approaches became the twin foundations of the discipline.

Methods

Because an exact solution of the Schrödinger equation is impossible for systems of more than one electron, quantum chemistry relies on approximation methods:

  • Hartree–Fock method — each electron moves in the average field of the others.
  • Post-Hartree–Fock methods — configuration interaction, coupled cluster and many-body perturbation theory (MP2), which recover electron correlation.
  • Density functional theory (DFT) — based on the electron density rather than the many-electron wavefunction; hugely popular for its balance of accuracy and cost.
  • Semi-empirical methods — simplified treatments parametrised against experimental data.

The Born–Oppenheimer approximation, which separates nuclear and electronic motion, underlies nearly all of these calculations.

Applications

Quantum chemistry is used to predict molecular structures, reaction rates and pathways, spectroscopic signatures, and properties of new materials and drug candidates. Modern computational chemistry software such as Gaussian, ORCA and VASP implements these methods on high-performance computers.
