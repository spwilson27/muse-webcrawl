Dancing mania

Dancing mania (also known as dancing plague, choreomania, and St. Vitus's Dance) was a social phenomenon that occurred primarily in mainland Europe between the 14th and 17th centuries. It involved groups of people dancing erratically, sometimes thousands at a time, occasionally until they collapsed from exhaustion or died.

Outbreaks

One of the earliest known outbreaks followed the flooding of 1374 along the Rhine, and the most famous episode occurred in Strasbourg in July 1518, when a woman reportedly began dancing in the street and was joined by hundreds of others over the following weeks. Contemporary explanations invoked divine punishment, possession, or the curse of St. Vitus. Authorities sometimes encouraged the dancing, hiring musicians and clearing stages in the belief that the afflicted could dance the affliction away.

Explanations

Modern hypotheses include mass psychogenic illness triggered by extreme stress, famine, and religious fervour, and ergot poisoning from fungi growing on damp rye, though the latter is contested because ergotism typically causes convulsions and restricted blood flow rather than sustained dancing. Dancing mania is often cited as an early example of a dancing-related mass hysteria and has been compared with tarantism in southern Italy.
