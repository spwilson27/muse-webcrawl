Genome annotation

Genome annotation is the process of identifying the locations of genes and other functional elements in a genome sequence and determining what they do. It turns raw DNA sequence — the output of a sequencing and assembly project — into the biological information needed for further research. Annotation proceeds in stages of increasing complexity, from finding genes to assigning their functions.

Structural annotation

Structural annotation identifies genomic elements: protein-coding genes (with their exon–intron structure), non-coding RNA genes, promoters, repetitive elements and other features. Gene prediction combines ab initio statistical models (such as hidden Markov models trained on known gene structure) with evidence-based methods that align transcripts (ESTs, RNA-seq reads) and homologous protein sequences to the genome. Widely used pipelines include MAKER, AUGUSTUS and the Ensembl gene build.

Functional annotation

Functional annotation attaches biological information to the predicted features: probable protein function inferred from similarity to characterised proteins, membership of pathways, and standardised vocabulary terms from the Gene Ontology. Databases such as UniProt, Pfam and InterPro provide the reference data. Because most annotation is inferred computationally, errors in early genome annotations can propagate, and major reference genomes such as the human genome are continually re-annotated by projects like GENCODE.
