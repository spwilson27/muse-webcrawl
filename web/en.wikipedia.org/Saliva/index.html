Saliva

Saliva (commonly referred to as spit or drool) is an extracellular fluid produced and secreted by salivary glands in the mouth. In humans, saliva is around 99 percent water, plus electrolytes, mucus, white blood cells, epithelial cells, enzymes (such as amylase and lipase), and antimicrobial agents (such as secretory IgA and lysozyme). A healthy adult produces between 1 and 2 litres of saliva per day.

Functions

Saliva lubricates and moistens food to form a bolus that can be swallowed, and its enzymes begin the digestion of starches and fats. It protects the teeth and oral tissues by buffering acids, supplying calcium and phosphate for remineralisation, and controlling bacterial growth. Saliva also dissolves flavour molecules for taste, facilitates speech, and is essential to oral health; insufficient production (xerostomia, or dry mouth) greatly increases the risk of dental caries and infection.

Production

Saliva is secreted by three pairs of major glands — the parotid, submandibular, and sublingual glands — together with hundreds of minor glands. Secretion is controlled by the autonomic nervous system and is stimulated by the sight, smell, and taste of food as well as by chewing. Saliva has found diagnostic use because many hormones, antibodies, and pathogens can be detected in it.
