Angel investor

An angel investor (also known as a business angel, informal investor, angel funder, private investor, or seed investor) is an individual who provides capital to a business or businesses, including start-ups, usually in exchange for convertible debt or ownership equity. Angel investors often provide the bridge between the self-funding of a business and the level at which it becomes attractive to venture capital funds. Because they typically invest at a very early, high-risk stage, angels frequently contribute experience, contacts and mentoring as well as money.

Etymology and history

The term "angel" originated in Broadway theatre, where wealthy patrons bankrolled productions that would otherwise not be staged. It was applied to early-stage investors by William Wetzel of the University of New Hampshire in a 1978 study of how entrepreneurs raised seed capital. Angel investing grew markedly from the 1990s onward, and angels organised themselves into angel groups and networks that screen deals collectively and co-invest through syndicates.

Profile and activity

Angels are usually high-net-worth individuals, often successful entrepreneurs or executives who have sold a business. They invest their own money — unlike venture capitalists, who manage pooled funds — and typically take stakes in businesses they understand, in their own region. In the United States, accredited-investor rules restrict most angel activity to individuals meeting income or net-worth thresholds. Estimates by the Center for Venture Research put U.S. angel investment at tens of billions of dollars annually across tens of thousands of ventures, a total comparable in size to institutional venture capital but spread over far more companies.

Risks and returns

Most start-ups fail, so angel portfolios are built on the expectation that a small number of successes will offset many losses. Studies of angel groups suggest roughly half of investments lose money, while a minority return ten times the capital or more. Angels therefore diversify, invest in stages tied to milestones, and often insist on board seats or advisory roles.

Angel groups and syndicates

Networks such as the Angel Capital Association in the U.S. and national federations in Europe professionalise the sector, offering shared due diligence, standard term sheets and education. Online platforms have added equity crowdfunding and syndicate models in which a lead angel raises a special-purpose vehicle from backers.
