Stanley Cohen (23 February 1942 – 7 January 2013) was a South African-British sociologist and criminologist, professor at the London School of Economics, best known for introducing the concept of the moral panic in his 1972 book Folk Devils and Moral Panics.

Life and career

Born in Johannesburg, Cohen studied at the University of the Witwatersrand and was active in anti-apartheid politics before leaving South Africa for Britain in 1964. After teaching at Durham University he worked at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem, where he founded a centre for human rights study, and joined the LSE in 1994. He was a fellow of the British Academy and a leading figure in critical criminology.

Moral panics

Studying the media reaction to clashes between Mods and Rockers in 1960s England, Cohen described how a condition or group becomes defined as a threat to societal values: the media present the group in stylised, stereotypical fashion as "folk devils", moral barricades are manned by editors, bishops and politicians, and socially accredited experts pronounce diagnoses and solutions. The concept became one of the most cited in media studies and sociology, later applied to panics over video games, drugs, migration and social media.

Later work

His Visions of Social Control (1985) examined the dispersal of discipline in modern societies, and States of Denial (2001) explored how individuals and governments deny atrocities and suffering — work informed by his involvement with human rights organisations such as Amnesty International and his reporting on torture in Israel.
