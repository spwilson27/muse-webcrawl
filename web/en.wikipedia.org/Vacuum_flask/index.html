Vacuum flask
Other namesDewar flask, Thermos (genericised trademark)
InventorJames Dewar (1892)
UsesThermal insulation of liquids; cryogenics; laboratory storage

A vacuum flask (also known as a Dewar flask, Dewar bottle or Thermos) is an insulating storage vessel that greatly lengthens the time over which its contents remain hotter or cooler than the flask's surroundings. Invented by Sir James Dewar in 1892, the vacuum flask consists of two flasks, placed one within the other and joined at the neck. The gap between the two flasks is partially evacuated of air, creating a near-vacuum that prevents heat transfer by conduction or convection; silvering of the facing surfaces suppresses transfer by radiation.

History

Dewar invented the flask in 1892 while working on cryogenics at the Royal Institution in London, needing a vessel to keep liquefied gases cold during experiments; he silvered the inner walls after noticing the insulating value of a vacuum in the work of earlier researchers. Dewar never patented the design for domestic use. In 1904 the German glassblowers Reinhold Burger and Albert Aschenbrenner, who had manufactured Dewar's vessels, recognised its commercial potential, held a naming competition — "Thermos" (from Greek therme, heat) was chosen — and founded the Thermos company to market it. Dewar lost a court action over the design and received only limited recognition. The flask became famous through polar exploration and aviation, and was mass-produced in America by the American Thermos Bottle Company from 1907.

Design and physics

Heat reaches or leaves the contents of a flask by three routes, each blocked by the design: conduction and convection are nearly eliminated by the vacuum gap, and radiation is reduced by reflective (historically silvered, now usually aluminium-coated) surfaces. Remaining losses occur through the neck and stopper — often made of cork, plastic or foam — which is why the neck is kept narrow. Modern household flasks are typically stainless steel double-walled bottles; laboratory Dewars are borosilicate glass, sometimes filled additionally with superinsulation in large cryogenic storage vessels. Large vacuum vessels store liquefied gases such as nitrogen, oxygen and helium, and vacuum-insulated panels apply the same principle to buildings and refrigerated transport.

Scientific and other uses

Beyond drinks containers, Dewar vessels are essential in low-temperature physics, in the transport of vaccines and biological samples, in calorimetry, and in rocketry, where vacuum-jacketed tanks and transfer lines handle cryogenic propellants. The term "Dewar" remains standard in laboratory practice for any vacuum-insulated vessel for cryogenic liquids.
