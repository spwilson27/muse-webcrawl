Cognitive neuroscience

Cognitive neuroscience is the scientific field that studies the biological processes that underlie cognition, with a specific focus on the neural connections in the brain involved in mental processes. It addresses questions such as how cognitive activities are affected or controlled by neural circuits. Cognitive neuroscience is a branch of both neuroscience and psychology, overlapping with disciplines including behavioral neuroscience, cognitive psychology, physiological psychology, and affective neuroscience.

Methods

The field relies on methods from many disciplines. Experimental approaches include psychophysical and behavioural experiments in healthy volunteers, lesion studies in patients with brain damage, single-cell and multi-unit recording in animals, electroencephalography (EEG) and magnetoencephalography (MEG) for timing, and positron emission tomography (PET) and functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) for localising activity. Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) allows researchers to establish causal roles for brain regions, and computational modelling integrates the results into theories of how neural systems implement cognitive functions.

History

The roots of the field lie in 19th-century neurology — Broca's and Wernicke's language areas, and the case of Phineas Gage — and in the cognitive revolution in psychology. The term "cognitive neuroscience" was coined in the late 1970s by George Armitage Miller and Michael Gazzaniga. The field expanded rapidly in the 1990s with the spread of fMRI, and has since extended to social, affective, and developmental questions, as well as to large-scale brain network analysis.

Topics

Major topics include attention, perception, memory, language, executive function, decision making, consciousness, and the neural basis of mental disorders.
