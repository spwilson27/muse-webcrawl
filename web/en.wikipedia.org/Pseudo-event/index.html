Pseudo-event

A pseudo-event is an event or activity that is planned primarily for the purpose of being reported or reproduced in the media, rather than occurring spontaneously. The term was coined by the historian Daniel J. Boorstin in his 1961 book The Image: A Guide to Pseudo-events in America. Typical examples include press conferences, ribbon-cutting ceremonies, groundbreakings, product launches, photo opportunities, and many publicity stunts.

Characteristics

Boorstin identified several distinguishing features of a pseudo-event: it is not spontaneous but planned and planted; it is arranged primarily for the convenience of the media; its relation to the underlying reality is ambiguous; it is usually intended to become a self-fulfilling prophecy; and it generates further pseudo-events, such as interviews about the event itself. Because pseudo-events are scheduled in advance, dramatized, and designed to be easily reported, they can crowd out spontaneous news in coverage.

Criticism and influence

Boorstin's critique anticipated later theories of the spectacle and media manipulation, and the concept remains influential in media studies, public relations scholarship, and political communication. Critics have noted that the boundary between pseudo-events and genuine events is contested, since many historically significant events — trials, debates, summits, and demonstrations — are also staged partly for public attention. The term is closely related to the modern "photo op" and to Jean Baudrillard's notion of hyperreality, in which media representations precede and shape the reality they claim to describe.
