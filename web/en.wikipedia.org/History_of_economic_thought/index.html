History of economic thought

The history of economic thought deals with the thinkers and theories in the field of political economy and economics from the ancient world to the present day. It includes ancient and medieval reflections on wealth and justice, mercantilism, the classical economics of Adam Smith and his successors, Marxian economics, the marginalist revolution, Keynesian economics, and the diverse schools of the modern discipline.

Ancient and medieval thought

Greek writers such as Xenophon (who coined oikonomia, household management) and Aristotle analysed money, exchange and usury; Aristotle's distinctions between use value and exchange value proved enduring. Indian thought produced the Arthashastra attributed to Chanakya; Chinese statecraft debated price regulation for centuries. Medieval scholastics such as Thomas Aquinas and the School of Salamanca developed ideas of the just price and anticipated elements of monetary and value theory.

Mercantilism and the physiocrats

From the sixteenth to the eighteenth century, mercantilist writers identified national wealth with treasure and a favourable balance of trade, advising state intervention. In reaction, the French physiocrats under François Quesnay — whose Tableau économique (1758) modelled the economy's circular flow — argued that land alone produced a surplus and advocated laissez-faire.

Classical economics

Adam Smith's The Wealth of Nations (1776) founded classical economics with its analysis of the division of labour, markets, and the "invisible hand". David Ricardo developed theories of rent, comparative advantage and distribution; Thomas Malthus wrote on population; John Stuart Mill's Principles (1848) synthesised the tradition. Karl Marx subjected classical theory to a radical critique in Capital (1867), centring on labour value, exploitation and crisis.

The marginalist revolution and neoclassical economics

In the 1870s William Stanley Jevons, Carl Menger and Léon Walras independently recast value in terms of marginal utility, founding neoclassical economics; Alfred Marshall's Principles of Economics (1890) integrated supply and demand analysis. The Austrian school (Menger, Böhm-Bawerk, Mises, Hayek), institutionalists (Veblen), and welfare economics (Pigou) offered alternative approaches, while general equilibrium theory was formalised in the twentieth century.

Keynes and the modern era

The Great Depression prompted John Maynard Keynes's General Theory (1936), which founded macroeconomics and justified demand management. Post-war economics was dominated by the "neoclassical synthesis", challenged in the 1970s by monetarism (Milton Friedman) and new classical economics amid stagflation. Since then the field has diversified into game theory, behavioural economics, development and information economics, and renewed macro debates after the 2008 financial crisis.
