Governmentality

Coined byMichel Foucault
Key textsSecurity, Territory, Population (lectures 1977–78); The Birth of Biopolitics (1978–79)
Related conceptsBiopolitics, biopower, disciplinary power, pastoral power
FieldPolitical theory, sociology, geography

Governmentality is a concept developed by the French philosopher Michel Foucault, most fully in his Collège de France lectures of 1977–79. It fuses the words "government" and "mentality" (rationality) and refers both to the ensemble of institutions, procedures, calculations and tactics by which populations are governed, and to the historical process by which the state became "governmentalised" — concerned less with ruling territory than with managing the welfare, health and productivity of a population.

Foucault's account

Foucault contrasted three forms of power: sovereignty, exercised over territory through law and the threat of death; discipline, exercised over individual bodies in institutions such as prisons, schools and barracks; and government (or security apparatuses), exercised over populations through statistics, political economy and techniques that regulate processes such as birth rates, disease and markets. Modern power, he argued, combines all three in a "triangle: sovereignty–discipline–government".

Pastoral power and raison d'État

Foucault traced governmentality's genealogy to the Christian pastorate — the shepherd's individualising care for the flock — and to early-modern raison d'État and "police" science, before liberalism recast government as a limited art that asks whether it governs too much. His lectures on neoliberalism analysed how market competition and the model of the "entrepreneur of the self" become general principles for governing conduct.

Governmentality studies

After Foucault's death, scholars including Peter Miller, Nikolas Rose and Mitchell Dean developed an interdisciplinary field of "governmentality studies", using the concept to analyse welfare states, risk management, audit culture, global governance and the "conduct of conduct" in organisations. Critics have questioned the concept's breadth and Foucault's selective reading of liberalism, but it remains influential across political science, sociology, anthropology and geography.
