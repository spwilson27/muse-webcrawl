The Garden of Earthly Delights

The Garden of Earthly Delights is the modern title given to a triptych oil painting on oak panel by the Early Netherlandish master Hieronymus Bosch, painted between about 1490 and 1510. It is housed in the Museo del Prado in Madrid, where it has been since 1939.

The panels

The left panel shows the Garden of Eden, God presenting Eve to Adam amid strange animals and rock formations. The vast central panel depicts a fantastical garden crowded with nude figures cavorting among oversized fruit, birds and hybrid creatures — a scene traditionally read as humanity revelling in sin before the Fall's consequences, though scholars debate whether it is a moral warning, a lost paradise or an alchemical allegory. The right panel is a nightmarish Hell of musical instruments turned to torture devices, a burning city, and the hollow "Tree-Man", whose torso shelters a tavern scene. Closed, the shutters show the Earth on the third day of Creation, painted in grisaille inside a transparent sphere.

Interpretation and legacy

No contemporary account of the work's commission survives; it was recorded in the Brussels palace of the counts of Nassau in 1517. Its surreal imagery, five centuries before Surrealism, has made it Bosch's most famous work and a fixture of popular culture, endlessly reproduced and analysed.
