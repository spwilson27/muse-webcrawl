Queen dowager

A queen dowager (also dowager queen or queen mother, although queen mother may also refer specifically to a dowager queen who is the mother of the reigning monarch) is a title or status generally held by the widow of a king. In the case of the widow of an emperor, the title is empress dowager. Its full meaning is clear from the two words from which it is composed: queen indicates someone who was the wife of a king, and dowager indicates a widow who holds a title or property—a "dower"—derived from her deceased husband.

Historical examples

The institution of the queen dowager has been significant in monarchies worldwide. In China, empresses dowager such as Cixi exercised enormous political power during the late Qing dynasty, ruling in the name of child emperors. In the Ottoman Empire, the valide sultan (mother of the sultan) was a powerful figure, and several women effectively governed the empire during the period sometimes called the Sultanate of Women.

In European monarchies, queens dowager often retained their households, residences, and considerable influence. Examples include Catherine de' Medici of France, who as queen mother dominated French politics during the reigns of her sons, and Queen Mary of the United Kingdom, widow of George V.

Queen mother

A queen mother is a dowager queen who is also the mother of the reigning sovereign. The term was notably borne by Queen Elizabeth The Queen Mother, widow of George VI and mother of Elizabeth II, who held the title from 1952 until her death in 2002. Not all dowager queens are queen mothers: a king's widow whose successor is not her child is a dowager but not a queen mother.

Dower rights

Historically, a queen dowager was entitled to a dower—income, lands, or residences assigned for her maintenance after her husband's death. These arrangements were often negotiated in marriage contracts and could involve substantial estates, jointures, and palaces. The size and security of a dowager's settlement could affect her independence and political weight at court.
