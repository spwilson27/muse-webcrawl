Allopatric speciation

Allopatric speciation (from Greek allos, "other," and patra, "fatherland") is the formation of new species when biological populations of the same species become geographically isolated from one another so that gene flow between them is prevented or severely reduced. Separated populations then diverge genetically — through mutation, genetic drift, and differing natural selection — until they become reproductively isolated and can no longer interbreed even if the barrier disappears. It is widely regarded as the most common mode of speciation in animals. The concept was championed by Ernst Mayr in his 1942 book Systematics and the Origin of Species.

Mechanisms

Vicariance

A new barrier arises within a population's range — a rising mountain chain, a river changing course, a drying lake, or continental drift — dividing it into two or more subpopulations. The split of snail populations by the formation of the Isthmus of Panama, and of freshwater fishes by fragmenting lakes, are standard examples.

Dispersal and founder effect

A few individuals colonize a new, isolated habitat (peripatric speciation when the new population is small and at the periphery). Chance differences in the founders' gene pool, amplified by drift in the small population, can drive rapid divergence. Island colonizations — such as Darwin's finches on the Galápagos and the honeycreepers and Drosophila of Hawaii — are classic cases.

Reproductive isolation

Divergence produces isolating mechanisms almost as byproducts: changes in mating signals, breeding seasons, genitalia, or chromosome arrangements (prezygotic barriers), or reduced hybrid viability and fertility (postzygotic barriers), sometimes formalized by the Bateson–Dobzhansky–Muller model of genetic incompatibilities. If the populations meet again before isolation is complete, they may merge, or selection may strengthen the barriers — a process called reinforcement.

Evidence and examples

  • Ring species-like patterns and sister species on opposite sides of barriers (e.g., across the Isthmus of Panama, snapping shrimps of the genus Alpheus fall into geminate species pairs).
  • Antelope squirrels on the north and south rims of the Grand Canyon have diverged into distinct species.
  • Pupfish in Death Valley's isolated springs, separated when Pleistocene lakes dried, are distinct species per spring system.

Contrast with other modes

Allopatric speciation contrasts with sympatric speciation (divergence without geographic separation, common in plants via polyploidy) and parapatric speciation (divergence across an environmental gradient with limited gene flow). Modern phylogeography and genomics often show a continuum rather than a sharp boundary between these modes.
