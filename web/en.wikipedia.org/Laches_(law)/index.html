Laches (law)

Laches (from the French lachesse, meaning looseness or negligence) is a legal doctrine in equity — historically a feature of common law systems — which states that a claimant who unreasonably delays in pursuing a remedy or claim may lose their entitlement to that remedy. The doctrine is an equitable defence: even where a claim is otherwise valid, a court of equity may refuse relief if the claimant's delay, combined with prejudice to the defendant, makes it unjust to grant it.

Elements

To establish laches, a defendant generally must show: (1) the claimant knew of the facts constituting the claim; (2) the claimant unreasonably delayed in asserting it; and (3) the defendant was materially prejudiced by the delay, for example through lost evidence, faded memories, or changes of position made in reliance on the claimant's inaction.

Comparison with statutes of limitations

Unlike a statute of limitations, which bars an action after a fixed statutory period, laches is flexible and fact-specific: there is no set time limit, and the court weighs the reasonableness of the delay and the resulting prejudice in the circumstances of each case. Laches typically applies to equitable claims, while statutes of limitations govern claims at law, though courts sometimes apply laches by analogy.

Origins

The doctrine grew out of the English Court of Chancery, guided by the maxim that "equity aids the vigilant, not those who slumber on their rights." Its most famous early statement appears in Lindsay Petroleum Co v Hurd (1874), where the Privy Council described the two main considerations as the length of the delay and the nature of the acts done during the interval.
