Polish Enlightenment

Periodc. 1730s – 1795
Key figuresHugo Kołłątaj, Stanisław Staszic, Ignacy Krasicki
Key institutionCommission of National Education (1773)

The Polish Enlightenment (Polish: Oświecenie) was the Polish chapter of the European Enlightenment, conventionally dated from the 1730s–40s to 1795, the year of the third and final partition of the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth. Unlike in much of Western Europe, its dominant concern was political reform: saving the Commonwealth from internal anarchy and foreign domination.

Development

Early reformers such as Stanisław Konarski, who founded the Collegium Nobilium in Warsaw in 1740, attacked the liberum veto and promoted modern education. Under King Stanisław August Poniatowski (reigned 1764–1795), Warsaw became a cultural centre: the king's Thursday Dinners gathered writers and artists, the journal Monitor promoted reform, and the Załuski Library opened to the public in 1747.

Education and reform

In 1773 the Commonwealth created the Commission of National Education, often described as Europe's first ministry of education, which reformed schools and universities in a secular, Enlightenment spirit. Political writers including Hugo Kołłątaj and Stanisław Staszic prepared the ground for the Constitution of 3 May 1791, Europe's first modern codified constitution, adopted by the Great Sejm.

Literature and science

The period's leading literary figure was Ignacy Krasicki, bishop and fabulist, author of the satirical epic Monachomachia and the novel The Adventures of Mr. Nicholas Wisdom. The Enlightenment ended abruptly with the partitions of 1795, but its reform tradition shaped Polish thought throughout the nineteenth century.
