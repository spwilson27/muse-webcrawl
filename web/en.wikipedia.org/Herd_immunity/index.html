Herd immunity

MechanismIndirect protection of susceptible individuals when a large proportion of a population is immune

Herd immunity (also called herd effect, community immunity, population immunity, or mass immunity) is a form of indirect protection that applies only to contagious diseases. It occurs when a sufficient percentage of a population has become immune to an infection — whether through previous infections or vaccination — thereby reducing the likelihood that individuals lacking immunity will come into contact with an infectious individual.

Mechanism

Once a certain threshold has been reached, herd immunity may gradually eliminate a disease from a population. Immune individuals act as barriers that slow or block chains of transmission: an infected person surrounded by immune people is unlikely to pass the pathogen onward, so outbreaks die out even though some individuals remain susceptible. This protection is especially important for people who cannot be vaccinated, such as newborns, the elderly, and the immunocompromised.

Threshold

The herd immunity threshold depends on the basic reproduction number R₀ of the disease — the average number of secondary infections produced by one case in a fully susceptible population. In the simplest model the threshold is 1 − 1/R₀. For measles (R₀ ≈ 12–18) this implies immunity in roughly 92–95% of the population; for seasonal influenza (R₀ ≈ 1.3) the threshold is far lower. Real-world thresholds are affected by heterogeneity in contact patterns, waning immunity, and viral evolution.

History and use

The concept emerged in veterinary and epidemiological research in the early 20th century and was developed mathematically by researchers including Anderson and May. It underpins mass-vaccination strategy: diseases such as smallpox were eradicated, and polio nearly so, by driving population immunity above threshold. The concept featured prominently in public debate during the COVID-19 pandemic, in which high transmission, waning immunity, and immune-evading variants complicated attempts to reach durable population-level protection through infection alone.

Limitations

Herd immunity is not a fixed wall: if immunity wanes, new susceptible people are born, or the pathogen evolves, transmission can resume. It also protects only against person-to-person contagious diseases, not infections acquired from the environment (such as tetanus).
