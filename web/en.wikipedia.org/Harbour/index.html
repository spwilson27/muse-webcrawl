A harbour (American English: harbor) is a sheltered body of water where ships, boats, and barges can be docked or anchored in safety. The term is often used interchangeably with port, though a port is more precisely a location on a coast or shore containing one or more harbours where ships can dock and transfer people or cargo to or from land. Harbours may be natural or artificial: a natural harbour is a landform where a section of a body of water is protected and deep enough to allow anchorage, while an artificial harbour has deliberately constructed breakwaters, sea walls, or jetties, or may be created by dredging.

Types of harbour

Natural harbours

Natural harbours have long been of great strategic and economic importance. Many of the world's great cities grew up around them, because a sheltered anchorage protected vessels from storms and enemy attack. Famous natural harbours include Sydney Harbour in Australia, one of the largest in the world; the deep-water harbour of Halifax, Nova Scotia; Trincomalee in Sri Lanka; and Pearl Harbor in Hawaii. Natural harbours are typically formed by rias (drowned river valleys), fjords, bays, or the lee side of islands and headlands.

Artificial harbours

Artificial harbours are created where natural shelter is absent or insufficient. Engineers construct breakwaters—massive barriers of rock, concrete, or caissons—that absorb wave energy and create calm water behind them. The ancient Phoenician harbour at Tyre and the Roman harbour of Ostia were early examples. In modern times, artificial harbours may be entirely human-made, such as the Port of Rotterdam's Maasvlakte extensions, or the Mulberry harbours towed across the English Channel for the Normandy landings of 1944.

Harbour structures

A working harbour typically contains several elements. Breakwaters shield the anchorage from waves. Quays and wharves provide solid edges where vessels berth. Piers and jetties extend into the water for mooring. Docks may be enclosed basins whose water level is controlled by lock gates, allowing ships to float at all states of the tide. Channels are kept navigable by dredging, and navigation is assisted by buoys, beacons, and lighthouses. Many harbours also maintain designated anchorages where vessels wait for a berth, a pilot, or favourable tides.

Harbour versus port

Although commonly conflated, the terms differ in emphasis: a harbour is primarily the sheltered water itself, while a port is the whole facility—harbour plus the land-side infrastructure of terminals, warehouses, cranes, railways, and roads used for commerce. A harbour may exist with no port facilities at all, serving only as a refuge or anchorage, whereas every port requires a harbour (natural or artificial) to function.

Ecology

Harbours are ecologically significant and vulnerable environments. Sheltered, nutrient-rich waters support estuarine habitats, but dredging, pollution from shipping, and introduced species carried in ballast water can damage them. Many harbour authorities now operate environmental management programmes covering water quality, sediment contamination, and habitat restoration.
