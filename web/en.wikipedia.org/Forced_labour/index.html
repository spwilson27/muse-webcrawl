Forced labour

Forced labour, or unfree labour, is any work relation in which persons are employed against their will under threat of destitution, detention, violence, or other extreme hardship. The International Labour Organization (ILO) defines forced labour as "all work or service which is exacted from any person under the menace of any penalty and for which the said person has not offered himself voluntarily." The ILO estimated in 2022 that some 27.6 million people were in forced labour on any given day, including in private industry, agriculture, domestic work, and state-imposed programmes.

Penal labour is one historical form of forced labour.

Forms

Forced labour encompasses chattel slavery, serfdom, debt bondage (bonded labour), prison labour, corvée, human trafficking for labour exploitation, and forced marriage in some definitions. Historical systems include the Soviet Gulag, Nazi Germany's use of concentration-camp and occupied-population labour, colonial corvée and the Congo Free State rubber regime, and convict leasing in the post-bellum United States.

International law

The ILO Forced Labour Convention, 1930 (No. 29) and its 2014 Protocol, together with the Abolition of Forced Labour Convention, 1957 (No. 105), form the core treaty framework, supplemented by Article 4 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights and Article 8 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights. Narrow exceptions exist for military service, normal civic obligations, and prison labour under public supervision.
