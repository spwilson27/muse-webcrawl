Financial Services Authority

The Financial Services Authority (FSA) was a quasi-judicial body that regulated the financial services industry in the United Kingdom between 2001 and 2013. A company limited by guarantee, it was funded by fees levied on the firms it regulated and was accountable to HM Treasury and Parliament.

History

The FSA was created as part of reforms announced in 1997, when the incoming Labour government granted the Bank of England operational independence and consolidated the work of several existing regulators — including the Securities and Investments Board, whose functions and staff formed the FSA's core — into a single regulator. It received its full statutory powers under the Financial Services and Markets Act 2000, which came into force in December 2001. Its remit eventually covered banks, insurers, investment firms, and mortgage and general insurance intermediaries, as well as the listing of securities.

Abolition

The FSA was widely criticised for its "light touch" approach to supervision in the years before the financial crisis of 2007–08, including its handling of the failure of Northern Rock. In 2010 Chancellor George Osborne announced its abolition. On 1 April 2013 the FSA was split into the Prudential Regulation Authority, a subsidiary of the Bank of England responsible for prudential supervision, and the Financial Conduct Authority, responsible for conduct regulation and consumer protection.
