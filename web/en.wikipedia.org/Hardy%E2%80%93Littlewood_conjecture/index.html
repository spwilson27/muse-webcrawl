The Hardy–Littlewood conjectures are a pair of conjectures in number theory proposed by G. H. Hardy and John Edensor Littlewood in their 1923 paper "Some problems of 'Partitio Numerorum'". They predict precise asymptotic counts for patterns of prime numbers.

First conjecture

The first conjecture generalises the twin prime conjecture. It predicts that the number of prime k-tuples (constellations) with a given pattern grows like a constant — the Hardy–Littlewood singular series — times x/(log x)^k. For twin primes the constant is the twin prime constant, approximately 0.66016. The conjecture implies there are infinitely many twin primes, prime quadruplets, and sexy primes, but none of these has been proved.

Second conjecture

The second conjecture states that π(x + y) ≤ π(x) + π(y) for x, y ≥ 2, where π is the prime-counting function: primes are never more densely packed in a later interval than at the start. In 1974 Ian Richards and Douglas Hensley showed the second conjecture is incompatible with the first, so at least one of them is false; most number theorists believe the first.

Status

Both conjectures remain open. The first is regarded as a special case of the broader Bateman–Horn conjecture and Schinzel's hypothesis H.
