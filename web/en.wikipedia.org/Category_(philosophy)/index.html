Category (philosophy)

BranchOntology, metaphysics, logic
Key figuresAristotle, Kant, Peirce, Ryle
RelatedPredicables, universals

In philosophy, a category is a maximally general kind or class under which entities can be subsumed. Systems of categories attempt to list the most fundamental types of being — the highest genera to which everything that exists belongs. The notion originates with Aristotle, who proposed ten categories in his work the Categories and in the Topics.


Aristotle's ten categories

Aristotle's categories

Aristotle distinguished ten categories: substance, quantity, quality, relation, place, time, posture, possession, action, and passion. Of these, substance (ousia) is primary: individual things such as a particular man or horse are primary substances, while species and genera are secondary substances. The other nine categories describe accidents that inhere in substances.

Kant's categories

Immanuel Kant recast the doctrine in the Critique of Pure Reason (1781). Kant held that the mind possesses twelve a priori categories of the understanding, grouped under quantity (unity, plurality, totality), quality (reality, negation, limitation), relation (substance, causality, community), and modality (possibility, existence, necessity). These categories structure all possible experience and are derived from the forms of judgement.

Later developments

Charles Sanders Peirce reduced the categories to three — Firstness, Secondness, and Thirdness. Gilbert Ryle popularised the notion of a "category mistake", the error of treating something as belonging to one category when it belongs to another. In ontology and knowledge engineering, category systems continue to underpin formal classification and semantic web standards.
