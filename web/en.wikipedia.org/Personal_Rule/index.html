Personal Rule

The Personal Rule (also known to its critics as the Eleven Years' Tyranny) was the period from 1629 to 1640 during which King Charles I of England ruled without recourse to Parliament. The King claimed the royal prerogative was sufficient to govern, and that he was accountable only to God. The period ended when the costs of the Bishops' Wars with Scotland forced Charles to recall Parliament.

Background

Charles's relations with Parliament had broken down over taxation, religion and the influence of his favourite, the Duke of Buckingham, assassinated in 1628. After the confrontations surrounding the Petition of Right (1628), Charles dissolved Parliament in March 1629 and resolved to govern alone.

Government without Parliament

To raise revenue without parliamentary grants, the regime revived and extended non-parliamentary levies, most notoriously Ship Money, a tax traditionally raised from coastal towns for the navy, extended inland in 1635. Its legality was challenged by John Hampden in a celebrated case of 1637–38; the judges found narrowly for the King, but the verdict damaged his legitimacy. Other expedients included fines for distraint of knighthood, forest fines, and monopolies.

Government was directed by figures such as Thomas Wentworth, Earl of Strafford, who pursued "Thorough" — efficient, authoritarian administration — and Archbishop William Laud, whose Arminian religious reforms alienated Puritans and Scots alike.

End and consequences

The attempt to impose Laud's prayer book on Scotland provoked the Bishops' Wars (1639–40). Unable to fund an army, Charles summoned the Short Parliament in April 1640 and, when it refused supply without redress of grievances, dissolved it; the Long Parliament followed in November. The grievances of the Personal Rule — taxation without consent and perceived religious innovation — became central causes of the English Civil War.
