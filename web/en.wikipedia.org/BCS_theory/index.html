BCS theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

BCS theory or Bardeen–Cooper–Schrieffer theory (named after John Bardeen, Leon Cooper, and John Robert Schrieffer) is the first microscopic theory of superconductivity since Heike Kamerlingh Onnes's 1911 discovery. The theory describes superconductivity as a microscopic effect caused by a condensation of Cooper pairs. It was proposed in 1957, and its authors received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1972.

Overview

Rapid progress in the understanding of superconductivity came in 1957 when Bardeen, Cooper, and Schrieffer published their microscopic theory. Building on Cooper's demonstration that an arbitrarily weak attraction between electrons in a metal can bind them into pairs, the theory shows that electrons near the Fermi surface form Cooper pairs through exchange of phonons, opening an energy gap Δ at the Fermi level. Below a critical temperature Tc, the pairs condense into a coherent quantum state that carries current without resistance and expels magnetic fields (the Meissner effect).

2Δ(0) / kBTc ≈ 3.53

This universal ratio between the zero-temperature gap and the critical temperature is one of the theory's hallmark predictions, confirmed approximately in many conventional superconductors such as aluminium, tin, and lead.

Successes and limits

BCS theory explained the isotope effect, the energy gap observed in tunnelling, the temperature dependence of the critical field, and flux quantisation in units of h/2e, which directly demonstrated pairing. However, it does not describe high-temperature superconductors discovered from 1986 onward, such as the cuprates, whose pairing mechanism remains an open question; these are termed unconventional superconductors.
