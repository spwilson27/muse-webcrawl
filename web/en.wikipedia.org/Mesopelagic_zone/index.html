Mesopelagic zone

The mesopelagic zone, also known as the twilight zone, is the layer of the ocean between about 200 and 1,000 metres below the surface. It lies beneath the sunlit epipelagic zone and above the darker bathypelagic zone.

Conditions

Sunlight in the mesopelagic is too weak for photosynthesis but sufficient for vision, so many resident animals have large eyes, photophores, and countershading or bioluminescence. Temperatures decline steadily through the zone, and pressure increases with depth.

Life and migration

The zone hosts an enormous biomass of fish — including lanternfish and bristlemouths — estimated in billions of tonnes. Many mesopelagic animals undertake diel vertical migration, rising toward the surface at night to feed and descending during the day, a behavior that also transports carbon into the deep ocean. Squid, crustaceans, and gelatinous animals such as siphonophores are common.

Human interest

Because of its huge fish stocks and role in the biological carbon pump, the mesopelagic has attracted scientific and commercial interest, balanced by concern about the ecological effects of harvesting it.
