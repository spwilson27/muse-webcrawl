McCulloch v. Maryland, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316 (1819), is a landmark decision of the U.S. Supreme Court that established that the federal government has implied powers under the Necessary and Proper Clause, and that states may not tax federal institutions because the Constitution and federal laws are supreme. Chief Justice John Marshall's unanimous opinion is regarded as one of the most important in American constitutional law.

Background

In 1816 Congress chartered the Second Bank of the United States. Several states, hostile to the Bank, levied taxes on its operations. In 1818 Maryland imposed a tax on bank notes issued by any bank not chartered by the state — in practice only the Bank's Baltimore branch. James William McCulloch, the branch's head cashier, issued notes without paying the tax and was sued by the state. Maryland's courts upheld the tax, and McCulloch appealed to the Supreme Court. The case was argued over nine days by leading advocates including Daniel Webster and Luther Martin.

Holding

Marshall answered two questions. First, Congress had the power to charter the Bank: the Constitution derives from the people, and the Necessary and Proper Clause grants Congress broad discretion to choose means "plainly adapted" to legitimate enumerated ends — "we must never forget that it is a constitution we are expounding." Second, Maryland's tax was unconstitutional: the power to tax involves the power to destroy, and under the Supremacy Clause states cannot tax, and thereby control or destroy, an instrument of the federal government.

Legacy

McCulloch provided the foundational justification for expansive federal authority — later invoked for everything from the federal regulatory state to Gibbons v. Ogden — and its intergovernmental tax-immunity principle long shielded federal operations and salaries from state taxation. The phrase "the power to tax involves the power to destroy" became a staple of American jurisprudence.
