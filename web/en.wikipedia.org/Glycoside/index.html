Glycoside

A glycoside is a molecule in which a sugar is bound by a glycosidic bond to another functional group. The sugar part is called the glycone and the non-sugar part the aglycone (or genin). Glycosides are extremely common in plants, where they serve in defence, pigmentation and the storage of reactive compounds in an inactive form, and many are important medicines or poisons.

Classification

Glycosides are classified by the atom through which the sugar is attached:

  • O-glycosides — linked through oxygen; the most common type, including most plant glycosides.
  • S-glycosides (thioglycosides) — linked through sulfur, as in the glucosinolates of mustard and cabbage family plants, which release pungent isothiocyanates when tissue is damaged.
  • N-glycosides — linked through nitrogen, as in the nucleosides of DNA and RNA.
  • C-glycosides — linked through carbon, resistant to acid and enzymatic hydrolysis.

Important groups

  • Cardiac glycosides such as digoxin and digitoxin from foxgloves (Digitalis) increase the force of heart contraction and have long been used to treat heart failure.
  • Cyanogenic glycosides such as amygdalin in bitter almonds and linamarin in cassava release hydrogen cyanide on hydrolysis.
  • Anthraquinone glycosides in senna and aloe act as stimulant laxatives.
  • Flavonoid glycosides provide much of the colour and antioxidant content of fruits and vegetables.
  • Saponins are glycosides with soap-like foaming properties, found in soapwort, quinoa and ginseng.
  • Steviol glycosides from the stevia plant are used as high-intensity sweeteners.

Hydrolysis

When a glycoside is hydrolysed by enzymes (glycosidases) or acid, the sugar is cleaved from the aglycone, often activating it. Plants exploit this by storing the glycoside and its hydrolysing enzyme in separate compartments, so that damage to the tissue mixes them and releases a toxic aglycone — a "chemical bomb" defence against herbivores.
