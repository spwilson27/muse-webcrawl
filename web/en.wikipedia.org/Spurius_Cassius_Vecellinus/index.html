Spurius Cassius Vecellinus (died 485 BC) was a Roman politician of the gens Cassia who served as consul three times in the early Roman Republic and was one of the most prominent statesmen of his generation before his execution on a charge of aspiring to kingship.

Career

Cassius first held the consulship in 502 BC. In his second consulship in 493 BC he concluded the foedus Cassianum, the Treaty of Cassius, a treaty of alliance between Rome and the Latin League that endured, in tradition, until 338 BC. He celebrated a triumph over the Hernici and dedicated the Temple of Ceres, Liber and Libera on the Aventine Hill.

Agrarian law and downfall

During his third consulship in 486 BC, Cassius proposed the first recorded Roman agrarian law, providing for the distribution of conquered public land to the plebeians and Rome's Latin allies. The proposal made him popular with the people but aroused the suspicion of the patricians and of his fellow consul. After leaving office he was accused of perduellio — of aiming at royal power (regnum) — condemned, and executed, according to some accounts by being thrown from the Tarpeian Rock, in others scourged and beheaded by his own father after a family trial. His house was demolished and a temple to Tellus built on the site.

Legacy

Cassius became the archetype in Roman political memory of the demagogue destroyed by the suspicion of tyranny, a precedent later invoked against figures such as Maelius, Manlius Capitolinus and the Gracchi. Modern historians treat the broad outline of his career as historical, though details of his fall are heavily overlaid with later legend.
