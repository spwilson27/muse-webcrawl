Five precepts

The five precepts (Sanskrit: pañcaśīla; Pali: pañcasīla) or five rules of training are the most important system of morality for Buddhist lay people. They constitute the basic code of ethics undertaken by lay followers of Buddhism, voluntarily undertaken to support meditation practice and progress toward enlightenment.

The precepts

The five precepts are commitments to abstain from:

  1. Harming living beings (killing)
  2. Taking what is not given (stealing)
  3. Sexual misconduct
  4. False speech (lying)
  5. Intoxicants that cloud the mind, such as alcohol and recreational drugs

Practice

Lay Buddhists traditionally recite the precepts in Pali after taking refuge in the Triple Gem (the Buddha, Dharma and Sangha), often on observance days (uposatha) or during ceremonies at temples. The precepts are formulated negatively as abstentions but are understood to have positive counterparts — compassion, generosity, contentment, honesty and mindfulness. Monastics follow much more extensive codes (the Vinaya), while devoted laypeople may additionally observe eight or ten precepts on retreat or holy days.

Origins and interpretation

The five precepts appear throughout early Buddhist scriptures and were likely influenced by, and shared with, contemporary Indian śramaṇa movements such as Jainism. Modern interpreters such as Thich Nhat Hanh have restated them positively as the "Five Mindfulness Trainings," extending their scope to issues such as consumption and communication in the digital age.
