Pulsar

A pulsar (from pulsating radio source) is a highly magnetized rotating neutron star that emits beams of electromagnetic radiation from its magnetic poles. Because the magnetic axis is typically tilted relative to the rotation axis, these beams sweep through space like the light of a lighthouse, and are observed as regular pulses when they cross the Earth. Pulsars have very short and extremely regular rotational periods, from about 1.4 milliseconds to several seconds, making them some of the most precise clocks known in astrophysics.

Discovery

The first pulsar, PSR B1919+21, was discovered in 1967 by Jocelyn Bell Burnell and Antony Hewish at the Mullard Radio Astronomy Observatory in Cambridge, England. The scrupulously regular pulses, initially nicknamed "LGM-1" ("little green men"), were soon explained as rotating neutron stars — objects whose existence had been proposed theoretically by Walter Baade and Fritz Zwicky in 1934. Hewish shared the 1974 Nobel Prize in Physics for the discovery; the omission of Bell Burnell from the award remains controversial.

Properties

Pulsars are the collapsed cores of massive stars that exploded as supernovae. A typical pulsar has a mass greater than the Sun's compressed into a sphere about 20 kilometers in diameter, giving densities comparable to an atomic nucleus. Their magnetic fields can reach trillions of gauss, and the fastest, the millisecond pulsars, are thought to have been "spun up" by accreting matter from a binary companion. The Crab Pulsar, created by the supernova of 1054 CE, rotates 30 times per second and powers the Crab Nebula.

Uses

The regularity of pulsar signals allows them to be used as precision timing standards, in tests of general relativity — the binary pulsar PSR B1913+16, discovered by Russell Hulse and Joseph Taylor, provided the first indirect evidence for gravitational waves and won the 1993 Nobel Prize — and as potential navigation beacons for spacecraft. Pulsar timing arrays monitoring dozens of millisecond pulsars reported evidence in 2023 for a background of low-frequency gravitational waves.

The discovery in 1992 by Aleksander Wolszczan and Dale Frail of planets orbiting the pulsar PSR B1257+12 constituted the first confirmed exoplanetary system detected.

Types

Rotation-powered pulsars derive their emission from rotational energy loss. Magnetars, by contrast, are powered by the decay of exceptionally strong magnetic fields and produce bursts of X-rays and gamma rays. Accretion-powered pulsars in binary systems emit X-rays as matter falls onto them.
