Balance theory

Balance theory is a theory of attitude change in social psychology, proposed by Fritz Heider in the 1940s and 1950s. It concerns the tendency of people to keep their sentiments toward other people and objects in a consistent, "balanced" state.

The model

Heider analysed triads consisting of a person (P), another person (O), and an object or third person (X). Each relationship is positive or negative. A triad is balanced when the product of the three signs is positive — for example, P likes O, O likes X, and P likes X ("the friend of my friend is my friend"), or P dislikes O, O likes X, and P dislikes X ("the enemy of my friend is my enemy"). Imbalanced triads generate psychological tension that motivates people to change an attitude or reinterpret a relationship.

Development and applications

Dorwin Cartwright and Frank Harary formalised balance theory using signed graphs, providing a mathematical structure that later influenced social network analysis: in a balanced network, members can be divided into groups within which ties are positive and between which ties are negative. The theory has been applied to interpersonal attraction, group dynamics, international relations ("the enemy of my enemy is my friend") and consumer attitudes toward brands and endorsers.
