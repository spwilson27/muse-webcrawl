CauseThe Moon passes between the Sun and Earth

A solar eclipse occurs when the Moon passes between the Sun and Earth and the Moon fully or partially blocks the Sun for a viewer on Earth. This can happen only at new moon, when the three bodies align (a configuration called syzygy). A solar eclipse is unrelated to the solar cycle, the Sun's 11-year activity variation.

Types

In a total eclipse, the Moon's dark inner shadow (umbra) reaches the surface and the Sun's disc is fully covered, revealing the pearly corona and prominences; day briefly turns to twilight. In an annular eclipse, the Moon is too far away to cover the Sun completely, leaving a "ring of fire". Partial eclipses occur when only the Moon's penumbra sweeps the observer, and rare hybrid eclipses shift between total and annular along their path.

Geometry and frequency

Eclipses are possible because the Sun and Moon have almost the same apparent size in Earth's sky—the Sun is about 400 times wider but also about 400 times farther away. Because the Moon's orbit is tilted about 5° to the ecliptic, eclipses occur only near the lunar nodes, in "eclipse seasons" roughly every six months. The repeating pattern of eclipses is governed by the saros cycle of about 18 years 11 days.

Observing and significance

Looking directly at an eclipse outside the brief phase of totality can cause permanent eye damage; certified eclipse glasses or projection methods are required. Total eclipses have great scientific value: the 1919 eclipse expeditions led by Arthur Eddington provided early evidence for Einstein's general relativity by measuring starlight deflected by the Sun's gravity.
