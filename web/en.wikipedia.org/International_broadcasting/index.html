International broadcasting

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

International broadcasting, also called external broadcasting, consists of radio and television transmissions deliberately directed at audiences in foreign countries. Historically carried mainly by shortwave radio, it is used by states and public broadcasters to present news and cultural programming, promote their language and interests, reach diaspora communities, and serve audiences whose domestic media are censored. It is a classic instrument of public diplomacy and, in wartime, of propaganda.

History

International shortwave services began in the 1920s and 1930s: the Dutch PCJJ, the Vatican Radio, Germany's Zeesen transmitter and the BBC Empire Service (founded 1932, the ancestor of the BBC World Service) were pioneers. During the Second World War, services such as the Voice of America (founded 1942) broadcast news and propaganda, and jamming of foreign broadcasts became common. In the Cold War, Radio Free Europe and Radio Liberty, covertly funded by the United States, broadcast to Eastern Europe and the Soviet Union, while the USSR, China and others operated large external services of their own.

Broadcasters

Major international broadcasters have included the BBC World Service, Voice of America, Deutsche Welle, Radio France Internationale, NHK World, China Radio International, Radio Moscow (later Voice of Russia and Sputnik), and television networks such as Al Jazeera, RT, France 24, DW-TV and BBC World News.

Shift from shortwave

Since the 1990s, satellite television, the internet, podcasts and social media have displaced shortwave for most services, leading many broadcasters to cut shortwave transmissions drastically—though shortwave persists for regions with limited internet access and as a resilient medium during crises and conflicts.
