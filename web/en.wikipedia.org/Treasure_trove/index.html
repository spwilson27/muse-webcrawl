Treasure trove

A treasure trove is a quantity of money or coin, gold, silver, plate or bullion found hidden underground or in places such as church walls or roof timbers, the owner of which is unknown. The phrase comes through French trésor trouvé from Latin thesaurus inventus, "treasure found." The term is also used figuratively for any rich, unexpected collection.

English law

Under the common law of England and Wales, treasure trove belonged to the Crown (or its grantees), provided the material was substantially gold or silver, had been deliberately hidden, and the owner could not be traced. Treasure found casually lying on the surface did not qualify. The ancient doctrine was replaced by the Treasure Act 1996, which broadened the definition of treasure (including prehistoric base-metal hoards) and requires finders to report qualifying finds to the coroner; museums may acquire the objects, and rewards are normally shared between finder and landowner. The Portable Antiquities Scheme records other archaeological finds voluntarily.

Famous finds treated under these rules include the Hoxne Hoard (1992), the largest hoard of late Roman gold and silver found in Britain, and the Staffordshire Hoard (2009), a vast collection of Anglo-Saxon gold and silver metalwork.

Other jurisdictions

Rules elsewhere vary widely. In many civil law countries treasure is divided between the finder and the landowner; in the United States, found property generally falls under state lost-property statutes, and historic finds on federal land may be governed by antiquities legislation. Scots law treats ownerless property, including treasure, as falling to the Crown under the principle of bona vacantia.
