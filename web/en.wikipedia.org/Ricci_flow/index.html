The Ricci flow is a partial differential equation for evolving the metric of a Riemannian manifold, introduced by Richard Hamilton in 1982. Formally it is the system

∂/∂t gij(x, t) = −2 Rij(x, t),

where g is the metric and Rij its Ricci curvature. The flow acts like a diffusion (heat) equation for geometry: regions of positive Ricci curvature shrink, regions of negative curvature expand, and irregularities in curvature tend to smooth out over time.

Under Ricci flow a lumpy manifold deforms toward a more uniform shape, though neck-pinch singularities can form.

History and the Poincaré conjecture

Hamilton used the flow to prove that closed three-manifolds with positive Ricci curvature are spherical space forms, and proposed a general programme to prove Thurston's geometrization conjecture: let the flow run, cut out singularities as they form ("surgery"), and show the remaining pieces have recognisable geometry. Grigori Perelman completed the programme in preprints posted in 2002–2003, introducing key tools including entropy monotonicity and κ-noncollapsing. The result proved the Poincaré conjecture, one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems; Perelman declined both the Fields Medal (2006) and the Millennium Prize (2010).

Behaviour and singularities

On surfaces, Ricci flow (normalised) converges to a constant-curvature metric, giving a uniformization proof. In three dimensions the flow can develop finite-time singularities, notably neck pinches modelled on shrinking cylinders; Hamilton's surgery procedure excises these regions and restarts the flow. Analogies with the heat equation — such as maximum principles for curvature — are central to the analysis.

Related flows

Ricci flow belongs to a family of geometric evolution equations including mean curvature flow, the Yamabe flow, and Calabi flow, and has inspired purely analytic and even applied uses, from image processing to network analysis.
