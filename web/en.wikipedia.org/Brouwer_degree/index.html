Brouwer degree

In mathematics, the Brouwer degree (or topological degree) is a fundamental tool of algebraic topology that counts, with sign, the number of solutions of an equation f(x) = y, where f is a continuous map between manifolds of the same dimension. It is an integer and a homotopy invariant: it does not change when the map is deformed continuously, provided the point y stays outside the image of the boundary.

Definition

For a smooth map f between closed oriented n-manifolds and a regular value y, the degree is defined as the sum over all preimages x of y of the sign of the Jacobian determinant of f at x — that is, counting preimages with +1 where f preserves orientation and −1 where it reverses it. L. E. J. Brouwer introduced the notion around 1911, extending the classical concept of the winding number of a closed curve around a point, which is the degree of a map between circles.

Properties

  • The degree of the identity map is 1; the degree of a constant map is 0.
  • If two maps are homotopic, they have the same degree.
  • The degree of a composition of maps is the product of the degrees.
  • A map of nonzero degree is surjective, and any map of degree zero between spheres need not be.
  • The degree of the antipodal map on the n-sphere is (−1)n+1.

Applications

The Brouwer degree underlies several major theorems. The Brouwer fixed-point theorem follows because a retraction-like map would have conflicting degrees, and the hairy ball theorem follows from the fact that the identity on an even-dimensional sphere cannot have degree behaviour compatible with a nowhere-vanishing vector field. Degree theory also gives existence proofs for solutions of nonlinear equations: if the degree of a map on a region is nonzero, a solution must exist inside it. The theory extends to infinite-dimensional settings in the Leray–Schauder degree, used widely in the analysis of partial differential equations.
