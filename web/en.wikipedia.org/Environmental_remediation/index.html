Environmental remediation

Environmental remediation is the removal of pollution or contaminants from environmental media such as soil, groundwater, sediment, or surface water. Remediation may be required by law — for example under the United States Superfund program (CERCLA) — or undertaken voluntarily by landowners and developers, particularly on brownfield sites.

Technologies

Remediation technologies fall into two broad classes. Ex situ methods remove contaminated material for treatment elsewhere, including excavation ("dig and haul"), soil washing, and thermal desorption. In situ methods treat contamination in place; these include bioremediation, which uses microorganisms to degrade pollutants; phytoremediation, which uses plants; chemical oxidation; soil vapor extraction; and pump-and-treat systems for groundwater.

Process

A typical remediation project begins with site investigation and characterization, followed by a feasibility study comparing remedial alternatives against criteria such as effectiveness, implementability, and cost. After a remedy is selected and implemented, long-term monitoring confirms that cleanup goals — usually risk-based standards tied to the intended land use — have been met.

Standards

Cleanup standards vary by jurisdiction and by whether the site will be used for residential, commercial, or industrial purposes. Critics note that remediation can be extremely expensive and slow, and that some sites remain under institutional controls for decades.
