Treaty of Tordesillas

The Treaty of Tordesillas, signed at Tordesillas, Spain, on 7 June 1494 between the crowns of Castile (Spain) and Portugal, divided the lands newly discovered outside Europe between the two Iberian powers along a meridian 370 leagues west of the Cape Verde islands. It superseded the earlier papal line of demarcation drawn by Pope Alexander VI in the bull Inter caetera of 1493, which Portugal had deemed too favourable to Spain.

Terms and effects

Lands east of the line were reserved to Portugal and those to the west to Castile. The placement of the line meant that the eastern bulge of South America — discovered for Portugal by Pedro Álvares Cabral in 1500 — fell within the Portuguese sphere, giving rise to Portuguese Brazil, the only Portuguese-speaking nation in the Americas. Because the line would complete a circle around the globe, a complementary boundary in the Pacific was later set by the Treaty of Zaragoza (1529). The two maritime powers largely respected the division in their age of exploration, though other European states ignored it as they built their own empires.

Legacy

The treaty is often cited as the origin of the linguistic map of Latin America and is included in UNESCO's Memory of the World Register on the basis of copies held in Spain and Portugal.
