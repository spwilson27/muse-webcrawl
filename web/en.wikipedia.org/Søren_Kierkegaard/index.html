Søren Kierkegaard

Søren Aabye Kierkegaard (5 May 1813 – 11 November 1855) was a Danish philosopher, theologian, poet, and religious author, widely considered the first existentialist philosopher. His work crossed the boundaries of philosophy, theology, psychology, and literary criticism, and much of it deals with religious themes such as faith in God, the institution of the Church, and Christian ethics and theology.

Life

Kierkegaard was born in Copenhagen to a wealthy, devoutly Lutheran family. His father's guilt-ridden Pietism shaped his outlook. He studied theology at the University of Copenhagen, where his dissertation treated irony in Socrates. In 1840 he became engaged to Regine Olsen but broke the engagement a year later — an event that haunted his writing. Living on his inheritance, he published prolifically under a series of pseudonyms (Victor Eremita, Johannes de Silentio, Anti-Climacus, and others), often presenting opposing viewpoints in dialogue. In his final years he launched a fierce public attack on the established Danish Church in the pamphlet series The Moment. He collapsed in the street in October 1855 and died weeks later, aged 42.

Thought

Kierkegaard emphasised subjective truth — "truth is subjectivity" — arguing that faith involves a passionate personal commitment beyond objective evidence, famously described as a "leap of faith". In Fear and Trembling he retold the story of Abraham and Isaac to explore the "teleological suspension of the ethical". The Concept of Anxiety analysed anxiety as the "dizziness of freedom", anticipating later existential psychology. He described three stages of existence — the aesthetic, the ethical, and the religious — and attacked Hegel's systematic philosophy for neglecting the existing individual.

Legacy

Largely neglected outside Denmark during his lifetime, Kierkegaard was rediscovered in the early 20th century and came to influence existentialism, dialectical theology (Karl Barth), postmodernism, and modern psychology. His works also became a standard point of reference in debates about faith and reason.
