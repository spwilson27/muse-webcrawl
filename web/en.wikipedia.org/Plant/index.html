Plants are the eukaryotes that form the kingdom Plantae: predominantly photosynthetic organisms that capture light energy with the pigment chlorophyll, stored in chloroplasts derived from ancient cyanobacteria. Most plants are multicellular, anchored in place, and built of cells with cellulose walls. They dominate terrestrial ecosystems and produce, as a by-product of photosynthesis, nearly all the oxygen in Earth's atmosphere. Roughly 380,000 species are known, the great majority of them flowering plants (angiosperms).

Characteristics

Plants grow by means of meristems — regions of undifferentiated cells at root and shoot tips — and reproduce through an alternation of generations between a haploid gametophyte and a diploid sporophyte. Vascular plants transport water in xylem and sugars in phloem. Unlike animals, plants cannot move from place to place, but they sense and respond to light, gravity, touch, and chemical signals, coordinating growth and defence through hormones such as auxin and abscisic acid — the subject of plant physiology.

Evolution and diversity

Plants descend from charophyte green algae. They colonised land in the Ordovician, about 470 million years ago, evolving cuticles, stomata, spores with tough sporopollenin walls, and eventually vascular tissue, roots, leaves, seeds (in the Devonian, ~380 million years ago), and flowers (in the Cretaceous, ~130 million years ago). Living diversity spans mosses, liverworts, and hornworts (bryophytes); ferns and clubmosses; conifers, cycads, ginkgo, and gnetophytes (gymnosperms); and the flowering plants — from grasses and oaks to orchids — that dominate most modern floras.

Importance

Plants underpin nearly all life on land: they are the primary producers eaten by herbivores, the source of staple foods (rice, wheat, maize, potatoes), timber, fibres such as cotton, medicines (aspirin, quinine, morphine, taxol), and fossil fuels formed from ancient plant matter. Through photosynthesis they sequester roughly 120 billion tonnes of carbon per year. Deforestation and habitat loss now threaten an estimated two in five plant species with extinction.
