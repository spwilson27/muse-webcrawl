Fannie Merritt Farmer (23 March 1857 – 15 January 1915) was an American culinary expert whose Boston Cooking-School Cook Book became a widely used culinary text. She is best known for introducing the concept of using standardised measuring spoons and cups and level measurements in recipes.

Early life and career

Farmer was born in Boston, Massachusetts. A stroke at age sixteen ended her formal schooling, and she later worked as a mother's helper in the household of Mrs Charles Shaw, who encouraged her to attend the Boston Cooking School. Farmer graduated in 1889 and became assistant director, then principal of the school in 1894.

The Boston Cooking-School Cook Book

In 1896 Farmer published her best-known work, The Boston Cooking-School Cook Book, which contained some 1,849 recipes. Little, Brown and Company published it at her expense because the firm was sceptical of its prospects; it went on to become the best-selling cookbook of its era and remains in print, now simply titled The Fannie Farmer Cookbook. Its standardised level measurements revolutionised American home cooking by making recipes reproducible.

Later work

In 1902 Farmer left the school and opened Miss Farmer's School of Cookery. She also lectured, including at Harvard Medical School, and wrote on food for invalids and convalescents, reflecting her interest in nutrition and digestion.
