Russell's paradox

Russell's paradox is a paradox in naive set theory discovered by Bertrand Russell in 1901, which shows that the unrestricted principle of set formation leads to contradiction. It is often phrased as: consider the set of all sets that do not contain themselves; does it contain itself?

The paradox

Let R be the set of all sets that are not members of themselves. If R is not a member of itself, then by its definition it must be a member of itself; but if R is a member of itself, it violates its defining property — a contradiction. A popular version, the barber paradox, speaks of a barber who shaves all and only those men who do not shave themselves.

Consequences

Russell communicated the paradox to Gottlob Frege in 1902, just as the second volume of Frege's Grundgesetze — built on naive comprehension — was going to press. The paradox drove the development of axiomatic set theory, such as Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory, which restricts set formation, and of Russell's own theory of types.
