Wind quintet

InstrumentationFlute, oboe, clarinet, horn, bassoon
TypeChamber ensemble
OriginLate 18th / early 19th century

A wind quintet, also known as a woodwind quintet, is a group of five wind players (most commonly flute, oboe, clarinet, French horn, and bassoon) or the music written for such an ensemble. Unlike the string quartet, whose members all belong to the same family of instruments, the instruments in a wind quintet differ from each other considerably in technique, timbre, and character, which provides composers with a wide palette of contrasting colours.

History

The wind quintet began to take shape in the late eighteenth century, when Parisian publisher Jean-Georges Sieber and others issued works for the combination of flute, oboe, clarinet, horn, and bassoon. The Czech-born composer Anton Reicha, who wrote twenty-four quintets in the early nineteenth century, is often credited with establishing the genre; his contemporary Franz Danzi wrote nine quintets. The form declined somewhat during the Romantic era but was revived in the twentieth century by composers including Paul Hindemith, Carl Nielsen, Arnold Schoenberg, Jacques Ibert, and György Ligeti.

Notable repertoire

Standard repertoire includes Nielsen's Wind Quintet, Op. 43 (1922), Hindemith's Kleine Kammermusik, Op. 24 No. 2 (1922), Ibert's Trois pièces brèves (1930), and Ligeti's Six Bagatelles (1953) and Ten Pieces for Wind Quintet (1968). American composers such as Samuel Barber (Summer Music, 1956) and Elliott Carter have also made major contributions.
