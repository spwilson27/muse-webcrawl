The Golden Horde, self-designated as Ulug Ulus ("Great State"), was originally a Mongol and later Turkicized khanate established in the 13th century as the northwestern sector of the Mongol Empire. It originated with the appanage (ulus) granted to Jochi, the eldest son of Genghis Khan, and was consolidated under Jochi's son Batu Khan, who led the Mongol invasion of Kievan Rus' and Eastern Europe in 1236–1242.

At its height the Golden Horde controlled the Pontic-Caspian steppe and much of Eastern Europe, from the Carpathians to Siberia, and exacted tribute from the Russian principalities — a period remembered in Russian historiography as the "Mongol yoke". Its capital was Sarai on the lower Volga.

History

Under Batu and his brother Berke, the Horde became effectively independent of the Great Khan. Berke was the first Mongol ruler to convert to Islam, and under Özbeg Khan (r. 1313–1341) Islam became the state religion and the Horde reached its greatest territorial extent and prosperity, trading through Black Sea ports such as Kaffa. The khanate suffered defeat when Timur invaded in 1391–1395, sacking Sarai. In the 15th century it fragmented into successor khanates — Kazan, Crimea, Astrakhan, and the Kazakh and Uzbek hordes — while the rump "Great Horde" was destroyed by the Crimean Khanate in 1502. Ivan III of Moscow had already thrown off tributary dependence in 1480 at the Great Stand on the Ugra River.

Name

The name "Golden Horde" appears in Russian chronicles only from the later 16th century; it is thought to refer to the gilded tent of the khan, or to the golden banner and treasure of the Jochid court.
