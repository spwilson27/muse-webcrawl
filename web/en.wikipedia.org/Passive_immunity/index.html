Passive immunity

Passive immunity is the transfer of active humoral immunity of ready-made antibodies from one individual to another. Unlike active immunity, in which the body's own immune system produces antibodies in response to an antigen, passive immunity provides immediate but short-lived protection, lasting only as long as the transferred antibodies survive — typically weeks to a few months.

Natural passive immunity: maternal antibodies protect the newborn.

Natural passive immunity

Naturally occurring passive immunity arises when a mother passes antibodies to her offspring. IgG antibodies cross the placenta during pregnancy, protecting the fetus and newborn against infections the mother has encountered, while IgA antibodies in colostrum and breast milk protect the infant's gut and respiratory tract during the first months of life.

Artificial passive immunity

Artificially acquired passive immunity is achieved by administering antibody preparations, or antisera, from an immune donor. Examples include immune globulin given after exposure to hepatitis A or measles, rabies immunoglobulin, antivenoms for snake and scorpion bites, and monoclonal antibody treatments such as palivizumab for respiratory syncytial virus. Because the recipient's own immune system is not stimulated, no immunological memory is formed, and protection decays as the antibodies are catabolized.

History

The therapeutic use of passive immunity began in the 1890s, when Emil von Behring and Shibasaburo Kitasato showed that serum from animals immunized against diphtheria and tetanus could cure infected animals; serum therapy became the first effective treatment for diphtheria and earned Behring the first Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1901.
