Soil erosion

Soil erosion is the displacement of the upper layer of soil, or topsoil, by agents such as water, wind, ice, and gravity. It is a form of soil degradation and a naturally occurring process that human activity — especially deforestation, overgrazing, and intensive agriculture — can accelerate far beyond the rate of soil formation. Erosion removes the most fertile part of the soil profile, reducing agricultural productivity, and carries sediment and attached nutrients and pollutants into waterways, where they cause siltation and eutrophication.

Processes

Water erosion

Water erosion begins with raindrop impact detaching soil particles (splash erosion). Water flowing over the surface removes a thin, fairly uniform layer in sheet erosion, concentrates into small channels in rill erosion, and, where flow converges, cuts deep channels in gully erosion that can consume whole fields. Stream and river banks are eroded by channel flow, and coastal erosion is driven by wave action.

Wind erosion

In arid and semi-arid regions, and on bare or sandy fields, wind lifts fine particles into suspension, rolls and bounces coarser grains (saltation and surface creep), and deposits material downwind in drifts and dunes. The American Dust Bowl of the 1930s — when drought and ploughed-up prairie soils produced enormous dust storms — remains the emblematic case; wind erosion also exports fertile dust from deserts such as the Sahara, fertilising distant ecosystems.

Other forms

Gravity-driven mass wasting (landslides, slumps, soil creep), glacial erosion, and tillage erosion — the net downslope movement of soil caused by ploughing — also redistribute soil on landscapes.

Causes and estimation

Erosion rates depend on rainfall intensity and runoff, soil erodibility, slope length and steepness, vegetation cover, and land management — the factors combined in the Universal Soil Loss Equation (USLE) and its revised form RUSLE, developed from the work of Walter Wischmeier and Dwight Smith for the US Department of Agriculture. Loss of vegetation cover through deforestation, overgrazing, or bare fallow is the factor most often responsible for severe acceleration.

Impacts and control

On-site impacts include declining crop yields, loss of soil organic matter and water-holding capacity, and exposure of subsoil; off-site impacts include reservoir siltation, damaged fisheries and coral reefs, flooding, and nutrient runoff. Control measures include contour ploughing, terracing, cover crops and crop residues, conservation (reduced) tillage, shelterbelts and windbreaks, riparian buffers, and reforestation of steep or fragile land. Modern policy frameworks — from the US Soil Conservation Service (created in 1935 in response to the Dust Bowl) to the UN Convention to Combat Desertification — treat soil erosion as a global sustainability problem, with estimates suggesting a substantial fraction of the world's agricultural land has been degraded since the mid-20th century.
