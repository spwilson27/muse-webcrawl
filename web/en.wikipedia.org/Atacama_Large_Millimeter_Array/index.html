The Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array (ALMA) is an astronomical interferometer of 66 radio telescopes in the Atacama Desert of northern Chile, which observes electromagnetic radiation at millimetre and submillimetre wavelengths. Built on the 5,000-metre-high Chajnantor plateau — one of the driest, clearest observing sites on Earth — the array cost about US$1.4 billion and is the most expensive ground-based telescope in operation.

Operation

ALMA's antennas can be moved across the plateau into configurations with baselines from 150 metres to 16 km, giving it a "zoom" capability and angular resolution exceeding that of the Hubble Space Telescope at its operating wavelengths. Signals are combined by the ALMA Correlator, one of the most powerful supercomputers used in astronomy.

Science

ALMA studies the cold universe: star and planet formation, protoplanetary discs (its 2014 image of HL Tauri revealed rings suggesting planets forming), distant galaxies, astrochemistry and the cosmic microwave background. As part of the Event Horizon Telescope array, ALMA contributed to the first images of black holes (M87* in 2019 and Sagittarius A* in 2022).
