Achelous

In ancient Greek religion and mythology, Achelous (Ancient Greek: Ἀχελώϊος, Akhelóios) was the god associated with the Achelous River, the largest river in Greece. As a river god and a shapeshifter, Achelous was the father of the Sirens by one account and a celebrated suitor of Deianira, whose hand he lost to Heracles in a wrestling match. The Greeks considered river gods generically under his name, and his image — often a bearded man's head with a bull's horns — appeared on coins across the Greek world.

Mythology

Achelous was numbered among the three thousand river gods, sons of Oceanus and Tethys. In the contest for Deianira, daughter of King Oeneus of Calydon, he successively transformed himself into a serpent and a bull; Heracles tore off one of his horns and defeated him. The broken horn became, in one tradition, the cornucopia, the horn of plenty later filled with fruit and grain by the naiads. The story was retold by Sophocles in the Women of Trachis and by Ovid in the Metamorphoses.

Worship and representation

Because of the oracle of Dodona's instruction to "sacrifice to Achelous", his name came to stand for fresh water generally; Homer even ranks him above Oceanus in the Iliad. Greek and Etruscan art exported the mask-like "Achelous mask" as an architectural and jewelry motif throughout the Mediterranean, and it later appeared on early coinage as far away as Sicily and Italy.
