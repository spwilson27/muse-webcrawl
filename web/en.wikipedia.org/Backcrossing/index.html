Backcrossing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Backcrossing is a crossing of a hybrid with one of its parents or an individual genetically similar to its parent, in order to achieve offspring with a genetic identity closer to that of the parent. It is used in animal and plant breeding, in conservation genetics, and in research to create congenic strains of laboratory organisms.

Principle

An F1 hybrid carries, on average, 50% of its genome from each parent. Each backcross to the recurrent parent halves the expected contribution of the donor parent: after one backcross the offspring average 75% recurrent-parent genome, after two 87.5%, and so on. Breeders typically backcross for five or more generations while selecting for a desired trait — such as a disease-resistance gene — introduced from the donor, then self-pollinate or intermate to fix the trait. Marker-assisted selection can speed the recovery of the recurrent parent's genome by identifying individuals carrying the least donor DNA.

Applications

In plant breeding, backcrossing is the classic method for introgressing a single valuable character (for example, rust resistance in wheat) into an elite cultivar without otherwise changing it. In animal husbandry it is used in grading up, and in conservation it has been used cautiously to rescue inbred populations. In mouse genetics, repeated backcrossing for ten or more generations produces congenic strains that differ from the recipient strain at only a small chromosomal segment, allowing the effect of individual genes to be studied. A related technique in microbiology and genetics is the backcross to a parental strain to remove unwanted mutations accumulated during mutagenesis.

Limitations

Genes closely linked to the selected trait tend to be carried along with it ("linkage drag"), potentially introducing undesirable donor characteristics. Backcrossing also narrows genetic diversity if practised excessively, and for quantitative traits controlled by many genes the method is less effective, since selection cannot retain all of the donor's contributing alleles.
