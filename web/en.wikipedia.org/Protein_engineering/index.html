Protein engineering

Engineered proteins are designed or evolved to acquire new or improved functions.

Protein engineering is the process of developing useful or valuable proteins through the design and production of unnatural polypeptides, often by modifying amino acid sequences found in nature or by creating entirely new sequences. It sits at the interface of biochemistry, molecular biology and, increasingly, computational biology, and it underpins much of modern biotechnology, from industrial enzymes to therapeutic antibodies.

Approaches

Two broad strategies dominate. Rational design uses knowledge of a protein's structure and mechanism — from X-ray crystallography, NMR, cryo-electron microscopy or predicted models — to introduce specific mutations expected to produce a desired property. Directed evolution instead generates large libraries of random or recombined variants and screens or selects them for improved function, mimicking natural selection in the laboratory; Frances Arnold received the 2018 Nobel Prize in Chemistry in part for this method.

More recent approaches include de novo design, in which computational methods such as Rosetta and machine-learning tools design sequences that fold into specified structures, and semi-rational methods that focus mutagenesis on promising regions of a protein.

Applications

Engineered enzymes are used in detergents, food processing, biofuel production and pharmaceutical synthesis, where they often replace harsher chemical catalysts. In medicine, protein engineering produced humanised antibodies, long-acting insulin analogues, engineered cytokines and enzymes for replacement therapy. Engineered binding proteins also serve as biosensors, research reagents and components of synthetic biological circuits.

Challenges

Predicting how a mutation will affect folding, stability and activity remains difficult because of epistasis — interactions between residues — and because most random mutations are neutral or deleterious. Advances in structure prediction, notably AlphaFold, and in high-throughput screening continue to expand what engineers can attempt.
