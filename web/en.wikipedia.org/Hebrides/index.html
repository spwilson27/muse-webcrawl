The Hebrides (Scottish Gaelic: Innse Gall, "Isles of the Foreigners") are an archipelago off the west coast of mainland Scotland, comprising the Inner Hebrides, close to the mainland, and the more remote Outer Hebrides to the northwest. The two island groups form part of the Hebridean Terrane and include some of the oldest rocks in Britain.

Geography

The Inner Hebrides include Skye, Mull, Islay, Jura, Arran (sometimes grouped separately), and the Small Isles. The Outer Hebrides — also called the Western Isles — form a chain running from Lewis in the north through Harris, North Uist, Benbecula, South Uist, and Barra. The largest settlement in the Outer Hebrides is Stornoway on Lewis.

History

The islands were settled in the Mesolithic period and host celebrated Neolithic and Iron Age sites, including the Callanish Stones on Lewis and Skara Brae's Orcadian counterparts further north. From the 9th century the Hebrides fell under Norse control — hence the Gaelic name "Isles of the Foreigners" — until the Treaty of Perth (1266) transferred them from Norway to Scotland. The Lordship of the Isles, semi-independent under Clan Donald, dominated the region until its forfeiture in 1493. The 19th-century Highland Clearances and land agitation profoundly shaped the islands' population and crofting culture.

Culture and language

The Hebrides are the heartland of Scottish Gaelic, which remains widely spoken, especially in the Outer Hebrides. Crofting, fishing, tweed weaving (Harris Tweed), whisky distilling (notably on Islay), and tourism are mainstays of the island economies.
