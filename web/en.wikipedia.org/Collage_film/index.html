Collage film

Collage film is a style of filmmaking created by juxtaposing found footage from disparate sources. Rather than shooting original material, the filmmaker constructs a new work through the editing — montaging archival film, newsreels, industrial films, home movies, and other pre-existing images, often combined with new sound, music, or narration. Collage film is closely related to the found footage film and to the essay film.

History

Early examples include Esfir Shub's Soviet compilation films of the 1920s, such as The Fall of the Romanov Dynasty (1927). The technique flourished in post-war avant-garde cinema: Joseph Cornell's Rose Hobart (1936) re-edited a B-movie into a dreamlike work, and Bruce Conner became the form's leading American exponent with films such as A Movie (1958), Report (1963–67), and Crossroads (1976). Arthur Lipsett in Canada, and later filmmakers such as Craig Baldwin, extended the approach to media critique and satire. Collage aesthetics also underpin works like the compilation documentaries of Adam Curtis.

Distinctions

Collage film differs from a simple compilation documentary in that the meaning arises from the juxtaposition itself, often ironically or critically, rather than from an explanatory narration. It raises legal and artistic questions about appropriation and authorship similar to those of collage in the visual arts.
