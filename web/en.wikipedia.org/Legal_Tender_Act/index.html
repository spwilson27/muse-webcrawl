Legal Tender Act

The Legal Tender Act of 1862 was an act of the United States Congress, signed into law by President Abraham Lincoln on 25 February 1862, authorizing the issuance of United States Notes — paper currency known as "greenbacks" — as legal tender not backed by gold or silver coin. It was passed to finance the Union's effort in the American Civil War.


A greenback issued under the Legal Tender Acts

Provisions

The Act authorized an initial $150 million in United States Notes; two further acts in 1862 and 1863 increased the total to $450 million. The notes were declared legal tender for all debts, public and private, except duties on imports and interest on the public debt, which remained payable in coin.

Constitutional challenges

The constitutionality of making paper money legal tender was contested in the Legal Tender Cases before the Supreme Court. In Hepburn v. Griswold (1870), the Court initially held the acts unconstitutional as applied to pre-existing debts, but reversed itself the following year in Knox v. Lee and Parker v. Davis (1871), upholding Congress's power. Juilliard v. Greenman (1884) confirmed the power in peacetime as well.
