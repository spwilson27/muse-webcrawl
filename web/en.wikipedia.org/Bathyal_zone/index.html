Bathyal zone

The bathyal zone or bathypelagic — from Greek βαθύς (bathýs), "deep" — is the part of the open ocean that extends from a depth of about 1,000 to 4,000 metres (3,300 to 13,000 ft) below the ocean surface. It lies between the mesopelagic (twilight) zone above and the abyssal zone below, and corresponds roughly to the continental slope and rise of the seabed.

The bathyal zone lies between the mesopelagic and abyssal zones.

Conditions and life

Sunlight does not reach the bathyal zone, so photosynthesis is impossible and the only light is bioluminescence produced by animals themselves. Temperatures fall to about 4 °C (39 °F), pressures reach hundreds of atmospheres, and dissolved oxygen and food are scarce; most energy arrives as "marine snow" sinking from the surface layers. Average depth of the world's oceans is about 4,000 metres, so the bathyal spans much of the ocean volume.

Despite the harsh conditions, the zone supports a diverse fauna, including many species of fish (such as rattails and cusk-eels), cephalopods including the giant squid's predators, gulper eels, sponges, starfish, and deep-sea corals on slope habitats. Bathyal animals generally lack swim bladders, have reduced musculature and slow metabolisms, and many are black or red to remain invisible.
