Killing Fields

The Killing Fields (Khmer: វាលពិឃាត) are a number of sites in Cambodia where collectively more than one million people were killed and buried by the Khmer Rouge regime between 1975 and 1979, during the Cambodian genocide. The massacres formed part of the Khmer Rouge's campaign to remake Cambodian society under Pol Pot's Democratic Kampuchea.

Process

Victims — including former officials, professionals, intellectuals, ethnic minorities, and suspected dissidents — were typically arrested, interrogated and tortured at security prisons such as S-21 (Tuol Sleng) in Phnom Penh, then transported at night to execution sites. To save bullets, prisoners were often killed with blows from agricultural tools, spikes, or sharpened palm fronds, and buried in mass graves.

Choeung Ek

The best-known site is Choeung Ek, about 15 kilometres south of Phnom Penh, where an estimated 17,000 people from S-21 were executed. A Buddhist memorial stupa built there in 1988 contains more than 5,000 human skulls, displayed behind glass panels. Mass graves across the country — at least 20,000 have been mapped by the Documentation Center of Cambodia — continue to yield remains.

Legacy

The term was popularised worldwide by the 1984 film The Killing Fields, based on the experiences of journalists Dith Pran and Sydney Schanberg. The Extraordinary Chambers in the Courts of Cambodia (the Khmer Rouge Tribunal) later prosecuted senior leaders, including S-21 commandant Kaing Guek Eav ("Duch"), for crimes committed at the killing sites. Choeung Ek and Tuol Sleng are now memorials and museums visited by hundreds of thousands of people each year.
