Territoriality principle

The territoriality principle (also called the territorial principle) is a principle of public international law and criminal law under which a sovereign state may exercise jurisdiction over conduct that occurs within its territory, regardless of the nationality of the person who commits it.

Basis

The principle rests on state sovereignty: within its borders a state's law applies to everyone present, and its courts may try offences committed there. It is the oldest and least controversial basis of prescriptive and enforcement jurisdiction, recognized since the classic formulation in the Lotus case before the Permanent Court of International Justice (1927), which held that states may not exercise power in another state's territory absent a permissive rule.

Subjective and objective territoriality

Subjective territoriality covers offences begun within the state but completed abroad (for example, a shot fired across a border). Objective territoriality covers offences begun abroad but completed, or producing substantial effects, within the state — the basis of the American "effects doctrine" in antitrust law.

Related principles

Territoriality contrasts with the nationality principle (jurisdiction over a state's own nationals wherever they act), the protective principle (jurisdiction over acts threatening state security), passive personality (based on the victim's nationality) and universal jurisdiction over grave crimes. In taxation, territorial systems tax only income sourced within the country, unlike worldwide residence-based systems.
