Canvas

Woven canvas fabric (placeholder)

Canvas is an extremely durable plain-woven fabric used for making sails, tents, marquees, backpacks, shelters, and other items for which sturdiness is required, as well as in such fashion objects as handbags and shoes. It is also popularly used by artists as a painting surface, typically stretched across a wooden frame. The word derives from the Old French canevas, ultimately from the Latin cannabis (hemp), the fibre from which early canvas was woven.

Materials and manufacture

Historically canvas was made from hemp; later flax (linen) became standard for artists' canvas, and modern canvas is usually made of cotton or linen, or sometimes polyvinyl chloride (PVC) and other synthetics. Cotton canvas known as "cotton duck" (from the Dutch doek) comes in weights graded by number — the lower the number, the heavier the fabric. Canvas differs from other heavy cotton fabrics, such as denim, in being plain weave rather than twill weave.

Canvas for painting

Canvas became the dominant support for oil painting during the Renaissance, gradually replacing wooden panels, particularly in Italy in the sixteenth century. Artists' canvas is typically stretched over a wooden stretcher frame and primed with gesso before use. Linen is prized for its strength and fine, irregular texture; cotton is cheaper and more uniform. Splined canvases and canvas boards are modern variants.

Other uses

Because of its toughness and resistance to tearing, canvas is used for sails, tents and tarpaulins, work clothing, bags and luggage, conveyor belts, and bookbinding. Water-repellent and fire-retardant treatments extend its outdoor applications. In boxing and wrestling, the floor covering of the ring is traditionally called "the canvas".
