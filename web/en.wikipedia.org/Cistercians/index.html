The Cistercians, officially the Order of Cistercians (Latin: Ordo Cisterciensis, abbreviated OCist or SOCist), are a Roman Catholic religious order of monks and nuns founded in 1098 at Cîteaux Abbey in Burgundy, France, by Robert of Molesme and a small group of monks seeking to follow the Rule of Saint Benedict more strictly.

History

The order grew explosively after the charismatic Bernard of Clairvaux joined in 1112 and founded Clairvaux Abbey. By 1153, the year of Bernard's death, there were over 340 Cistercian abbeys across Europe. The order was governed by the Carta Caritatis (Charter of Love), with an annual General Chapter at Cîteaux and visitations of daughter houses.

Life and work

Cistercians emphasised manual labour, self-sufficiency, and simplicity in liturgy and architecture; their austere churches influenced Gothic design. Lay brothers (conversi) worked large agricultural estates (granges), making the order a major force in medieval land reclamation, sheep farming, and water management. They are traditionally known as "White Monks" from their undyed white habits.

Later developments

After decline in the late medieval period and losses during the Reformation and the French Revolution, the order revived in the nineteenth century. In 1892 the stricter observance became a separate order, the Trappists (Order of Cistercians of the Strict Observance, OCSO), known for figures such as Thomas Merton.
