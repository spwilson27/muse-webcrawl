Zaragoza (in English historically Saragossa) is the capital of the autonomous community of Aragon and of the province of Zaragoza, in northeastern Spain. With about 675,000 inhabitants it is Spain's fifth-largest city, situated on the Ebro river midway between Madrid and Barcelona.

History

The city was founded by the Romans as Caesaraugusta around 14 BC, a colony for veterans of the Cantabrian Wars, from which its modern name derives by way of Arabic Saraqusta. Under Muslim rule it became capital of an important taifa kingdom, whose Aljafería palace survives as a masterpiece of Islamic architecture in Spain. Alfonso I of Aragon captured the city in 1118, making it the capital of the Kingdom of Aragon.

Zaragoza is celebrated for its heroic resistance in the two French sieges of 1808–09 during the Peninsular War, in which figures such as Agustina de Aragón became national symbols; the city was devastated and lost much of its population. In the Spanish Civil War it was held by the Nationalists from the outset.

Sights and culture

The city's great landmark is the Baroque Basilica-Cathedral of Our Lady of the Pillar, patroness of Spain and the Hispanic world, said to commemorate an apparition of the Virgin to St James in 40 AD; its towers rise over the Ebro at the Plaza del Pilar, which also holds La Seo cathedral — famed for its Mudéjar wall, part of the UNESCO-listed Mudéjar Architecture of Aragon. The painter Francisco Goya, born nearby in Fuendetodos and trained in Zaragoza, has a major presence in the city's museums. Zaragoza hosted the Expo 2008 world's fair on the theme of water and sustainable development.
