Bunraku (文楽), also known as ningyō jōruri, is a form of traditional Japanese puppet theatre founded in Osaka in the beginning of the 17th century, still performed today. Three kinds of performers take part: the ningyōtsukai or ningyōzukai (puppeteers), the tayū (chanters), and shamisen musicians. Each main puppet is operated by three puppeteers working in full view of the audience — the chief puppeteer with face uncovered, and two assistants hooded in black — a system perfected in the 18th century. The tayū narrates and voices all characters while the shamisen provides musical accompaniment in the gidayū-bushi style.

History

Bunraku grew from the combination of jōruri narrative chanting, shamisen music, and puppetry, named after the theatre organizer Uemura Bunrakuken. Its golden age came with the chanter Takemoto Gidayū and the playwright Chikamatsu Monzaemon, often called Japan's Shakespeare, whose history plays and domestic tragedies — including The Love Suicides at Sonezaki (1703) and The Battles of Coxinga — remain central to the repertoire. Many bunraku plays were later adapted into kabuki.

Puppets and performance

The puppets are large — roughly one-half to two-thirds human size — with articulated heads, eyes, mouths, and fingers controlled by the chief puppeteer. Puppeteers train for decades, traditionally spending ten years operating the feet, ten the left arm, before graduating to the head and right arm. The National Bunraku Theatre in Osaka and the National Theatre in Tokyo are the principal venues. In 2008 bunraku was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.
