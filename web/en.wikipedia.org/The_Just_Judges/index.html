The Just Judges is the lower left panel of the Ghent Altarpiece, painted by Jan van Eyck or his brother Hubert and completed in 1432 for St Bavo's Cathedral in Ghent, Belgium. The panel depicts a procession of righteous judges on horseback. It is famous less for its artistry than for its disappearance: stolen in 1934, it has never been recovered, and the panel displayed in the altarpiece today is a copy painted in 1945 by the Belgian art restorer Jef Van der Veken.

The theft

On the night of 10–11 April 1934, two panels were stolen from the altarpiece. The reverse panel, depicting St John the Baptist in grisaille, was later returned after a ransom demand, but The Just Judges was withheld. The thief demanded one million Belgian francs from the Bishop of Ghent; negotiations failed. On his deathbed in November 1934, Arsène Goedertier, a stockbroker from Wetteren, confessed to his lawyer that he alone knew where the panel was hidden and that he was taking the secret to his grave. Despite extensive searches — Goedertier's papers reportedly contained carbon copies of the ransom letters — the panel was never found.

Theories and investigations

The theft remains one of the most famous unsolved art crimes. Theories have held that the panel was hidden in a church, a public building in Ghent, or destroyed; amateur and professional investigators have periodically announced new leads, and Belgian police have opened new lines of inquiry as recently as the 21st century. During the Second World War the rest of the altarpiece was seized by Nazi forces and recovered from the Altaussee salt mine by the Monuments Men.

The copy

Van der Veken's copy, made with the aid of photographs, has hung in the altarpiece in place of the original since 1945; a subtle change to one of the riders' faces is sometimes said to mark it as a copy. If the original survives, it is among the most valuable missing artworks in the world.
