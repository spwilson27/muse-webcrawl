The New York State Legislature is the bicameral state legislature of the U.S. state of New York. It consists of two houses: the New York State Senate and the New York State Assembly. The Constitution of New York does not designate an official term for the combined body, but it is sometimes referred to in statutes as the Legislature.

The State Senate has 63 members, each elected from a single-member district for two-year terms. The State Assembly has 150 members, likewise elected for two-year terms from single-member districts. There are no term limits for members of either house. The Legislature meets at the New York State Capitol in Albany, typically in session from January through June.

Powers and functions

The Legislature enacts state laws, adopts the state budget jointly with the Governor, levies taxes, and may propose amendments to the state constitution. Bills passed by both houses are presented to the Governor, who may sign or veto them; a veto may be overridden by a two-thirds vote of both houses. The Senate has the additional power to confirm certain gubernatorial appointments and to try officials impeached by the Assembly.

History

The first New York state legislature met in 1777 during the American Revolutionary War. Over the centuries the body has been at the center of major episodes in the state's history, from the construction of the Erie Canal to Progressive Era reforms following the Triangle Shirtwaist fire. For much of the late 20th century, control of the Legislature was split between the parties — Republicans holding the Senate and Democrats the Assembly — producing the system of negotiation among the "three men in a room": the Governor, the Senate Majority Leader, and the Assembly Speaker. In 2019, Democrats gained control of both houses for a sustained period for the first time in decades.
