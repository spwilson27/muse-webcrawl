Adenoma

An adenoma is a benign tumor of epithelial tissue with glandular origin, glandular characteristics, or both. Adenomas can grow in many organs, including the colon, pituitary gland, thyroid, adrenal glands, liver and breast. Although benign, some adenomas are precancerous and can transform over time into malignant adenocarcinomas; the adenoma–carcinoma sequence in the colon is the classic example.

Types by site

  • Colorectal adenomas (adenomatous polyps) are common, increasing in frequency with age. They are classified as tubular, tubulovillous or villous; larger size, villous architecture and high-grade dysplasia increase cancer risk. They are removed at colonoscopy.
  • Pituitary adenomas may secrete hormones (e.g. prolactinomas secreting prolactin, growth-hormone adenomas causing acromegaly) or cause effects by compressing the optic chiasm.
  • Adrenal adenomas are frequently incidental findings on imaging; some secrete cortisol (causing Cushing's syndrome) or aldosterone (Conn's syndrome).
  • Hepatic adenomas are associated with oral contraceptive use and anabolic steroids and carry risks of bleeding and malignant transformation.
  • Thyroid adenomas and parathyroid adenomas (the usual cause of primary hyperparathyroidism).

Diagnosis and treatment

Adenomas are often detected incidentally on imaging or endoscopy. Diagnosis is confirmed histologically after biopsy or excision. Treatment depends on the site: endoscopic or surgical removal for colonic and other accessible adenomas, medication (such as dopamine agonists for prolactinomas) or surgery for pituitary tumors, and surveillance for small, non-functioning incidental lesions.
