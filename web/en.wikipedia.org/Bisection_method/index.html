Bisection method

In mathematics, the bisection method is a root-finding method that repeatedly bisects an interval and selects a subinterval in which a root must lie, for further processing. It is a simple and robust method, but relatively slow compared with methods such as Newton's method.

Method

Given a continuous function f and an interval [a, b] with f(a) and f(b) of opposite sign, the intermediate value theorem guarantees a root inside. Evaluate f at the midpoint c; replace the endpoint with the same sign as f(c) by c, halving the interval. Iterate until the interval is sufficiently small.

Convergence

The error after n iterations is at most (b−a)/2n — linear convergence, gaining roughly one decimal digit every three iterations. The method cannot fail for continuous functions with a sign change, but it cannot detect roots that touch without crossing, and it requires a bracketing interval.

Related methods

Regula falsi, the secant method and Brent's method improve speed while retaining or relaxing bracketing; bisection is often used to obtain a safe starting approximation.
