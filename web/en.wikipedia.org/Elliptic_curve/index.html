In mathematics, an elliptic curve is a smooth, projective, algebraic curve of genus one, on which there is a specified point O. Over the real or complex numbers an elliptic curve can be written in Weierstrass form

y² = x³ + ax + b,

with discriminant 4a³ + 27b² ≠ 0 ensuring smoothness. Despite the name, elliptic curves are not ellipses; the name comes from their historical connection with elliptic integrals arising in the computation of arc lengths of ellipses.

Group law

The rational points on an elliptic curve form an abelian group, with O as identity: geometrically, a line through two points on the curve meets it in a third, whose reflection gives their sum. The Mordell–Weil theorem states that the group of rational points of an elliptic curve over the rationals is finitely generated — a finite torsion part plus a free part whose rank is a deep invariant.

Applications

Elliptic curves are central to modern number theory: the modularity theorem for elliptic curves over Q was the key to Andrew Wiles's proof of Fermat's Last Theorem. Over finite fields, the difficulty of the discrete logarithm problem in the group of points underlies elliptic-curve cryptography, and Lenstra's elliptic-curve method is a powerful integer factorisation algorithm. The Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture, relating the rank to the curve's L-function, is one of the Millennium Prize Problems.
