Minimal surface

In mathematics, a minimal surface is a surface that locally minimizes its area. Equivalently, it has zero mean curvature at every point. Minimal surfaces arise naturally as the shapes of soap films spanning wire frames, since surface tension drives a film to minimize its area.

History

Joseph-Louis Lagrange introduced the subject in 1762; Jean Baptiste Meusnier identified the first examples, the catenoid and the helicoid, in 1776. The study of the Plateau problem — proving that a minimal surface exists spanning any given boundary — was a major 20th-century achievement of Jesse Douglas and Tibor Radó.

Examples

Classical examples include the plane, the catenoid (a surface of revolution of a catenary), the helicoid, and Enneper's surface. In the 1980s, Celso Costa discovered the Costa surface, the first new complete embedded example in over a century, followed by the Hoffman–Meeks family.

Applications

Minimal surfaces appear in architecture (tensile structures by Frei Otto), materials science, molecular engineering, and general relativity (apparent horizons).
