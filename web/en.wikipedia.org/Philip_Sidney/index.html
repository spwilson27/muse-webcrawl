Philip Sidney

Sir Philip Sidney (30 November 1554 – 17 October 1586) was an English poet, courtier, scholar, and soldier who is remembered as one of the most prominent figures of the Elizabethan age. His works include a prose romance, The Countess of Pembroke's Arcadia, a sonnet sequence, Astrophil and Stella, and a treatise, The Defence of Poesy. He died fighting the Spanish at the Battle of Zutphen.

Life

Sidney was born at Penshurst Place, Kent, the eldest son of Sir Henry Sidney and Lady Mary Dudley. Educated at Shrewsbury School and Christ Church, Oxford, he travelled in Europe during the early 1570s and witnessed the St. Bartholomew's Day Massacre in Paris in 1572. As a leading Protestant courtier he advocateded confrontation with Spain, and in 1585 he joined the English expedition to the Netherlands under his uncle Robert Dudley, Earl of Leicester.

Death and legend

Sidney was mortally wounded in the thigh at the Battle of Zutphen on 22 September 1586 and died 25 days later at Arnhem. Legend holds that, lying wounded, he gave his water to a wounded fellow soldier, saying, "Thy necessity is yet greater than mine." His body was returned to London and interred at St Paul's Cathedral with a state funeral.

Literary works

Sidney's writings, mostly published posthumously, transformed English letters. Astrophil and Stella (1591) initiated the Elizabethan sonnet vogue; the Arcadia is a landmark of English prose fiction; and The Defence of Poesy (1595) remains a foundational work of English literary criticism.
