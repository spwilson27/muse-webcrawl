Crystal structure

In crystallography, crystal structure is a description of the ordered arrangement of atoms, ions, or molecules in a crystalline material. Ordered structures arise because the constituent particles arrange themselves in a regular, repeating pattern that minimizes energy. The structure of a crystal can be described in terms of its unit cell — the smallest repeating unit — together with the symmetry operations that generate the full lattice.

Unit cell and lattice

A crystal is built by translating a unit cell, defined by three lattice vectors and the angles between them, throughout space. There are seven crystal systems (triclinic, monoclinic, orthorhombic, tetragonal, trigonal, hexagonal, and cubic) and 14 Bravais lattices describing all possible translational symmetries in three dimensions. Combined with point-group symmetries, these yield 230 space groups.

Determination

Crystal structures are most commonly determined by X-ray crystallography, in which a beam of X-rays diffracts from the periodic electron density of the crystal. Neutron and electron diffraction are used for special cases, such as locating hydrogen atoms or studying tiny crystals. Famous structure determinations include those of DNA, table salt, and thousands of proteins.

Defects and properties

Real crystals contain defects — vacancies, interstitial atoms, dislocations, and grain boundaries — that strongly influence mechanical, electrical, and optical properties. The study of crystal structure underlies materials science, mineralogy, chemistry, and solid-state physics.
