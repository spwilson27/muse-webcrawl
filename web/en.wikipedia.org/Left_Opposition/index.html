The Left Opposition was a faction within the Russian Communist Party (Bolsheviks) formed in 1923 and led by Leon Trotsky. It opposed the policies and growing bureaucratic power of the party leadership triumvirate of Joseph Stalin, Grigory Zinoviev and Lev Kamenev, and later of the Stalin–Bukharin bloc.

The Left Opposition criticised the theory of "socialism in one country," advocated rapid industrialisation financed by taxing the wealthier peasantry, defended inner-party democracy, and upheld Trotsky's theory of permanent revolution. Its members were progressively removed from office, and after the Fifteenth Party Congress in December 1927 its leading members were expelled from the party; Trotsky was exiled to Alma-Ata in 1928 and deported from the Soviet Union in 1929.

Origins

The faction emerged in October 1923 with the "Declaration of the 46," a letter signed by 46 prominent party members protesting against the economic policies of the leadership and the suppression of discussion within the party. Trotsky's own open letter, published as The New Course in December 1923, attacked the "bureaucratic degeneration" of the party apparatus controlled by the secretariat under Stalin.

Platform

The Left Opposition's programme, set out most fully in The Platform of the Joint Opposition (1927), included:

  • Rapid, planned industrialisation, funded by progressive taxation of kulaks and Nepmen;
  • Opposition to the theory of socialism in one country, and support for international revolution;
  • Restoration of soviet and party democracy, including freedom of factional discussion;
  • Voluntary, gradual collectivisation of agriculture rather than reliance on market mechanisms.

United Opposition

In 1926 the Left Opposition formed the United Opposition with the former Zinoviev–Kamenev "New Opposition." The bloc organised demonstrations on the tenth anniversary of the October Revolution in November 1927, after which its leaders were expelled from the Central Committee and then from the party itself.

Aftermath

Many oppositionists capitulated to Stalin in 1928–29 in the hope of reinstatement; most perished in the Great Purge of the 1930s. Trotsky continued the tradition abroad through the International Left Opposition, founded in 1930, which became the Fourth International in 1938.
