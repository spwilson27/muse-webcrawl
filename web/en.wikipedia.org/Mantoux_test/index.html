Mantoux test

Administration of the Mantoux test by intradermal injection into the forearm.

The Mantoux test or Mendel–Mantoux test (also called the tuberculin skin test, TST, or PPD test) is a tool for screening for tuberculosis (TB) and for tuberculosis diagnosis. It is one of the major tuberculin skin tests used around the world, largely replacing multiple-puncture tests such as the tine test. The test is named after Charles Mantoux, a French physician who developed it from the work of Clemens von Pirquet and Robert Koch in the early twentieth century.

Procedure

A standard dose of tuberculin purified protein derivative (PPD) — typically 5 tuberculin units in the United States or 2 units in much of Europe — is injected intradermally into the volar surface of the forearm, raising a small wheal. The injection site is examined 48 to 72 hours later, and the diameter of the induration (the firm raised area, not the surrounding redness) is measured in millimetres across the forearm.

Interpretation

The result is interpreted according to the person's risk of TB. In guidelines such as those of the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, an induration of 5 mm or more is positive in people with HIV, recent contacts of active cases, or those with fibrotic chest X-ray changes; 10 mm or more is positive in people from high-prevalence countries, healthcare workers and other risk groups; and 15 mm or more is used for people with no known risk factors. A positive result indicates immune sensitisation to mycobacterial antigens but does not by itself distinguish latent infection from active disease.

Limitations

The test can give false-positive results in people vaccinated with BCG or exposed to non-tuberculous mycobacteria, and false-negative results in the very young, the elderly and the immunocompromised, or in the weeks immediately after infection. Interferon-gamma release assays (IGRAs) such as QuantiFERON are blood-based alternatives that are not affected by BCG vaccination and require only one visit.
