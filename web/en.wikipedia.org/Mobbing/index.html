Mobbing

Mobbing is the collective bullying or harassment of an individual by a group, particularly in the workplace. The term was popularised by Swedish psychologist Heinz Leymann in the 1980s, drawing on earlier ethological usage by Konrad Lorenz for group attacks by animals on a predator or outsider. In workplace research, mobbing refers to sustained hostile behaviour — gossip, exclusion, humiliation, unreasonable workloads, and undermining — directed at a target over an extended period, typically with the effect or aim of driving the person out of the organisation.

Characteristics

Leymann identified dozens of mobbing behaviours grouped into attacks on communication, social relations, reputation, occupational situation, and health. Unlike a single conflict, mobbing is defined by its frequency (at least weekly) and duration (at least six months in Leymann's operational definition). Targets often suffer serious psychological and physical consequences, including anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress symptoms.

Causes and organisational factors

Research associates mobbing with poor leadership, unclear roles, organisational change, and a workplace culture that tolerates incivility. Group dynamics such as scapegoating and conformity can escalate individual conflicts into collective persecution, sometimes with tacit management approval.

Legal and policy responses

Several countries, including Sweden (1993) and France (2002), have enacted legislation addressing psychological harassment at work. In other jurisdictions victims must rely on general employment, discrimination, or health-and-safety law. Prevention strategies emphasise clear anti-harassment policies, complaint procedures, bystander training, and management accountability. Mobbing is distinguished from ordinary workplace bullying by its collective character, though usage of the terms overlaps.
