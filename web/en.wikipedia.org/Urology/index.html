Urology is the branch of medicine and surgery concerned with diseases of the urinary tract in both sexes and of the male reproductive system. A physician who practices urology is called a urologist. The specialty combines medical management with surgery, including minimally invasive and robotic techniques, and has a long tradition of technological innovation, particularly in endoscopy.

Scope

Urologists treat urinary stones, urinary tract infections, bladder dysfunction and incontinence, benign prostatic hyperplasia, and cancers of the kidney, bladder, prostate, testis and penis. They also manage male infertility and erectile dysfunction, congenital abnormalities of the urinary tract in children, and trauma to the urinary system. Urologists perform or participate in kidney transplantation in many health systems.

Procedures

Characteristic procedures include cystoscopy, ureteroscopy with laser lithotripsy for stones, transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP), radical prostatectomy (often robot-assisted), nephrectomy, vasectomy, and extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy (ESWL), introduced in the 1980s and a landmark in non-invasive stone treatment.

History

Treatment of urinary stones and retention dates to antiquity, but urology emerged as a distinct specialty in the late 19th and early 20th centuries with the development of the cystoscope. Professional bodies include the American Urological Association (founded 1902) and the European Association of Urology. Training typically involves a residency of five to six years combining general surgery and urology, with optional fellowship subspecialization.
