Moscow Conceptualism

[Image: Unofficial art in a Soviet apartment exhibition]

Moscow Conceptualism was a movement of unofficial art in the Soviet Union that emerged in Moscow in the late 1960s and 1970s, in parallel with Western conceptual art but under very different conditions. Excluded from official exhibition venues for rejecting Socialist Realism, its artists worked in studios and apartments, staging private "apartment exhibitions" for small circles of viewers. The term is associated above all with Ilya Kabakov, and with artists including Erik Bulatov, Vitaly Komar and Alexander Melamid, Ivan Chuikov, and the Collective Actions group led by Andrei Monastyrski.

Characteristics

Moscow Conceptualism drew its material from the language and imagery of Soviet everyday life - bureaucratic documents, communal apartment routines, slogans, posters and propaganda texts - which it treated with irony, emptiness and deadpan literalism. Boris Groys, who coined the term "Moscow Romantic Conceptualism" in 1979, argued that where Western conceptualism dematerialized the art object, its Soviet counterpart confronted an environment already saturated with ideological text. Kabakov's albums and installations populated by hapless communal characters, Bulatov's paintings overlaying slogans across landscapes, and Collective Actions' near-empty performances on snowy fields outside Moscow are canonical works.

Legacy

Closely related to the movement's satirical wing is Sots Art, developed by Komar and Melamid. After emigration and perestroika, Moscow Conceptualism gained wide international recognition, with Kabakov becoming one of Russia's most exhibited artists abroad and representing Russia at the Venice Biennale in 1993.
