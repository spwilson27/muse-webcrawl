Valence and conduction bands

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In solids, electrons occupy energy bands; the size of the gap between the valence and conduction bands determines electrical behaviour.

In solid-state physics, the valence band and conduction band are the bands closest to the Fermi level, and thus determine the electrical conductivity of the solid. In nonmetals, the valence band is the highest range of electron energies in which electrons are normally present at absolute zero temperature, while the conduction band is the lowest range of vacant electronic states. On a graph of the electronic band structure, the valence band is located below the Fermi level and the conduction band above it.

Band gap

The energy difference between the top of the valence band and the bottom of the conduction band is the band gap. Its size classifies materials:

  • Conductors (metals): the valence and conduction bands overlap, or the valence band is only partly filled, so electrons move freely.
  • Semiconductors: a small gap (about 0.1–3 eV; silicon 1.14 eV, germanium 0.67 eV, gallium arsenide 1.43 eV at room temperature) that thermal energy or doping can bridge.
  • Insulators: a large gap (roughly above 4 eV; diamond 5.5 eV, silicon dioxide about 9 eV), so essentially no electrons reach the conduction band.

Electrical conduction

An electron excited into the conduction band — by heat, light (photoconduction) or an electric field — is free to move through the crystal and carry current. It leaves behind a hole in the valence band, a quasiparticle of positive charge that also migrates as neighbouring electrons shift to fill it. In semiconductors both electrons and holes contribute to conduction; their concentrations in a pure (intrinsic) semiconductor are equal and rise steeply with temperature.

Doping

Adding impurities (doping) introduces energy levels within the gap: donor atoms (e.g., phosphorus in silicon) create an n-type semiconductor with levels just below the conduction band, while acceptor atoms (e.g., boron) create a p-type semiconductor with levels just above the valence band, greatly increasing conductivity at room temperature. Junctions between p- and n-type material underlie diodes, transistors, solar cells and LEDs. In a light-emitting diode, electrons falling from the conduction band to the valence band across a direct gap release photons whose colour is set by the gap energy.

Band structure

The bands arise because the discrete energy levels of isolated atoms split into quasi-continuous bands when atoms form a crystal (Bloch's theorem). Whether the gap is direct (band extrema at the same crystal momentum, as in GaAs) or indirect (different momenta, as in silicon) determines a material's optical efficiency. Band diagrams — energy plotted against position — are the standard tool for analysing semiconductor devices, showing how bands bend at surfaces, junctions and under applied bias.
