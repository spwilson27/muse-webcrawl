Commedia dell'arte

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Commedia dell'arte (Italian for "comedy of the profession") was an early form of professional theatre that began in Italy in the sixteenth century and spread throughout Europe. It was characterised by masked "types" — fixed stock characters — and by performances built on improvisation around a loose scenario rather than a fully scripted text.

Performance style

Troupes of about ten professional actors travelled from town to town, performing outdoors or in rented halls. A short plot outline (the canovaccio) was pinned up backstage; the actors improvised dialogue and business around it, drawing on memorised speeches, songs, acrobatics, and comic set pieces called lazzi. Each actor specialised in a single character for life, and companies were notable for employing actresses — such as the celebrated Isabella Andreini — at a time when women were excluded from many European stages.

Stock characters

  • Pantalone — the lecherous, miserly old Venetian merchant.
  • Il Dottore — the pompous Bolognese doctor, full of bogus learning.
  • Il Capitano — the braggart soldier, secretly a coward.
  • Arlecchino (Harlequin) — the acrobatic, diamond-costumed servant.
  • Pulcinella — the hunchbacked Neapolitan trickster, ancestor of Punch.
  • Brighella, Colombina, Pedrolino (Pierrot) — servants and confidants of varying wit and innocence.
  • Gli Innamorati — the unmasked young lovers whose romance drives the plot.

Influence

Commedia dell'arte shaped European comedy for three centuries: it fed into Molière's farces, Goldoni's scripted comedies (and Gozzi's reaction against them), Shakespearean clowns, English pantomime and Punch and Judy, and the ballet and opera buffa traditions. Its masked types, improvisational structure, and physical comedy remain foundational to modern actor training and devised theatre.
