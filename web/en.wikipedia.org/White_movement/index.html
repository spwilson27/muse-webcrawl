White movement

The White movement (Russian: Белое движение, Beloe dvizhenie) was a loose confederation of political and military forces that opposed the Bolsheviks (the "Reds") during the Russian Civil War of 1917–1922/1923. Its members, the Whites, ranged from republican liberals and moderate socialists to monarchists and nationalists, united mainly by anti-communism. Their armed forces were known collectively as the White Army.

Composition

The White movement was never a single organisation. Its core was former Imperial Russian Army officers, but it also drew in Kadet liberals, Socialist Revolutionaries, Cossack hosts of the Don and Kuban, and regional governments. Their common programme was rarely more specific than defeating the Bolsheviks and restoring a "united and indivisible Russia"; the slogan of a future Constituent Assembly was used to defer political arguments. This vagueness, and rivalry between leaders, fatally weakened the cause.

The Civil War

Major White fronts included Admiral Alexander Kolchak's forces in Siberia (Kolchak was recognised as "Supreme Ruler" in 1918–19), General Anton Denikin's Armed Forces of South Russia, which advanced to within reach of Moscow in autumn 1919, Nikolai Yudenich's Northwestern Army threatening Petrograd, and Baron Pyotr Wrangel's army in Crimea, the last major White stronghold, evacuated in November 1920. Allied powers intervened with troops and supplies at Archangel, Murmansk, Vladivostok and elsewhere, though their commitment was limited.

Defeat and exile

By 1921–22 the Red Army under Leon Trotsky had defeated the main White armies. Hundreds of thousands of White supporters fled into exile — to Paris, Berlin, Belgrade, Harbin and elsewhere — forming the "White émigré" diaspora, which maintained monarchist and anti-Soviet organisations for decades. White resistance in the Far East ended with the fall of Vladivostok in October 1922 and the last pockets in 1923.
