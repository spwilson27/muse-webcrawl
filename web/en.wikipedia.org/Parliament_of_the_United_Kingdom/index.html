Parliament of the United Kingdom

Parliament of the United Kingdom Founded: 1801 (as Parliament of the UK of Great Britain and Ireland)
Seat: Palace of Westminster, London
Chambers: House of Commons (650 MPs); House of Lords
Head: The Sovereign (King-in-Parliament)
Legislature type: Bicameral

The Parliament of the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Northern Ireland is the supreme legislative body of the United Kingdom, and may also legislate for British overseas territories. It alone possesses legislative supremacy and thereby ultimate power over all other political bodies in the UK and the overseas territories. Parliament is bicameral, consisting of an upper house (the House of Lords) and a lower house (the House of Commons), and the monarch — currently Charles III — who together constitute the King-in-Parliament. It meets at the Palace of Westminster in London.

Composition and procedure

The House of Commons is directly elected: 650 Members of Parliament (MPs) are chosen by first-past-the-post in single-member constituencies. The House of Lords is unelected, comprising life peers appointed under the Life Peerages Act 1958, 92 hereditary peers retained after the 1999 reform, and 26 Church of England bishops (Lords Spiritual). A bill must pass both Houses and receive royal assent to become an Act of Parliament, though under the Parliament Acts 1911 and 1949 the Lords can only delay most legislation, and money bills cannot be blocked. The government is formed from, and accountable to, the Commons; the prime minister is the leader able to command its confidence.

History

The Parliament of the United Kingdom succeeded the Parliament of Great Britain in 1801 on the union with Ireland. Through the 19th century the franchise was progressively widened by the Reform Acts of 1832, 1867, and 1884, and the Parliament Act 1911 ended the Lords' veto over Commons legislation. Women gained the vote in 1918 (equalised in 1928). Irish independence in 1922 removed most Irish MPs; devolution in 1998–1999 created the Scottish Parliament, the Senedd in Wales, and the Northern Ireland Assembly, to which many domestic powers are devolved, while "English votes for English laws" procedures operated briefly in the Commons (2015–2021).

Sovereignty

The classic doctrine, stated by the jurist A. V. Dicey, is that Parliament can "make or unmake any law whatever," and no parliament can bind its successors. In practice this sovereignty has been qualified by membership of international bodies (formerly the European Union), the Human Rights Act 1998, devolution, and the courts' development of constitutional principles, continuing debates about the proper limits of parliamentary power.
