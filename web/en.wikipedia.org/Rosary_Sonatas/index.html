Rosary Sonatas

The Rosary Sonatas (also known as the Mystery Sonatas) are a cycle of sixteen sonatas for violin and basso continuo composed by the Bohemian-Austrian composer Heinrich Ignaz Franz Biber around 1676. Each sonata illustrates one of the fifteen Mysteries of the Rosary — the Joyful, Sorrowful, and Glorious Mysteries — followed by a concluding Passacaglia for solo violin. The cycle is one of the most remarkable works of the Baroque violin repertoire, famous for its extensive use of scordatura, in which the violin is retuned for almost every sonata.

Structure

The fifteen sonatas are grouped into three sets of five. The Joyful Mysteries cover the Annunciation, the Visitation, the Nativity, the Presentation in the Temple, and the Finding in the Temple. The Sorrowful Mysteries cover the Agony in the Garden, the Scourging, the Crowning with Thorns, the Carrying of the Cross, and the Crucifixion. The Glorious Mysteries cover the Resurrection, the Ascension, Pentecost, the Assumption of Mary, and the Coronation of the Virgin. In the surviving manuscript, each sonata is preceded by a small engraving depicting the relevant mystery.

Scordatura

Biber uses a different scordatura tuning for fourteen of the sixteen pieces; only the first sonata and the closing Passacaglia use normal tuning. The retunings alter the instrument's resonance and enable unusual chords and effects. For the Sonata of the Resurrection, the two middle strings are crossed over one another. The notation is written so that the player reads the notes where the fingers are placed rather than the sounding pitches, making the score a kind of tablature.

The Passacaglia

The concluding Passacaglia in G minor, for unaccompanied violin, is built on a repeating four-note descending bass pattern. It is regarded as a masterpiece of early solo violin writing and a precursor of Bach's famous Chaconne. The piece is associated with the Guardian Angel, depicted in the manuscript's final engraving.

History and reception

The cycle was probably composed for performance at the court of Salzburg, where Biber was employed, possibly for Rosary devotions during October. The work survived in a single beautifully prepared manuscript and was largely forgotten until its rediscovery in the early twentieth century. It is now a cornerstone of the Baroque violin repertoire and has been recorded many times.
