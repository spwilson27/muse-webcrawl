Looking-glass self

The looking-glass self is a social psychological concept introduced by sociologist Charles Horton Cooley in his 1902 work Human Nature and the Social Order. It describes how a person's self-image arises from interpersonal interactions and the perceptions of others: “I am not what I think I am and I am not what you think I am; I am what I think that you think I am.”

The three components

Cooley proposed that the process has three elements: we imagine how we appear to another person; we imagine that person's judgement of our appearance; and we develop feelings about ourselves — such as pride or shame — based on that imagined judgement.

Influence

The concept influenced George Herbert Mead's symbolic interactionism and later work on self-concept and socialisation, and is frequently invoked in studies of social media, where quantified feedback may act as a powerful “mirror”.
