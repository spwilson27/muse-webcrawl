Commutative algebra is the branch of algebra that studies commutative rings, their ideals, and modules over such rings. Both algebraic geometry and algebraic number theory build on commutative algebra; indeed it is often described as the "local" theory underlying algebraic geometry, much as analysis underlies differential geometry.

Prominent examples of commutative rings are polynomial rings, rings of algebraic integers (such as the Gaussian integers), and the p-adic integers.

History

The subject grew out of work on algebraic number theory and invariant theory in the nineteenth century. Richard Dedekind introduced ideals to restore unique factorisation in rings of algebraic integers. David Hilbert's basis theorem (1890) showed that every ideal in a polynomial ring over a field is finitely generated, and his Nullstellensatz linked ideals to algebraic varieties.

Emmy Noether's 1921 paper "Idealtheorie in Ringbereichen" placed the subject on an axiomatic footing, introducing the ascending chain condition; rings satisfying it are now called Noetherian rings. Wolfgang Krull developed dimension theory and the theory of local rings, while Oscar Zariski and others made the algebra–geometry dictionary precise.

Main concepts

Rings and ideals

A commutative ring is a set equipped with addition and multiplication, both commutative, with a multiplicative identity. An ideal is a subset closed under addition and under multiplication by arbitrary ring elements. Prime ideals and maximal ideals generalise prime numbers and form the points of the spectrum of a ring, Spec R, the basic object of scheme theory.

Modules

Modules over a ring generalise both vector spaces and abelian groups. Exact sequences, tensor products, localisation and completion are central tools. Localisation at a prime ideal produces a local ring, allowing problems to be studied "near a point".

Dimension and primary decomposition

The Krull dimension of a ring measures the length of chains of prime ideals. In a Noetherian ring every ideal admits a primary decomposition, an analogue of factorising an integer into prime powers. The Hilbert–Samuel function and Hilbert polynomial connect dimension with growth of graded pieces.

Standard texts

The subject's classic references include Atiyah and Macdonald's Introduction to Commutative Algebra (1969), Matsumura's Commutative Ring Theory, and Eisenbud's Commutative Algebra with a View Toward Algebraic Geometry.
