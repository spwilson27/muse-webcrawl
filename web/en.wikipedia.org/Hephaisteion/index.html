The Hephaisteion, also known as the Theseion, is a well-preserved ancient Greek temple on the north-west side of the Agora of Athens, on top of the Kolonos Agoraios hill. Construction began in 449 BC, and it is believed to be the first temple of the Periclean building programme. It was dedicated to Hephaestus, god of metalworking, and Athena Ergane, patroness of crafts.

Architecture

The temple is a peripteral Doric hexastyle temple, measuring about 13.7 by 31.8 metres, with 6 columns on the short ends and 13 along the sides. Built mainly of Pentelic marble with Parian marble sculpture, it stands on a three-stepped crepidoma. Its sculptural programme includes metopes depicting the labours of Heracles and Theseus — the latter giving rise to the popular name "Theseion" — and an Ionic frieze around the cella showing scenes including the battle of Theseus against the Pallantidae and a centauromachy. Inside stood bronze cult statues of Hephaestus and Athena by Alcamenes, made in 421–415 BC.

History

The temple overlooked the metalworkers' and potters' quarter of the Agora, appropriate to its patron deities. Around the sanctuary, excavations have revealed planting pits for a formal garden dating to the Hellenistic period. In the 7th century AD the building was converted into the Christian church of St George Akamates, which preserved much of its fabric; it remained in use until the 19th century, and Athens' last Ottoman-era burials were held there. During the Greek War of Independence it served as a burial place for philhellenes, and in 1834 it hosted the official welcome of King Otto to Athens.

Conservation

Because of its conversion to a church, the Hephaisteion is the best-preserved classical temple in Greece, retaining its roof and much of its sculpture. It is now an open-air monument within the archaeological site of the Ancient Agora of Athens.
