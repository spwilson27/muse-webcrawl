Bravais lattice

In geometry and crystallography, a Bravais lattice, named after Auguste Bravais (1850), is an infinite array of discrete points generated by a set of discrete translation operations. A Bravais lattice specifies the periodic array in which the repeated units of a crystal are arranged; the points themselves need not correspond to atoms, which sit in a basis attached to each lattice point.

The fourteen lattices

Combining the seven crystal systems with the possible centerings yields fourteen distinct lattices: one triclinic; three monoclinic (primitive and base-centered); four orthorhombic (primitive, base-, body-, and face-centered); two tetragonal (primitive and body-centered); one trigonal (rhombohedral); one hexagonal; and three cubic (primitive, body-centered, and face-centered). Other apparent centerings either violate the system's symmetry or can be redescribed with a smaller primitive cell.

Unit cells

The conventional unit cell of a Bravais lattice may contain more than one lattice point; the primitive cell contains exactly one. The Wigner–Seitz cell, formed by bisecting the lines to neighboring points, is a primitive cell displaying the full symmetry of the lattice. The reciprocal lattice of a Bravais lattice is also a Bravais lattice — for example, the reciprocal of face-centered cubic is body-centered cubic — a fact central to X-ray crystallography and solid-state physics.

Lower dimensions

In two dimensions there are five Bravais lattices (oblique, rectangular, centered rectangular, hexagonal, and square); in one dimension there is only one.
