Four Books

Chinese四書 (Sìshū)
ContentsGreat Learning, Doctrine of the Mean, Analects, Mencius
Compiled byZhu Xi (Song dynasty, 1190)

The Four Books (Chinese: 四書; pinyin: Sìshū) are Chinese classic texts illustrating the core value and belief systems in Confucianism. They were selected by the scholar Zhu Xi in the Song dynasty and have been revered as an authoritative exposition of Confucian thinking. Together with the Five Classics, they formed the core curriculum of the imperial civil service examinations from 1313 until the examinations' abolition in 1905.

The four texts

  • Great Learning (大學) — a short text on self-cultivation as the root of social order, traditionally attributed to Zengzi, a disciple of Confucius.
  • Doctrine of the Mean (中庸) — on harmony, equilibrium and sincerity, traditionally attributed to Zisi, Confucius's grandson.
  • Analects (論語) — sayings and conversations of Confucius compiled by his followers.
  • Mencius (孟子) — dialogues of the philosopher Mencius, the "Second Sage" of Confucianism, defending the innate goodness of human nature.

Significance

Zhu Xi's interlinear commentaries on the Four Books became the orthodox interpretation for the examinations; generations of officials across China (and, by extension, Korea, Vietnam and Japan) memorised the texts. The first two books were chapters extracted from the Book of Rites, elevated by Zhu Xi to stand alongside the Analects and Mencius. Like China's Four Great Inventions, the Four Books became a canonical shorthand for the foundations of traditional Chinese civilisation.
