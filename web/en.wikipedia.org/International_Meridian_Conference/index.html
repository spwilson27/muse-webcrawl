The International Meridian Conference was a conference held in October 1884 in Washington, D.C., in the United States, to determine a prime meridian for international use. The conference was held at the request of U.S. President Chester A. Arthur. The subject to be discussed was the choice of "a meridian to be employed as a common zero of longitude and standard of time reckoning throughout the world". It resulted in the recommendation of the Greenwich Meridian as the international standard for zero degrees longitude.

Background

By the 1870s there was growing pressure to establish one prime meridian for navigation, mapping, railway timetables and telegraphic time signals. Most maritime charts already used the meridian of the Royal Observatory at Greenwich, London, but many countries used their own national meridians, such as Paris, Cádiz or Washington, leading to confusion.

Proceedings and resolutions

Forty-one delegates from 25 nations met in October 1884. The conference adopted resolutions recommending: a single prime meridian passing through the centre of the transit instrument at Greenwich; longitude counted in two directions up to 180 degrees, with east longitude positive; a universal day beginning at midnight at Greenwich; and that the Conference express its hope that the astronomical and nautical days would be arranged to begin at midnight. The vote on the Greenwich meridian was 22–1, with San Domingo voting against and France and Brazil abstaining.

Legacy

Countries adopted Greenwich Mean Time progressively over the following decades; France held out with Paris Mean Time until 1911. The conference is a landmark in the standardisation of time and geography that underlies today's Coordinated Universal Time and worldwide time zones.
