Intarsia

Intarsia is a form of wood inlaying that is similar to marquetry. The term is also used for a similar technique with stone, and for a knitting technique. The start of the practice of intarsia in wood dates from before the seventh century AD. The technique of intarsia was perfected in Islamic Egypt and spread through Europe, reaching its zenith in Renaissance Italy, where panels decorated cathedrals, choir stalls, studioli and palace furniture.

Technique

Unlike marquetry, in which thin veneers are applied to the surface of an object, intarsia pieces are set into a solid ground, often at appreciable thickness. The craftsman cuts pieces of differently coloured woods — and sometimes ivory, bone, mother-of-pearl, brass or tortoiseshell — to fit hollows carved in the ground panel, so that the contrasting materials form a picture or pattern flush with the surface. Shading and three-dimensional effects can be achieved by scorching the edges of pieces in hot sand.

History

The word derives from the Italian intarsiare, "to inlay". Intarsia flourished in Italian cities such as Florence, Siena and Urbino in the 15th and 16th centuries; famous examples include the studiolo of Federico da Montefeltro in the Palazzo Ducale, Urbino, and the choir stalls of many Italian churches. Perspective designs — cityscapes, cupboards with half-open doors, and still lifes — were a specialty. In stone, the cognate technique is known as pietra dura or Florentine mosaic.

Knitting

In knitting, intarsia is a technique used to create patterns with multiple colours: blocks of colour are worked with separate balls or bobbins of yarn, the yarns being twisted around each other at the colour boundaries to prevent holes.
