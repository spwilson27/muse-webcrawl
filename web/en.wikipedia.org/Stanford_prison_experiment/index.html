Stanford prison experiment

The simulated prison in the basement of Jordan Hall, Stanford.

The Stanford prison experiment (SPE) was a social psychology experiment conducted in August 1971 by a research team led by Philip Zimbardo at Stanford University. Twenty-four male college students were randomly assigned roles of prisoners and guards in a mock prison built in the basement of the university's psychology building. Intended to last two weeks, the experiment was terminated after six days because the guards' behaviour became increasingly abusive and several prisoners showed signs of severe distress.

Interpretation

Zimbardo concluded that the situation, rather than the participants' personalities, drove the behaviour: ordinary people absorbed into institutional roles acted in ways consistent with those roles. The study became one of the most famous in psychology and is routinely cited in discussions of authority, conformity and the power of situations, alongside the obedience studies of Stanley Milgram.

Criticism

The experiment has been heavily criticized on methodological and ethical grounds. Critics charge that Zimbardo, acting as prison superintendent, encouraged guard toughness; that demand characteristics led participants to play expected roles; and that the study lacked controls and could not be replicated. Archival research published in the 2010s revealed that guards were coached and that some prisoner breakdowns may have been performed. The SPE is now often taught as a cautionary example about research ethics and scientific rigor as much as about situational power.
