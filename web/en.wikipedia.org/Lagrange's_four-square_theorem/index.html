Lagrange's four-square theorem

Every natural number is a sum of four integer squares

Lagrange's four-square theorem, also known as Bachet's conjecture, states that every natural number can be represented as a sum of four integer squares:

p = a² + b² + c² + d²

where a, b, c, d are integers. For illustration, 3 = 1² + 1² + 1² + 0², and 31 = 5² + 2² + 1² + 1². The theorem was stated without proof by Bachet in his 1621 edition of Diophantus's Arithmetica; Fermat claimed to have a proof by infinite descent but never published it. Joseph-Louis Lagrange gave the first published proof in 1770, building on work of Euler.

Proof outline

A standard proof uses Euler's four-square identity, which shows that the product of two numbers each expressible as a sum of four squares is itself such a sum. It therefore suffices to prove the theorem for prime numbers. For an odd prime p, one shows that some multiple mp (with m < p) is a sum of four squares each less than p², and then applies a descent argument — related to Fermat's method — to reduce the multiplier until m = 1.

Generalisations and related results

  • Jacobi's four-square theorem (1834) gives an exact formula for the number of ways a number can be written as a sum of four squares, in terms of its divisors.
  • Lagrange's theorem is the case g(2) = 4 of Waring's problem: every natural number is a sum of at most nine cubes, at most 19 fourth powers, and so on.
  • The 15 theorem of Conway and Schneeberger characterises the positive-definite quadratic forms that represent all positive integers.
  • Three squares suffice except for numbers of the form 4a(8b + 7) (Legendre's three-square theorem), and two squares suffice exactly for numbers whose prime factors of the form 4k + 3 occur with even exponent.
