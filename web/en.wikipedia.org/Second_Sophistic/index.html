Second Sophistic

The Second Sophistic is a term coined by the Greek writer Philostratus in his Lives of the Sophists (c. AD 230) for the flourishing of Greek oratory and rhetoric in the Roman Empire, roughly from the first to the third century AD, centred on the cities of Roman Asia — Smyrna, Ephesus and Athens. Its practitioners, the sophists, were celebrity public speakers, teachers of rhetoric and ambassadors for their cities.

Characteristics

Sophists performed improvised declamations on historical and fictional themes (meletai) in an archaizing Attic Greek, imitating classical models such as Demosthenes — part of the wider cultural movement of Atticism. Oratory was entertainment, education and civic politics at once: leading sophists such as Herodes Atticus, Aelius Aristides, Dio Chrysostom (associated with the movement's beginnings), Polemon of Laodicea and Lucian (an ironic commentator on it) won wealth, imperial favour and civic honours, interceded with emperors for their cities, and filled endowed chairs of rhetoric at Athens and Rome.

Literary legacy

The period produced much surviving Greek literature of the Roman era: the novels of Achilles Tatius and Longus, the essays and dialogues of Plutarch (slightly earlier) and Lucian, the sacred autobiographical oratory of Aelius Aristides, and Philostratus's own works, which remain the main source for the movement. Modern scholarship since the 1970s has treated the Second Sophistic as a key expression of Greek identity under Roman rule.
