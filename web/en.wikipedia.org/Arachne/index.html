Arachne

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Arachne (Greek: Ἀράχνη, "spider") is the protagonist of a tale in Greek mythology known primarily from the version told by the Roman poet Ovid in his Metamorphoses (Book VI). According to the story, Arachne was a shepherd's daughter from Lydia who became famous for her weaving. Boasting that her skill surpassed even that of Athena, the goddess of wisdom and crafts, she challenged the goddess to a contest. Arachne's tapestry depicted the amorous adventures of the gods; finding no fault in the work, Athena destroyed it in anger and struck Arachne, who hanged herself in shame and despair. The goddess, in a gesture of mingled pity and punishment, sprinkled Arachne with the juice of Hecate's herb and transformed her into a spider, condemning her and her descendants to weave forever.

The myth

Ovid's account gives Arachne's home as Hypaepa in Lydia and her father as Idmon, a dyer of wool in purple. Her fame spread through Lydia, and nymphs came to watch her work. When she denied that Athena had taught her and issued her challenge, the goddess appeared first disguised as an old woman to warn her, then in her true form. In the contest, Athena wove the triumph of the gods over mortals and her victory over Poseidon for the patronage of Athens, bordered with scenes of mortals punished for hubris. Arachne's tapestry instead catalogued the deceptions and abductions of Zeus, Poseidon, and Apollo — a technically flawless but impious work. Athena tore the cloth and struck Arachne with her shuttle; Arachne, unable to endure the shame, hanged herself. Athena loosed the noose and transformed her, saying she and her offspring would hang and weave forever.

Interpretations

Las Hilanderas by Velázquez
The myth depicted in the background

Commentators read the tale as a warning against hubris, a meditation on art that challenges power, and an aetiological myth explaining the spider and its web — the Greek word arakhnē gives zoology the class name Arachnida for spiders and their relatives. Some scholars see in Arachne's tapestry a critique of divine misconduct unusual for its boldness, and in her transformation a preservation of the artist in her art: she weaves still.

In later art and culture

The story appears in Dante's Purgatorio, is alluded to in Spenser's Muiopotmos, and forms the backdrop of Velázquez's painting Las Hilanderas (The Spinners). Arachne has lent her name to literary works, spacecraft components, and the clinical term arachnophobia.
