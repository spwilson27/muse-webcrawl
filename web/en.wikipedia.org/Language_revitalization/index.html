Language revitalization

Language revitalization, also called language revival or reversing language shift, is an effort by governments, communities, and linguists to halt or reverse the decline of a language or to revive an extinct one. Of the roughly 7,000 languages spoken in the world today, a large proportion are endangered, and linguists predict that many will cease to be spoken during the 21st century.

Methods

Common strategies include immersion preschools (such as the Māori kōhanga reo "language nests" and Hawaiian Pūnana Leo schools), master–apprentice programmes pairing fluent elders with learners, documentation and archiving of recordings and texts, development of teaching materials and dictionaries, and official status measures that give the language a role in education, media, and public life. Joshua Fishman's Graded Intergenerational Disruption Scale is widely used to assess how far shift has progressed and what steps are feasible.

Successful and notable cases

The revival of Hebrew as a spoken vernacular in the late 19th and 20th centuries is the most famous case of a language returning to everyday use. Other notable efforts include those for Irish, Welsh, Basque, Catalan, Hawaiian, Māori, and many Indigenous languages of North America and Australia, such as Wampanoag (Wôpanâak), which was revived from written records after more than a century without native speakers.

Challenges

Revitalization faces obstacles including the dominance of global languages in education and employment, small numbers of remaining speakers, dialect disputes, and debates over authenticity when a revived variety differs from historical forms. Scholars emphasise that long-term success depends above all on restoring transmission of the language to children in the home.
