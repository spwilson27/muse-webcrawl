Vladimir Nikolaievich Lossky (Russian: Влади́мир Никола́евич Ло́сский; 8 June 1903 – 7 February 1958) was a Russian Orthodox theologian exiled in Paris. He emphasized theosis saved by grace as the main principle of Eastern Orthodox Christianity, and his best-known work, The Mystical Theology of the Eastern Church (1944), remains a standard introduction to Orthodox doctrine for Western readers.

Life

Lossky was the son of the philosopher Nikolai Lossky. The family was expelled from Soviet Russia in 1922 on the "philosophers' ship" and settled first in Prague and then in Paris. Lossky studied at the Sorbonne, became a founding figure of the Brotherhood of Saint Photius, and taught dogmatic theology at the St. Sergius Orthodox Theological Institute and later at the Institut Saint-Denys, which he helped found for French-speaking Orthodox students.

Theology

Lossky championed a "neo-patristic" return to the Greek Fathers, an apophatic (negative) theology that stresses the unknowability of God's essence while affirming real participation in his uncreated energies, following Gregory Palamas. He was a sharp critic of the sophiology of Sergei Bulgakov and a leading figure in the 20th-century ressourcement of Orthodox theology in the West. His unfinished Dogmatic Theology and essay collection In the Image and Likeness of God were published posthumously.
