Single-camera setup

The single-camera setup, or single-camera mode of production, also known as portable single crew, portable single camera, single cam, and in some contexts ENG, is a method of filmmaking and television production.

Single-camera production was originally developed in the early 1910s as filmmaking shifted from the shorter one-, two- and three-reel films to feature-length films. By the 1930s it had become the standard method for cinema. For television, the multiple-camera setup was developed in the early 1950s and became dominant for sitcoms and talk shows, but the single-camera approach remained favoured for drama, and from the 1990s onward became increasingly common in comedy series as well.

Method

In a single-camera production, each scene is filmed with one camera. Every shot — wide shots, close-ups, over-the-shoulder angles and cutaways — is set up and shot separately, usually in a non-chronological order, and assembled in the editing room. This gives the director and cinematographer full control over lighting, composition and performance for each individual set-up, but it is slower and often more expensive than multi-camera work.

Comparison with multiple-camera setup

Single-camera productions are not typically filmed before a live studio audience and therefore lack a laugh track (unless one is added in post-production). Series shot single-camera include many prestige dramas and mockumentary-style comedies such as The Office, while the multiple-camera format is associated with traditional studio sitcoms filmed on a permanent set.
