Chemical warfare

Chemical warfare (CW) involves using the toxic properties of chemical substances as weapons. This type of warfare is distinct from nuclear warfare, biological warfare and radiological warfare, which together make up CBRN, the military acronym for chemical, biological, radiological, and nuclear. Chemical warfare involves the use of chemical agents—such as choking, blister, blood, and nerve agents—delivered by artillery shells, bombs, rockets, or spraying, to kill, injure, or incapacitate an enemy.

History

Poisoned arrows, toxic smokes, and contaminated water supplies were used in ancient and medieval warfare, and early attempts at toxic weapons were reported from antiquity through the American Civil War. Industrial-scale chemical warfare began in the First World War: on 22 April 1915 German forces released chlorine gas at the Second Battle of Ypres, opening an era in which both sides deployed chlorine, phosgene, and mustard gas on a massive scale. Chemical weapons caused an estimated 90,000 deaths and over a million casualties in that war and hastened the development of gas masks and protective equipment.

The 1925 Geneva Protocol prohibited the use of chemical and biological weapons in war. Nevertheless, chemical weapons were used by Italy in Ethiopia, by Japan in China, and by Egypt in Yemen. During the Second World War the belligerents stockpiled chemical weapons—including the newly discovered nerve agents tabun and sarin in Germany—but largely refrained from battlefield use in Europe. Iraq used mustard and nerve agents extensively in the Iran–Iraq War of the 1980s and against Kurdish civilians at Halabja in 1988. Sarin was used in attacks in Syria during its civil war, and by the Aum Shinrikyo cult in the 1995 Tokyo subway attack.

Agents and effects

Chemical agents are classified by their physiological effects. Choking agents such as phosgene damage the lungs; blister agents such as sulfur mustard burn skin and eyes; blood agents such as hydrogen cyanide interfere with cellular respiration; and nerve agents such as sarin and VX disrupt the nervous system by inhibiting acetylcholinesterase. Incapacitating and riot-control agents form further categories.

Disarmament

The Chemical Weapons Convention of 1993, administered by the Organisation for the Prohibition of Chemical Weapons (OPCW) in The Hague, bans the development, production, stockpiling, and use of chemical weapons and requires the destruction of declared stockpiles. Nearly all states are parties, and tens of thousands of tonnes of declared agent have been verifiably destroyed; the last declared United States munitions were destroyed in 2023. The OPCW received the Nobel Peace Prize in 2013.
