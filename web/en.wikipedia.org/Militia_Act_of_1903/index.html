Militia Act of 1903

Militia Act of 1903 ("Dick Act")
Enacted: 21 January 1903
Sponsor: Representative Charles W. F. Dick
Effect: Organised state militias as the National Guard with federal standards and funding

The Militia Act of 1903, also known as the Dick Act, was United States federal legislation that reorganised the militia system. It divided the militia into the organised militia — to be called the National Guard of the several states — and the reserve (unorganised) militia of able-bodied male citizens.

Provisions

Named for its sponsor, Congressman and Ohio National Guard commander Charles W. F. Dick, the act required state guards to meet federal standards of organisation, armament and discipline modelled on the regular Army within five years, provided federal money for arms, uniforms and annual training camps, and mandated regular federal inspections and joint exercises. In return, the President gained clearer authority to call the Guard into federal service.

Background and legacy

The law responded to the poor mobilisation performance of volunteer forces in the Spanish-American War of 1898 and to decades of advocacy by the National Guard Association. It superseded the archaic Militia Act of 1792 and began the modern transformation of the Guard into an effective organised reserve, a process continued by amendments in 1908 and the National Defense Act of 1916.
