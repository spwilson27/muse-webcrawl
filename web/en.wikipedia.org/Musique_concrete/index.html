Musique concrète

Musique concrète (French for "concrete music") is a type of music composition that utilizes recorded sounds as raw material. Developed in Paris in the 1940s by Pierre Schaeffer, it treats sounds drawn from the environment, instruments, and the human voice as musical objects to be cut, spliced, looped, and transformed on tape.

Origins

Schaeffer, working at French radio (RTF), premiered his Cinq études de bruits (Five noise studies) in 1948, made from recordings of trains, spinning tops, and percussion. With Pierre Henry he composed Symphonie pour un homme seul (1949–50), and in 1951 the RTF established the Groupe de Recherche de Musique Concrète, later the GRM, with a purpose-built studio.

Techniques and theory

Composers manipulated recordings by changing playback speed, reversing tape, cutting and reordering fragments, and filtering. Schaeffer developed a theory of the objet sonore (sound object) and "reduced listening", in which sounds are heard for their intrinsic qualities rather than their sources — an approach he called acousmatic. The contrasting German approach, elektronische Musik, synthesized material electronically from sine tones.

Influence

Musique concrète fed directly into electronic music, sampling culture, plunderphonics, and sound design. Figures associated with the tradition include Pierre Henry, Luc Ferrari, Bernard Parmegiani, and François Bayle; Karlheinz Stockhausen worked in the Paris studio before establishing the Cologne studio for electronic music.
