Oceanic art

Oceanic art or Oceanian art comprises the creative works made by the native people of the Pacific Islands and Australia, including areas as far apart as Hawaii and Easter Island. Specifically it comprises the works of the two groups of people who settled the area, though these two groups have common ancestry: the Aboriginal Australians and the Polynesians and Melanesians from the various island clusters. The area is often broken down into four separate regions: Micronesia, Melanesia, Polynesia and Australia.

Overview

Oceanic art is characterized by a strong connection to spirituality, ancestors, and the natural world. Artistic traditions include elaborate wood carving, feather work, bark cloth (tapa), tattooing, and monumental stone sculpture. Many objects served ritual purposes, embodying ancestral spirits or deities, while others were prestige items exchanged in ceremonies.

Melanesia

Melanesian art is known for its dramatic carved spirit figures, masks, and ceremonial houses. The Asmat people of New Guinea are famous for their bis (mbis) poles, tall carved memorial structures. Sepik River carving traditions produced some of the most intricate wooden sculpture in the Pacific.

Polynesia

Polynesian art includes the monumental moai statues of Easter Island, Hawaiian feather cloaks ('ahu 'ula) made from thousands of bird feathers, Māori wood carving and jade (pounamu) ornaments, and finely worked bone and ivory pendants. Tattooing traditions, such as Samoan tatau and Māori moko, are highly developed art forms in their own right.

Micronesia

Micronesian art features woven textiles, shell valuables, stick charts used for navigation, and the immense stone architecture of Nan Madol in Pohnpei.

Australia

Aboriginal Australian art includes some of the world's oldest continuous artistic traditions, notably rock art dating back tens of thousands of years, bark paintings, and the dot painting movement that emerged in the Western Desert in the 1970s.

Influence

Oceanic art profoundly influenced Western modernism; artists such as Pablo Picasso, Henri Matisse, and the Surrealists collected and drew inspiration from Pacific sculpture and masks.
