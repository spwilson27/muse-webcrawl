Madonna (art)

In art, a Madonna (from Italian ma donna, "my lady") is a representation of the Virgin Mary, alone or with the Christ Child. Images of the Madonna have been central to Christian art from the early centuries of the Church through the Byzantine, medieval, and Renaissance periods, developing a rich iconography of poses, gestures, and symbols that conveyed theological ideas about Mary's role as mother of God (Theotokos), queen of heaven, and intercessor.

The Madonna and Child was among the most frequently painted subjects in European art.

Development

Early images in the Roman catacombs (3rd–4th centuries) show Mary with the Christ Child, while Byzantine icons codified the Theotokos types: the Hodegetria ("she who points the way"), gesturing toward Christ, and the tender Eleusa, in which mother and child press cheeks together. In the medieval West, Gothic sculpture and painting made the Madonna increasingly gracious and humanised. Italian Renaissance masters transformed the genre: Giotto gave figures weight and emotion, and Raphael's Madonnas — such as the Sistine Madonna (1512) — became ideals of serene beauty. Leonardo's Madonna of the Rocks and Michelangelo's marble Bruges Madonna are other landmarks.

Iconography and types

Recurring types include the Madonna and Child enthroned (Maestà), flanked by saints and angels; the Madonna of humility, seated low on a cushion; the Maria lactans, nursing the infant; the Madonna del Latte and Madonna of the Rosary; and the Black Madonna, dark-skinned images venerated at pilgrimage shrines such as Częstochowa and Montserrat. Mary is typically shown in a blue mantle over a red dress, the blue symbolising heaven and purity, often within a gold ground in earlier works. Symbols such as the lily, rose, enclosed garden (hortus conclusus), and crescent moon encode attributes from scripture and liturgy.

Later art

After the Reformation, Madonna imagery largely disappeared from Protestant art but flourished in Counter-Reformation Catholic painting by Rubens, Velázquez, and Murillo, whose Immaculate Conceptions show Mary ascending on clouds. Interest revived with the Pre-Raphaelites, and modern artists from Edvard Munch to Andy Warhol have reinterpreted the image — Warhol's silkscreens after Raphael and Munch's provocative Madonna (1894) among them.
