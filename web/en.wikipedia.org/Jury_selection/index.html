Jury selection

Jury selection is the process by which the members of a trial jury are chosen from a pool of prospective jurors (the venire). In the United States and other common-law systems, selection centres on the examination known as voir dire, in which the judge and attorneys question candidates about their backgrounds and possible biases.

Process

Prospective jurors are summoned from lists such as voter registrations and driving records. During voir dire, candidates may be removed in two ways: challenges for cause, where a specific reason suggests the juror cannot be impartial (allowed in unlimited number, subject to the judge's approval), and peremptory challenges, which each side may use a limited number of times without stating a reason.

Constitutional limits

Peremptory challenges may not be used to exclude jurors on the basis of race or sex. In Batson v. Kentucky (1986), the U.S. Supreme Court held that striking jurors because of race violates the Equal Protection Clause; J.E.B. v. Alabama (1994) extended the rule to sex. Some jurisdictions, including Arizona, have abolished peremptory challenges altogether.

Jury consultants

In high-stakes cases, parties may hire jury consultants, who use surveys, mock trials and research on demographics and attitudes to advise on selection strategy — a practice critics call "scientific jury selection".
