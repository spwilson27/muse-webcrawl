The Battle of Toulouse was fought on 9 June 721 between the forces of Odo the Great, Duke of Aquitaine, and an Umayyad Muslim army from al-Andalus commanded by the governor Al-Samh ibn Malik al-Khawlani, who had laid siege to the city of Toulouse. The Aquitanian relief army won a crushing victory, killing Al-Samh and breaking the siege—the first major check to Muslim expansion north of the Pyrenees.

Background

After conquering Visigothic Iberia, Umayyad forces crossed the Pyrenees and took Narbonne in 719, making it a base for raids into southern Gaul. In 721 Al-Samh advanced on Toulouse, the principal city of Aquitaine, and invested it with a large army equipped with siege engines. Odo, absent raising reinforcements—including, by some accounts, Frankish troops—returned as the city's defences neared collapse.

The battle

On 9 June Odo fell unexpectedly on the besiegers, reportedly attacking from several directions at once and catching the Umayyad army between his relief force and a sortie from the city. The Muslim army was routed with enormous losses in the medieval chronicles (figures such as 375,000 dead in the Liber pontificalis are clearly exaggerated). Al-Samh was mortally wounded and died during the retreat to Narbonne.

Aftermath

Toulouse preserved Aquitaine's independence for a generation and bought southern Gaul a decade's respite from major invasion, though raiding resumed and culminated in the campaigns defeated by Odo's sometime rival Charles Martel at Tours in 732. The two victories—Toulouse by Odo, Tours by Charles—are distinct events, eleven years apart, involving different Christian commanders, though they are sometimes conflated in popular memory.
