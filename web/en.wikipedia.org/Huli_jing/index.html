Huli jing (Chinese: 狐狸精; pinyin: húlí jīng) are fox spirits of Chinese mythology — magical foxes able to shapeshift, most famously into beautiful women. Depending on the tale they may be benevolent or mischievous, scholarly or seductive, and their depiction ranges from divine messengers to dangerous femme fatales.

Development

Fox cults and fox lore are attested from early Chinese history; the nine-tailed fox (jiuweihu) appears in the Classic of Mountains and Seas as an auspicious creature eaten to ward off poison. By the Tang dynasty, fox spirits were widely worshipped and feared, and literary tales treated the fox as the archetypal outsider spirit. In later vernacular fiction the huli jing became associated with seduction: the fox spirit Daji, said to have possessed the consort of King Zhou, is blamed in the Ming novel Investiture of the Gods for the fall of the Shang dynasty.

Characteristics

Foxes gain transformative powers with age and cultivation: after fifty or a hundred years a fox may take human form, and the oldest acquire nine tails and great spiritual power. In Pu Songling's Strange Tales from a Chinese Studio (1740), fox women are often sympathetic — witty, loyal and wronged — a humanising turn on the tradition. The term "huli jing" is still used colloquially in Chinese to describe a seductive woman.
