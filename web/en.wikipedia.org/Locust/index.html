Locust

Locusts are certain species of short-horned grasshoppers in the family Acrididae that have a swarming phase. These insects are normally solitary, but under suitable conditions they multiply rapidly, become gregarious and migratory, and form destructive swarms.

Phase change

When population density rises — for example after rains produce abundant vegetation in arid regions — locusts undergo behavioural, colour and body changes called phase polyphenism, shifting from the solitary to the gregarious phase, a transformation driven partly by serotonin. Gregarious bands of flightless nymphs (hoppers) march together, and adult swarms can contain billions of individuals covering hundreds of square kilometres.

Important species

The desert locust (Schistocerca gregaria) of Africa, the Middle East and Asia is the most destructive migratory pest in the world; the migratory locust, Australian plague locust and the extinct Rocky Mountain locust are other notable species.

Impact and control

A swarm can consume its own weight in vegetation daily per insect, devastating crops and pasture and threatening food security. Control relies on monitoring, early warning systems coordinated internationally, and targeted application of insecticides and biopesticides such as the fungus Metarhizium acridum. Locust plagues are mentioned in ancient texts including the Bible and have recurred throughout history, including major upsurges in East Africa in 2019–2021.
