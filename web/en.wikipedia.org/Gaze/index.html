Gaze

In critical theory, sociology, and psychoanalysis, the gaze (French: le regard) is the act of seeing in which the viewer projects their desire, power, or knowledge onto the subject being viewed. The term also refers, in everyday and psychological usage, to the direction in which a person is looking, studied as a channel of nonverbal communication.

Psychoanalysis and philosophy

Jacques Lacan developed the concept of the gaze in his seminars on psychoanalysis, notably in The Four Fundamental Concepts of Psychoanalysis (1964), where the gaze (le regard) figures as the unattainable object of desire in the scopic drive, emerging in the mirror stage and in his reading of Holbein's painting The Ambassadors. Jean-Paul Sartre had earlier analysed "the look" (le regard) in Being and Nothingness (1943) as the experience of being seen by another, which constitutes one's awareness of oneself as an object. Michel Foucault extended the idea to institutions in his accounts of the medical gaze and of panoptic surveillance in Discipline and Punish.

The male gaze

In feminist film theory, Laura Mulvey's 1975 essay "Visual Pleasure and Narrative Cinema" introduced the male gaze: the way mainstream cinema structures looking so that women are displayed as passive objects of heterosexual male desire, while men act and look. The concept has since been applied widely in media studies, art history and advertising criticism, and has prompted discussion of opposing or alternative gazes.

Gaze in psychology

In experimental psychology, gaze direction and eye contact are studied as social signals: humans are highly sensitive to where others are looking, and gaze cueing affects attention, joint attention in infants, and mind perception. Eye-tracking research measures fixations and saccades to infer attention and cognitive processing.
