Phalaris

Phalaris (Greek: Φάλαρις) was the tyrant of Akragas (modern Agrigento) in Sicily, ruling from approximately 570 to 554 BC. He became proverbial in antiquity for his exceptional cruelty, most famously for the brazen bull, a hollow bronze statue devised by the sculptor Perillos in which victims were roasted alive, their screams transformed by an acoustic device into the bellowing of the bull. According to legend, Perillos himself was the first victim of his invention.

Life and rule

Phalaris was originally entrusted with overseeing the construction of the temple of Zeus Atabyrius on the citadel of Akragas, and used the position, funds, and armed workforce to seize power. As tyrant he extended the city's dominion over neighbouring territory, conquered several Sicanian towns, and made Akragas one of the most powerful Greek cities in Sicily. His rule was also credited with public works and patronage; some traditions, including letters attributed (spuriously) to him, portray a more cultivated side, and he was said to have corresponded with the poet Stesichorus of Himera.

Overthrow and death

After some sixteen years, Phalaris was overthrown in a popular revolt led by Telemachus, an ancestor of Theron. Later tradition held that he was tortured to death, in some accounts inside his own brazen bull.

Legacy

The Letters of Phalaris, long read as genuine, were famously demonstrated to be forgeries by the classical scholar Richard Bentley in a 1699 dissertation that became a landmark of philological method. The brazen bull remained a byword for tyrannical cruelty throughout classical and later literature, cited by Pindar, Aristotle, Cicero, and Dante.
