Cuttlefish

Cuttlefish are marine molluscs of the order Sepiida, class Cephalopoda, which also includes squid, octopuses, and nautiluses. Despite their name, they are not fish. They possess a unique internal shell, the cuttlebone, which is gas-filled and used to control buoyancy. Cuttlefish have eight arms and two longer feeding tentacles, large W-shaped pupils, and highly developed eyes.

Colour change and camouflage

Cuttlefish are celebrated as masters of camouflage. Their skin contains millions of pigment cells called chromatophores, along with iridophores and leucophores, allowing them to change colour and pattern in a fraction of a second to match their surroundings, signal to other cuttlefish, or startle predators. They can also alter the texture of their skin with papillae. Remarkably, they accomplish this despite being colour-blind, apparently by detecting contrast and possibly light polarisation.

Intelligence and behaviour

Cuttlefish have one of the largest brain-to-body size ratios of any invertebrate and display learning, memory, and problem-solving abilities. They hunt shrimp, crabs, and small fish, striking with rapidly extended tentacles, and subdue prey with venom. When threatened they eject ink to confuse predators.

Life cycle and uses

Most cuttlefish live only one to two years. Males display vivid patterns during courtship and guard females after mating; females lay eggs and die soon after. Cuttlebones are sold as calcium supplements for caged birds, and cuttlefish are fished commercially for food across the Mediterranean and Asia. Their ink was historically the source of the pigment sepia.
