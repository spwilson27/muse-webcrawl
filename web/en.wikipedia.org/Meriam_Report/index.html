The Meriam Report, officially titled The Problem of Indian Administration (1928), was a landmark survey of the conditions of Native Americans in the United States. Commissioned by Secretary of the Interior Hubert Work and carried out by the Institute for Government Research (the forerunner of the Brookings Institution) under the technical direction of Lewis Meriam, the report documented the devastating effects of federal Indian policy, above all the allotment policy of the Dawes Act of 1887, and it laid the intellectual groundwork for the Indian Reorganization Act of 1934.

Findings

The survey staff spent seven months visiting reservations, agencies, and boarding schools. Among its findings:

  • Native Americans were the poorest population in the country, with incomes far below subsistence and widespread ill health, including trachoma and tuberculosis.
  • Allotment had stripped tribes of roughly two-thirds of their land base — about 90 million acres lost since 1887 — and left remaining holdings fractionated and unusable.
  • Government Indian boarding schools were overcrowded, underfunded, and unsanitary; children were underfed, overworked, and subjected to harsh discipline.
  • Health services were grossly inadequate, and the Indian Service was understaffed and poorly paid.

Recommendations and impact

The report recommended abolishing the allotment policy, improving health and education services, hiring qualified personnel (including Native Americans), providing day schools near children's homes instead of distant boarding schools, and involving Indians in planning their own affairs. Charles J. Rhoads and J. Henry Scattergood began implementing reforms under President Hoover, and under Franklin D. Roosevelt, Commissioner of Indian Affairs John Collier used the report's findings to press for the Indian Reorganization Act of 1934 (the "Indian New Deal"), which ended allotment, restored a measure of tribal self-government, and returned some lands to tribal ownership.
