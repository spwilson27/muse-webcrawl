Biodiversity hotspot

A biodiversity hotspot is a biogeographic region with significant levels of biodiversity that is threatened by human habitation. The concept was coined by British ecologist Norman Myers in 1988 and developed with Conservation International, which adopted hotspots as an institutional priority for directing conservation funding.

Criteria

To qualify as a hotspot, a region must meet two criteria: it must contain at least 1,500 endemic vascular plant species (more than 0.5% of the world's total), and it must have lost at least 70% of its original habitat. On this basis, 36 regions have been recognised as hotspots, including the Tropical Andes, Madagascar and the Indian Ocean Islands, the Mediterranean Basin, Sundaland, the Atlantic Forest of Brazil, the Cape Floristic Region of South Africa, Japan, and the California Floristic Province. Together these regions, occupying only about 2.5% of Earth's land surface, support more than half of the world's endemic plant species and a large share of endemic vertebrates.

Criticism

The hotspots approach has been criticised for under-representing other taxa, ecosystem services, and less species-rich but important regions such as intact wilderness areas, and for not fully accounting for conservation costs.
