Conventional wisdom

Conventional wisdom is the body of ideas or explanations generally accepted as true by the public or by experts in a field. Such ideas, although widely held, are not necessarily true, and may in some cases be resistant to revision even when confronted with contrary evidence.

Origin of the term

The economist John Kenneth Galbraith popularised the phrase in his 1958 book The Affluent Society, where he wrote that "the conventional wisdom" describes ideas that are esteemed for their acceptability and predictability rather than their accuracy, noting that it is not "the triumph of the conventional wisdom to be right, but to be acceptable."

Relationship to evidence

Conventional wisdom can lag behind scientific findings: beliefs once conventionally held, such as the fundamental role of stress alone in causing peptic ulcers, were later overturned by evidence. In other cases, conventional wisdom simply encapsulates accumulated practical experience. The phrase is often used rhetorically to introduce a challenge to accepted assumptions.
