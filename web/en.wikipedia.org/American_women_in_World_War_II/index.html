American women in World War II

American women in World War II participated variously in the war effort, at home and abroad, in numbers and roles unprecedented in United States history. More than 19 million women worked in paid jobs during the war, and some 350,000 served in uniform.

On the home front

With millions of men in the armed forces, the government and industry recruited women into war production through campaigns symbolised by "Rosie the Riveter". Women made up a third of the aircraft workforce, operated heavy machinery, worked in shipyards, and produced munitions, while others served as nurses, volunteers, air-raid wardens, and members of organisations such as the American Red Cross and the USO. Women's employment expanded opportunities for African American, Latina, and working-class women, although discrimination persisted.

In uniform

Women served in the Women's Army Corps (WAC), the Navy's WAVES, the Marine Corps Women's Reserve, the Coast Guard SPARS, the Army and Navy Nurse Corps, and as Women Airforce Service Pilots (WASP), who ferried aircraft and towed targets. More than 1,600 Army nurses received combat decorations, and dozens of servicewomen were killed or taken prisoner, including nurses captured in the Philippines.

Legacy

Although many women were pressed to leave industrial jobs when servicemen returned, the wartime experience permanently raised women's labour-force participation and laid groundwork for the later women's movement. The Women in Military Service for America Memorial at Arlington National Cemetery, dedicated in 1997, commemorates their service.
