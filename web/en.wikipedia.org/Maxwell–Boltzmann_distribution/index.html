Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution

In physics (in particular in statistical mechanics), the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution, or Maxwell(ian) distribution, is a particular probability distribution named after James Clerk Maxwell and Ludwig Boltzmann.

It was first defined and used for describing particle speeds in idealized gases, where the particles move freely inside a stationary container without interacting with one another, except for very brief collisions in which they exchange energy and momentum with each other or with their thermal environment. The term "particle" in this context refers to gaseous particles only (atoms or molecules), and the system of particles is assumed to have reached thermodynamic equilibrium. The energies of such particles follow what is known as Maxwell–Boltzmann statistics.

Distribution function

The probability density function for the speed v of a particle of mass m in a gas at temperature T is:
f(v) = (m / 2πkBT)3/2 · 4πv2 · exp(−mv2 / 2kBT),
where kB is the Boltzmann constant. The distribution yields the most probable speed √(2kBT/m), the mean speed √(8kBT/πm), and the root-mean-square speed √(3kBT/m).

History and applications

Maxwell derived the distribution in 1860 on heuristic mechanical grounds; Boltzmann (1872, 1877) gave it a rigorous foundation in kinetic theory and statistical mechanics. The distribution underpins the kinetic theory of gases, explaining macroscopic properties such as pressure, diffusion, and thermal conductivity, and it applies whenever quantum effects on particle motion are negligible — that is, for most gases at ordinary temperatures.
