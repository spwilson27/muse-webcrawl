Paraneoplastic syndrome

A paraneoplastic syndrome is a group of uncommon disorders that are triggered by an abnormal immune system response to a cancerous tumor (neoplasm). They are signs or symptoms that result from the presence of a malignant tumour elsewhere in the body but are not caused by the local presence of cancer cells, their mass effect, or their metastases. Instead, effects are mediated by hormones or cytokines secreted by the tumour, or by immune responses — particularly antibodies — directed against tumour antigens that cross-react with normal tissues.

Types

Paraneoplastic syndromes are classified by the system affected. Endocrine syndromes arise when tumours secrete hormones or hormone-like substances: examples include Cushing syndrome from ectopic ACTH production (often in small-cell lung cancer), the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH), and hypercalcemia caused by parathyroid hormone-related protein. Neurological syndromes include Lambert–Eaton myasthenic syndrome, paraneoplastic cerebellar degeneration, limbic encephalitis, and opsoclonus-myoclonus; these are associated with onconeural antibodies such as anti-Hu, anti-Yo, and anti-Ri. Skin (dermatologic) manifestations include acanthosis nigricans and dermatomyositis, and haematologic effects include Trousseau syndrome of migratory thrombophlebitis and pure red cell aplasia.

Diagnosis and treatment

Paraneoplastic symptoms may appear before the cancer is diagnosed, so their recognition can lead to earlier detection of an occult malignancy. Diagnosis involves clinical assessment, antibody testing, and imaging to find the underlying tumour. The most effective treatment is control of the cancer itself; immunosuppression (corticosteroids, intravenous immunoglobulin, plasma exchange) may help in autoimmune neurological cases, though responses are often limited once neuronal damage has occurred.
