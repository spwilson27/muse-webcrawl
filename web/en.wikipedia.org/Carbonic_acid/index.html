Carbonic acid

Carbonic acid is a chemical compound with the formula H2CO3. It forms when carbon dioxide dissolves in water, and it plays a central role in the chemistry of the oceans, rainwater, blood and carbonated drinks. In aqueous solution only a small fraction of dissolved CO2 is actually present as H2CO3, but the equilibrium between the two is fundamental to acid–base balance in nature and physiology.

[Image: Molecular structure of carbonic acid, showing a central carbon double-bonded to one oxygen and single-bonded to two hydroxyl groups]

Chemistry

Carbonic acid is a weak acid that ionises in two steps, first to bicarbonate (HCO3−), with a pKa of about 3.6 for the true acid (an apparent pKa of about 6.35 when dissolved CO2 is counted together), and then to carbonate (CO32−), with a pKa of about 10.3. The pure compound was long thought unstable at ambient conditions, but it has been isolated at low temperatures and can exist in the gas phase; in space, carbonic acid has been detected in interstellar ices.

Role in nature

Rainwater is naturally slightly acidic (pH ≈ 5.6) because of carbonic acid, and this acidity drives the slow chemical weathering of silicate and carbonate rocks, a key part of the long-term carbon cycle. In blood, the carbonic acid–bicarbonate buffer system, accelerated by the enzyme carbonic anhydrase, carries CO2 from tissues to the lungs and stabilises blood pH. Increasing atmospheric CO2 raises carbonic acid concentrations in the oceans, causing ocean acidification.
