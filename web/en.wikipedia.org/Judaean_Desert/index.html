Judaean Desert

The Judaean Desert (Hebrew: מִדְבַּר יְהוּדָה, Midbar Yehuda; Arabic: برية الخليل) is a desert region in the southern Levant, lying east of Jerusalem and descending to the Dead Sea. It stretches from the northeastern Negev, east of Beersheba, to east of Beit El, and is marked by terraces, escarpments, and deep wadis cut by seasonal streams. In the Bible it is also called Jeshimon. Despite its aridity, the desert lies only about 20 minutes' drive from Jerusalem, owing to the rain-shadow effect of the Judaean Mountains.

Geography and climate

The desert occupies the eastern slopes of the Judaean Hills, dropping from around 1,000 metres above sea level to the Dead Sea shore, the lowest land on Earth at more than 400 metres below sea level. Rainfall is low and decreases eastward, from about 400 mm in the west to under 100 mm near the Dead Sea. The landscape is cut by canyons such as Wadi Qelt, Nahal Darga, and the Kidron Valley, some containing springs that support oases and monasteries.

History

The Judaean Desert has served as a refuge throughout history: David is said to have fled here from Saul, and the Hasmoneans and Herod the Great built fortresses including Masada, Herodium, and Hyrcania. During the Bar Kokhba revolt (132–136 CE), Jewish rebels hid in caves such as those of Nahal Hever, where the "Cave of Letters" archive was found. The caves near Qumran yielded the Dead Sea Scrolls, discovered from 1946–47, associated with the Essene community that lived nearby. The desert also became a centre of early Christian monasticism; monasteries such as Mar Saba (founded 483 CE) and St George's in Wadi Qelt still function today. Bedouin tribes have grazed flocks in the region for centuries.

Nature

Reserves such as Ein Gedi protect ibex, hyrax, leopards (now rare), and desert birds. Flash floods in the wadis remain a hazard to hikers.
