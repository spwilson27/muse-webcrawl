Werewolf

A werewolf (from Old English werwulf, "man-wolf") is a mythological human with the ability to shapeshift into a wolf, either purposely or after being placed under a curse or affliction, often a bite or scratch from another werewolf. The transformation is frequently associated with the appearance of the full moon. Belief in werewolves — lycanthropy — is found in folklore worldwide.

Folklore and history

Ancient sources such as Herodotus and Petronius describe men turning into wolves; Norse sagas tell of wolf-warriors, and the Greek myth of Lycaon, transformed by Zeus, gives the condition its name. In early modern Europe, werewolf panics paralleled the witch trials: accused "werewolves" such as Peter Stumpp of Bedburg (executed 1589) and the French cases investigated by Henri Boguet were prosecuted across France, Germany and Livonia. In France, the Beast of Gévaudan terrorized a province in the 1760s.

Characteristics

In folklore, werewolves could be recognized by signs such as eyebrows meeting at the bridge of the nose, curved fingernails, or hair growing inside the skin. Silver bullets, wolfsbane and exorcism were traditional remedies. Modern fiction — from The Wolf Man (1941) to contemporary fantasy — standardised the ideas of lunar transformation and silver as the creature's fatal weakness.
