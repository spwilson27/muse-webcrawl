Tonicity

Tonicity
ClassesHypertonic, isotonic, hypotonic
Governing processOsmosis across a semipermeable membrane
Depends onConcentration of non-penetrating solutes
ReferenceIntracellular fluid of the cell in question

Tonicity is a measure of the effective osmotic pressure gradient between two solutions separated by a semipermeable membrane. It describes the relative concentration of solutes that cannot cross the membrane, and therefore determines the direction and magnitude of water flow by osmosis and whether a cell placed in the solution will swell, shrink or stay the same size. Tonicity is always expressed relative to a particular cell or solution; unlike osmolarity, it takes account only of non-penetrating solutes.

The three states

A hypertonic solution has a higher concentration of non-penetrating solutes than the cell interior, so water leaves the cell, which shrinks (crenation in red blood cells; plasmolysis in plant cells). A hypotonic solution has a lower concentration, so water enters the cell, which swells and may burst (haemolysis in red blood cells). An isotonic solution has the same effective solute concentration, so there is no net water movement and cell volume is stable. Normal saline (0.9% sodium chloride) is approximately isotonic with blood plasma, which is why it is used for intravenous fluid replacement.

Tonicity versus osmolarity

Osmolarity counts all dissolved particles per litre, including solutes such as urea that freely cross cell membranes. Such penetrating solutes equalise across the membrane and cannot sustain water movement, so a solution can be iso-osmotic yet functionally hypotonic. Tonicity therefore predicts cell behaviour, while osmolarity alone does not.
