Sentence (music)


Structure of a classical musical sentence: presentation plus continuation.

In music theory, a sentence is a type of musical theme or phrase structure common in the Classical style. The term was developed in the writings of Arnold Schoenberg and has been extensively refined by William Caplin in his theory of formal functions. A sentence is contrasted with a period: whereas a period is organised by antecedent and consequent phrases, a sentence is organised by a presentation phrase followed by a continuation phrase.

Structure

In the prototypical eight-bar sentence, the presentation phrase (bars 1–4) consists of a basic idea (typically two bars) and its repetition, which may be exact, varied, or transposed in a statement–response pattern, ending on a weak cadence or half cadence. The continuation phrase (bars 5–8) then develops the idea through fragmentation — breaking it into shorter units — combined with increased harmonic activity, sequence, and an acceleration of harmonic rhythm, driving toward a perfect authentic cadence that closes the theme.

Relation to the period

Like the period, the sentence typically forms a complete, self-sufficient theme of eight bars, but its internal logic is developmental rather than balanced: the continuation destabilises the material presented at the start. Schoenberg used the German term Satz and emphasised the "liquidation" of characteristic features of the motif in the concluding portion. Sentences are a hallmark of the themes of Haydn, Mozart and especially Beethoven, whose opening of the Piano Sonata Op. 2 No. 1 is a frequently cited example.

Variants

Sentences may be expanded or compressed, with repeated continuations, evaded cadences, or "continuation ⇒ cadential" subdivisions in longer forms. Caplin further distinguishes sentence types by the model of repetition in the presentation (tonal or sequential) and acknowledges hybrid themes combining features of sentence and period.
