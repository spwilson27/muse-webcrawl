Air Force One is the official air traffic control call sign for any United States Air Force aircraft carrying the president of the United States. The name is most closely associated with the two Boeing VC-25A aircraft — highly modified Boeing 747-200Bs, tail numbers 28000 and 29000 — that serve as the primary presidential transports.

History

Presidential air travel began in 1943 with Franklin D. Roosevelt's flight aboard the Dixie Clipper flying boat. Dedicated aircraft followed, including the VC-54C Sacred Cow, the Independence, and Columbine II and III. The call sign "Air Force One" was adopted in 1953 after a confusion incident between Eastern Air Lines flight 8610 and Air Force flight 8610 carrying President Eisenhower. The Boeing 707-based VC-137s entered service under President Kennedy, with the famous livery designed by Raymond Loewy. The current VC-25As entered service in 1990 under George H. W. Bush.

The VC-25A

The VC-25A has three decks, about 370 m² of floor space, a presidential suite, offices, a medical facility, communications equipment, and capacity for about 70 passengers plus crew. It can be refuelled in flight and is hardened against electromagnetic pulse. Replacement VC-25B aircraft based on the Boeing 747-8 are under conversion.

In culture

Air Force One is a symbol of the American presidency and has featured in numerous films, including the 1997 action film Air Force One. Whenever the president is aboard a Marine Corps helicopter, it uses the call sign Marine One.
