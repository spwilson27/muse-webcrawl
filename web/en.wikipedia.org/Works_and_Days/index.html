Works and Days

Works and Days (Ancient Greek: Ἔργα καὶ Ἡμέραι, Erga kai Hemerai) is a didactic poem of some 800 lines written by the ancient Greek poet Hesiod around 700 BC. It is addressed to Hesiod's brother Perses and combines moral exhortation, practical farming instruction, and myth.

Content

The poem is framed by a real dispute: Hesiod accuses his brother Perses of bribing judges to cheat him of his inheritance. From this grievance Hesiod unfolds a vision of a world governed by Zeus's justice, in which honest work — not violence or fraud — is humanity's lot and salvation.

The poem retells the myths of Prometheus and Pandora (here told fully for the first time: Pandora opens her jar, releasing all evils into the world, leaving only Hope) and the Five Ages of Man — Golden, Silver, Bronze, Heroic and the present degenerate Iron Age. Later sections give a farmer's calendar keyed to the stars (notably the Pleiades), advice on seafaring, social conduct, and a catalogue of lucky and unlucky days of the month.

Significance

Alongside Hesiod's Theogony, Works and Days is one of the earliest surviving works of Greek literature and a key source for Greek mythology, early Greek agrarian life, and the concept of dikē (justice). Its myth of the ages influenced later classical and European literature, and its agricultural lore parallels Near Eastern wisdom traditions.
