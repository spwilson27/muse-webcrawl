Christmas pudding

Christmas pudding is a type of steamed pudding traditionally served as part of Christmas dinner in Britain and other countries influenced by British tradition. Made with suet, dried fruits, breadcrumbs, flour, eggs and spices, it is dark, dense and heavily fruited, and is usually aged for weeks or months before being steamed again and served on Christmas Day.

History

The pudding descends from medieval dishes such as plum pottage and frumenty. By the Victorian era it had taken its modern form as a spherical, cloth-boiled or basin-steamed pudding, a change popularised by Eliza Acton's 1845 recipe, the first to call the dish "Christmas pudding". In the 1920s the Empire Christmas Pudding campaign promoted a recipe using ingredients drawn from across the British Empire.

Traditions

By tradition the pudding is made on "Stir-up Sunday", the last Sunday before Advent, when each family member stirs the mixture and makes a wish. A silver coin was often hidden in the pudding for luck. Before serving, the pudding may be doused in brandy and set alight, then carried flaming to the table and eaten with brandy butter, custard or cream, often garnished with a sprig of holly.
