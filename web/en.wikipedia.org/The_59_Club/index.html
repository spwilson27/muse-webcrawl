The 59 Club

The 59 Club, also written as Fifty Nine Club, is a British motorcycle club founded in Hackney Wick, London, in 1959. Originally a church-run youth club for motorcyclists associated with the Eton Mission, it became the most famous gathering point of the "rocker" or café racer subculture of 1960s Britain, and it survives today as one of the best-known motorcycle clubs in the world.

History

The club was founded under the leadership of Canon Lionel Evans of the Eton Mission and grew rapidly with the involvement of the Reverend Fred ("Father Bill") Shergold, a motorcycling Church of England priest who took over its leadership in the early 1960s. Unlike many youth clubs of the time, the 59 Club welcomed the leather-jacketed young riders — "rockers" — who centred their social lives on transport cafés such as the Ace Cafe on London's North Circular Road, and who raced modified British motorcycles ("café racers") between cafés while setting records to jukebox songs.

Membership cards of the club became prized symbols of the era, and the club attracted famous visitors, members, and extensive press coverage. Sections sprang up internationally. In later decades the club refounded itself as a classic-motorcycling organisation, maintaining ties to the Ace Cafe revival and to charitable work, and Father Bill remained its president until his death in 2009.

Legacy

  • The 59 Club badge — a circled "59" — remains an emblem of British café racer culture.
  • The club helped define the rocker style: British bikes (Triumph, Norton, BSA), leather jackets, and ton-up runs.
  • It maintains a historic link with the Ace Cafe London, reopened in the 1990s as a hub of the revival scene.
