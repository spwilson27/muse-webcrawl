Zhang Qian (Chinese: 張騫; died c. 114 BC) was a Chinese diplomat and explorer of the Han dynasty whose journeys to Central Asia in the 2nd century BC opened the routes later known as the Silk Road and brought China its first reliable knowledge of the "Western Regions".

First mission (138–126 BC)

Emperor Wu sent Zhang Qian west in 138 BC to seek an alliance with the Yuezhi against the Xiongnu confederation, which had driven the Yuezhi from their homeland. Captured by the Xiongnu almost immediately, Zhang was held for about ten years, married, and eventually escaped, continuing west through Dayuan (Ferghana) and Kangju to the Yuezhi in Bactria. The Yuezhi, now settled and prosperous, declined the alliance, but Zhang gathered intelligence on Bactria, Sogdiana, Ferghana, and even reports of India and Parthia. On the return journey he was captured again and detained for another year before reaching Chang'an in 126 BC with his Xiongnu wife and his aide Ganfu — the only survivors of an original party of over one hundred.

Second mission and legacy

In 119 BC Zhang led a large embassy to the Wusun, carrying thousands of cattle, sheep, and gifts of gold and silk; its sub-missions contacted states across Central Asia. His reports, preserved in Sima Qian's Records of the Grand Historian, introduced grapes, alfalfa, and the famed "heavenly horses" of Ferghana to China and paved the way for Han expansion into the Tarim Basin. Zhang Qian is celebrated in China as a pioneer of East–West exchange, and his raft voyage is a common motif in Silk Road art.
