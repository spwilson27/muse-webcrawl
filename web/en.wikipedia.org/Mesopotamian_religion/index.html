Ancient Mesopotamian religion

Ancient Mesopotamian religion refers to the religious beliefs and practices of the civilisations of ancient Mesopotamia — Sumerians, Akkadians, Babylonians and Assyrians — from roughly the 4th millennium BC until the decline of the cuneiform tradition in the first centuries AD. It was polytheistic, with a pantheon of thousands of deities, and it profoundly influenced neighbouring religions.

The pantheon

Chief deities included Anu, god of the sky; Enlil, god of wind and chief executive of the gods; Enki (Ea), god of wisdom and fresh water; Inanna (Ishtar), goddess of love and war; Utu (Shamash), the sun god and god of justice; and Nanna (Sin), the moon god. In the first millennium BC Marduk rose to supremacy in Babylon, and Ashur in Assyria. Each city had a patron deity whose temple — often a stepped ziggurat — dominated it.

Mythology

Major surviving works include the Enūma Eliš, the Babylonian creation epic; the Epic of Gilgamesh, with its flood narrative; the myth of Adapa; and the Descent of Inanna into the underworld. Mesopotamian cosmology pictured a flat earth beneath a domed heaven, with the dead descending to a gloomy underworld (Kur) regardless of their earthly conduct.

Practice

Religion centred on temple cults maintained by priesthoods: daily offerings of food and drink to the god's statue, great festivals such as the Babylonian New Year (Akitu), and divination — by extispicy, astrology and dreams — to learn the gods' will. Humans were believed to have been created to serve the gods and relieve them of labour.
