Allergic rhinitis

Other namesHay fever (seasonal form)
SpecialtyAllergy, immunology, otorhinolaryngology
SymptomsSneezing, nasal congestion, itchy nose and eyes, runny nose
CausesAllergens such as pollen, dust mites, mould, animal dander
TreatmentNasal corticosteroids, antihistamines, allergen immunotherapy, avoidance

Allergic rhinitis, of which the seasonal type is called hay fever, is inflammation of the inside of the nose caused by an allergen, such as pollen, dust, mould or animal dander. Symptoms include sneezing, a blocked or runny nose, itching, coughing, watery eyes, and fatigue. It is an allergic reaction of the nasal mucosa mediated by immunoglobulin E (IgE).

Allergic rhinitis is one of the most common chronic conditions worldwide, affecting an estimated 10–30% of the global population. Although usually not life-threatening, it significantly impairs quality of life, sleep, school and work performance, and is frequently associated with asthma and sinusitis.

Classification

Allergic rhinitis was traditionally classified as seasonal (occurring during pollen seasons) or perennial (occurring year-round, usually from indoor allergens such as house dust mites and pets). Current guidelines classify it by duration as intermittent or persistent, and by severity as mild or moderate-to-severe, based on the impact on sleep and daily activities.

Mechanism

On first exposure, the immune system of a susceptible person produces IgE antibodies against a normally harmless substance. On re-exposure, the allergen cross-links IgE on mast cells in the nasal mucosa, causing the release of histamine and other mediators, which produce the immediate symptoms; a late-phase reaction involving eosinophils sustains inflammation and congestion. Genetic predisposition (atopy) and environmental factors both contribute to developing the condition.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is usually based on the history and examination; skin-prick testing or blood tests for specific IgE can identify the responsible allergens. Management includes avoiding triggers, saline irrigation, and medication. Intranasal corticosteroid sprays are the most effective single therapy; oral and intranasal antihistamines help with itching and sneezing. Allergen immunotherapy (allergy shots or sublingual tablets) can modify the disease over a course of several years and is recommended when symptoms are inadequately controlled by medication and avoidance.
