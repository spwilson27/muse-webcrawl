Breonna Taylor

Breonna Taylor (June 5, 1993 – March 13, 2020) was a 26-year-old African-American emergency medical technician from Louisville, Kentucky, who was fatally shot in her apartment by plainclothes officers of the Louisville Metro Police Department (LMPD) during the execution of a search warrant in the early hours of March 13, 2020. Her death, together with the murders of George Floyd and Ahmaud Arbery, sparked widespread protests across the United States in 2020 against police brutality and systemic racism, and became a rallying point for the Black Lives Matter movement.

The shooting

On March 13, 2020, LMPD officers Jonathan Mattingly, Brett Hankison, and Myles Cosgrove executed a search warrant at Taylor's apartment as part of an investigation into her ex-boyfriend, who was suspected of drug dealing; no drugs were found in the home. Police said they knocked and announced themselves, though that account was disputed. Taylor's boyfriend, Kenneth Walker, a licensed gun owner who believed intruders were breaking in, fired one shot that struck Sergeant Mattingly in the leg. Officers returned fire with 32 shots, six of which struck Taylor. She received no medical attention for more than 20 minutes.

Legal proceedings

No officer was charged directly with Taylor's death. In September 2020, a grand jury indicted Hankison on three counts of wanton endangerment for firing shots that entered a neighboring apartment; he was acquitted in March 2022. In August 2022, the U.S. Department of Justice charged four current and former officers with federal civil rights offenses, alleging that the warrant affidavit contained knowingly false statements. Hankison was convicted in federal court in November 2024 of violating Taylor's civil rights. The city of Louisville settled a wrongful-death lawsuit with Taylor's family for $12 million and agreed to a package of police reforms, including a ban on no-knock warrants, which Louisville enacted as "Breonna's Law."

Legacy

Taylor's name became central to the protests of 2020, often framed by the slogan "Say her name." Kentucky later passed a partial statewide ban on no-knock warrants, and her case drove national debate over warrant practices, qualified immunity, and police accountability.
