Medicine in the American Civil War

[Image: A Civil War field hospital and ambulance corps]

The state of medicine in the American Civil War (1861–1865) was very primitive by modern standards. Roughly two-thirds of the approximately 620,000–750,000 soldier deaths of the war were caused by disease rather than combat wounds — pneumonia, typhoid, dysentery, malaria and measles ravaged both armies — yet the conflict forced rapid organisational innovations that laid the foundations of modern military and emergency medicine.

Organisation

The Union Army's medical department was transformed under Surgeon General William A. Hammond (from 1862), while Jonathan Letterman, medical director of the Army of the Potomac, created the modern system of battlefield evacuation: organised ambulance corps, forward aid stations, division field hospitals and triage, later adopted wholesale by the U.S. Army and known as the Letterman Plan. Large pavilion general hospitals, such as Chimborazo Hospital in Richmond (Confederate) and Satterlee Hospital in Philadelphia, treated hundreds of thousands of patients. Clara Barton and other volunteers supplied relief work through the U.S. Sanitary Commission.

Treatment

Germ theory was not yet accepted; surgeons operated with unsterilised instruments and believed pus was a healthy sign ("laudable pus"). Anaesthesia, however, was widely used — chloroform and ether in tens of thousands of operations. Amputation was the most common major surgery for shattered limbs; about three in four operations were amputations, and speed was prized, earning surgeons the misleading nickname "sawbones". Quinine was used against malaria, and opium and morphine liberally against pain and dysentery — contributing to post-war addiction ("soldier's disease").

Legacy

The war's medical records were compiled in the monumental Medical and Surgical History of the War of the Rebellion, and the Army Medical Museum (now the National Museum of Health and Medicine) was founded in 1862. Civil War experience in triage, evacuation, hospital design and nursing professionalised American medicine.
