Catholic emancipation or Catholic relief was a process in the kingdoms of Great Britain and Ireland, and later the United Kingdom, in the late 18th and early 19th centuries that removed many of the restrictions on Roman Catholics imposed by the Act of Uniformity, the Test Acts, and the penal laws. Requirements to abjure (renounce) the temporal and spiritual authority of the pope and to reject transubstantiation placed major burdens on Catholics and were the source of widespread grievance, particularly in Ireland, where Catholics formed the large majority of the population.

Background

After the Protestant Reformation and the conflicts of the 17th century, English and Irish law excluded Catholics from voting (in Ireland, from 1728), sitting in Parliament, holding public office, bearing arms, and — under the Irish penal laws — restricted their rights to own land, inherit, and educate their children.

Relief acts

Partial relief began with the Papists Act 1778 and further Catholic Relief Acts in 1782, 1791, and 1793; the 1793 Irish act restored the vote to Catholic freeholders. Emancipation was expected to accompany the Act of Union 1800, but George III's opposition — he believed consenting would violate his coronation oath — blocked it, causing Pitt the Younger's resignation.

The campaign and the 1829 Act

In the 1820s Daniel O'Connell founded the Catholic Association, funded by the "Catholic rent," which mobilized mass peaceful pressure. O'Connell's election for County Clare in 1828 — though as a Catholic he could not take his seat — forced the issue. Fearing unrest in Ireland, the Duke of Wellington's government, with Robert Peel managing the bill in the Commons, passed the Roman Catholic Relief Act 1829, which allowed Catholics to sit in Parliament and hold nearly all public offices. As a concession to opponents, the Irish county franchise was simultaneously raised, disenfranchising many forty-shilling freeholders.

Aftermath

Emancipation transformed Irish and British politics, though some restrictions lingered (Catholics remained barred from the throne, a restriction that persists for the monarch). It established a model of mass constitutional agitation that O'Connell next applied to the campaign for repeal of the Union.
