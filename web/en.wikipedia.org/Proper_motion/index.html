Proper motion

Proper motion is the astrometric measure of the observed changes in the apparent places of stars or other celestial objects in the sky, as seen from the centre of mass of the Solar System, compared to the abstract background of the more distant stars. It is the angular change per year in a star's position, usually expressed in arcseconds or milliarcseconds per year, and excludes apparent motions caused by parallax or Earth's rotation.

Introduction

The proper motion is the component of a star's actual space velocity projected onto the plane of the sky. Combined with the star's distance (from parallax) and its radial velocity (from the Doppler shift), it gives the star's full three-dimensional velocity. For 99% of stars, proper motions are tiny — most are less than 0.1 arcsecond per year — because the stars are so distant.

History

Proper motion was discovered in 1718 by Edmond Halley, who noticed that the stars Sirius, Arcturus, and Aldebaran had moved significantly from the positions recorded by Hipparchus some 1,850 years earlier. Modern space missions such as Hipparcos and Gaia have measured proper motions for hundreds of millions to billions of stars with extraordinary precision.

High proper motion stars

Stars with large proper motions tend to be relatively close to the Sun. Barnard's Star, about six light-years away, has the largest known proper motion at about 10.3 arcseconds per year. Other high proper motion stars include Kapteyn's Star, Groombridge 1830, and Lacaille 9352. Over tens of thousands of years, proper motions visibly alter the shapes of constellations.
