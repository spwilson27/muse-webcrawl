United States Environmental Protection Agency

The Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) is an independent agency of the United States government tasked with environmental protection matters. President Richard Nixon proposed the establishment of EPA on July 9, 1970; it began operation on December 2, 1970, after Nixon signed an executive order. The agency is led by its administrator, who is appointed by the president and approved by the Senate.

Mission

The EPA's stated mission is to protect human health and the environment. It writes and enforces regulations based on laws passed by Congress, including the Clean Air Act, the Clean Water Act, the Safe Drinking Water Act, and the Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation, and Liability Act (CERCLA, commonly known as Superfund), which addresses the cleanup of contaminated sites. The agency conducts environmental assessment, research, and education, and it delegates permitting and enforcement responsibilities to states and federally recognized tribes.

History

The EPA arose from the environmental movement of the 1960s and the first Earth Day in April 1970. William Ruckelshaus was confirmed as the agency's first administrator. During its first years the EPA banned the pesticide DDT, set the first national air quality standards, and oversaw the phase-out of leaded gasoline. The agency operates ten regional offices, each responsible for programs within several states, along with national program offices and research laboratories.
