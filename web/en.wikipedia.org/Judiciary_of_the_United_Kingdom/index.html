Judiciary of the United Kingdom

Legal systems: England and Wales; Scotland; Northern Ireland
Highest court: Supreme Court of the United Kingdom
Head of judiciary (England and Wales): Lady Chief Justice
Key reform: Constitutional Reform Act 2005

The judiciary of the United Kingdom comprises the judges and courts of the country's three distinct legal jurisdictions: England and Wales, Scotland, and Northern Ireland. The United Kingdom has no single unified legal system, a legacy of the terms on which Scotland retained its own law under the Acts of Union 1707. Since 2009 the Supreme Court of the United Kingdom has stood at the apex of all three systems for most purposes.

Structure

In England and Wales, the senior courts consist of the Court of Appeal, the High Court of Justice (with its King's Bench, Chancery, and Family Divisions), and the Crown Court, sitting above county courts and magistrates' courts. Scotland's superior courts are the Court of Session in civil matters and the High Court of Justiciary in criminal matters; uniquely, Scottish criminal appeals do not go to the UK Supreme Court. Northern Ireland has its own Court of Appeal and High Court. A UK-wide system of tribunals handles administrative and specialist disputes.

Independence and the 2005 reforms

Judicial independence, protected since the Act of Settlement 1701 by security of tenure, was strengthened by the Constitutional Reform Act 2005. The Act created the Supreme Court, which took over the appellate work of the House of Lords in October 2009, established the Judicial Appointments Commission to select judges on merit, and made the Lord Chief Justice — from 2023 the Lady Chief Justice — head of the judiciary of England and Wales, a role previously held by the Lord Chancellor.

Judges and magistrates

The professional judiciary ranges from the Justices of the Supreme Court down through Lords and Lady Justices of Appeal, High Court judges, circuit judges, and district judges. Much of the criminal work at first instance is done by some 12,000 lay magistrates, unpaid volunteers advised on law by legal advisers. Judges in the UK traditionally do not wear wigs in most civil and family cases following reforms, though wigs and robes persist in criminal courts.
