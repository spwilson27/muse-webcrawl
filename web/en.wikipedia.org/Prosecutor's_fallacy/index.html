Prosecutor's fallacy

The prosecutor's fallacy is a fallacy of statistical reasoning involving the probability of the evidence given innocence being mistaken for the probability of innocence given the evidence — a confusion of conditional probabilities. It is named for its occurrence in criminal trials, where a prosecutor may argue that because the probability of a DNA match occurring by chance is tiny, the probability that the defendant is innocent must likewise be tiny. This ignores the base rate: in a large population, even a one-in-a-million match probability can leave many potential matches, so the probability of innocence depends on how the suspect was identified and on the prior odds.

Explanation

Formally, the fallacy confuses P(E | innocent) with P(innocent | E). By Bayes' theorem, the two quantities differ by the prior probability of guilt and the probability of the evidence if guilty. If a DNA profile matches one person in a million and the suspect was found by trawling a database of ten million people, roughly ten other people would also match; presenting only the one-in-a-million figure dramatically overstates the case. The corresponding defence-side error, sometimes called the defence attorney's fallacy, dismisses match evidence entirely by pointing to those other potential matches while ignoring the additional evidence that narrows the field.

Cases

The fallacy has been discussed in connection with several trials. In the case of Sally Clark, a British solicitor convicted in 1999 of murdering her two infant sons, an expert testified that the probability of two cot deaths in one family was 1 in 73 million — obtained by squaring a base rate as if the deaths were independent — and the figure was reportedly presented as the probability of her innocence. Her conviction was overturned on a second appeal in 2003. Courts and statisticians have since urged greater care in presenting forensic probabilities, and professional bodies such as the Royal Statistical Society have issued guidance on statistical evidence in court.
