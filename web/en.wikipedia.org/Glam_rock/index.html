Glam rock is a style of rock music that developed in the United Kingdom in the early 1970s. Performed by musicians who wore outrageous costumes, makeup and hairstyles — platform boots, glitter and satin — glam combined deliberately theatrical, often androgynous stage personas with direct, anthemic rock and roll drawing on 1950s rock, bubblegum pop and hard rock. It was one of the dominant British pop styles of 1971–1975 and exerted a lasting influence on punk, new wave and pop fashion.

History

Glam emerged as Marc Bolan transformed his folk duo Tyrannosaurus Rex into the electric T. Rex, scoring hits from 1970 such as "Ride a White Swan" and "Get It On." David Bowie brought glam its greatest artistic ambition and international profile with his Ziggy Stardust persona and the album The Rise and Fall of Ziggy Stardust and the Spiders from Mars (1972). A chart-friendly wing — often called glitter rock — included Slade, Sweet, Gary Glitter, Wizzard and Mud, whose stomping, chant-along singles dominated British charts, while Roxy Music, Mott the Hoople and Sparks offered artier variants. In the United States, glam found expression in Alice Cooper, Kiss, Lou Reed's Transformer and the New York Dolls, though it never matched its British commercial dominance.

Style and themes

Visually, glam rejected the denim informality of late-1960s rock in favour of science-fiction imagery, retro Hollywood glamour, satin and sequins. Musically it favoured catchy riffs, singalong choruses and a heavy backbeat; lyrically it dealt in youthful hedonism, nostalgia, celebrity and sexual ambiguity. Its playful gender-bending — epitomized by Bowie's declaration of bisexuality in 1972 — challenged the norms of British popular culture.

Decline and legacy

By 1975 glam had faded as its stars moved on — Bowie retired Ziggy Stardust in 1973 and reinvented himself, Bolan's career declined before his death in 1977 — and disco, pub rock and then punk took over. Glam's DIY theatricality and disdain for "authenticity" directly influenced punk rock and the new romantics; its sound and image resurged in 1980s glam metal, in 1990s Britpop and Suede, and in 21st-century acts. Historians credit glam with redefining what a pop star could look and sound like.
