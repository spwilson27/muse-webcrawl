Venule

A venule is a very small vein in the microcirculation that allows blood to return from the capillary beds to drain into the venous system via increasingly larger veins. Post-capillary venules are the smallest of the veins, typically 8–30 micrometres in diameter, and are composed of endothelium and a thin outer layer. Multiple venules join to form a vein.

Structure

Venules are classified into post-capillary, collecting and muscular venules. Post-capillary venules have a wall of endothelium surrounded by a basement membrane and sparse pericytes; their walls are highly permeable, and they are the principal site at which white blood cells leave the bloodstream during inflammation, a process called extravasation or diapedesis. As venules enlarge, they acquire a layer of smooth muscle (media) and a fibrous outer adventitia, gradually taking on the three-layered structure of larger veins.

Function

Venules collect deoxygenated blood from the capillaries and return it toward the heart. Because pressure in the venular system is low, venules and veins together act as capacitance vessels, holding a large proportion of the blood volume. Inflammatory mediators such as histamine increase venular permeability, contributing to the redness, swelling and plasma leakage of inflamed tissue.
