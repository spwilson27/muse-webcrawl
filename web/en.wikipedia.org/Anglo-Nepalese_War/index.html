Anglo-Nepalese War

The Anglo-Nepalese War (1 November 1814 – 4 March 1816), also known as the Gurkha War, was fought between the Gorkha Kingdom (present-day Nepal) and the British East India Company. It ended with the Treaty of Sugauli, under which Nepal ceded roughly a third of its territory but retained its independence.

Causes

The conflict arose from border disputes along the Terai plain and Nepalese expansion westward into Kumaon and Garhwal and eastward toward Sikkim, which collided with the Company's frontier. Incursions into the disputed districts of Butwal and Sheoraj, and the killing of British police in 1814, led Governor-General Lord Hastings to declare war.

Campaigns

British forces attacked along a broad front with four divisions. Initial operations went badly: General Gillespie was killed assaulting the hill fort of Kalanga (Nalapani), where Balbhadra Kunwar's small garrison held out for a month. In 1815 General David Ochterlony conducted a skilful campaign in the western hills, outmanoeuvring Amar Singh Thapa and forcing the surrender of Almora. A second advance toward Kathmandu in early 1816, including victory at Makwanpur, compelled Nepal to negotiate.

Treaty of Sugauli

The treaty, ratified on 4 March 1816, ceded Sikkim's conquered lands, Kumaon, Garhwal, and much of the Terai to the Company, fixed the Mechi and Mahakali rivers as Nepal's eastern and western boundaries, and established a British residency in Kathmandu.

Legacy

British respect for Gurkha fighting qualities, won in this war, led to the recruitment of Gurkha regiments into the Indian Army — a tradition continuing today in the British and Indian armies. Nepal was never colonised, and the war fixed the borders that largely define the modern state.
