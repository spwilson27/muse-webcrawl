Klondike Gold Rush — the Carcross crossing

The Klondike Gold Rush (1897–1899) brought an estimated 100,000 prospectors toward the goldfields around Dawson City in Canada's Yukon. The community now known as Carcross, Yukon — then called Caribou Crossing — stood at one of the rush's great chokepoints: the narrows between Bennett Lake and Tagish Lake, where stampeders who had crossed the White Pass or Chilkoot trails built boats to float down the lakes and the Yukon River to Dawson.

Caribou Crossing during the rush

The settlement's Tlingit name, Nadashaa Héeni, refers to the caribou that crossed the narrows between the lakes, and the Tagish and Inland Tlingit people had used the site for centuries. In the spring of 1898 thousands of stampeders camped around Bennett Lake, building a tent city of boats; after breakup they raced downriver. Caribou Crossing became a station on the route, offering supplies, a North-West Mounted Police post, and later a station of the White Pass and Yukon Route railway, completed in 1900.

Transport links

The completion of the White Pass and Yukon Route between Skagway, Alaska, and Whitehorse made Carcross a transfer point where passengers moved between trains and sternwheelers. The railway's golden spike was driven at Carcross on 29 July 1900, an event presided over by railroad officials — the last spike ceremony of the line.

After the rush

Gold-rush traffic collapsed after 1899, but Carcross endured as a Tagish First Nation community and transportation hub. Its name was shortened from Caribou Crossing to Carcross in 1904 to avoid confusion with Cariboo, British Columbia. Today the gold-rush era is remembered in the historic station, the desert-like Carcross Desert nearby, and heritage sternwheeler remains on the shore of Bennett Lake.
