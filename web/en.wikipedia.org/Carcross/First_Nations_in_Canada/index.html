First Nations in Canada

First Nations (French: Premières Nations) is a term used to describe Indigenous peoples in Canada who are not Métis or Inuit. There are over 630 First Nations communities in Canada, representing more than 50 distinct nations and languages. First Nations peoples have inhabited what is now Canada for at least 12,000 years, developing diverse societies, trade networks and systems of governance long before European contact.

Terminology and legal status

The term "First Nation" came into general use in the 1970s and 1980s, replacing older terms such as "Indian", which survives only in legal and constitutional contexts. Section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982 recognises and affirms the existing Aboriginal and treaty rights of the "aboriginal peoples of Canada". The Indian Act, first passed in 1876, remains the principal statute defining federal jurisdiction, band governance and reserve lands, though many First Nations have negotiated modern treaties and self-government agreements — in Yukon, for example, most First Nations, including the Carcross/Tagish First Nation, signed final agreements under the Umbrella Final Agreement.

History

Contact with Europeans brought trade, new alliances and devastating epidemics, followed by colonisation, the numbered treaties in the west, and policies of assimilation including the residential school system, whose legacy is addressed by the Truth and Reconciliation Commission (2008–2015). First Nations political organisation includes the Assembly of First Nations, founded in 1982.

Culture

First Nations cultures are highly diverse, organised into broad cultural areas — among them the Northwest Coast, Plateau, Plains, Eastern Woodlands and Subarctic — each with characteristic languages, art forms, housing, foodways and ceremonies. Languages belong to families such as Algonquian, Athabaskan, Iroquoian, Salishan, Siouan and Wakashan, many now endangered and the focus of revitalisation efforts.
