Yukon

Yukon is the smallest and westernmost of Canada's three northern territories, bordered by Alaska to the west, the Northwest Territories to the east and British Columbia to the south. Its capital and largest city is Whitehorse, where most of the territory's roughly 44,000 residents live. The territory takes its name from the Yukon River, whose name derives from a Gwich'in phrase meaning "great river".

Geography

Yukon covers about 482,000 km² of mountains, plateaus and boreal forest. Canada's highest peak, Mount Logan (5,959 m), rises in the St. Elias Mountains within Kluane National Park, a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Major rivers include the Yukon, Pelly, Stewart and Liard. The territory has a subarctic climate with long, cold winters and short, warm summers; at Snag in 1947 the temperature fell to −63.0 °C, the lowest reliably recorded in North America.

History

Indigenous peoples have lived in Yukon for millennia; the region contains some of the earliest evidence of human habitation in the Americas. Fur traders arrived in the 19th century, but the territory's history was transformed by the Klondike Gold Rush of 1896–1899, which brought tens of thousands of stampeders and led to the creation of Yukon as a separate territory in 1898, with Dawson City as capital. Whitehorse became capital in 1953. In 1993 the Umbrella Final Agreement established the framework under which most Yukon First Nations have settled land claims and self-government.

Economy

Mining — historically gold, silver, lead and zinc, and today also copper — remains central, alongside tourism drawn by wilderness, the aurora borealis and gold-rush heritage sites such as Dawson City and the White Pass and Yukon Route railway through communities like Carcross.
