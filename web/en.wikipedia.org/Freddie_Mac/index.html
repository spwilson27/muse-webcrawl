Freddie Mac — the Federal Home Loan Mortgage Corporation (FHLMC) — is a government-sponsored enterprise (GSE) of the United States, created by Congress in 1970 to expand the secondary market for residential mortgages. It buys mortgages from lenders, packages them into mortgage-backed securities (MBS), and sells them to investors with a guarantee of timely payment of principal and interest.

Role in housing finance

Together with its larger sibling Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac supports liquidity in the US mortgage market, enabling lenders to make long-term, fixed-rate loans such as the 30-year mortgage. The nickname "Freddie Mac" derives from a phonetic reading of its initials, paralleling "Fannie Mae" for the Federal National Mortgage Association. Freddie Mac was privatised and listed on the New York Stock Exchange in 1989, combining private ownership with its federal charter and an implicit government guarantee.

Financial crisis and conservatorship

During the US housing bubble, Freddie Mac accumulated a vast portfolio of mortgages and securities exposed to subprime and Alt-A loans. When house prices collapsed in 2007–08, losses threatened its solvency. In September 2008 the Federal Housing Finance Agency placed Freddie Mac (and Fannie Mae) into federal conservatorship, and the US Treasury injected capital under a senior preferred stock agreement — ultimately about $71 billion for Freddie Mac. The enterprise has since returned to profitability and paid dividends to the Treasury exceeding its draws, but it remains in conservatorship, with its long-term status a recurring question in US housing policy.
