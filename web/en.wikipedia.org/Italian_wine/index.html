Italian wine

Vineyards in Chianti, Tuscany

Italian wine is produced in every region of Italy and is among the world's oldest and largest wine traditions. Italy is one of the world's largest wine producers and exporters, with viticulture stretching back to the Etruscans and the Greek colonists of Magna Graecia, who called southern Italy Oenotria — "land of vines."

Regions and grapes

Italy has 20 wine regions corresponding to its administrative regions. Famous areas include Piedmont (Barolo and Barbaresco from Nebbiolo), Tuscany (Chianti, Brunello di Montalcino and the "Super Tuscan" blends from Sangiovese), the Veneto (Valpolicella, Amarone and Prosecco), Friuli and Trentino-Alto Adige for whites, and Sicily and Puglia in the south. The country grows hundreds of native grape varieties — more than any other — including Nebbiolo, Sangiovese, Barbera, Montepulciano, Pinot Grigio, Glera, Vermentino and Aglianico.

Classification

Italian wine law organizes quality designations into a hierarchy: Vino (table wine), IGP (Indicazione Geografica Protetta), DOC (Denominazione di Origine Controllata), and the top tier DOCG (Denominazione di Origine Controllata e Garantita), created in 1963 with the first DOC awarded in 1966 and the first DOCGs (Barolo, Barbaresco, Brunello, Chianti, Vino Nobile) in 1980.

History

Winemaking flourished under Rome, declined in the early Middle Ages but survived through monasteries, and revived with Renaissance and modern-era estates. After quality crises in the 20th century, reforms and international success of Italian styles reshaped the industry; Italy regularly contends with France and Spain for the position of largest producer by volume.
