Control theory

Control theory is a field of control engineering and applied mathematics that deals with the control of dynamical systems in engineered processes and machines. The objective is to develop a model or algorithm governing the application of system inputs to drive the system to a desired state, while minimising any delay, overshoot or steady-state error and ensuring a level of control stability. A device that does this, usually by measuring the output through feedback, is a controller.

Feedback

The central idea is feedback: the system's output is measured by a sensor, compared against a reference (set point) to form an error signal, and the controller adjusts the input to reduce that error. A familiar example is a thermostat. The most widely used controller in industry is the PID controller, which combines proportional, integral and derivative action on the error:

History

Feedback devices are ancient — float regulators were used in Hellenistic water clocks — but systematic theory began with James Clerk Maxwell's 1868 stability analysis of the centrifugal governor, "On Governors". In the twentieth century, work at Bell Laboratories produced Harold Black's feedback amplifier, Harry Nyquist's stability criterion (1932) and Hendrik Bode's frequency-response methods. Post-war "classical control" was joined from about 1960 by "modern control": state-space methods, Rudolf Kálmán's optimal filtering and LQR theory, and Lyapunov stability analysis.

Core concepts

Systems are modelled by differential equations, transfer functions in the Laplace domain, or state-space representations. Analysis asks whether a system is stable, controllable (steerable to any state) and observable (its state inferable from outputs). Robust control (H∞ and μ-synthesis) designs controllers that perform despite model uncertainty, while optimal control minimises a cost functional. Nonlinear, adaptive, stochastic and digital control extend the theory beyond the linear time-invariant core.

Applications

Control theory underlies autopilots and spacecraft guidance, industrial process control, robotics, power-system stabilisation, anti-lock brakes and hard-disk servo mechanisms — virtually every technology in which a machine must regulate its own behaviour continuously.
