Daimyo

Daimyo (Japanese: 大名) were powerful Japanese magnates and feudal lords who, from the 10th century to the mid-19th century, ruled most of Japan from their vast hereditary land holdings. They were subordinate to the shogun and served as the core of the samurai ruling class. The term literally means "great name".

History

The daimyo class evolved from the shugo, the military governors of provinces appointed during the Kamakura and Muromachi periods. During the Sengoku period (1467–1600), powerful regional warlords — the sengoku daimyo — fought for control of the country until Japan was reunified under Oda Nobunaga, Toyotomi Hideyoshi, and Tokugawa Ieyasu. Under the Tokugawa shogunate of the Edo period, daimyo were classified by their relationship to the Tokugawa house (shinpan, fudai, and tozama) and were required to maintain domains (han) assessed at a minimum of 10,000 koku of rice. The sankin-kōtai system obliged them to alternate residence between their domains and Edo, keeping them under shogunal control.

Abolition

The daimyo system was abolished during the Meiji Restoration. In 1869 the daimyo returned their land registers to the emperor, and in 1871 the domains were abolished and replaced with prefectures. Former daimyo were later incorporated into the new kazoku peerage.
