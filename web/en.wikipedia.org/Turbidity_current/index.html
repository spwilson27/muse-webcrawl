Turbidity current

A turbidity current is a type of sediment gravity flow characterised by the rapid, downslope movement of a dense fluid — a mixture of sediment and water — along the seafloor or the bottom of a lake. Because the sediment-laden mixture is denser than the fluid through which it flows, it moves under gravity, often triggered by earthquakes, slope failure, or storm activity. Turbidity currents are among the most important processes transporting sediment from continental shelves to the deep ocean floor, and they are a principal agent in carving submarine canyons and building deep-sea fans.

Characteristics

Turbidity currents flow downslope at speeds ranging from a few centimetres per second to more than 20 metres per second in the largest events. As a current decelerates, it deposits sediment in a graded sequence — coarsest material first, finest last — producing a characteristic deposit known as a turbidite. The idealised succession of sedimentary structures in a single turbidite bed was described by Arnold Bouma in 1962 and is known as the Bouma sequence. Repeated turbidity currents at the same site stack these beds into thick turbidite successions, which form important hydrocarbon reservoir rocks.

Triggering mechanisms

Triggers include earthquakes that shake loose unstable sediment on slopes, the collapse of river-mouth deltas during floods, storm-wave loading of the seabed, and the failure of gas-hydrate-bearing sediments. The 1929 Grand Banks earthquake off Newfoundland triggered a turbidity current that sequentially broke transatlantic telegraph cables, allowing its speed and path to be timed — one of the earliest direct observations of the phenomenon.
