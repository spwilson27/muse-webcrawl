Minoan civilization

The Minoan civilization was a Bronze Age culture centred on the island of Crete in the Aegean Sea, flourishing from about 3100 to about 1100 BCE. It is considered the earliest advanced civilisation in Europe, noted for its monumental palaces, vibrant frescoes, sophisticated plumbing and administration, and extensive maritime trade. Its name was coined by British archaeologist Sir Arthur Evans after the mythic king Minos.

[Image: The restored north entrance of the palace of Knossos with its bull fresco]

Palaces and society

Great palace complexes at Knossos, Phaistos, Malia and Zakros served as administrative, religious and storage centres, with magazines of giant pithoi jars, paved courts and elaborate drainage. Minoan art depicts bull-leaping rituals, processions, marine life and apparently powerful female figures, leading Evans to suggest a prominent priestess or goddess cult. The Minoans wrote first in Cretan hieroglyphs and then in Linear A, which remains undeciphered; Linear B, used after the Mycenaean takeover, was deciphered as early Greek by Michael Ventris in 1952.

Decline

Around 1450 BCE most Cretan palaces were destroyed, possibly in connection with the aftermath of the Thera (Santorini) eruption some decades earlier and Mycenaean expansion from mainland Greece. Knossos survived longest under Mycenaean administration before its final destruction around 1375–1350 BCE.
