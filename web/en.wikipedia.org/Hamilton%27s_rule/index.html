Hamilton's rule

FieldEvolutionary biology
Formulated byW. D. Hamilton (1964)
FormularB > C

Hamilton's rule is a central principle of kin selection theory in evolutionary biology, formulated by W. D. Hamilton in 1964. It states that an altruistic behaviour will be favoured by natural selection when the benefit to the recipient, multiplied by the coefficient of relatedness between actor and recipient, exceeds the cost to the actor: rB > C.

Explanation

Here r is the genetic relatedness (for example, 0.5 between full siblings), B is the reproductive benefit gained by the recipient, and C is the reproductive cost borne by the individual performing the act. The rule explains how genes promoting altruism toward relatives can spread, because relatives share genes by common descent — a concept summarised by inclusive fitness.

Applications

Hamilton's rule is used to explain alarm calling, cooperative breeding, worker sterility in eusocial insects, and other social behaviours. Empirical tests measure the costs, benefits, and relatedness in natural populations; the rule's generality and the proper measurement of its terms remain subjects of scientific debate.
