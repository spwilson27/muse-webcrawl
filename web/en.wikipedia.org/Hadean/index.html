The Hadean is the first and oldest of the four geologic eons of Earth's history, starting with the planet's formation about 4.54 billion years ago and ending, as defined by the International Commission on Stratigraphy, 4 billion years ago. The name, coined by geologist Preston Cloud in 1972, derives from Hades, the Greek god of the underworld, a reference to the hellish conditions then prevailing on Earth: frequent collisions with other bodies, extensive volcanism from a high geothermal gradient, and a largely molten surface.

Geology

Because few rocks survive from this period — most were recycled by plate tectonics and erosion — knowledge of the Hadean comes chiefly from detrital zircon grains, some dated to about 4.4 billion years ago in the Jack Hills of Western Australia, and from lunar samples and meteorites. Evidence from oxygen isotopes in these zircons suggests liquid water oceans may have existed surprisingly early, within a few hundred million years of Earth's formation.

The Moon-forming impact

Early in the Hadean, a Mars-sized body called Theia is hypothesized to have collided with the proto-Earth, ejecting debris that coalesced into the Moon. The impact melted much of the Earth, producing a magma ocean that gradually cooled and differentiated into core, mantle and crust.

Late Heavy Bombardment

Toward the end of the Hadean, around 4.1–3.8 billion years ago, the inner Solar System experienced a spike in impacts known as the Late Heavy Bombardment, recorded in the cratering of the Moon. Despite these conditions, evidence from carbon isotopes in Hadean zircons has been interpreted as a possible hint of very early life, though this remains controversial.
