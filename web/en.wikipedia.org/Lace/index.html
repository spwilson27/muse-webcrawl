Lace

Lace is a delicate fabric made of yarn or thread in an open web-like pattern, made by hand or machine. Originally made of linen, silk, gold or silver thread, lace is now most often produced with cotton thread, though manufactured and synthetic fibres are also used. It is used to embellish clothing, furnishings, table linen and bridal wear.

Types and techniques

The two principal handmade techniques are needle lace, built up with a single needle and thread (as in Venetian gros point), and bobbin lace, in which many threads wound on bobbins are plaited and twisted over a pinned pattern (as in Honiton, Bruges and Chantilly lace). Other forms include crocheted lace, knitted lace, tatting, macramé and chemical (guipure) lace. Machine-made lace, developed with the Heathcoat bobbin net machine in the early 19th century, transformed production in centres such as Nottingham and Calais.

History

Lacemaking emerged in late medieval Italy and Flanders and became a major luxury industry in the 16th–18th centuries, employing large numbers of women and girls, often in harsh conditions. Sumptuary laws repeatedly tried to restrict its import and wear. Production declined with mechanisation and changing fashion, but fine handmade lace survives as a heritage craft and an element of haute couture.
