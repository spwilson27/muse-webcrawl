Declaration of Helsinki

Declaration of Helsinki
Full titleWMA Declaration of Helsinki – Ethical Principles for Medical Research Involving Human Participants
AdoptedJune 1964, Helsinki, Finland
Issued byWorld Medical Association (WMA)
Revisions1975, 1983, 1989, 1996, 2000, 2008, 2013, 2024
PurposeEthical principles for medical research involving human subjects

The Declaration of Helsinki is a set of ethical principles, developed by the World Medical Association (WMA), governing medical research involving human participants, including research on identifiable human material and data. First adopted in 1964 in Helsinki, Finland, it is widely regarded as the cornerstone document of human research ethics, succeeding the Nuremberg Code of 1947 while softening its absolute requirement of consent so that research could also proceed under defined safeguards.

Principles

The Declaration states that the health and rights of the individual research subject must take precedence over the interests of science and society. Its core requirements include: research must rest on adequate scientific knowledge and, where relevant, prior animal experimentation; protocols must be reviewed by an independent research ethics committee; participation requires free, informed consent, ordinarily in writing; particular protection is owed to vulnerable groups; risks must be continuously assessed against expected benefits; and results, including negative ones, must be published. Physicians are told to protect the life, health, dignity, integrity, and privacy of research subjects.

Revisions

The Declaration has been amended repeatedly — in Tokyo (1975), Venice (1983), Hong Kong (1989), South Africa (1996), Edinburgh (2000, with notes of clarification in 2002 and 2004), Seoul (2008), Fortaleza (2013), and Chicago (2024). Debated points have included the ethics of placebo controls when proven treatment exists, post-trial access to beneficial interventions, standards of care in trials in low-income countries, and the registration of trials in public databases before recruitment.

Influence

Although not legally binding, the Declaration has been incorporated into national laws, regulations, and professional codes worldwide, and is cited by regulators such as the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (historically) and in the good-clinical-practice standards used across the pharmaceutical industry. Together with the Belmont Report and the CIOMS guidelines, it forms the framework of modern research ethics.
