Igbo (Igbo: Ásụ̀sụ̀ Ìgbò) is the principal native language of the Igbo people of southeastern Nigeria. It belongs to the Igboid branch of the Volta–Niger languages within the Niger–Congo family and is one of Nigeria's three largest languages, alongside Hausa and Yoruba. It has numerous dialects—some not mutually intelligible—a standard literary form, Igbo izugbe, having been developed in the 1970s on the basis of central dialects.

Features

Igbo is a tonal language with two distinctive tones, high and low, used both lexically and grammatically. It has an open syllable structure, vowel harmony involving eight vowels, and an agglutinative verbal system with extensive use of extensional suffixes. Noun classes are largely vestigial compared with other Niger–Congo languages.

Writing

Modern Igbo is written in the Önywū alphabet, a Latin-based orthography adopted officially in 1961, which uses dotted letters (à, ị, ụ, ọ) for sub-dotted vowels and ch, gh, gw, kp, kw, nw, ny, sh digraphs. Before European contact, the Nsibidi ideographic system was used by secret societies in the Cross River region. Igbo literature includes early works by Pita Nwana, whose Omenuko (1933) was the first Igbo-language novel, and the language is widely used in Nigerian media, education, and worship.

Status

Igbo is a national language of Nigeria taught in schools in the southeast, though commentators have warned of pressure from English and Nigerian Pidgin on intergenerational transmission, especially in urban areas and the diaspora.
