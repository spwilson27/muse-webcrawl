John Maynard Keynes, 1st Baron Keynes (5 June 1883 – 21 April 1946) was an English economist whose ideas fundamentally changed the theory and practice of macroeconomics and the economic policies of governments. Originally trained in mathematics, he built on and greatly refined earlier work on the causes of business cycles and came to be widely considered the founder of modern macroeconomics.

Early life and career

Keynes was born in Cambridge, the son of the economist John Neville Keynes and the social reformer Florence Ada Keynes. Educated at Eton and King's College, Cambridge, where he studied mathematics and joined the Apostles society and the Bloomsbury Group circle, he entered the India Office in 1906 before returning to Cambridge as a lecturer. His Treatise on Probability (1921) grew out of his fellowship dissertation. During the First World War he worked at the Treasury, and represented it at the Paris Peace Conference of 1919.

The Economic Consequences of the Peace

Resigning in protest at the reparations imposed on Germany, Keynes published The Economic Consequences of the Peace (1919), a brilliant polemic arguing that the Treaty of Versailles would impoverish Germany and destabilise Europe. The book made him internationally famous.

The General Theory

In The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money (1936), written amid the Great Depression, Keynes argued that aggregate demand — total spending in the economy — determines the overall level of economic activity and employment, and that inadequate demand can lead to prolonged involuntary unemployment. He advocated government intervention, including deficit spending during downturns, and challenged the classical view that free markets automatically provide full employment. His concepts of the marginal propensity to consume, the multiplier, and liquidity preference became central to subsequent economics.

Second World War and Bretton Woods

During the Second World War Keynes advised the British government on war finance (How to Pay for the War, 1940) and played a leading role in the negotiations that produced the Bretton Woods system in 1944, helping design the International Monetary Fund and the World Bank, though his proposal for an international clearing union with a currency called "bancor" was not adopted. He was raised to the peerage as Baron Keynes in 1942.

Death and legacy

Keynes died of a heart attack at his home at Tilton, near Firle in Sussex, on 21 April 1946. Keynesian economics dominated post-war policy in much of the West until the stagflation of the 1970s; his ideas experienced a revival during the financial crisis of 2007–08. Keynes is widely ranked among the most influential economists of the twentieth century.
