Trade bloc

A trade bloc is a type of intergovernmental agreement, often part of a regional intergovernmental organization, where barriers to trade (tariffs and others) are reduced or eliminated among the participating states. Trade blocs can be stand-alone agreements between several states (such as the North American Free Trade Agreement/USMCA) or part of a regional organization (such as the European Union).

Stages of integration

Economists, following Béla Balassa, commonly classify regional integration into stages of increasing depth:

  • Preferential trading area — reduced tariffs on certain goods between members
  • Free trade area — tariffs and quotas largely abolished among members, each keeping its own external tariffs (e.g., USMCA, CPTPP)
  • Customs union — a free trade area with a common external tariff (e.g., Mercosur, the EU customs union)
  • Common market — free movement of goods, services, capital, and labour (e.g., the European Single Market)
  • Economic and monetary union — a common market with harmonized economic policy and possibly a single currency (e.g., the Eurozone)

Notable trade blocs

Major trade blocs include the European Union, the United States–Mexico–Canada Agreement (USMCA), the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) and its free trade area, Mercosur in South America, the African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA), and the Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership (RCEP) in the Asia-Pacific.

Effects

Economists debate trade blocs' effects in terms of trade creation (replacing expensive domestic production with cheaper partner imports) versus trade diversion (shifting imports from efficient outsiders to less efficient members because of tariff preferences). Critics also note that blocs may complicate global multilateral liberalization under the World Trade Organization.
