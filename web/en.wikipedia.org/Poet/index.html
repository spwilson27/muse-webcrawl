A poet composes verse, traditionally regarded as among the oldest literary arts.

A poet is a person who studies and creates poetry. Poets may describe themselves as such or be described by others. Historically, poets occupied honoured positions as bards, scops, griots and court singers who preserved a culture's memory in verse; in many societies the poet was also seen as a prophet or visionary — the Latin vates and the Greek poiētēs ("maker") both carried such weight.

History

The earliest named author in history is a poet: Enheduanna, an Akkadian priestess of the 23rd century BCE whose hymns to Inanna survive. Epic poets such as Homer and Virgil, lyric poets such as Sappho, court poets of Tang China (Li Bai, Du Fu), Persian masters (Rumi, Hafez), and Sanskrit, Arabic and Japanese traditions all made the poet a central cultural figure. From Dante, Chaucer and Shakespeare through the Romantics (Wordsworth, Keats, Shelley) to modernists such as T. S. Eliot and contemporary figures, poets have repeatedly reshaped the language itself.

The poet's role

Poets work with metre, rhyme, image and compression to concentrate meaning and feeling. Their social roles have included laureate (an official national or royal poet, an office held in Britain since 1668 and in the United States as poet laureate consultant), laureates of cities, and unofficial voices of dissent and national identity. Very few poets earn a living from poetry alone; many teach, edit or work in other fields. Poetry prizes such as the Nobel Prize in Literature (awarded to poets including Wisława Szymborska, Seamus Heaney, Louise Glück and Bob Dylan) mark the art's continuing prestige.

Modern developments

Performance traditions — spoken word, slam poetry and rap — and social-media "Instapoetry" have created mass new audiences, while small presses and literary journals remain poetry's core institutions.
