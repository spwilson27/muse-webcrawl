Human skin color

Human skin color ranges from the darkest brown to the lightest hues. Differences in skin color among individuals is caused by variation in pigmentation, which is the result of genetics (inherited from one's biological parents), exposure to the sun, disorders, or some combination thereof. Differences across populations evolved via natural selection and/or sexual selection, because of social norms and differences in environment, and regulate the amount of ultraviolet radiation penetrating the skin, controlling important metabolic processes such as production of vitamin D and folate.

Main pigmentMelanin (eumelanin and pheomelanin)
Producing cellsMelanocytes

Measurement scalesVon Luschan scale; Fitzpatrick scale

Biology

Skin colour is determined chiefly by the amount and type of melanin produced by melanocytes and packaged into melanosomes. All human populations have similar numbers of melanocytes; differences arise from melanosome size, number and activity. Melanin absorbs ultraviolet radiation, protecting against sunburn, skin cancer and the breakdown of folate, while lighter skin at high latitudes permits sufficient UVB for vitamin D synthesis.

Evolution and distribution

Dark skin is thought to have evolved as early humans lost body hair in equatorial Africa. As populations migrated to regions of lower UV intensity, selection for dark pigmentation relaxed and lighter skin evolved independently in several regions. Consequently skin colour varies clinally with latitude and UV exposure rather than falling into discrete categories, and it correlates poorly with other traits.

Social and health aspects

Skin colour has been used as a basis for discrimination ("colorism") within and between societies. Medically, pigmentation influences risk of skin cancer and vitamin D deficiency, and pulse oximetry and other optical devices can perform differently across skin tones — an active area of research and design.
