Autonomous communities of Spain

The autonomous communities (Spanish: comunidades autónomas) are the first-level political and administrative divisions of Spain, created by the Spanish Constitution of 1978 to guarantee a degree of self-government to the nationalities and regions that compose the country.

Structure

There are seventeen autonomous communities, among them Andalusia, Catalonia, the Community of Madrid, the Valencian Community, Galicia, Castile and León, the Basque Country, and Castile-La Mancha, plus the autonomous cities of Ceuta and Melilla in North Africa. Each community has a Statute of Autonomy, its own parliament and government, and competencies that commonly include health, education, and culture; the Basque Country and Navarre also enjoy distinctive fiscal arrangements.

Historical development

The system emerged during the transition to democracy after the Franco era. The "historic nationalities" — Catalonia, the Basque Country, and Galicia — obtained autonomy first by a fast-track route, followed by the others; the process was largely complete by 1983. The resulting decentralized but not formally federal system is often called the Estado de las Autonomías.

Politics

The extent of devolution and regional nationalist movements, particularly in Catalonia and the Basque Country, remain central themes in Spanish politics.
