The United States Secretary of State is the head of the United States Department of State and the nation's chief diplomat, responsible for the conduct of U.S. foreign policy. The Secretary is the highest-ranking member of the Cabinet, a member of the National Security Council, and fourth in the presidential line of succession.

History

The office derives from the Secretary of Foreign Affairs created under the Articles of Confederation in 1781. On 27 July 1789, President George Washington signed legislation creating the Department of Foreign Affairs; the department was soon renamed the Department of State and given additional domestic duties. John Jay served as acting secretary until Thomas Jefferson, the first Secretary of State under the Constitution, took office in March 1790.

Early Secretaries of State — Jefferson, James Madison, James Monroe, John Quincy Adams, Martin Van Buren, and James Buchanan — went on to become president, making the office a traditional stepping stone to the presidency in the republic's first century.

Duties and responsibilities

  • Serves as the president's principal adviser on foreign policy and conducts negotiations with foreign governments
  • Directs the Department of State and the U.S. Foreign Service, including embassies and consulates worldwide
  • Countersigns and keeps custody of the Great Seal of the United States, which is affixed to certain official documents
  • Administers consular services, passport issuance, and visa policy
  • Receives formal notices in certain constitutional processes, such as a president's resignation (Richard Nixon's resignation letter was delivered to Secretary of State Henry Kissinger in 1974)

Notable secretaries

Influential holders of the office include John Marshall, William H. Seward (who negotiated the Alaska Purchase), John Hay, Charles Evans Hughes, George Marshall (author of the Marshall Plan and later Nobel Peace Prize laureate), Dean Acheson, John Foster Dulles, Henry Kissinger, George Shultz, Madeleine Albright (the first woman to hold the office, in 1997), Colin Powell (the first African American, in 2001), Condoleezza Rice, and Hillary Clinton.
