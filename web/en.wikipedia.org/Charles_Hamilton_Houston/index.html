Charles Hamilton Houston

Charles Hamilton Houston (September 3, 1895 – April 22, 1950) was an African-American lawyer, dean of Howard University Law School, and the first special counsel of the NAACP. Known as "the man who killed Jim Crow", he designed the legal strategy that culminated in the outlawing of school segregation in Brown v. Board of Education (1954), four years after his death.

Education and Howard

Born in Washington, D.C., Houston graduated from Amherst College and served as an officer in the segregated U.S. Army in the First World War, an experience that deepened his commitment to fighting racism. He earned his law degree from Harvard Law School, where he was the first Black editor of the Harvard Law Review, and studied in Spain after graduating. As vice-dean of Howard University Law School (1929–1935) he transformed it into a rigorous, fully accredited institution that trained a generation of civil rights lawyers, including Thurgood Marshall.

NAACP litigation

As the NAACP's chief litigator from 1935, Houston travelled the South by train, documenting discrimination and arguing cases challenging unequal teachers' pay and exclusion from state universities, as in Murray v. Pearson (1936) and Missouri ex rel. Gaines v. Canada (1938). His strategy was to attack segregation first in graduate and professional education, where the inequality with the "equal" facilities promised by Plessy v. Ferguson was most glaring.

Legacy

Poor health forced Houston to resign from the NAACP in 1938, but he continued to practise law in Washington, including labour cases for Black railway workers. He died of a heart attack in 1950. Harvard Law School's Charles Hamilton Houston Institute for Race and Justice is named for him.
