The James Bay Project (French: projet de la Baie-James) is a series of hydroelectric power stations on the La Grande River in northwestern Quebec, Canada, built by the state-owned utility Hydro-Québec. Announced in 1971 by Premier Robert Bourassa as the "project of the century," it is one of the largest hydroelectric complexes in the world and a defining symbol of Quebec's economic nationalism.

The Robert-Bourassa (formerly La Grande-2) generating station, the largest station of the James Bay Project

Phase I

The first phase, built between 1971 and 1985, involved damming the La Grande River and diverting the Eastmain and Opinaca rivers into it. It created huge reservoirs and three giant generating stations: La Grande-2 (later renamed for Robert Bourassa, 5,616 MW), La Grande-3 (2,417 MW) and La Grande-4 (2,779 MW). The Robert-Bourassa reservoir, covering about 2,835 square kilometres, is among the largest artificial lakes in Canada.

Opposition and agreements

The Cree and Inuit of northern Quebec, whose traditional lands were flooded, challenged the project in court. A landmark 1973 ruling briefly halted construction and led to the James Bay and Northern Quebec Agreement of 1975, the first modern land-claims treaty in Canada. The Cree and Inuit ceded certain claims in exchange for compensation, hunting and fishing rights, and measures of self-government.

Later phases

Subsequent phases added the La Grande-1, Laforge-1, Laforge-2 and Brisay stations on the La Grande system, and the Eastmain-1 and Eastmain-1-A/Sarcelle/Rupert diversion works. A proposed expansion known as the "Great Whale Project" on the Great Whale River was shelved in 1994 after sustained Cree opposition and the loss of electricity contracts with New York State.

Environmental impact

The project flooded more than 11,000 square kilometres of boreal forest and taiga. Reservoir creation released methylmercury into the food chain, affecting fish and the communities that depend on them, and altering caribou migration routes and river ecosystems. At the same time, the complex supplies a large share of Quebec's low-carbon electricity and exports power to the northeastern United States.
