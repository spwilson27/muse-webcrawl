Dropstone

Dropstones are isolated fragments of rock found within finer-grained sedimentary rocks of marine or lacustrine origin. They range in size from small pebbles to large boulders, and are distinguished by their out-of-place composition and by evidence that they fell vertically into soft sediment, deforming the layers beneath them while younger layers drape undisturbed over the top.

Origins

The most widely recognised origin is ice rafting: stones frozen into icebergs or seasonal lake ice are carried out over a water body and drop to the bottom when the ice melts. In this sense dropstones in ancient sediments are strong evidence of glacial conditions, and they occur in deposits associated with glaciations as old as the Huronian and in the "Snowball Earth" deposits of the Cryogenian.

Other mechanisms include rafting in the roots of floating trees, in kelp holdfasts, in the stomachs of animals (gastroliths), and volcanic bombs ejected into water.

Significance

Because fine laminated sediments record quiet-water deposition, the sudden arrival of a coarse clast requires a rafting agent. Dropstones are therefore used as palaeoclimatic indicators, and their occurrence in equatorial Neoproterozoic sequences was an important line of evidence for global glaciation.
