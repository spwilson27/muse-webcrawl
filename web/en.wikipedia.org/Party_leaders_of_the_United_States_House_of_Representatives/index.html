Party leaders of the United States House of Representatives, also known as floor leaders, are the majority leader and the minority leader — the two highest-ranking members of their respective party organizations in the House of Representatives, subordinate only to the Speaker in the majority party. Each leader is elected by the full membership of their party caucus (Democrats) or conference (Republicans).

History

The formal floor-leader positions emerged in the late 19th and early 20th centuries as party organization in the House became more structured. The minority leader position was first formally recognized around 1899, while the majority leader role developed as Speakers delegated floor management. Leaders became powerful figures in their own right after the 1910 revolt against Speaker Joseph Cannon redistributed power within the chamber.

Majority leader

The House majority leader is the second-highest-ranking member of the majority party and the party's chief floor strategist. Working with the Speaker, the majority leader schedules legislation, plans the party's legislative program, and directs floor debate for the majority. Notable majority leaders have included Tom DeLay, Steny Hoyer, Eric Cantor, Kevin McCarthy and Steve Scalise.

Minority leader

The House minority leader is the highest-ranking member of the minority party and serves as its principal spokesperson and strategist, leading opposition to the majority's agenda and positioning the party to regain the majority. Notable minority leaders include Gerald Ford, John Boehner, Nancy Pelosi, Kevin McCarthy and Hakeem Jeffries.

Whips

Each party also elects a whip — the majority whip and minority whip — who count votes, persuade members, and communicate the leadership's position to the rank and file. The whip organizations include chief deputy whips and regional whips. The term derives from the "whipper-in" of British fox hunting.

Role in the legislative process

Floor leaders manage recognition for debate, negotiate unanimous-consent agreements and scheduling with the other party, and serve as their party's public face on legislative matters. The minority leader is traditionally granted certain procedural rights, including offering a motion to recommit.
