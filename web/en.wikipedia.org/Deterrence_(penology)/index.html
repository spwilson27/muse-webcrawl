Deterrence (penology)

Deterrence in penology is the theory that criminal penalties reduce crime by discouraging would-be offenders through fear of punishment. It is one of the classical justifications for punishment, alongside retribution, incapacitation and rehabilitation, and was articulated by Cesare Beccaria and Jeremy Bentham in the 18th century.

Types of deterrence

General deterrence aims to discourage the public at large by making an example of those convicted. Specific (or individual) deterrence aims to discourage the particular offender from reoffending. A related concept, marginal deterrence, holds that penalties should be graded so that offenders are not pushed toward more serious crimes carrying no greater punishment.

Key conditions

Classical theory identifies three properties of punishment that determine deterrent effect: certainty (the likelihood of being caught and punished), celerity (swiftness) and severity. Modern empirical research — notably reviews by criminologist Daniel Nardo and others — generally finds that the certainty of punishment has a far more consistent deterrent effect than its severity, and that large increases in sentence length produce little additional deterrence.

Criticism

Critics argue that deterrence assumes rational calculation, whereas many crimes are impulsive or committed under the influence of drugs or desperation; that offenders often do not know the applicable penalties; and that the perceived risk of apprehension matters more than statutory punishments. The deterrent effect of capital punishment remains especially contested in the research literature.
