Protein degradation

Protein degradation (proteolysis within cells) is the controlled breakdown of proteins into smaller polypeptides or amino acids, carried out by enzymes called proteases. Cells continuously degrade and re-synthesise their proteins, allowing them to remove damaged or misfolded proteins, regulate the abundance of enzymes and signalling molecules, recycle amino acids, and control processes such as the cell cycle. The half-lives of cellular proteins range from minutes to many days.

Ubiquitin–proteasome system

In the principal pathway for selective protein degradation in the cytosol and nucleus, proteins are tagged with chains of the small protein ubiquitin by a cascade of E1, E2, and E3 enzymes. Polyubiquitinated proteins are recognised by the 26S proteasome, a large barrel-shaped protease complex that unfolds the target and cleaves it into short peptides, releasing ubiquitin for reuse. Irwin Rose, Aaron Ciechanover, and Avram Hershko received the 2004 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for discovering ubiquitin-mediated protein degradation.

Lysosomal degradation and autophagy

Proteins and organelles are also degraded in lysosomes. Through autophagy, portions of cytoplasm are enclosed in double-membrane autophagosomes that fuse with lysosomes, whose acid hydrolases break down the contents. Autophagy is especially important during starvation, stress, and the clearance of protein aggregates.

Regulation and disease

Degradation rates are controlled by signals such as the N-end rule, which relates a protein's half-life to its N-terminal residue, and by degrons exposed on damage or folding defects. Defective protein degradation contributes to neurodegenerative diseases characterised by protein aggregates, such as Alzheimer's and Parkinson's diseases. Proteasome inhibitors like bortezomib are used as anticancer drugs, and targeted protein degradation technologies — including PROTACs and molecular glues — deliberately hijack the ubiquitin system to destroy disease-causing proteins.
