Ancient Greek calendars

Ancient Greek calendars were a diverse family of lunisolar calendars used by the city-states of ancient Greece. Because each polis had its own calendar — with different month names, different starting points for the year, and different intercalation practices — there was never a single unified Greek calendar.

The Attic calendar

The best-documented example is the Attic calendar of Athens. It had twelve lunar months of 29 or 30 days: Hekatombaion, Metageitnion, Boedromion, Pyanepsion, Maimakterion, Poseideon, Gamelion, Anthesterion, Elaphebolion, Mounichion, Thargelion, and Skirophorion. The year began with Hekatombaion, around midsummer. To keep the lunar year aligned with the seasons, an extra month (a second Poseideon) was intercalated in some years.

Cycles

Greek astronomers developed cycles to regularise intercalation. The octaeteris was an eight-year cycle; the Metonic cycle, introduced by Meton of Athens in 432 BC, equated 19 solar years with 235 lunar months and is still used in computing the date of Easter.

Festivals and dating

Months began at the new moon, and days within the month were counted by the phases of the moon. Major festivals such as the Panathenaia and the Dionysia were fixed to calendar dates. Years were dated by eponymous magistrates — in Athens, the archon — rather than by a continuous era, though the four-year Olympiad cycle later provided a common chronological framework.
