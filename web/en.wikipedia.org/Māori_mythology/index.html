Māori mythology

Māori mythology and Māori traditions are the body of Polynesian narratives, cosmogony and beliefs held by the Māori people of New Zealand (Aotearoa). Passed down through oral tradition — whaikōrero (oratory), waiata (song), whakapapa (genealogical recitation) and carving — they explain the origins of the world, the gods (atua), humanity, and natural phenomena.

Māori mythology forms part of the wider Polynesian mythology, adapted to the new homeland after the settlement of Aotearoa by voyaging canoes (waka) from Hawaiki.

Cosmogony

In the best-known tradition, in the beginning was Te Kore (the void) and Te Pō (the night), from which emerged Ranginui (the Sky Father) and Papatūānuku (the Earth Mother), locked in a close embrace. Their children, including Tāne (god of forests), Tangaroa (god of the sea), Tūmatauenga (god of war and humanity), Rongo (god of cultivation) and Tāwhirimātea (god of weather), chafed in the darkness between them; Tāne finally forced his parents apart, bringing light and space into the world. Tāwhirimātea, who had opposed the separation, attacked his brothers with storms.

Māui and other figures

The demigod and trickster Māui is the hero of many of the most famous tales: he fished up the North Island (Te Ika-a-Māui, 'the fish of Māui') from the sea, slowed the sun to lengthen the days, obtained fire for humankind from the ancestress Mahuika, and died attempting to win immortality from Hine-nui-te-pō, goddess of death.

Tāne fashioned the first woman, Hineahuone, from earth and breathed life into her. Other important figures include Rongo-mā-Tāne, Haumia-tiketike (god of uncultivated food), and the voyager Kupe, credited in tradition with discovering Aotearoa.

Sources and survival

Traditions vary between iwi (tribes), each maintaining its own whakapapa and local versions. In the nineteenth century, Māori scholars and informants such as Wiremu Maihi Te Rangikāheke provided manuscripts that were published by Governor George Grey in works such as Polynesian Mythology (1855), preserving narratives that remain foundational, though shaped by their editor. Māori religion also features concepts such as tapu (sacred restriction), mana (spiritual prestige) and mauri (life force).
