Secondary predication

In linguistics, secondary predication is the phenomenon in which a clause contains, in addition to its main (primary) predicate, a further predicative element — the secondary predicate — that predicates a property of one of the arguments of the main verb without being the main verb itself.

Types

Two main types are distinguished. Depictive secondary predicates describe the state of a participant during the event: in "John ate the meat raw", raw describes the meat; in "She arrived drunk", drunk describes the subject. Resultative secondary predicates describe the state resulting from the event: in "They hammered the metal flat", flat describes the state the metal ends up in.

Analysis

Secondary predication has been a major topic in syntax and semantics because secondary predicates are adjunct-like in form but argument-like in interpretation, and their distribution varies across languages — English, for example, allows productive resultatives that many related languages do not.
