Open-pit mining, also known as opencast mining, is a surface mining technique that extracts minerals from the earth by removing them from an open-air pit. It is used when deposits of commercially useful ore or rocks are found near the surface. The mine is excavated as a series of horizontal layers called benches, which form a stepped, inverted cone in the ground.

Open-pit mining contrasts with underground mining, which requires tunnelling into the earth, and with strip mining, which removes long strips of surface material over relatively shallow deposits.

Method

Before mining begins, the deposit is delineated by drilling, and a pit design is prepared specifying bench height, bench width, and the overall slope angle of the pit walls. Vegetation and topsoil are cleared, and waste rock (overburden) above the ore body is removed. Each bench is typically 10–15 metres high. Ore and waste are drilled, blasted, loaded by excavators or wheel loaders into large haul trucks—some carrying more than 300 tonnes—and transported out of the pit along switchback ramps.

As mining progresses, the pit deepens and the ratio of waste to ore (the stripping ratio) usually increases. When the cost of removing waste exceeds the value of the remaining ore, the open pit reaches its economic limit, and mining may cease or continue underground beneath the pit.

Materials extracted

Open pits produce much of the world's copper, iron ore, gold, and phosphate, as well as construction aggregates, limestone, and some coal and diamonds. Famous examples include the Bingham Canyon Mine in Utah, the largest man-made excavation on Earth; the Chuquicamata copper mine in Chile; the Super Pit gold mine in Kalgoorlie, Australia; and the Mirny diamond mine in Siberia.

Waste and tailings

Waste rock is deposited in dumps near the pit, while processed ore residues—tailings—are stored in impoundments. Managing acid mine drainage, which forms when sulfide minerals in exposed rock oxidize and acidify runoff, is a major environmental concern.

Environmental impact and reclamation

Open-pit mining transforms landforms, removes habitat, and can affect groundwater. Modern mines are generally required to post reclamation bonds and to restore the site after closure—by regrading waste dumps, replacing topsoil, and revegetating. Abandoned pits may fill with water to form pit lakes, some of which, such as the Berkeley Pit in Montana, are heavily contaminated.
