Extinction

Extinction is the termination of a taxon — typically a species — by the death of its last member. A taxon may become functionally extinct before the death of its last individual if it loses the capacity to reproduce and recover. Because a species' potential range may be very large, determining the moment of extinction is difficult and is usually done retrospectively. This difficulty leads to phenomena such as Lazarus taxa, where a species presumed extinct abruptly "reappears" after a period of apparent absence.

Through evolution, species arise through the process of speciation and disappear through extinction when they can no longer survive in changing conditions or against superior competition. Animals that are very well-adapted to their environment may also become extinct if the environment changes rapidly enough. Typically, a species becomes extinct within 10 million years of its first appearance, although some species, called living fossils, survive with little or no morphological change for hundreds of millions of years.

Definitions

A species is considered extinct when the last living individual dies. Extinction therefore becomes a certainty when there are no surviving individuals that can reproduce and create a new generation. A species may become functionally extinct when only a handful of individuals survive, which are unable to reproduce due to poor health, age, sparse distribution over a large range, a lack of individuals of one sex, or other reasons.

Causes

Extinction occurs naturally as a result of environmental change, competition, predation, and disease, but the current rate of extinction is estimated to be 100 to 1,000 times the background rate, largely because of human activity: habitat destruction, overexploitation, pollution, introduced invasive species, and climate change. Genetic factors also matter: small populations are vulnerable to inbreeding depression and genetic drift, and species with long generation times may be unable to adapt quickly.

Mass extinctions

A mass extinction is a widespread and rapid decrease in the biodiversity on Earth. The "Big Five" mass extinctions identified in the fossil record are the Ordovician–Silurian, Late Devonian, Permian–Triassic (the largest, eliminating roughly 90% of marine species), Triassic–Jurassic, and Cretaceous–Paleogene events, the last of which ended the reign of the non-avian dinosaurs 66 million years ago. Many scientists argue that a sixth mass extinction, the Holocene extinction, is now under way.

In modern times

Well-documented recent extinctions include the dodo (1681), Steller's sea cow (1768), the great auk (1852), the passenger pigeon (1914), the thylacine (1936), and the baiji (declared functionally extinct in 2006). The IUCN Red List classifies species by extinction risk, and conservation measures — protected areas, captive breeding, and reintroduction — have brought some species, such as the Arabian oryx and the California condor, back from the brink.
