A neutron star is the collapsed core of a massive supergiant star, formed in a supernova explosion. Neutron stars are the smallest and densest known class of stars: a typical one has a mass around 1.4 times that of the Sun packed into a sphere only about 10 kilometres in radius, so dense that a teaspoon of its material would weigh billions of tonnes.

Structure

The star is supported against further collapse by neutron degeneracy pressure. Its outer crust is a lattice of neutron-rich nuclei; the interior is thought to be a neutron superfluid, and the core may contain exotic states of matter. Surface gravity is immense, and escape velocity reaches a substantial fraction of the speed of light.

Pulsars and magnetars

Many neutron stars are observed as pulsars, emitting beams of radiation that sweep across Earth with clock-like regularity; the first pulsar was discovered by Jocelyn Bell Burnell and Antony Hewish in 1967. Neutron stars with extreme magnetic fields are called magnetars. In 2017 the merger of two neutron stars was detected in gravitational waves (GW170817) together with a gamma-ray burst and kilonova, opening the era of multi-messenger astronomy.
