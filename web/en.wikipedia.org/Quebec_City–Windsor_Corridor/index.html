The Quebec City–Windsor Corridor (French: Corridor Québec-Windsor) is the most densely populated and heavily industrialized region of Canada. Stretching about 1,150 kilometres (710 mi) along the St. Lawrence River, Lake Ontario, Lake Erie, and the Detroit River, from Quebec City in the northeast to Windsor, Ontario, in the southwest, it is home to more than 18 million people — roughly half of Canada's population — including the national capital Ottawa and the country's two largest cities, Toronto and Montreal.

The corridor runs along the St. Lawrence River and the lower Great Lakes

Geography and demography

The corridor occupies the St. Lawrence Lowlands and the southern part of the Great Lakes–St. Lawrence region, the warmest and most agriculturally productive part of Canada. It includes Canada's largest metropolitan areas and a continuous chain of mid-sized cities such as Trois-Rivières, Kingston, Oshawa, Hamilton, Kitchener–Waterloo, and London. The region accounts for a majority of Canada's manufacturing output, corporate headquarters, and financial activity, forming the Canadian portion of the wider Great Lakes megalopolis sometimes called "ChiPitts."

Transportation

The corridor is served by a dense transport network. Highway 401 in Ontario — one of the widest and busiest highways in North America — and Autoroute 20 in Quebec form its road spine. Via Rail's Corridor passenger service links the region's cities and is the busiest intercity rail service in Canada; proposals for high-speed or high-frequency rail between Quebec City and Toronto (and in some studies Windsor) have been debated for decades, most recently under the Alto project. The St. Lawrence Seaway and the Great Lakes ports handle bulk shipping, and Toronto Pearson International Airport is Canada's busiest airport.

History

The corridor follows the principal route of French settlement in New France and of the early fur trade, and after 1763 the axis of British North America's development. Industrialization concentrated along the St. Lawrence and the lakes in the 19th and 20th centuries, reinforced by railway construction, hydroelectric power, and — after 1959 — the Seaway. The region's position opposite the American industrial Midwest tied its economy closely to that of the United States, particularly the automotive industry centred on Windsor, Oshawa, and Oakville.
