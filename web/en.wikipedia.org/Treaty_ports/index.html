Treaty ports

Treaty ports is the collective term for the ports of China, Japan, and Korea that were opened to foreign trade and residence by treaty during the 19th and early 20th centuries. The term also denotes the wider system of foreign privileges — concessions, settlements, extraterritorial jurisdiction, and treaty tariffs — that operated in those cities.

List and development

The system began in China with the five ports opened by the Treaty of Nanking in 1842: Guangzhou, Xiamen, Fuzhou, Ningbo, and Shanghai. Successive treaties after the Second Opium War, the First Sino-Japanese War, and the Boxer settlement expanded the list to include Tianjin, Hankou, Chongqing, and many others, including ports in Manchuria and Taiwan. Japan's treaty ports, opened from 1854, included Yokohama, Kobe, Nagasaki, Hakodate, and Niigata; Korea's included Busan, Incheon, and Wonsan.

Administration

Some treaty ports contained foreign concessions governed by municipal councils and policed by foreign authorities, most famously the Shanghai International Settlement and the French Concession. The Chinese Maritime Customs Service, staffed largely by foreigners, administered trade at the treaty ports and became a major source of revenue pledged to China's indemnities and foreign loans.

Legacy

The treaty ports left a lasting architectural and institutional legacy in cities such as Shanghai and Tianjin, and they feature prominently in Chinese historical memory as symbols of the "century of humiliation".
