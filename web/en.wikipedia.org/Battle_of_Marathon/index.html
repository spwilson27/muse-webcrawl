Battle of Marathon

The Battle of Marathon (Greek: Μάχη τοῦ Μαραθῶνος) took place in 490 BC during the first Persian invasion of Greece. It was fought between the citizens of Athens, aided by Plataea, and a Persian force commanded by Datis and Artaphernes. The battle was the culmination of the first attempt by Persia, under King Darius I, to subjugate Greece. The Greek victory marked a turning point in the Greco-Persian Wars and demonstrated the effectiveness of the hoplite phalanx.

Background

After Athens supported the Ionian Revolt against Persian rule, Darius vowed revenge. In 490 BC a Persian expedition under Datis and Artaphernes captured Eretria and sailed to the bay of Marathon, some 40 kilometres from Athens, guided by the exiled Athenian tyrant Hippias. About 9,000–10,000 Athenian hoplites, joined by 1,000 Plataeans, marched out to block the plain. A plea for Spartan help was answered with a delay for religious reasons.

The battle

According to Herodotus, after a standoff of several days the Athenian generals—Miltiades chief among them—decided to attack. The Greek line was deliberately weakened in the centre and strengthened on the wings, and the hoplites advanced at a run over roughly a mile of ground to reduce exposure to Persian arrows. The Persian centre broke through the thin Greek middle, but the strong Greek wings routed the Persian flanks and then closed inward, enveloping the Persian centre in a double envelopment. Herodotus gives Persian losses as 6,400 men against 192 Athenians, among them the polemarch Callimachus and the general Stesilaos. The Persians fled to their ships and sailed around Cape Sounion hoping to strike Athens undefended, but the victorious army marched back in time to deter them.

Legacy

Marathon secured Athens' independence, enhanced the prestige of the young democracy, and became a foundation myth of Athenian identity. Later tradition connected the battle with the runner Pheidippides, whose legendary run from Marathon to Athens to announce the victory inspired the modern marathon race, introduced at the 1896 Olympic Games over approximately the same route.
