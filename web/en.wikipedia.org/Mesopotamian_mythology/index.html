Mesopotamian mythology refers to the myths, religious texts, and cosmology of the ancient peoples of Mesopotamia — the Sumerians, Akkadians, Babylonians, and Assyrians — who occupied the land between the Tigris and Euphrates rivers from the fourth millennium BCE until the early centuries CE. It is among the oldest recorded mythological traditions in the world and profoundly influenced the Hebrew Bible and later Western literature, most famously through the flood story that parallels the account of Noah.

Cosmology and creation

The Babylonian creation epic Enuma Elish tells how the god Marduk defeated the primordial salt-water goddess Tiamat, split her body to form heaven and earth, and created humankind from the blood of the slain god Kingu to serve the gods. The universe was imagined as a flat earth beneath a solid dome of heaven, surrounded by a cosmic ocean, with the gloomy netherworld (Kur or Irkalla) below, where all the dead led shadowy existences regardless of their deeds in life.

The pantheon

  • Anu — remote god of the sky, head of the pantheon.
  • Enlil — god of wind and storm, executive of divine decrees, whose city was Nippur.
  • Enki (Akkadian Ea) — god of fresh waters, wisdom, and magic, friend of humankind.
  • Inanna (Akkadian Ishtar) — goddess of love, sex, and war, associated with Venus.
  • Marduk — city god of Babylon, elevated to kingship of the gods in the second millennium BCE.
  • Utu/Shamash — the sun god and god of justice; Nanna/Sin — the moon god.

Major myths

The Epic of Gilgamesh, first composed in Sumerian poems about the legendary king of Uruk, follows Gilgamesh and his companion Enkidu through adventures including the slaying of Humbaba and the Bull of Heaven, and, after Enkidu's death, Gilgamesh's futile quest for immortality — during which Utnapishtim tells him the story of the great flood, a tale also told in the Atrahasis epic. The Descent of Inanna recounts the goddess's journey to the netherworld, her death, and her rescue and return, bound to the cycle of the seasons through the fate of her consort Dumuzi (Tammuz).

Legacy

After the decipherment of cuneiform in the nineteenth century — notably George Smith's 1872 translation of the flood tablet — Mesopotamian texts reshaped understanding of the Bible's sources and remain foundational for the study of ancient religion.
