Compton scattering

Compton scattering is the inelastic scattering of a photon by a charged particle, usually an electron. It results in a decrease in energy (increase in wavelength) of the photon — typically an X-ray or gamma ray — known as the Compton effect. Part of the energy of the photon is transferred to the recoiling electron.

The effect was discovered by Arthur Holly Compton in 1923 at Washington University in St. Louis, and earned him the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1927. It provided decisive evidence that light has particle-like properties, since the wavelength shift could not be explained by classical wave theory.

The Compton formula

The change in wavelength of the scattered photon depends only on the scattering angle:

λ′ − λ = (h / mec) (1 − cos θ)

where h is the Planck constant, me is the electron rest mass, c is the speed of light, and θ is the photon's scattering angle. The quantity h / mec is known as the Compton wavelength of the electron, approximately 2.43 × 10−12 m.

Inverse Compton scattering

In inverse Compton scattering, a charged particle transfers part of its energy to a photon, increasing the photon's energy. This process is important in astrophysics, for example in the Sunyaev–Zeldovich effect and in the emission of X-rays and gamma rays by energetic electrons in jets and supernova remnants.

Applications

Compton scattering is used in gamma-ray spectroscopy, in Compton cameras for imaging and security scanning, and in radiotherapy physics, where it is the dominant interaction of X-rays with soft tissue at diagnostic and therapeutic energies.
