The Fifth French Republic is France's current republican system of government, established by the Constitution of 4 October 1958. It emerged from the collapse of the Fourth Republic amid the Algerian War and was designed under the leadership of Charles de Gaulle to strengthen the executive and end the governmental instability of assembly-dominated regimes.

Institutions

The Fifth Republic is a semi-presidential system. The President of the Republic — elected directly by universal suffrage since a 1962 referendum, for a five-year term since 2000 — appoints the Prime Minister, presides over the Council of Ministers, commands the armed forces, and may dissolve the National Assembly and call referendums. The government, led by the Prime Minister, is responsible to Parliament, which consists of the National Assembly and the Senate. A Constitutional Council reviews the constitutionality of legislation.

History

De Gaulle served as first president (1959–1969), surviving the Évian Accords that ended the Algerian War and the crisis of May 1968. Successive presidents included Georges Pompidou, Valéry Giscard d'Estaing, François Mitterrand — whose 1981 election brought the left to power and abolished the death penalty — Jacques Chirac, Nicolas Sarkozy, François Hollande, and Emmanuel Macron. Periods of "cohabitation" (1986–88, 1993–95, 1997–2002) occurred when the president and parliamentary majority were of opposed camps, shifting effective power toward the prime minister.

Assessment

The Fifth Republic has proved by far the most durable French regime since 1870 (apart from the Third Republic), combining strong presidential authority with democratic alternation in power, though critics periodically propose a Sixth Republic with a more parliamentary balance.
