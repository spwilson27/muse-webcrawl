Interplanetary dust cloud

The interplanetary dust cloud, or zodiacal cloud, is a cloud of cosmic dust — small particles ranging from micrometres to fractions of a millimetre in size — that pervades the space between the planets within planetary systems such as the Solar System. It has been studied for many years, not least because the sunlit dust produces the zodiacal light, a faint glow visible along the ecliptic after sunset and before sunrise.

Sources and dynamics

The dust is continually replenished by collisions in the asteroid belt and by material shed from comets as they pass through the inner Solar System. Individual grains are short-lived on astronomical timescales: radiation pressure blows the smallest particles out of the system, while the Poynting–Robertson effect causes larger grains to spiral slowly into the Sun. Particles that reach Earth produce meteors and contribute an estimated tens of thousands of tonnes of dust to the upper atmosphere each year.

Structure

The cloud is broadly lens-shaped, concentrated toward the plane of the ecliptic, with density decreasing with distance from the Sun. Spacecraft measurements and infrared observations (such as those by IRAS and COBE) revealed additional structure, including dust bands associated with asteroid families and a ring of dust trailing Earth in its orbit.

Other systems

Analogous "exozodiacal dust" around other stars is both a clue to planet formation and a source of noise for attempts to image Earth-like exoplanets directly.
