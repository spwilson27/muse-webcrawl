Alcohol-related liver disease (ARLD), also called alcoholic liver disease, is a spectrum of liver damage caused by excessive alcohol consumption. It ranges from fatty liver (steatosis) through alcoholic hepatitis to fibrosis and cirrhosis, and is one of the leading causes of chronic liver disease and liver-related death worldwide.

Stages and pathology

Alcohol is metabolised in the liver to acetaldehyde, a toxic compound that damages hepatocytes. Hepatic steatosis, the accumulation of fat in liver cells, develops in most heavy drinkers and is reversible with abstinence. In some individuals, inflammation supervenes as alcoholic hepatitis, which can present acutely with jaundice and carry a high short-term mortality. Continued injury leads to fibrosis and ultimately cirrhosis — irreversible scarring that distorts the liver's architecture, causing portal hypertension, ascites, variceal bleeding, hepatic encephalopathy, and increased risk of hepatocellular carcinoma.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on the history of alcohol use, blood tests (often showing raised GGT and an AST:ALT ratio above 2), imaging, and sometimes liver biopsy; severity scores such as Maddrey's discriminant function and MELD guide prognosis in alcoholic hepatitis. The cornerstone of treatment is complete abstinence, supported by addiction treatment, nutritional support, and corticosteroids in selected severe cases. Liver transplantation may be offered to carefully selected patients with decompensated disease.

Epidemiology

Alcohol accounts for a large share of global cirrhosis deaths — roughly half in many estimates — with risk rising steeply above sustained intakes of about 30 g of alcohol per day, and modified by sex, genetics, obesity, and coexisting viral hepatitis.
