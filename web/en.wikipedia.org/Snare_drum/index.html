The snare drum is a percussion instrument with a set of stiff wires (snares) stretched across its bottom head, which vibrate against the head when the top is struck, producing a crisp, staccato, buzzing sound. It is a central component of the modern drum kit, of marching bands, and of the orchestral percussion section.

Construction

The drum consists of a cylindrical shell — usually of maple, birch, or metal such as steel, brass, or aluminium — with a batter head on top and a thinner resonant head below. Snare wires, held by a strainer mechanism that can engage or release them, span the bottom head. With the snares off, the drum produces a tom-tom-like tone; with them on, it gives the characteristic snare crack.

History

The snare drum descends from the medieval tabor and from the side drums carried by soldiers from the 15th century onward, where its rattling tone cut through battlefield noise for signalling and cadence. It entered the orchestra in the 19th century and became the rhythmic heart of jazz, rock, and popular drum kits in the 20th.

Technique and use

Players use matched or traditional grip with sticks, brushes, or rods. Rudiments — standard patterns such as rolls, flams, and paradiddles — form the technical vocabulary of snare drumming, especially in marching and drum-corps styles. In popular music the snare typically sounds the backbeat on beats two and four. Orchestral works such as Ravel's Boléro feature prominent snare parts.
