Maat

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Maat

Name in hieroglyphs: feather of Maat (Gardiner H6)

Symbol: ostrich feather, scales

Parents: Ra (father, in some traditions)

Consort: sometimes Thoth

Maat or Maʽat (Ancient Egyptian: mꜢʽt) was the ancient Egyptian concept of truth, balance, order, harmony, law, morality and justice. Maat was also the name of the goddess who personified these concepts; she regulated the stars, the seasons, and the actions of mortals and deities, and she embodied the cosmic order established at the creation of the world. Her opposite was isfet, chaos and injustice.

The concept

Maat was central to Egyptian ethics and cosmology from the Old Kingdom onward. Pharaohs presented themselves as upholders of maat, restoring temples, dispensing justice and defeating disorder. In daily life, maat governed social conduct: honesty, generosity, respect for elders and care for the vulnerable were all framed as living according to maat. Wisdom literature, such as the Instruction of Ptahhotep, is largely devoted to expounding maat for officials and scribes.

The goddess

The goddess Maat is usually depicted as a woman wearing a single tall ostrich feather — the feather of truth — on her head, sometimes with wings on her arms. She was considered a daughter of the sun god Ra. Her feather played a decisive role in the judgment of the dead described in the Book of the Dead: in the Hall of Two Truths, the heart of the deceased was weighed on a balance against Maat's feather. A heart heavy with wrongdoing failed the test and was devoured by the monster Ammit; a balanced heart allowed the deceased to pass into the afterlife.

Maat and the law

Egyptian judges were priests of Maat, and the chief justice wore a small image of the goddess. The vizier, Egypt's highest official, was styled "priest of Maat". Rather than a codified legal system on the Mesopotamian model, Egyptian justice appealed to maat as its guiding principle, with the king as final arbiter. Temples of Maat are known at Karnak and Deir el-Medina, among other sites.

Legacy

The concept has continued to interest Egyptologists and ethicists, and modern movements, including some within African philosophy, draw on maat as a model of a virtue-centred ethical system. See also the related article on magical thinking in ancient religion.
