Bloomery


A bloomery furnace producing a spongy mass of iron (bloom)
TypeSmelting furnace
FuelCharcoal
ProductWrought iron bloom
Typical temp.1,100–1,200 °C

A bloomery is a type of metallurgical furnace once used widely for the smelting of iron from its oxides. The bloomery was the earliest form of smelter capable of smelting iron. Cast iron is first produced in a blast furnace, but the direct product of a bloomery is a porous mass of iron and slag called a bloom. This mix of slag and iron is usually consolidated and further forged into wrought iron.

Process

In a bloomery, charcoal is burned at temperatures approaching 1,200 °C, hot enough to reduce iron oxide ore to metallic iron but below the melting point of iron itself (1,538 °C). Air is supplied through a tuyère, originally by hand-operated bellows and later by water-powered bellows. As the charge of ore and charcoal descends, iron oxide is reduced by carbon monoxide. The reduced iron particles coalesce into a spongy bloom at the base of the furnace, while molten slag (mostly fayalite, Fe2SiO4) drains or is trapped within the bloom.

After several hours, the bloom—typically weighing between 10 and 100 kg depending on furnace size—is extracted while still white-hot and hammered repeatedly to expel slag and weld the iron particles together. The resulting wrought iron has a fibrous structure of iron interleaved with thin slag stringers.

History

Bloomeries were used across Europe, Africa, and Asia from the early Iron Age. In sub-Saharan Africa, bloomery smelting developed independently and persisted into the 20th century. In Europe, the bloomery was gradually displaced from the late medieval period by the blast furnace, which produced liquid pig iron that could be cast. Catalan forges and Stücköfen were enlarged variants producing larger blooms. Bloomery smelting survives today mainly in experimental archaeology and among artisan blacksmiths.
