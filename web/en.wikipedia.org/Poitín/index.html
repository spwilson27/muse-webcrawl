Poitín

Type: Distilled spirit
Country of origin: Ireland
Alcohol by volume: 40–90%
Colour: Colourless

Poitín (Irish pronunciation: [ˈpˠɛtʲiːnʲ]), anglicized as poteen or potcheen, is a traditional Irish distilled beverage (either in a pot still or a patent still) and is often made illegally, similar to moonshine. In modern parlance, it is sometimes referred to as "Irish moonshine". It was traditionally distilled in a small pot still and the term is a diminutive of the Irish word pota, meaning "pot". The traditional base for poitín can be a variety of crops, but the most common is barley, grain, treacle, sugar beet, wheat, and potatoes.

History

Poitín has been produced since the advent of distillation in Ireland, and was historically produced illegally in remote rural areas to avoid taxation. In 1661, the English Crown made the private distillation of spirits illegal in Ireland without a licence, driving production underground. In 1997, the Irish Revenue Commissioners began granting licences for the legal production and sale of poitín, and poitín products are now legally produced by a number of Irish distilleries. In 2008, Irish poitín was accorded geographical indication (GI) status by the EU Council and Parliament.

Production

Poitín was traditionally produced in isolated areas, in simple pot stills heated over a turf fire. Because it was often produced without regard to quality control, homemade poitín could vary greatly in strength, typically ranging from 40% to as high as 90% ABV. Legal modern poitín is generally triple-distilled and bottled at around 40–50% ABV.

In culture

Poitín features prominently in Irish folk songs, poetry and literature, including the famous folk song "The Hills of Connemara" and works by writers such as John B. Keane. It has long been associated with rural Irish life and resistance to excise authorities.
