The Essex was an American whaleship from Nantucket, Massachusetts, which was sunk by a sperm whale in the Pacific Ocean in 1820. The surviving crew drifted for months in small whaleboats, resorting in extremity to cannibalism, in one of the most famous survival ordeals in maritime history. The story was a principal inspiration for Herman Melville's Moby-Dick (1851).

The sinking

The Essex, 238 tons and already more than twenty years old, sailed from Nantucket in August 1819 under Captain George Pollard Jr., with first mate Owen Chase, on a whaling voyage to the Pacific. On 20 November 1820, while the boats were pursuing a pod of sperm whales far from land, an exceptionally large bull sperm whale — estimated at 85 feet — twice rammed the ship, stoving in her bow. The Essex sank within minutes; the crew of twenty took to three lightly built whaleboats with minimal provisions.

The ordeal

Fearing hostile natives on nearer islands, the men attempted to sail some 3,000 nautical miles to South America against prevailing winds and currents. Starving, they ate the bodies of shipmates who died; in Captain Pollard's boat, lots were drawn and Pollard's young cousin Owen Coffin was shot to provide food for the others. Only eight men survived. Chase's boat was rescued in February 1821, and Pollard's two remaining men, found sucking marrow from bones, days later; a third boat was never seen again.

Legacy

Owen Chase published his Narrative of the Most Extraordinary and Distressing Shipwreck of the Whale-Ship Essex in 1821, and cabin boy Thomas Nickerson's account, written later, was published in 1984. Melville read Chase's narrative and met Pollard; the Essex story underlies Moby-Dick's climax. The saga has been retold in Nathaniel Philbrick's In the Heart of the Sea (2000) and the 2015 film of the same name.
