Gemistus Pletho

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

George Gemistus Pletho (Greek: Γεώργιος Γεμιστὸς Πλήθων; c. 1355/1360 – 26 June 1452/1454), commonly known as Gemistus Pletho, was a Byzantine philosopher and historian who was one of the chief pioneers of the revival of Greek learning in Western Europe. As a proponent of Plato, he stood in marked opposition to the dominant Aristotelianism of the Byzantine and Latin schools of his time. In Italy he re-introduced Plato's ideas to Western European intellectuals during the Council of Ferrara-Florence (1438–1439), and helped ignite the interest in Plato that culminated in the Platonic Academy of Florence.

Life

Pletho was born in Constantinople and later settled in Mystras in the Despotate of the Morea, where he served as a counsellor and judge and taught philosophy. In 1438 he travelled with Emperor John VIII to Italy for the council that sought to reunite the Eastern and Western churches. In Florence his lectures on Plato, and his treatise On the Differences of Aristotle from Plato, deeply influenced Cosimo de' Medici, who later founded the Platonic Academy under Marsilio Ficino.

Philosophy

Pletho's major work, the Nomoi or Book of Laws, written after his return from Italy, set out a complete religious and political system based on Neoplatonism, Zoroastrianism and a revived Hellenic paganism centred on Zeus and the Olympian gods. After his death the Orthodox patriarch Gennadius Scholarius, once his student and later his critic, had the work burned; only fragments survive, preserved in copies made before the destruction. His proposals for reform of the Byzantine state included land and social reforms for the Peloponnese, presented in memoranda to Emperor Manuel II and Despot Theodore II.

Legacy

Pletho is remembered as a key transmitter of Platonic scholarship to the Renaissance West and, in Greece, as a controversial figure: a late champion of Hellenic identity whose religious project clashed with Orthodox Christianity.
