Front-wheel drive

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Front-wheel drive (FWD) is a form of engine and transmission layout in which the engine drives the front wheels only. It is the most common drivetrain configuration in modern passenger cars, typically combined with a transverse (sideways-mounted) front engine. Alternatives include rear-wheel drive and all-wheel drive.

History

Experiments with front-wheel drive date to the earliest automobiles, and cars such as the Cord L-29 (1929) and DKW models of the 1930s brought it to market. The Citroën Traction Avant of 1934 combined front-wheel drive with unitary body construction. The layout became dominant after the success of the 1959 Mini, whose transverse engine and gearbox-in-sump design, engineered by Alec Issigonis, showed how compact the arrangement could be. The Volkswagen Golf (1974) and the fuel crises of the 1970s accelerated the industry-wide shift to front-wheel drive for small and mid-size cars.

Characteristics

Packaging the engine, gearbox, and final drive together at the front eliminates the propshaft and transmission tunnel, freeing cabin space and reducing weight and manufacturing cost. The weight of the engine over the driven wheels aids traction on snow and wet roads. Drawbacks include torque steer (a pull on the steering wheel under hard acceleration, especially with unequal-length driveshafts), a tendency to understeer, and limits on how much power can be put through the front tyres, which is why most high-performance and luxury cars use rear- or all-wheel drive.
