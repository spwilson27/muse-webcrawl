Seneca Falls Convention

The Seneca Falls Convention was the first women's rights convention in public history in the United States. It was organized by Elizabeth Cady Stanton and Lucretia Mott and advertised as "a convention to discuss the social, civil, and religious condition and rights of woman". It took place in Seneca Falls, New York, on July 19–20, 1848, at the Wesleyan Methodist Chapel, and drew about 300 attendees, including some 40 men.

Background

Stanton and Mott had met at the 1840 World Anti-Slavery Convention in London, where women delegates were excluded from participating. In July 1848, Mott visited the area of Waterloo, New York, and a tea gathering with Jane Hunt, Mary Ann M'Clintock, Martha Coffin Wright, Stanton, and Mott resolved to hold a women's rights convention within days.

Declaration of Sentiments

The convention's central document was the Declaration of Sentiments, drafted principally by Stanton and modeled on the United States Declaration of Independence. It declared that "all men and women are created equal" and listed grievances concerning women's legal status, education, employment, and the franchise. Twelve resolutions were adopted, the most controversial being the ninth, demanding women's suffrage; it passed narrowly, aided by the support of Frederick Douglass. One hundred of the attendees — 68 women and 32 men — signed the Declaration.

Legacy

The convention is widely regarded as the beginning of the organized women's rights movement in the United States, leading directly to campaigns for property rights, education, and ultimately the Nineteenth Amendment (1920), which guaranteed women's right to vote. The site is now preserved as part of Women's Rights National Historical Park.
