Astronomical interferometer

An astronomical interferometer or telescope array is a set of separate telescopes, mirror segments, or radio telescope antennas that work together as a single telescope to provide higher-resolution images of astronomical objects such as stars, nebulas, and galaxies by means of interferometry.

The resolution of an interferometer is set by the maximum separation (baseline) between its elements.

Principle

The angular resolution of a single telescope is limited by its diameter. An interferometer combines light from separated apertures so that the effective resolution equals that of a single telescope with a diameter equal to the longest baseline between elements, though with lower light-gathering power. Synthesis of images relies on the van Cittert–Zernike theorem, relating the measured correlations to the sky brightness distribution.

Examples

Radio astronomy pioneered the technique: arrays such as the Very Large Array and ALMA achieve milliarcsecond resolution, and the Event Horizon Telescope, a planet-scale very-long-baseline array, imaged the shadow of the black hole in M87 in 2019. In the optical and infrared, instruments include CHARA, the VLTI at Paranal, and the Keck Interferometer. Michelson and Pease's stellar interferometer at Mount Wilson first measured a star's diameter (Betelgeuse) in 1920.
