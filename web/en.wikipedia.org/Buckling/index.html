Buckling

A column buckling under an axial compressive load. (Image placeholder)

In structural engineering, buckling is the sudden change in shape (deformation) of a structural component under load, such as the bowing of a column under compression or the wrinkling of a plate under shear. It is an instability phenomenon: a structure can buckle at a load well below the load that would crush or yield its material, especially if it is slender.

Columns

For an ideal elastic column, Leonhard Euler derived the critical load at which buckling occurs: Pcr = π²EI / (KL)², where E is the modulus of elasticity, I the smallest second moment of area of the cross-section, L the unsupported length, and K a factor depending on the end conditions. Whether a column fails by buckling or by yielding depends on its slenderness ratio; real columns with imperfections are designed using empirical column curves and safety factors in structural codes.

Other forms

Thin plates and shells buckle locally, often wrinkling while still able to carry load (post-buckling strength). Beams can fail by lateral-torsional buckling, in which the compression flange deflects sideways and twists. Sudden "snap-through" buckling affects shallow arches and shells. Buckling also occurs outside civil engineering: railway tracks can buckle in hot weather ("sun kink"), pipelines can buckle on the seabed, and at microscopic scales buckling of microtubules and other filaments matters in cell biology.

Design

Engineers guard against buckling by increasing stiffness (bracing, stiffeners, thicker or better-shaped cross-sections), reducing unsupported lengths, and applying design codes that account for geometric imperfections and residual stresses.
