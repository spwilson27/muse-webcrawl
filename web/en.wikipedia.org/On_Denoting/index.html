On Denoting

"On Denoting" is an essay by Bertrand Russell, published in the philosophy journal Mind in 1905. It presents Russell's theory of descriptions, a landmark of analytic philosophy, which Frank Ramsey famously called "a paradigm of philosophy". The paper analyses how definite descriptions such as "the present King of France" can be meaningful even though they refer to nothing.

The problem

Sentences containing definite descriptions pose puzzles: "The present King of France is bald" seems meaningful and false, yet if its subject phrase denotes nothing, earlier theories (such as Alexius Meinong's) were driven to posit non-existent objects for it to refer to. Russell also addressed puzzles about identity statements ("Scott is the author of Waverley", valuable because the two terms differ in meaning) and about negative existentials ("the golden mountain does not exist").

The theory of descriptions

Russell's solution treats definite descriptions as "incomplete symbols" that have no meaning in isolation. A sentence of the form "The F is G" is analysed as a quantified statement: there exists an x such that x is F, nothing else is F, and x is G. "The present King of France is bald" thus comes out false, because no such x exists, while its negation is ambiguous between a false "primary" reading and a true "secondary" reading — a distinction that resolves the puzzle of negative existentials.

Influence

The essay helped found the analytic tradition's method of logical analysis and influenced Wittgenstein, Carnap and Quine. P. F. Strawson's 1950 paper "On Referring" mounted a famous challenge, arguing that Russell conflated sentences with their uses and that referring expressions presuppose rather than assert existence. The ensuing debate shaped the philosophy of language for decades.
