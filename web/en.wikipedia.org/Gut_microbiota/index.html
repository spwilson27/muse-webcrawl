Gut microbiota

The gut microbiota, also known as the gut flora or gastrointestinal microbiota, is the complex community of microorganisms that live in the digestive tract. In humans it includes bacteria, archaea, fungi, protists and viruses; the bacteria alone number roughly 38 trillion cells and encompass hundreds to over a thousand species. The gut microbiota is now often described as a "forgotten organ" because of its metabolic and immunological activity.

Development

The infant gut is colonised during and after birth; mode of delivery (vaginal versus caesarean), breastfeeding and early antibiotic exposure all influence the founding community. By about two to three years of age the microbiota resembles the relatively stable adult state, though it continues to change with diet, illness and ageing.

Metabolism and immunity

Gut microbes ferment fibre into short-chain fatty acids (acetate, propionate, butyrate), produce vitamins, transform bile acids and generate neuroactive compounds that participate in the gut–brain axis. They are essential to the development of gut-associated lymphoid tissue and help calibrate systemic immune responses; germ-free animals show profoundly underdeveloped immunity.

Disease associations and therapy

Altered gut microbiota composition has been linked to inflammatory bowel disease, irritable bowel syndrome, colorectal cancer, obesity, diabetes, asthma and Parkinson's disease. Faecal microbiota transplantation is an established treatment for recurrent Clostridioides difficile infection, and defined bacterial consortia are in development. Diet — particularly fibre diversity — is one of the strongest day-to-day influences on the community.
