Network is a 1976 American satirical drama film produced by Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer and released by United Artists, written by Paddy Chayefsky and directed by Sidney Lumet. A fictional television network, UBS, cynically exploits the on-air mental breakdown — and subsequent ravings — of its veteran news anchor for ratings. Widely regarded as one of the greatest films ever made and as uncannily prescient about the merging of news and entertainment, it is famous for the line "I'm as mad as hell, and I'm not going to take this anymore!"

Plot

Howard Beale (Peter Finch), the longtime anchor of the UBS Evening News, learns he is being fired for low ratings and announces on live television that he will kill himself on air. Instead of being removed, Beale's unhinged broadcasts — culminating in his exhortation that viewers shout "I'm as mad as hell" from their windows — become a sensation. Ambitious programming executive Diana Christensen (Faye Dunaway) builds the news division into an entertainment show around Beale, now styled a "mad prophet of the airwaves," while news president Max Schumacher (William Holden), Beale's old friend, watches in horror and falls into an affair with Diana. When Beale, after a thunderous boardroom lecture from corporate chairman Arthur Jensen (Ned Beatty) on the gospel of global corporate integration, preaches that gospel instead of populist anger, his ratings collapse — and the network arranges his on-air assassination, "the first known instance of a man who was killed because he had lousy ratings."

Reception and awards

Network was a critical and commercial success. At the 49th Academy Awards it won four Oscars: Best Actor (Finch, the first posthumous acting Oscar), Best Actress (Dunaway), Best Supporting Actress (Beatrice Straight, whose five minutes of screen time remain the shortest Oscar-winning performance), and Best Original Screenplay (Chayefsky). It was nominated for Best Picture and Best Director. In 2000 the film was selected for the U.S. National Film Registry as culturally, historically, and aesthetically significant, and the American Film Institute ranked it among the greatest American films; the Writers Guild of America voted Chayefsky's script among the best ever written.

Legacy

The film's satire of ratings-driven news, corporate media consolidation, and populist television rage has been repeatedly cited as prophetic of later developments in cable news and reality television. A stage adaptation by Lee Hall premiered at London's National Theatre in 2017 and transferred to Broadway with Bryan Cranston as Beale.
