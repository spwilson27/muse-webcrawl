Karakorum

Karakorum (Mongolian: Хархорум) was the capital of the Mongol Empire between 1235 and 1260 and of the Northern Yuan dynasty in the 14th and 15th centuries. Its ruins lie in the northwestern corner of Mongolia's Övörkhangai Province, near the present town of Kharkhorin and the Erdene Zuu Monastery.

History

The site in the Orkhon Valley had been significant to earlier steppe empires, but the city was founded by Ögedei Khan, son of Genghis Khan, in 1235, when a palace was built and walls raised. Under Ögedei and his successors, Karakorum became a cosmopolitan imperial capital visited by merchants, craftsmen, and envoys from across Eurasia, including the Franciscan friar William of Rubruck in 1254, whose account describes its temples, mosques, churches, and quarters for foreign artisans.

In 1260, Kublai Khan moved the imperial capital to Khanbaliq (modern Beijing), and Karakorum declined in importance. It briefly served as capital of the Northern Yuan after 1368, but was devastated in wars with the Ming dynasty in 1388. In 1585 the Erdene Zuu Monastery was built near the ruins, using stones from the old city.

Archaeology

Excavations, including Mongolian–German expeditions from the 1990s onward, have uncovered the palace area, workshops, kilns, and street layouts, confirming the city's role as a manufacturing and trading hub on the steppe.
