National missile defense

National missile defense (NMD) refers to systems designed to protect an entire country against incoming ballistic missiles, particularly intercontinental ballistic missiles (ICBMs) armed with nuclear warheads. It is distinct from theatre missile defense, which protects smaller areas such as deployed forces or cities against shorter-range threats.

United States programs

The United States has pursued missile defense since the 1950s, through programs such as Nike Zeus, Safeguard, and the Strategic Defense Initiative announced by President Ronald Reagan in 1983. After withdrawing from the Anti-Ballistic Missile Treaty in 2002, the US deployed the Ground-based Midcourse Defense (GMD) system, with interceptors based in Alaska and California intended to counter limited attacks from states such as North Korea. The Missile Defense Agency oversees these efforts, which also include Aegis Ballistic Missile Defense and THAAD.

Other countries

Russia maintains the A-135 (and its successor A-235) system defending Moscow. China has conducted midcourse interception tests, and India has developed a two-tier ballistic missile defence programme. Israel's Arrow system provides national-level defense in cooperation with the United States.

Debate

Critics argue that midcourse interception is technically difficult, easily countered by decoys and multiple warheads, and destabilising to deterrence. Supporters contend that even limited defenses are valuable against small arsenals and accidental launches. The effectiveness of hitting a warhead in space has often been compared to "hitting a bullet with a bullet."
