Isamu Noguchi (野口 勇, Noguchi Isamu; November 17, 1904 – December 30, 1988) was a Japanese-American artist and landscape architect whose career spanned six decades. Known for his sculpture, public spaces, gardens, furniture, and lighting designs, Noguchi sought to expand the definition of sculpture into the environment as a whole, believing that art should integrate with everyday life.

Early life and training

Noguchi was born in Los Angeles to the Japanese poet Yonejirō Noguchi and the American writer Léonie Gilmour. He spent much of his childhood in Japan before returning to the United States for schooling in Indiana. After abandoning pre-medical studies at Columbia University, he devoted himself to sculpture. On a Guggenheim Fellowship in Paris in 1927–28 he worked as an assistant in the studio of Constantin Brâncuși, whose simplified abstract forms deeply influenced him, and he encountered the Parisian avant-garde. Travels in China and Japan in 1930–31 added brush drawing and the aesthetics of Japanese gardens to his vocabulary.

Sculpture and public space

During the 1930s Noguchi supported himself with portrait busts while producing increasingly abstract work and ambitious proposals for public monuments such as Monument to the Plough and a model for a play mountain. Voluntarily entering a Japanese-American internment camp at Poston, Arizona, in 1942 in hopes of improving conditions, he found the experience a disillusioning confrontation with American racism. After the war he achieved major public commissions: the gardens of the UNESCO headquarters in Paris (1958), the Billy Rose Art Garden in Jerusalem (1965), the sunken garden for the Beinecke Library at Yale, and Red Cube (1968) in New York. His lifelong collaboration with choreographer Martha Graham produced sets for ballets including Appalachian Spring (1944).

Design

Noguchi's Akari light sculptures (from 1951), lamps of washi paper on bamboo frames made in Gifu, Japan, became icons of mid-century design, as did his sculptural glass-topped coffee table (1947) for Herman Miller. He maintained studios in New York and in Mure, Japan, where he carved stone. In 1985 he opened the Isamu Noguchi Garden Museum (now the Noguchi Museum) in Long Island City, New York, a converted industrial building housing a comprehensive collection of his work.
