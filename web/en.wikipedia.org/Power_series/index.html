Power series

In mathematics, a power series (in one variable) is an infinite series of the form

f(x) = Σn=0∞ an(x − c)n = a0 + a1(x − c) + a2(x − c)2 + ⋯

where an represents the coefficient of the n-th term and c is a constant, called the centre of the series. Power series arise naturally in mathematical analysis as Taylor series of infinitely differentiable functions, and they include ordinary polynomials as finite cases.

Power series
General formΣ an(x − c)n
Key notionRadius of convergence
Special casesTaylor series, Maclaurin series, geometric series

Radius of convergence

A power series converges absolutely for values of x within some radius R of the centre c, and diverges outside it. The radius of convergence can be computed, when the limit exists, via the ratio test or root test; it may be zero (convergence only at the centre), finite, or infinite (convergence everywhere), as with the exponential series ex = Σ xn/n!.

Operations

Within their interval of convergence, power series may be added, multiplied, differentiated, and integrated term by term, behaving much like polynomials. A function represented by a power series in this way is called an analytic function.

Examples

The geometric series 1/(1 − x) = Σ xn (|x| < 1); the exponential, sine, and cosine series; and the binomial series are classical examples used throughout calculus and complex analysis.
