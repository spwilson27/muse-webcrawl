Morton Feldman (January 12, 1926 – September 3, 1987) was an American composer and a major figure in 20th-century classical music. A pioneer of indeterminate music, Feldman was associated with the experimental New York School alongside John Cage, Earle Brown, and Christian Wolff. His mature music is characterised by quiet dynamics, slowly unfolding sonorities, and extreme length, with several late works lasting four to six hours.

Life

Born in Brooklyn to Russian-Jewish immigrants, Feldman studied piano with Vera Maurina Press and composition with Wallingford Riegger and Stefan Wolpe. In 1950, after a performance of Webern's Symphony, he met John Cage, beginning a formative friendship. Through Cage he entered the circle of abstract expressionist painters — Philip Guston, Jackson Pollock, Mark Rothko, and Robert Rauschenberg — whose work strongly influenced his musical thinking. From 1973 he taught at the University at Buffalo, where he held the Edgard Varèse Professor chair.

Music

Feldman's early works used graphic notation, in which performers choose pitches within specified registers and durations, reflecting his desire to free sounds from predetermined systems. His best-known works include Rothko Chapel (1971), written for the Rothko Chapel in Houston; For Philip Guston (1984), a four-hour trio; the opera Neither (1977), to a text by Samuel Beckett; and the String Quartet No. 2 (1983), which lasts around six hours in performance. His music typically hovers at the edge of audibility, using delicate, decaying sonorities spread across long spans of time.
