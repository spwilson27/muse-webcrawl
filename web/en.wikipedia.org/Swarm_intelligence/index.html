Swarm intelligence

Swarm intelligence (SI) is the collective behaviour of decentralised, self-organised systems, natural or artificial. Such systems consist typically of a population of simple agents interacting locally with one another and with their environment, following simple rules, so that a coherent global pattern emerges without any centralised control. The term was introduced by Gerardo Beni and Jing Wang in 1989 in the context of cellular robotic systems.

Biological examples

Natural examples include ant colonies, bee swarming, bird flocking, fish schooling, animal herding and bacterial growth. Through local interactions — pheromone trails in ants, the waggle dance in honey bees, alignment between neighbouring birds — colonies solve problems such as finding the shortest route to food, selecting nest sites and allocating labour, which no individual could solve alone.

Algorithms

Swarm intelligence has inspired optimisation algorithms including ant colony optimisation, which models foraging ants to solve routing and scheduling problems, and particle swarm optimisation, in which candidate solutions move through a search space influenced by their own best results and those of their neighbours. Other methods include artificial bee colony algorithms and stochastic diffusion search.

Applications

Swarm approaches are applied in telecommunications routing, robotics (swarm robotics), crowd simulation, data clustering and function optimisation. Their advantages include robustness, flexibility and scalability; their disadvantages include difficulty of analysis and of guaranteeing convergence to an optimal solution.
