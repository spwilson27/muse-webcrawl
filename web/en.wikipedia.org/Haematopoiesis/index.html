Haematopoiesis

Haematopoiesis (from Greek αἷμα, "blood" and ποιεῖν "to make"; also spelled hematopoiesis in American English) is the formation of blood cellular components. All cellular blood components are derived from haematopoietic stem cells. In a healthy adult human, roughly 1011–1012 new blood cells are produced daily in order to maintain steady state levels in the peripheral circulation.

Haematopoietic stem cells

Haematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) reside in the medulla of the bone (bone marrow) and have the unique ability to give rise to all of the different mature blood cell types and tissues. HSCs are self-renewing cells: when they differentiate, at least some of their daughter cells remain as HSCs, so the pool of stem cells is not depleted. The other daughters of HSCs (myeloid and lymphoid progenitor cells) can follow any of the other differentiation pathways that lead to the production of one or more specific types of blood cell, but cannot self-renew.

Lineages

The myeloid lineage gives rise to red blood cells (erythrocytes), platelets (via megakaryocytes), granulocytes (neutrophils, eosinophils, basophils), and monocytes/macrophages. The lymphoid lineage gives rise to T cells, B cells, and natural killer cells. The process is regulated by growth factors and cytokines such as erythropoietin, thrombopoietin, and the colony-stimulating factors.

Location

In developing embryos, blood formation occurs first in the yolk sac, then in the liver, spleen, and finally the bone marrow. After birth, haematopoiesis occurs almost exclusively in the bone marrow; in adults the active (red) marrow is concentrated in the pelvis, sternum, vertebrae, ribs, and ends of the long bones.
