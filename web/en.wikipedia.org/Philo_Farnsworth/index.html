Philo Taylor Farnsworth (1906–1971) was an American inventor best known for creating the first fully functional, fully electronic television system. As a teenager in Idaho he conceived the principle of scanning an image line by line with electrons—an idea he sketched for his high-school science teacher in 1922. On 7 September 1927, in his San Francisco laboratory, he transmitted the first electronic television image, a simple straight line.

The image dissector and patent wars

Farnsworth's camera tube, the image dissector, converted an optical image into an electrical signal without any mechanical moving parts, unlike the spinning-disc systems then being developed. He filed for a patent in 1927 and spent much of the 1930s in litigation with RCA, whose Vladimir Zworykin pursued a rival system. In a landmark 1935 ruling, the U.S. Patent Office awarded priority of invention to Farnsworth, and RCA eventually took a license—reportedly the first time it paid royalties to an outside inventor.

Later work

Farnsworth held some 300 patents, covering innovations in television, radar, night-vision devices, and the electron microscope. In his later years he worked on nuclear fusion with the Farnsworth–Hirsch fusor, a device for inertial electrostatic confinement still used as a neutron source. He died in 1971 feeling largely overlooked; recognition grew posthumously, including a statue in the U.S. Capitol representing Utah and induction into the National Inventors Hall of Fame.
