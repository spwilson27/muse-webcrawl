René Guénon

René Guénon (1886–1951), also known by his Muslim name ʿAbd al-Wāḥid Yaḥyá, was a French philosopher and metaphysician who founded the Traditionalist school (perennialism). His writings critique the modern world from the standpoint of what he regarded as primordial, universal metaphysical tradition.

Life

Born in Blois, Guénon moved in Parisian occultist circles as a young man before rejecting them as pseudo-spiritual. His Sorbonne thesis on Leibniz and the infinitesimal calculus was refused, but his Introduction to the Study of the Hindu Doctrines (1921) established his reputation. In 1930 he settled in Cairo, converted to Islam, and lived there until his death, writing in French for a European audience.

Thought

Key works include The Crisis of the Modern World (1927), which portrays modernity as the terminal, "dark age" (Kali Yuga) of a cosmic cycle, and The Reign of Quantity and the Signs of the Times (1945). Guénon championed Advaita Vedanta, Sufism, and medieval scholasticism as authentic forms of tradition, and sharply criticized Theosophy and spiritualism. His ideas influenced thinkers as diverse as Julius Evola, Mircea Eliade, and Seyyed Hossein Nasr, and continue to animate traditionalist movements.
