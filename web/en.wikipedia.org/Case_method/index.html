Case method

The case method is a teaching approach that uses decision-forcing cases — narrative accounts of real or realistic situations — to place students in the role of a protagonist who must decide what to do. Students prepare the case in advance, then analyse, debate, and defend courses of action in class discussion led by an instructor who questions rather than lectures. The method aims to develop analytical, decision-making, and communication skills and the capacity to act with incomplete information.

History

The method was pioneered at Harvard Law School by Christopher Columbus Langdell from the 1870s, where students induced legal principles from appellate cases. Harvard Business School adopted and reshaped it in the 1920s under Dean Wallace Donham, substituting business situations for legal precedents; the business case — a written description of an actual managerial problem, often based on field research in a company — became the school's signature pedagogy. Harvard's case collection, and later those of schools such as Ivey in Canada and INSEAD, supply cases used worldwide.

Application

Beyond law and business, case teaching is used in medicine, public policy, education, engineering ethics, and the social sciences. In a typical class, students first study the case individually, discuss it in small groups, and then participate in a plenary discussion in which the instructor uses questioning and the blackboard to structure competing analyses, often ending without a single "right" answer. Advocates praise the method's engagement and realism; critics note its cost, the age or cultural narrowness of some cases, uneven participation, and the risk that discussion rewards rhetoric over rigour.
