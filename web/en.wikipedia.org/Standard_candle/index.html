Standard candle

A standard candle is an astronomical object whose absolute luminosity (intrinsic brightness) is known, or can be inferred from observable properties, so that its distance can be calculated by comparing its apparent brightness with the inverse-square law of light. Standard candles are fundamental rungs of the cosmic distance ladder, allowing astronomers to measure distances from nearby stars out to cosmological scales.

Types

Cepheid variables were the first reliable standard candles: Henrietta Swan Leavitt discovered in 1908–1912 that a Cepheid's pulsation period correlates tightly with its luminosity. Edwin Hubble's use of Cepheids in the Andromeda Galaxy in the 1920s proved that "spiral nebulae" lie far beyond the Milky Way. RR Lyrae variables serve for older stellar populations within and around the Milky Way. The tip of the red-giant branch (TRGB) provides another distance indicator.

Type Ia supernovae

Type Ia supernovae — the thermonuclear explosion of a white dwarf — reach nearly uniform peak luminosities and can be "standardised" by the shape of their light curves (the Phillips relation). Because they are bright enough to be seen billions of light-years away, they were used by two teams in 1998 to discover the accelerating expansion of the Universe, attributed to dark energy — work recognised by the 2011 Nobel Prize in Physics.

Limitations

Measurements depend on correcting for interstellar dust extinction and on calibration of each rung of the distance ladder against the next; disagreements between early- and late-Universe measurements of the Hubble constant (the "Hubble tension") partly reflect these calibration challenges.
