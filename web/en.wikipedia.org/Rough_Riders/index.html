Rough Riders

The Rough Riders was the nickname given to the 1st United States Volunteer Cavalry, one of three regiments of volunteer cavalry raised for the Spanish–American War of 1898. Commanded by Colonel Leonard Wood, with Lieutenant Colonel Theodore Roosevelt as second in command, the regiment famously charged up Kettle Hill and San Juan Hill near Santiago de Cuba. The publicity surrounding the Rough Riders helped propel Roosevelt to the governorship of New York and ultimately the presidency.

Formation

The regiment was raised in the spring of 1898 from volunteers drawn largely from the American Southwest — cowboys, ranchers, miners, lawmen, and Native American scouts — supplemented by Ivy League athletes, polo players, and other Eastern volunteers attracted by Roosevelt. Because transport space was limited, the regiment left most of its horses in Florida and fought in Cuba largely as infantry.

Service in Cuba

The Rough Riders landed at Daiquirí on June 22, 1898, and fought their first action two days later at Las Guasimas. On July 1 they took part in the Battle of San Juan Hill, charging up the adjacent Kettle Hill under heavy fire, supported by Gatling guns, and then helping to capture the San Juan Heights. The regiment suffered heavy casualties — the highest rate of any US cavalry unit in the war — from Spanish fire and from disease, especially malaria and yellow fever. Roosevelt, who had resigned as Assistant Secretary of the Navy to serve, emerged as the war's most celebrated American hero.

Aftermath

After the surrender of Santiago, the regiment returned to the United States and was mustered out in September 1898. Roosevelt published his memoir The Rough Riders in 1899. In the 20th century the name was also applied to Theodore Roosevelt's proposed volunteer division in World War I, which the Wilson administration refused to authorise.

Legacy

The Rough Riders became an enduring part of American popular culture, celebrated in Buffalo Bill's Wild West shows, films, and the Medal of Honor awarded posthumously to Roosevelt in 2001 for his actions at San Juan Hill.
