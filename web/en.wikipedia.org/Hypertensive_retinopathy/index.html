Hypertensive retinopathy

Hypertensive retinopathy
FieldOphthalmology, cardiology
CauseChronic or acute hypertension
SignsArteriolar narrowing, AV nicking, flame haemorrhages, cotton-wool spots, papilledema (malignant phase)

Hypertensive retinopathy is damage to the retina and retinal circulation due to high blood pressure (hypertension). The retinal vessels are unique in that they can be viewed directly and non-invasively by ophthalmoscopy, so their changes provide a window on hypertensive damage elsewhere in the body. Most patients with early hypertensive retinopathy have no visual symptoms, but advanced disease can cause blurred vision and, in malignant hypertension, rapid visual loss.

Signs

Chronic hypertension produces generalised and focal arteriolar narrowing, arteriovenous nicking (where a stiffened arteriole compresses a venule at a crossing), and changes in the arteriolar light reflex described as "copper wire" and "silver wire" arteries. Breakdown of the blood–retinal barrier adds flame-shaped haemorrhages, cotton-wool spots (nerve-fibre-layer infarcts), hard exudates that may form a macular star, and retinal oedema. In malignant hypertension, swelling of the optic disc (papilledema) appears.

Classification

The Keith–Wagener–Barker classification (1939) grades severity from I (mild arteriolar narrowing) through II (more marked narrowing with AV nicking), III (haemorrhages, exudates, and cotton-wool spots), to IV (grade III changes plus optic disc swelling). A simplified scheme divides findings into "mild", "moderate", and "malignant" retinopathy.

Management

Treatment is control of blood pressure; moderate and malignant grades carry prognostic significance for cardiovascular events and warrant urgent assessment. Malignant retinopathy with papilledema is a hypertensive emergency requiring careful, controlled blood-pressure reduction.
