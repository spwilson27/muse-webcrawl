Schrödinger's cat

Schrödinger's cat is a thought experiment devised by Austrian physicist Erwin Schrödinger in 1935 to illustrate what he saw as the problem of the Copenhagen interpretation of quantum mechanics applied to everyday objects.

The scenario

A cat is sealed in a box with a flask of poison, a radioactive atom and a Geiger counter. If the atom decays, the counter triggers a hammer that breaks the flask and kills the cat; if it does not decay, the cat lives. According to the Copenhagen interpretation, until the box is opened the atom — and hence the cat — exists in a superposition of states: the cat is paradoxically both alive and dead, and only observation resolves the outcome.

Purpose and influence

Schrödinger intended the scenario not as a claim that cats can be alive and dead at once, but as a reductio ad absurdum highlighting the incompleteness of applying quantum superposition to macroscopic objects. The thought experiment remains central to debates about measurement, decoherence and the many-worlds interpretation, and "Schrödinger's cat" has entered popular culture as a byword for paradoxical superposition.
