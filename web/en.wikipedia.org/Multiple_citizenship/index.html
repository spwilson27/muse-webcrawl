Multiple citizenship

Multiple citizenship (or multiple nationality) is a person's citizenship status in which a person is concurrently regarded as a citizen of more than one country under the laws of those countries. The concept includes dual citizenship (two countries) and multiple citizenship held simultaneously by birth, descent, marriage, or naturalization. Whether a person may hold multiple citizenships depends entirely on the nationality laws of each state involved; a growing majority of countries now permit it in at least some circumstances.

Dual citizens may hold and travel on passports of two states.

How multiple citizenship arises

Multiple citizenship commonly arises automatically at birth: a child born in a country applying jus soli (citizenship by birthplace) to parents who are citizens of a country applying jus sanguinis (citizenship by descent) holds both from birth. It can also arise through naturalization where the original country does not require renunciation, through marriage, through restoration laws for descendants of emigrants (as in Ireland, Italy, and Poland), and through special schemes such as Spain's historical-memory laws. Some countries, including several Caribbean states and Malta, grant citizenship by investment.

Legal status worldwide

Historically, states discouraged multiple citizenship, fearing divided loyalty; the 1930 Hague Convention aimed to reduce it. Since the late twentieth century, tolerance has expanded sharply with globalization, labour mobility, and mixed-nationality marriages. Countries such as the United Kingdom, France, Canada, Australia, and the United States broadly permit it. Others restrict it: China and India (which instead offers the Overseas Citizenship of India status) do not recognize dual citizenship for their nationals, and Japan requires those with multiple citizenships acquired in childhood to choose one by early adulthood. Germany long restricted dual citizenship for non-EU naturalizations but liberalized its law in 2024.

Rights and obligations

Each state of nationality generally treats a multiple citizen exactly like any other citizen while they are within its jurisdiction, including obligations such as taxation, jury service, and, where applicable, military service — which historically caused conflicts when two states conscripted the same person. The "master nationality rule," codified in the 1930 Hague Convention, holds that a state may not extend diplomatic protection to its national against another state whose nationality that person also holds. Practical complications include exit and entry rules, security-clearance eligibility, and restrictions on holding certain public offices.
