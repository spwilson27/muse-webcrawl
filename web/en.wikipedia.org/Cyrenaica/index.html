Cyrenaica

Cyrenaica is the eastern coastal region of Libya, corresponding roughly to the area between the Gulf of Sidra and the Egyptian border. Its name derives from the ancient Greek colony of Cyrene, and its principal modern city is Benghazi.

Ancient history

Greek settlers from Thera founded Cyrene around 630 BC, and the region became a prosperous cluster of Greek cities known as the Pentapolis: Cyrene, Apollonia, Ptolemais, Tocra and Euhesperides (later Berenice, modern Benghazi). Cyrenaica was famed for the medicinal plant silphium, exported throughout the Mediterranean world until it became extinct. The region passed to the Ptolemaic kingdom and was bequeathed to Rome in 96 BC, becoming part of the province of Crete and Cyrenaica.

Later history

Cyrenaica came under Arab rule in the seventh century AD and later formed part of the Ottoman Empire. Italy seized it during the Italo-Turkish War of 1911–12 and ruled it as part of Italian Libya. During the Second World War the region was the theatre of the North Africa campaign, changing hands several times between Axis and Allied forces. After the war it was administered by Britain before joining the independent Kingdom of Libya in 1951.

Modern period

Cyrenaica was a centre of opposition to the Gaddafi government, and the Libyan civil war of 2011 began with protests in Benghazi. The region has remained politically distinct in Libya's subsequent conflicts, and movements advocating federalism or autonomy have drawn on its separate historical identity.
