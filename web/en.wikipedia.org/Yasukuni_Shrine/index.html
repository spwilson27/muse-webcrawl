Yasukuni Shrine

Yasukuni Jinja
Shinto shrine
Founded: 1869, by Emperor Meiji
Location: Chiyoda, Tokyo
Enshrined: c. 2,466,000 war dead

Yasukuni Shrine (Japanese: 靖國神社, Yasukuni Jinja, "Peaceful Country Shrine") is a Shinto shrine in central Tokyo founded by Emperor Meiji in 1869. It commemorates those who died in service of Japan, primarily soldiers, whose names, origins, and dates and places of death are recorded as kami (divinities) enshrined there.

History

Established as the Tokyo Shokonsha to honour loyalists who died in the Boshin War, the shrine was renamed Yasukuni in 1879. Its register grew with each of Japan's wars — the First Sino-Japanese War, the Russo-Japanese War, the First World War, and the Second World War — to about 2.47 million names. The shrine was administered by the military until 1946, when it became an independent religious institution under the postwar separation of religion and state.

Controversy

Yasukuni is a focus of diplomatic disputes with China and South Korea. In 1978, fourteen convicted Class-A war criminals from the Second World War — including wartime prime minister Hideki Tojo — were enshrined in a secret ceremony; their enshrinement became public in 1979. Visits by Japanese prime ministers and cabinet members regularly provoke protests abroad and constitutional debate at home over state sponsorship of religion. The shrine's on-site Yushukan war museum presents a revisionist-leaning narrative of Japan's wartime history.

Successive emperors since 1975 have not visited the shrine. Nearby stands the Chidorigafuchi National Cemetery, Japan's official state memorial for unidentified war dead.
