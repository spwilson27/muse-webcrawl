An apricot (US: /ˈæprɪkɒt/, UK: /ˈeɪprɪkɒt/) is a fruit, or the tree that bears the fruit, of several species in the genus Prunus. Usually, an apricot is from the species Prunus armeniaca, but the fruits of other species in the section Armeniaca are also called apricots.

Description

The apricot is a small tree, 8–12 m (26–39 ft) tall, with a trunk up to 40 cm (16 in) in diameter and a dense, spreading canopy. The leaves are ovate, 5–9 cm (2.0–3.5 in) long and 4–8 cm (1.6–3.1 in) wide, with a rounded base, a pointed tip and a finely serrated margin. The flowers are 2–4.5 cm (0.8–1.8 in) in diameter, with five white to pinkish petals; they are produced singly or in pairs in early spring before the leaves.

The fruit is a drupe (stone fruit) similar to a small peach, 1.5–2.5 cm (0.6–1.0 in) in diameter (larger in modern domesticated varieties), the colour ranging from yellow to orange, often tinged red on the side most exposed to the sun. The surface can be smooth (botanically described as glabrous) or velvety with very short hairs (pubescent). The flesh is usually firm and not very juicy. The taste can range from sweet to tart.

History and cultivation

The apricot was domesticated in Central Asia and China, and has been cultivated for thousands of years. Despite its botanical name armeniaca (referring to Armenia), the fruit likely originated further east; Armenia was an important centre of its cultivation and spread westward. Apricots have been cultivated in Persia since antiquity, and dried apricots were an important commodity on Persian trade routes.

World production is led by Turkey, which accounts for a large share of both fresh and dried apricots, followed by countries such as Uzbekistan, Iran, Italy and Algeria.

Uses

Apricots are eaten fresh or dried, and are used in jams, juices, and desserts. The kernels (seeds) inside the stone contain amygdalin and are used to produce apricot kernel oil; sweet varieties of kernel are eaten like almonds. In Middle Eastern and Mediterranean cuisines, apricots feature in dishes such as qamar al-din (apricot leather) and various stews and sweets.
