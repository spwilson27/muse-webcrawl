Ebonics

Ebonics
Coined: 1973, Robert Williams
Refers to: African-American Vernacular English and related varieties
Blend of: "ebony" + "phonics"

Ebonics is a term coined in 1973 by psychologist Robert Williams in discussions with other scholars, formed as a blend of "ebony" and "phonics". It originally denoted the language of Black people of African descent, particularly the speech of descendants of enslaved Africans in the Americas, studied as a distinct linguistic system with African roots.

Usage and controversy

In popular usage, Ebonics came to be used largely as a synonym for African-American Vernacular English (AAVE). The term achieved wide public attention in December 1996, when the Oakland Unified School District in California passed a resolution recognising Ebonics as a language system of African-American students and proposing to use it as a bridge to teaching Standard English. The resolution provoked a national debate over language, race and education.

Linguistics

Mainstream linguists regard AAVE as a systematic, rule-governed dialect (or set of varieties) of English, with distinctive phonology, grammar and vocabulary, including features such as habitual "be" and consonant cluster reduction. Many linguists prefer the term AAVE over Ebonics because of the latter's popular and sometimes pejorative connotations.
