Secret police (or political police) are intelligence and police organizations that engage in covert operations against a government's political opponents and dissidents. Unlike ordinary police, whose activities are public and bound by judicial process, secret police operate largely outside public scrutiny, and their existence is characteristic of authoritarian and totalitarian regimes, though democracies have also maintained political-policing units.

Characteristics

Secret police typically combine the powers of investigation, arrest, detention, and interrogation without effective judicial oversight. They rely on extensive networks of informants, censorship, surveillance of communications, and the threat — or use — of imprisonment, torture, and extrajudicial punishment. Their purpose is less to solve crime than to detect and neutralize opposition, real or perceived, and to create a climate of fear that discourages dissent.

History

Precursors include the tsarist Okhrana in Imperial Russia. The twentieth century produced the most notorious examples: Nazi Germany's Gestapo, the Soviet Union's successive agencies (Cheka, NKVD, and KGB), East Germany's Stasi — which by 1989 employed or enlisted an informant in a remarkable fraction of the population — Imperial Japan's Kempeitai, and Iran's SAVAK under the Shah. In several post-authoritarian states, opening secret police archives became a central and painful part of transitional justice.

Criticism

Secret police are widely associated with human rights abuses, including arbitrary detention, torture, enforced disappearance, and suppression of free expression. The term is often used pejoratively in political debate to accuse governments of similar conduct. See also police state and surveillance.
