Focal adhesion kinase

Focal adhesion kinase (FAK), also known as protein tyrosine kinase 2 (PTK2), is a non-receptor tyrosine kinase encoded in humans by the PTK2 gene. FAK is concentrated at focal adhesions — the sites where cells attach to the extracellular matrix through integrin receptors — and transduces adhesion-dependent signals that regulate cell migration, proliferation, survival and spreading.

Structure and activation

FAK has a central kinase domain flanked by an N-terminal FERM domain, which mediates interactions with integrin tails and growth factor receptors, and a C-terminal domain containing the focal adhesion targeting (FAT) sequence that binds paxillin and talin. When integrins engage the extracellular matrix, FAK autophosphorylates at tyrosine 397, creating a binding site for Src-family kinases. The FAK–Src complex phosphorylates further substrates — including paxillin, p130Cas and components of the ERK/MAPK pathway — coupling adhesion to cytoskeletal reorganisation and gene expression.

Functions and disease

FAK signalling promotes cell migration and protects cells from anoikis, the apoptosis triggered by loss of attachment. FAK is overexpressed and hyperactivated in many cancers, where it contributes to invasion and metastasis; small-molecule FAK inhibitors such as defactinib have entered clinical trials. Embryonic deletion of FAK in mice is lethal, with severe defects in mesoderm development and the vasculature.
