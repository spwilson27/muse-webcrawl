Tarantism

Tarantism is a form of hysterical behaviour once popular in southern Italy, especially in Apulia, associated with the bite of a spider known locally as the taranta. Sufferers, often called tarantati, were believed to be cured only by frenzied dancing accompanied by music, a practice that gave rise to the tarantella folk dance tradition.

History

The phenomenon is documented from the Middle Ages through the 20th century. The affliction was traditionally linked to the bite of a wolf spider (Lycosa tarantula) or, by some accounts, the Mediterranean widow spider. Symptoms included melancholy, lethargy, and compulsive dancing that could last for days during the summer harvest season.

Music and cure

Musicians were summoned to play the pizzica, a rapid 6/8 rhythm, switching melodies until a rhythm matching the patient's "taranta" was found, at which point the sufferer would dance exhaustively. The ritual was often centred on the chapel of Saint Paul in Galatina, whose feast day falls on 29 June. The ethnomusicologist Ernesto de Martino studied tarantism in his 1959 expedition, published as The Land of Remorse (1961).

Interpretation

Modern scholars generally interpret tarantism as a culturally conditioned psychological or social phenomenon rather than a direct effect of spider venom, functioning as a ritual outlet for distress within a peasant society.
