The Prose Edda, also known as the Younger Edda, Snorri's Edda (Icelandic: Snorra Edda) or, historically, simply as Edda, is an Old Norse textbook written in Iceland during the early 13th century. The work is often considered to have been to some extent written, or at least compiled, by the Icelandic scholar, lawspeaker, and historian Snorri Sturluson c. 1220. It is considered the fullest and most detailed source for modern knowledge of Norse mythology, the body of myths of the North Germanic peoples, and draws from a wide variety of sources, including versions of poems that survive into today in a collection known as the Poetic Edda.

The Prose Edda consists of four sections: The Prologue, a euhemerized account of the Norse gods; Gylfaginning, which provides a question and answer format that details aspects of Norse mythology (consisting of approximately 20,000 words); Skáldskaparmál, which continues this format before providing lists of kennings and heiti (approximately 50,000 words); and Háttatal, which discusses the composition of traditional skaldic poetry (approximately 20,000 words).

Contents and structure

Prologue

The Prologue is a euhemeristic account of the origins of Norse mythology: the Norse gods are described as human Trojan warriors who left Troy after the fall of that city (an origin which parallels Virgil's Aeneid).

Gylfaginning

Gylfaginning ("The Beguiling of Gylfi") deals with the creation and destruction of the world of the Norse gods, and many other aspects of Norse mythology. The section is written in prose interspersed with quotes from eddic poetry. Gylfi, a king of Sweden, journeys in disguise to Asgard and is questioned by the figures Hár, Jafnhár, and Thridi about the gods, the creation of the world, and its eventual destruction in Ragnarök.

Skáldskaparmál

Skáldskaparmál ("The Language of Poetry") is a dialogue between Ægir, a sea deity, and Bragi, the god of poetry, in which both Norse mythology and discourse on the nature of poetry are intertwined. The origins of a number of kennings are given and Bragi then delivers a systematic list of kennings for various people, places, and things.

Háttatal

Háttatal ("List of Verse Forms") is the last section. The section is composed by Snorri himself and demonstrates the types of verse forms used in Old Norse poetry, largely through a long praise poem of 102 stanzas.

Manuscripts

Four main manuscripts of the Edda survive into the present day. These manuscripts vary to some degree, and individual additions, possibly from scribes, appear in some. The texts form the basis of modern editions of the work.

Influence

The Prose Edda, together with the Poetic Edda, forms the backbone of modern popular understanding of Norse mythology, and has influenced writers such as J. R. R. Tolkien.
