Harry Houdini (born Erik Weisz; March 24, 1874 – October 31, 1926) was a Hungarian-American escape artist, illusionist, and stunt performer, noted for his escape acts. His pseudonym is a reference to his spiritual master, French magician Robert-Houdin (1805–1871).

Career

Weisz immigrated with his family to the United States in 1878, settling first in Appleton, Wisconsin, and then New York. After years of struggle in dime museums and circuses, he broke through in 1899 when manager Martin Beck booked him on the Orpheum vaudeville circuit with an act centred on handcuff escapes, including challenge escapes issued to local police. Tours of Europe made him an international star.

Famous escapes

Houdini's signature feats included the Chinese Water Torture Cell — in which he was suspended upside down in a locked glass-and-steel tank of water — escapes from straitjackets while hanging from buildings, the Milk Can Escape, escapes from nailed packing crates submerged in rivers, and a 1906 escape from the death cell of convicted assassin Charles Guiteau in Washington, D.C. In 1912 he was lowered, manacled, into New York's East River in a crate. He also made films, founded a film company, and in 1910 became the first person to fly a plane in Australia.

Skepticism and death

In the 1920s Houdini campaigned against fraudulent spirit mediums, exposing their methods and testifying before Congress in support of anti-fortune-telling legislation; his feud with Arthur Conan Doyle, a believer in spiritualism, ended their friendship. Houdini died on Halloween 1926 of peritonitis following a ruptured appendix; a famous but disputed story holds that a student, J. Gordon Whitehead, had days earlier punched him in the abdomen in a Montreal dressing room after Houdini said he could withstand any blow — the precise causal role of the punches remains debated.
