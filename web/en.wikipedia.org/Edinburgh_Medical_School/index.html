Edinburgh Medical School

The Edinburgh Medical School, now the College of Medicine and Veterinary Medicine of the University of Edinburgh, is one of the oldest and most distinguished medical schools in the English-speaking world. Founded in 1726, it was the pre-eminent centre of medical education in the 18th and 19th centuries and remains among the world's top-ranked medical schools.

The Old Medical School building at Teviot Place

History

Medical teaching in Edinburgh began with the incorporation of the surgeons and barbers in 1505, but the Faculty of Medicine was formally established in 1726, modelled on the University of Leiden. Under figures such as Alexander Monro and William Cullen, Edinburgh's systematic lectures in anatomy, chemistry, and clinical medicine attracted students from across Britain, its colonies, and America. Alumni and staff include Joseph Lister, pioneer of antiseptic surgery; James Young Simpson, who introduced chloroform anaesthesia; Charles Darwin briefly studied medicine here; and Arthur Conan Doyle, whose teacher Joseph Bell became a model for Sherlock Holmes.

The Edinburgh Seven

In 1869 Sophia Jex-Blake and six others became the first women to matriculate at a British university. Their studies were obstructed — including in the Surgeons' Hall riot of 1870 — and they were ultimately denied degrees, but their campaign led to legislation enabling women to qualify as doctors; the university finally awarded them honorary degrees in 2019.

Modern school

Teaching is centred on the Chancellor's Building at the Edinburgh BioQuarter beside the Royal Infirmary at Little France, with research strengths in regenerative medicine, genetics, inflammation, and neuroscience. The school admits roughly 250 medical students a year to its six-year MBChB programme.
