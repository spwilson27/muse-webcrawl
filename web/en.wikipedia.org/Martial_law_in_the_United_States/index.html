Martial law in the United States

Martial law in the United States refers to times in American history when a region has been placed under direct military control, with civilian authority and ordinary legal processes suspended or subordinated, usually during war, invasion, rebellion, or major civil disorder.

Legal basis

The U.S. Constitution does not expressly define martial law, but the Suspension Clause permits suspension of habeas corpus "when in Cases of Rebellion or Invasion the public Safety may require it." Landmark Supreme Court decisions include Ex parte Milligan (1866), which held that civilians could not be tried by military commissions where the civilian courts were open and functioning, and Duncan v. Kahanamoku (1946), which struck down military trials in Hawaii during World War II.

Historical instances

Notable declarations include New Orleans under Andrew Jackson during the War of 1812; widespread use during the Civil War under President Abraham Lincoln, including suspension of habeas corpus; Hawaii after the attack on Pearl Harbor (1941–1944); and various state-level declarations by governors during labor unrest and natural disasters. At the federal level, the Posse Comitatus Act of 1878 generally restricts use of federal troops for domestic law enforcement, while the Insurrection Act provides exceptions.

Modern usage

Full martial law has not been declared nationally in the modern era; domestic emergencies are usually handled through emergency declarations, the National Guard under state control, and federal disaster law rather than military rule.
