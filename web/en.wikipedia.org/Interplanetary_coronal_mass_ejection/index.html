Interplanetary coronal mass ejection
AbbreviationICME
OriginCoronal mass ejection from the Sun
Typical transit time to Earth1–4 days
EffectsGeomagnetic storms, aurorae, satellite disruption

An interplanetary coronal mass ejection (ICME) is the manifestation in interplanetary space of a coronal mass ejection (CME) — a huge bubble of plasma and magnetic field expelled from the Sun's corona into the solar wind. Once a CME leaves the solar corona and propagates outward through the heliosphere, spacecraft detect it in situ as an ICME.

Properties

ICMEs are identified in solar-wind data by signatures such as enhanced magnetic field strength, smooth rotation of the field direction (in "magnetic cloud" events), depressed proton temperatures, low plasma beta, bidirectional suprathermal electron flows and anomalous ion charge states and composition reflecting their coronal origin. A fast ICME drives an interplanetary shock ahead of it, often followed by a turbulent sheath region before the magnetic ejecta itself arrives.

Effects on Earth

When an ICME reaches Earth, its interaction with the magnetosphere — especially when its magnetic field points southward, opposite to Earth's — can trigger geomagnetic storms. Consequences range from enhanced aurorae at unusually low latitudes to satellite anomalies, radio blackouts, errors in navigation signals and geomagnetically induced currents in power grids. The extreme storm of 1859 (the Carrington Event) is attributed to an exceptionally fast ICME; a comparably powerful event in July 2012 narrowly missed Earth.

Observation

ICMEs are monitored by coronagraphs such as LASCO aboard SOHO, by heliospheric imagers, and by in-situ monitors at the Sun–Earth L1 point (ACE, DSCOVR), which provide 15–60 minutes of warning of arrival conditions. Their study is a central part of space weather forecasting.
