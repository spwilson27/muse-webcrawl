Registered partnership

A registered partnership is a form of civil union recognised by law that grants two people — historically, most often same-sex couples who were excluded from marriage — a legal status similar to marriage, with many of the same rights, responsibilities, and protections. Known by various national names, such as the French pacte civil de solidarité (PACS), the German Lebenspartnerschaft, and civil partnerships in the United Kingdom, these statuses typically cover matters such as property, inheritance, taxation, social security, immigration, and next-of-kin rights, though they may differ from marriage in adoption rights, names, ceremony, and how they are dissolved.

History

Denmark introduced the world's first registered partnership law for same-sex couples in 1989, and the model spread across the Nordic countries and western Europe in the 1990s and 2000s: Norway (1993), Sweden (1995), Iceland and the Netherlands (1998), France's PACS (1999, open also to opposite-sex couples), Germany (2001), and the United Kingdom (2005). In many jurisdictions registered partnership served as a stepping stone to full marriage equality; when marriage was opened to same-sex couples, some countries closed their partnership registers to new entrants (as in Scandinavia and Germany), while others, such as France and the UK, retained or extended them as an alternative status — the UK opened civil partnership to opposite-sex couples in 2019.

Status today

Registered partnerships remain available in a number of countries, in some (such as Hungary, Croatia, Greece, Italy, and several others) as the principal or only form of recognition for same-sex couples. Mutual recognition between countries is inconsistent, so a partnership valid in one state may have no legal effect in another.
