The Concerto for Orchestra, Sz. 116, BB 123, is a five-movement orchestral work composed by Béla Bartók in 1943. It is one of Bartók's best-known and most popular works, commissioned by the Koussevitzky Foundation while the composer, exiled from Hungary and in poor health, was living in the United States. Bartók wrote that the title derives from the work's tendency to treat individual orchestral instruments in a concertant, soloistic manner.

Movements

The work is structured in an arch form around a central slow movement. The first movement, Introduzione, opens with a mysterious introduction before an energetic Allegro vivace built on folk-like themes. The second, Giuoco delle coppie ("Game of Pairs"), presents pairs of instruments in turn, each pair playing in a characteristic interval. The third, Elegia, is a songful lament in Bartók's "night music" style. The fourth, Intermezzo interrotto, is interrupted by a crude, mocking tune often read as a parody of Shostakovich's Seventh Symphony. The Finale is a whirling, folk-dance-inspired movement of great virtuosity.

Premiere and reception

The concerto was premiered on 1 December 1944 by the Boston Symphony Orchestra conducted by Serge Koussevitzky, who reportedly called it "the best orchestral piece of the last 25 years." Its success brought Bartók renewed recognition and income in the final year of his life; he revised the ending in 1945. The work has since entered the standard orchestral repertoire worldwide.
