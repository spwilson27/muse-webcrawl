Octavo, a Latin word meaning "in eighth", is a technical term describing the format of a book made by folding a full sheet of paper three times to form eight leaves (sixteen pages). Abbreviated 8vo, it became the most common size for ordinary books from the sixteenth century onward.

Format and sizes

Book formats are defined by how many times the original sheet is folded: a folio is folded once, a quarto twice, and an octavo three times. The actual dimensions of an octavo therefore depend on the size of the sheet used; common historical sizes include crown octavo (about 7.5 × 5 in) and demy octavo (about 8.75 × 5.6 in), while modern octavos are typically around 9 × 6 in or smaller. In contemporary publishing "octavo" loosely denotes books of roughly this size regardless of folding.

History

The Venetian printer Aldus Manutius popularised the octavo around 1501 with his portable editions of classical texts, which made books cheaper and more convenient than the large folios and quartos of scholarly libraries. The format spread throughout Europe and became the standard for novels, poetry and general literature. In bibliographic description, format notation (folio, 4to, 8vo, 12mo) remains essential for identifying editions of older books.
