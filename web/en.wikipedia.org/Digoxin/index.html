Digoxin

Digoxin (better known as digitalis in earlier use) is a medication used to treat various heart conditions, most frequently atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter, and heart failure. Digoxin is one of the oldest medications used in cardiology, having been derived from the foxglove plant (Digitalis lanata). It is a purified cardiac glycoside and is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines.

Medical uses

Digoxin helps make the heartbeat stronger and more regular by increasing the force of contraction of heart muscle and slowing conduction through the atrioventricular node. It is used to control ventricular rate in atrial fibrillation and, less commonly than in the past, as an adjunct in symptomatic heart failure. Use declined after the 1997 DIG trial found no reduction in overall mortality, though hospitalisations for heart failure were reduced.

Mechanism of action

Digoxin inhibits the sodium–potassium ATPase pump in cardiac cell membranes. The resulting rise in intracellular sodium reduces sodium–calcium exchange, increasing intracellular calcium available for contraction. Increased vagal tone slows the heart rate and AV nodal conduction.

Adverse effects and toxicity

Digoxin has a narrow therapeutic index. Toxicity produces nausea, vomiting, visual disturbances (such as yellow-tinted vision), confusion and potentially fatal arrhythmias; risk rises with kidney impairment, low potassium or magnesium, and interacting drugs. Severe toxicity is treated with digoxin-specific antibody fragments (digoxin immune fab). Serum levels and electrolytes are monitored during therapy.

History

William Withering published his account of foxglove in the treatment of dropsy in 1785. The active glycosides were characterised in the 19th and 20th centuries, and digoxin was isolated from Digitalis lanata in 1930 by Sydney Smith at Burroughs Wellcome.
