Dacians

The Dacians were an Indo-European people, part of or related to the Thracians, who inhabited the region of Dacia, located in the area near the Carpathian Mountains and west of the Black Sea, corresponding broadly to modern Romania and Moldova.

History

The Dacians are first mentioned in ancient sources in connection with the kingdom that reached its height under King Burebista in the 1st century BC, who united the Dacian and Getic tribes and threatened Rome's Danubian frontier. Under Decebalus, Dacia fought two wars with the Roman Empire; Emperor Trajan's campaigns of AD 101–102 and 105–106 ended in Dacia's conquest and its incorporation as a Roman province. The campaigns are commemorated by Trajan's Column in Rome.

Culture and religion

The Dacians spoke the Dacian language, of which only fragmentary evidence survives. Their religion centred on the god Zalmoxis, described by Herodotus in connection with the Getae. Dacian settlements included fortified hilltop centres (davae), and their capital, Sarmizegetusa Regia, in the Orăștie Mountains, is now a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Legacy

The Dacians, together with the Roman colonisation of the province, occupy a central place in Romanian national historiography as ancestors of the Romanian people, a thesis known as Daco-Roman continuity.
