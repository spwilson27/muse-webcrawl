Coandă effect

The Coandă effect is the tendency of a fluid jet to stay attached to a convex surface. It is named after the Romanian aerodynamics pioneer Henri Coandă, who recognised the phenomenon while testing his Coandă-1910 aircraft: exhaust gases from its engine clung to the fuselage sides and set the fabric covering alight. Coandă patented designs exploiting the effect in 1932, describing how a fluid emerging from a slot would entrain the surrounding air and follow a curved surface placed nearby.

Explanation

As a jet issues into still air it entrains fluid from its surroundings. When a convex surface blocks entrainment on one side, the pressure on that side falls, and the resulting pressure gradient curves the jet toward the surface, where viscosity and the balance between centrifugal force and the pressure gradient hold it in an attached layer until separation eventually occurs. The same attachment explains familiar demonstrations such as the stream of water from a tap bending around the back of a spoon.

Applications

The effect underlies blown flaps and upper-surface blowing on STOL aircraft, where engine exhaust or bleed air is blown over curved flaps to augment lift; the Antonov An-72 and the Boeing YC-14 exploited this arrangement. The NOTAR helicopter tail-boom system uses the Coandă effect to generate anti-torque force with air blown through slots along a rotating boom. Other applications include fluidic devices (fluid amplifiers with no moving parts), air-conditioning diffusers that throw air along ceilings, pneumatic conveyors, and bladeless fans that use entrainment around an airfoil-shaped ring.

Limitations

Attachment fails when the surface curvature is too sharp, the jet velocity too low, or the surface ends abruptly, causing the flow to separate and reattach unpredictably. Designers must also account for noise, losses from skin friction, and the sensitivity of the effect to contamination and surface roughness.
