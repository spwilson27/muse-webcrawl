Hartman effect

The Hartman effect is a phenomenon in quantum mechanics in which the tunnelling time of a particle through a potential barrier becomes independent of the barrier's thickness once the barrier is sufficiently wide. It was described by Thomas E. Hartman in 1962.

Hartman effect
Field: Quantum mechanics
Described by: Thomas E. Hartman (1962)
Concerns: Quantum tunnelling times

Description

Calculating the phase time for a wave packet traversing a rectangular barrier, Hartman found that for opaque (thick) barriers the delay saturates rather than growing with width. Formally dividing the barrier width by this saturated delay yields an effective velocity that can exceed the speed of light for sufficiently thick barriers.

Interpretation

This apparent superluminality does not permit faster-than-light signalling: the transmitted pulse is strongly attenuated and reshaped from the leading edge of the incident wave packet, so no information travels faster than light. The Hartman effect remains an active topic in discussions of tunnelling-time definitions in quantum mechanics.
