Cross-link

In chemistry and biology, a cross-link is a bond or short sequence of bonds that links one polymer chain to another. Cross-links may be covalent or ionic. They can form during polymer synthesis or be introduced afterwards by heat, radiation, or chemical agents. Cross-linking dramatically changes a material's properties: lightly cross-linked polymers become elastic and swell rather than dissolve, while heavily cross-linked polymers form hard, infusible thermoset resins.

Examples

The most famous example is the vulcanisation of rubber, in which sulfur forms bridges between polyisoprene chains, converting a soft, sticky material into a durable elastomer. In biology, disulfide bonds cross-link protein chains, as in insulin and in keratin of hair and wool; collagen is stabilised by enzymatic cross-links, and cross-linking also occurs between DNA strands, a mechanism exploited by some chemotherapy drugs such as cisplatin.

Applications

Cross-linking is used in hydrogels for medicine and agriculture, in adhesives and coatings, in ion-exchange resins, and in fixing tissue samples for microscopy with agents such as formaldehyde. The degree of cross-linking — often expressed as cross-link density — is a key design parameter in polymer engineering.
