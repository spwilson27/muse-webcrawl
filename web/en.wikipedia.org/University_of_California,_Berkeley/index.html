The University of California, Berkeley (UC Berkeley, Berkeley, Cal, or California) is a public land-grant research university in Berkeley, California, United States. Founded in 1868, it is the state's first land-grant university and the founding campus of the University of California system. It is widely regarded as one of the top public universities in the world.

History

Berkeley was established in 1868 through the merger of the private College of California and the public Agricultural, Mining, and Mechanical Arts College. In the 20th century it became a powerhouse of scientific research: Ernest Lawrence invented the cyclotron there and founded the Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory; scientists at Berkeley and its labs discovered or synthesized 16 chemical elements, more than any other university. During the Second World War its Radiation Laboratory played a central role in the Manhattan Project.

Academics

The university comprises some 15 schools and colleges, including the Haas School of Business, the College of Engineering, and Berkeley Law. Its faculty and alumni have won numerous Nobel Prizes, Turing Awards, and Fields Medals. In the 1960s the campus was the epicentre of the Free Speech Movement, which reshaped student rights on American campuses.

Campus and athletics

The 1,232-acre campus overlooks San Francisco Bay and is anchored by the 307-foot Sather Tower (the Campanile). Berkeley's athletic teams, the California Golden Bears, compete in NCAA Division I, and the university's rivalry with Stanford — contested in the annual "Big Game" — is among the oldest in college football.
