Redshift survey

A redshift survey maps galaxies in three dimensions, revealing walls, clusters and voids in the large-scale structure of the universe.

In astronomy, a redshift survey is a survey of a section of the sky to measure the redshift of astronomical objects, usually galaxies, but sometimes other objects such as galaxy clusters or quasars. Because redshift is proportional to distance for objects whose light has been stretched by the expansion of the universe (via Hubble's law), a redshift survey converts the two-dimensional positions of galaxies on the sky into a three-dimensional map of large-scale structure.

Method

Surveys use multi-object spectrographs on large telescopes to record the spectra of hundreds or thousands of galaxies at once, measuring redshifts from emission and absorption lines. Photometric redshift surveys estimate redshifts from broad-band colours alone and can cover far more objects, though with lower precision per galaxy. The resulting catalogues are analysed statistically — through correlation functions and power spectra — to measure the growth of structure, the cosmic distance scale via baryon acoustic oscillations, and the effects of dark matter and dark energy.

History

The first large redshift surveys, carried out at the Center for Astrophysics in the 1980s, dramatically revealed that galaxies are not distributed randomly but form a foam-like network of filaments, sheets and clusters surrounding large voids. The CfA survey's maps exposed the "Great Wall", one of the largest known structures. Later surveys expanded enormously in scale: the 2dF Galaxy Redshift Survey catalogued some 220,000 galaxies, and the Sloan Digital Sky Survey measured millions of galaxies and quasars, transforming observational cosmology.

Major surveys

Prominent redshift surveys include the CfA Redshift Survey, the 2dF Galaxy Redshift Survey, the Sloan Digital Sky Survey, the 6dF Galaxy Survey, the Baryon Oscillation Spectroscopic Survey (BOSS), and the ongoing Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) survey, which aims to measure tens of millions of redshifts. Future facilities such as Euclid and the Vera C. Rubin Observatory will extend such mapping to billions of objects.
