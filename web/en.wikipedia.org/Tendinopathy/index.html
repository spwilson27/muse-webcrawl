Tendinopathy is a clinical syndrome of pain and dysfunction in a tendon, usually arising from overuse and a failed healing response rather than primary inflammation. The term replaced the older label "tendinitis" because biopsies of painful tendons typically show degeneration and disorganised collagen (tendinosis) with little inflammatory infiltrate. Tendinopathy is common in athletes and in middle-aged recreational exercisers; classic examples include Achilles tendinopathy in runners, patellar tendinopathy ("jumper's knee"), lateral elbow tendinopathy ("tennis elbow"), and rotator cuff tendinopathy.

Pathophysiology

Healthy tendon is mostly orderly type I collagen. In tendinopathy the collagen becomes disorganised, ground substance and small blood vessels and nerves proliferate, and the tendon thickens; tenocytes change shape and number. A widely used model describes a continuum from reactive tendinopathy (a reversible response to acute overload), through tendon disrepair, to degenerative tendinopathy, in which parts of the tendon have little capacity to recover.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is mainly clinical — pain localised to the tendon that worsens with loading — supported when needed by ultrasound or MRI. The cornerstone of management is progressive, heavy loading exercise (eccentric protocols pioneered by Alfredson for the Achilles, or heavy slow resistance training) together with modification of aggravating loads. Rest alone is discouraged. Other treatments — NSAIDs, corticosteroid injections (short-term relief but worse long-term outcomes), platelet-rich plasma, shockwave therapy, and surgery for refractory cases — have mixed or limited evidence. Fluoroquinolone antibiotics, diabetes, and lipid disorders are among the systemic risk factors.
