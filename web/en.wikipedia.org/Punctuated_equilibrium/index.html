Punctuated equilibrium

Proposed by: Niles Eldredge and Stephen Jay Gould (1972)
Claim: Species remain in stasis for most of their history; significant evolutionary change is concentrated in brief speciation events.

In evolutionary biology, punctuated equilibrium (also called punctuated equilibria) is a theory that proposes that once a species appears in the fossil record, its population will become stable, showing little evolutionary change for most of its geological history — a state called stasis. Significant evolutionary change, the theory holds, is typically confined to rare and geologically rapid events of branching speciation, called cladogenesis.

History

The theory was proposed in 1972 by paleontologists Niles Eldredge and Stephen Jay Gould, drawing on Eldredge's work on trilobites and on Ernst Mayr's model of allopatric and peripatric speciation, in which new species arise in small, isolated peripheral populations. Eldredge and Gould argued that the pattern predicted by this model — long intervals of stasis punctuated by sudden appearances of new species — matches the fossil record, rather than being merely an artefact of its incompleteness.

Debate and reception

Punctuated equilibrium is often contrasted with phyletic gradualism, the idea of slow, steady transformation within lineages. The theory provoked vigorous debate among evolutionary biologists; critics argued that stasis was already recognised or that the theory overstated the rarity of gradual change. Empirical studies have since documented both stasis and gradual change across many groups, and the concept has become widely (though not universally) accepted as describing a common tempo of evolution, while remaining complementary to standard population genetics.
