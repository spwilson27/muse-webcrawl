The giant squid (Architeuthis dux) is a species of deep-ocean dwelling squid in the family Architeuthidae. It can grow to a tremendous size, offering an example of abyssal gigantism; recent estimates put the maximum size at around 12–13 m (39–43 ft) for females and 10 m (33 ft) for males, from the posterior fins to the tip of the two long feeding tentacles — longer than the colossal squid, though the latter is heavier. The mantle is about 2 m (6.6 ft) long, and the eyes, at up to 27 cm across, are the largest known in the animal kingdom.

Distribution and habitat

Giant squid are distributed in all of the world's oceans, usually at depths of 300 to 1,000 metres, and are absent from polar waters and shallow tropical seas. Specimens have most often been recorded from the North Atlantic (notably Newfoundland, Norway, and northern Spain), South Africa, New Zealand, and Japan. Knowledge of the animal long came only from carcasses washed ashore, from beaks recovered from the stomachs of sperm whales — its main predator — and from rare encounters by fishermen.

Observation in the wild

For centuries the giant squid was known mainly through legend — it is widely thought to be the basis of the Norwegian kraken — and the species was formally described by Japetus Steenstrup in 1857 from stranded material. A living animal was first photographed in its natural habitat on 30 September 2004, when Japanese researchers Tsunemi Kubodera and Kyoichi Mori captured a series of images of a squid attacking baited lines at a depth of 900 m near the Ogasawara Islands. Kubodera filmed a live giant squid for the first time in 2006, and in July 2012 a team from Japan's National Museum of Nature and Science filmed one in its deep-water habitat, footage broadcast worldwide in 2013.

Biology

Like other squid, Architeuthis has eight arms and two longer feeding tentacles lined with powerful suckers ringed in sharp chitin. It propels itself by jet propulsion and by its fins, and its blood contains haemocyanin rather than haemoglobin. Genetic studies suggest that all giant squid worldwide belong to a single species, A. dux, despite the many names historically proposed. Very little is known of its reproduction and early life: tiny juveniles are occasionally found in plankton samples, and growth appears to be extremely rapid, with a lifespan of perhaps only about five years.
