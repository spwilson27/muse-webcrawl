Fig

The fig is the edible fruit of Ficus carica, a species of small tree or large shrub in the flowering plant family Moraceae, native to the Mediterranean region and western Asia. Figs have been cultivated since ancient times and are now grown throughout the world both for their fruit and as ornamental plants. The fruit is a hollow, fleshy structure called a syconium, lined on the inside with numerous tiny unisexual flowers and, when ripe, filled with small seed-like achenes.

History

Figs are among the earliest plants ever cultivated by humans; subfossil figs dated to around 9400–9200 BCE have been found in the Jordan Valley, predating the domestication of wheat and barley. The fig appears frequently in ancient literature and scripture: it is the first specific plant mentioned in the Book of Genesis, and the Buddha is said to have attained enlightenment under a related fig species, Ficus religiosa.

Cultivation and pollination

Common figs do not require pollination to set fruit, but the Smyrna type requires pollination by the fig wasp Blastophaga psenes, which lives in association with the inedible caprifig — a process called caprification. Major producing countries include Turkey, Egypt, Morocco, Iran and Algeria. Figs are eaten fresh or dried; drying concentrates their sugars, making them an important preserved food since antiquity.

Nutrition

Fresh figs are a good source of dietary fiber and contain potassium, calcium, magnesium and antioxidants. Dried figs are energy-dense and rich in fiber and minerals.
