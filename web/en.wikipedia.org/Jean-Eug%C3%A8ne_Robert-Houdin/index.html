Jean-Eugène Robert-Houdin (1805–1871) was a French watchmaker, inventor and magician widely considered the father of modern stage magic. Trained as a clockmaker, he applied mechanical ingenuity to conjuring, opening his Théâtre Robert-Houdin in Paris in 1845, where he performed in evening dress rather than wizard's robes and presented automata-based illusions.

Famous illusions

His celebrated effects included the Ethereal Suspension (in which his son appeared to float, supposedly rendered weightless by ether), the Orange Tree automaton that blossomed and bore fruit, and the "Second Sight" mind-reading act with his son. In 1856 Napoleon III sent him to Algeria to out-perform the marabout holy men whose apparent miracles encouraged resistance to French rule.

Legacy

Robert-Houdin's memoirs and technical writings systematised the art of conjuring. The young Ehrich Weiss took his stage name "Houdini" from Robert-Houdin, believing (mistakenly, via a French grammar error) that adding an "i" meant "like Houdin." Robert-Houdin also invented devices including an early electric doorbell system and ophthalmological instruments.
