Lysias (Greek: Λυσίας; c. 445 – c. 380 BC) was a logographer (speechwriter) in ancient Athens and one of the ten Attic orators included in the Alexandrian Canon. The son of Cephalus, a wealthy Syracusan shield-manufacturer whom Pericles had invited to Athens — the family home in Piraeus is the setting of Plato's Republic — Lysias lived as a metic and made his living writing court speeches for litigants to deliver themselves. His plain, graceful Attic style and skill at characterisation (ethopoeia) were admired throughout antiquity.

Lysias, speechwriter of classical Athens

Life

Lysias studied rhetoric at Thurii in Italy, returning to Athens in 412 BC. During the rule of the Thirty Tyrants (404–403 BC), his family's property was confiscated and his brother Polemarchus was executed; Lysias narrowly escaped to Megara. He returned with the democratic restoration and prosecuted the former Thirty member Eratosthenes in the one surviving speech he delivered in person, Against Eratosthenes — a major historical source for the period. Despite services to the democracy, including financing troops, his application for citizenship was annulled on a procedural point, and he remained a privileged metic (isoteles) for life.

Works

Ancient critics credited Lysias with over 230 speeches; 34 survive, some fragmentary or of disputed authenticity. Most are forensic speeches for private cases — inheritance disputes, assault, homicide, contract cases — which illuminate everyday Athenian law and society. Notable examples include On the Murder of Eratosthenes (Lysias 1), a husband's defence for killing his wife's lover; the brief, elegant On a Wound by Premeditation (Lysias 4); the Funeral Oration (Lysias 2); and For the Disabled Man (Lysias 24), a defence of a disability pension. Dionysius of Halicarnassus praised his clarity, purity of diction and persuasive naturalness, calling his style a model of the plain style.
