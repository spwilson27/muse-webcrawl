Sheriff

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: a six-pointed sheriff's badge]

A sheriff is a government official, with varying duties, existing in some countries with historical ties to England where the office originated. There is an equivalent to the sheriff in Scotland, where it is called a sheriff principal; in Ireland the office has been largely reformed; and in the United States a sheriff is generally the highest law enforcement officer of a county.

The word "sheriff" is a contraction of "shire reeve" — the reeve (an official appointed by the king) of a shire (county). The office dates to Anglo-Saxon England; in the years shortly after the Norman Conquest, the sheriff became the king's representative responsible for keeping the peace, collecting taxes, and administering royal justice in the shire.

United States

In the United States, a sheriff is typically the chief law enforcement officer of a county, an office that is elected in most states. Sheriff's offices commonly operate the county jail, provide court security, serve civil process, and patrol unincorporated areas. Deputies appointed by the sheriff carry out most day-to-day duties. The role is unusual among American law-enforcement leadership in being directly accountable to voters.

England and Wales

In modern England and Wales, the historic county sheriff survives mainly as the ceremonial office of High Sheriff, appointed annually. Court-related enforcement functions once performed by sheriffs are now carried out by High Court Enforcement Officers and bailiffs.
