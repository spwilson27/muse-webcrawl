English honorifics

English honorifics are titles and forms of address used in English to convey respect, status, or profession. Unlike languages with grammaticalized honorific systems, English relies mainly on titles placed before names, such as Mr., Mrs., Miss, Ms., and Mx., together with professional and official titles such as Dr., Professor, Sir, Dame, and forms used for royalty, nobility, judges, and clergy.

Personal titles

Mr. (from Mister, originally Master) is used for men regardless of marital status. Mrs. traditionally denotes a married woman, Miss an unmarried woman, and Ms., popularized in the 20th century, avoids specifying marital status and is now the default in much professional writing. Mx. is a gender-neutral title that has entered dictionaries and official use in the United Kingdom. The plural and formal styles include Messrs. and Mmes.

Professional and official titles

Doctors of medicine and holders of doctoral degrees are addressed as Dr.; professors by their rank. Knights and dames use Sir and Dame with their first names. Members of the British royal family are styled Your Majesty or Your Royal Highness; peers are Lord and Lady; judges in court are addressed as My Lord or Your Honour depending on the court. Styles such as The Right Honourable (for Privy Counsellors) and The Honourable precede names in formal contexts.

Sir and Madam

In direct address, sir and madam (or ma'am) function as polite vocatives without names, common in service encounters and in the military, where they are used to address superior officers.
