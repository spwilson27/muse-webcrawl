Mixed methods research

Mixed methods research is a methodology for conducting research that involves collecting, analysing, and integrating quantitative and qualitative research (and data) in a single study or a series of studies. Its central premise is that the combined use of quantitative and qualitative approaches provides a better understanding of research problems than either approach alone.

History

Although researchers had combined methods informally for decades, mixed methods emerged as a distinct methodology in the late 1980s and 1990s, drawing on work in sociology, education, health sciences, and evaluation. Scholars such as John W. Creswell, Vicki Plano Clark, Jennifer Greene, and Abbas Tashakkori and Charles Teddlie helped formalize its designs and philosophical foundations, often associated with pragmatism.

Core designs

Commonly described core designs include:

  • Convergent parallel design — quantitative and qualitative data are collected concurrently, analysed separately, and merged in interpretation.
  • Explanatory sequential design — quantitative data are collected first, followed by qualitative data to explain the quantitative results.
  • Exploratory sequential design — qualitative data are collected first to explore a phenomenon, then used to build a quantitative instrument or study.

More complex variants embed one strand within another (embedded design), span multiple phases (multiphase design), or integrate methods within intervention trials and case studies.

Purposes

Greene, Caracelli and Graham identified five purposes for mixing methods: triangulation (corroboration), complementarity (elaboration), development (using one method to inform another), initiation (uncovering contradictions), and expansion (extending the breadth or range of inquiry).

Criticism

Critics raise questions about paradigm compatibility between positivist and interpretive traditions, the added cost and skills required, and the risk that one strand becomes tokenistic. Proponents respond that pragmatism provides a workable philosophical grounding focused on the research question.
