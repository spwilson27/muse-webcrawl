Union Carbide

Union Carbide Corporation is an American chemical corporation founded in 1917. One of the oldest chemical companies in the United States, it became notorious for the Bhopal disaster of 1984, the world's worst industrial catastrophe. Since 2001 it has been a wholly owned subsidiary of Dow Chemical Company (now Dow Inc.), and it continues to operate as Union Carbide Corporation producing chemicals and polymers.

History

The company was formed in 1917 from the merger of several firms, including the National Carbon Company and the Union Carbide Company founded in 1898 to exploit the calcium carbide process developed by Thomas Willson. Its products ranged from Eveready batteries and Prestone antifreeze to industrial gases and plastics; its researchers pioneered work in petrochemicals, and Union Carbide was one of the original 30 components of the Dow Jones Industrial Average. During the Second World War, its subsidiary operated the K-25 gaseous diffusion plant at Oak Ridge for the Manhattan Project.

Bhopal disaster

On the night of 2–3 December 1984, a pesticide plant operated by Union Carbide India Limited in Bhopal, Madhya Pradesh, leaked approximately 40 tonnes of methyl isocyanate gas, killing thousands of residents in surrounding neighbourhoods — official estimates of immediate deaths run into the thousands, with many thousands more injured and later deaths attributed to exposure. In 1989 Union Carbide settled with the Government of India for US$470 million. The disaster reshaped process-safety regulation worldwide and remains a landmark case in corporate liability.

Acquisition by Dow

In August 1999, Dow Chemical announced it would acquire Union Carbide in a deal valued at about US$11.6 billion including assumed debt; the merger closed in February 2001. Union Carbide survives as a subsidiary, retaining responsibility for certain legacy liabilities, including aspects of the Bhopal settlement and asbestos claims.
