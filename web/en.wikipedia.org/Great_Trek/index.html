Great Trek

Date: 1835–1854

Participants: Voortrekkers (Dutch-speaking colonists)

Origin: Cape Colony

Destinations: Natal, Orange Free State, Transvaal

The Great Trek (Afrikaans: Die Groot Trek) was a northward migration of Dutch-speaking settlers who travelled by wagon from the Cape Colony into the interior of modern South Africa between 1835 and 1854, seeking to live beyond British colonial administration.

Causes

The migrants, known as Voortrekkers, left the Cape for several overlapping reasons: resentment of British rule and anglicisation policies, the emancipation of enslaved people in 1834 and dissatisfaction with the compensation arrangements, land hunger on a crowded frontier, and conflicts with Xhosa groups on the colony's eastern border. Piet Retief's manifesto of 1837 set out the trekkers' grievances and their intention to establish their own government.

The migration

Some 12,000–14,000 Boers — perhaps a fifth of the Dutch-speaking population of the eastern Cape — trekked in organised parties led by figures such as Louis Tregardt, Hendrik Potgieter, Piet Retief, Piet Uys and Andries Pretorius. Crossing the Orange River and the Drakensberg, they founded settlements in Natal and the highveld. The trek brought violent conflict with African peoples whose land they entered: Dingane's Zulu kingdom killed Retief's party in 1838 and massacred trekker families at Weenen, after which Pretorius's commando defeated the Zulu at the Battle of Blood River (16 December 1838). The trekkers also fought the Ndebele of Mzilikazi, who withdrew north into what became Zimbabwe.

Boer republics and legacy

The trekkers established several short-lived republics, consolidated eventually into the Natalia Republic, the Orange Free State and the South African Republic (Transvaal), whose independence Britain recognised in the Sand River (1852) and Bloemfontein (1854) Conventions. The Great Trek became a central founding narrative of Afrikaner nationalism, commemorated in the 1938 centenary celebrations and the Voortrekker Monument in Pretoria, although its celebration is inseparable from the dispossession of African communities and later apartheid-era politics.
