Lake Titicaca (Spanish: Lago Titicaca) is the largest lake in South America by volume of water and surface area, and the highest navigable lake in the world, lying at 3,812 metres above sea level on the Altiplano between Peru and Bolivia. In Andean belief, it is the birthplace of the sun and of the first Inca.

Geography

The lake consists of two nearly separate sub-basins joined by the Strait of Tiquina. More than 25 rivers flow into it; the Desaguadero River drains its southern end toward Lake Poopó. Some 41 islands dot the lake, including the natural islands of Taquile, Amantaní, and Isla del Sol, and the artificial floating islands of the Uros people, built entirely of bundled totora reeds.

Ecology

Titicaca supports unique high-altitude life, including the Titicaca water frog, among the world's largest fully aquatic frogs, and the flightless Titicaca grebe. Introduced rainbow trout transformed its fisheries in the 20th century, contributing to declines of native Orestias fish.

History and culture

The lake basin was home to the Tiwanaku civilisation (c. 300–1000 CE), one of the earliest Andean states. In Inca mythology the creator god Viracocha rose from the lake, and the Island of the Sun held a sacred rock shrine. The city of Puno on the Peruvian shore hosts the February Fiesta de la Candelaria, one of South America's great folk festivals. In 1968 Jacques Cousteau's team searched the lake for rumoured Inca gold, finding pottery and structures but no treasure.
