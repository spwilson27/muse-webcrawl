Fungiculture

Fungiculture is the cultivation of fungi — principally mushrooms — to produce food, medicine, and other products. It is one of the six primary sectors of agriculture and is practised both industrially and by smallholders and hobbyists worldwide.

Common species

The most widely cultivated species is the button mushroom, Agaricus bisporus, grown on composted substrates. Other major crops include shiitake (Lentinula edodes), long grown on hardwood logs in East Asia; oyster mushrooms (Pleurotus spp.), which fruit readily on straw and agricultural waste; enoki; maitake; and the wood ear fungus. Truffles are cultivated semi-wild in inoculated oak and hazel plantations.

Methods

Production typically begins with spawn — grain or another carrier colonised by mycelium — used to inoculate a prepared substrate such as compost, pasteurised straw, or sawdust. After a spawn run in which the mycelium colonises the substrate, changes in temperature, humidity, fresh air, and light trigger fruiting. Growing environments range from climate-controlled warehouses to caves, tunnels, and shaded outdoor beds.

History

Mushroom cultivation began in China over a thousand years ago with wood ear and shiitake. In France, Agaricus was farmed in Parisian quarries and catacombs from the 17th century, and the industry spread to Britain and the United States in the 19th century. China is now by far the world's largest producer, accounting for the great majority of global output.

Other applications

Beyond food, fungiculture supplies yeasts for baking and brewing, moulds for cheese and fermented foods, fungal enzymes, and medicinal preparations. Leafcutter ants and some termites independently evolved their own fungus-growing agriculture tens of millions of years before humans.
