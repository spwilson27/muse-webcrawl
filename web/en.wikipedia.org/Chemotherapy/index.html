Chemotherapy

Chemotherapy (often abbreviated chemo and sometimes CTX or CTx) is a type of cancer treatment that uses one or more anti-cancer drugs (chemotherapeutic agents or alkylating agents) in a standard regimen. Chemotherapy may be given with a curative intent (which almost always involves combinations of drugs), or it may aim to prolong life or to reduce symptoms (palliative chemotherapy). Chemotherapy is one of the major categories of the medical discipline specifically devoted to pharmacotherapy for cancer, which is called medical oncology.

How it works

Most cytotoxic chemotherapy drugs kill cells that divide rapidly — a hallmark of cancer — by damaging DNA or blocking mitosis. Classes include alkylating agents, antimetabolites (e.g., methotrexate, 5-fluorouracil), anthracyclines (e.g., doxorubicin), taxanes, platinum compounds (e.g., cisplatin), and topoisomerase inhibitors. Because the drugs also injure rapidly dividing normal tissues — bone marrow, gut lining, hair follicles — they cause characteristic side effects.

History

Modern chemotherapy began in the 1940s: nitrogen mustards, derived from chemical-weapons research, produced remissions in lymphoma, and Sidney Farber showed in 1948 that the antifolate aminopterin induced remissions in childhood leukaemia. Combination regimens in the 1960s made Hodgkin lymphoma and childhood ALL curable.

Patterns of use

Adjuvant chemotherapy follows primary treatment (e.g., surgery) to kill micrometastases; neoadjuvant therapy precedes it to shrink tumours; induction and consolidation phases are used in leukaemias. Dosing is usually by body surface area in cycles with rest periods for normal tissue recovery, limited by cumulative toxicities such as anthracycline cardiotoxicity.

Side effects

Common adverse effects include nausea and vomiting (much reduced by modern antiemetics such as 5-HT3 antagonists), myelosuppression with infection risk, anaemia, hair loss, mucositis, peripheral neuropathy, infertility, and a small long-term risk of secondary malignancy. Targeted therapies and immunotherapies are often distinguished from traditional cytotoxic chemotherapy.
