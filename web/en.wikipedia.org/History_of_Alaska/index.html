History of Alaska

The history of Alaska begins with the settlement of the region by Indigenous peoples — including the Iñupiat, Yup'ik, Aleut (Unangax), Tlingit, Haida, and Athabaskan peoples — thousands of years before European contact. Vitus Bering's expeditions of 1728 and 1741 opened Alaska to the Russian fur trade, and Russian America, administered by the Russian-American Company, was established with its capital at Sitka.

American Alaska

The United States purchased Alaska from Russia in 1867 for $7.2 million, a transaction derided at the time as "Seward's Folly" after Secretary of State William H. Seward. Gold rushes at Juneau, Nome, and Fairbanks, and later the Klondike rush in neighbouring Yukon, brought waves of settlers. Alaska's strategic value was underscored in the Second World War, when the Aleutian Islands campaign was fought on its soil.

Statehood and the oil era

Alaska became the 49th U.S. state on 3 January 1959. The Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act of 1971 resolved Indigenous land claims, and the discovery of oil at Prudhoe Bay in 1968 led to construction of the Trans-Alaska Pipeline, completed in 1977. Oil revenues fund the Alaska Permanent Fund, which pays an annual dividend to residents.
