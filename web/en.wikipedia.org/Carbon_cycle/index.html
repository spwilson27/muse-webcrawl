The carbon cycle is the biogeochemical cycle by which carbon is exchanged among the biosphere, pedosphere, geosphere, hydrosphere, and atmosphere of Earth. Carbon is the main component of biological compounds as well as a major component of many minerals, and its cycle, together with the nitrogen and water cycles, comprises a sequence of events essential for life on Earth.

Reservoirs and fluxes

Most of Earth's carbon is stored in rocks and sediments; the rest circulates among the ocean, atmosphere, soil, and living organisms. Photosynthesis draws about 120 billion tonnes of carbon per year from the atmosphere into land plants, balanced roughly by respiration and decomposition. The ocean holds around 38,000 billion tonnes, mostly as dissolved bicarbonate, and exchanges CO₂ with the atmosphere continuously across its surface.

The slow cycle

Over geological time, carbon moves between rocks, soil, ocean, and atmosphere through weathering of silicate rocks, sedimentation and burial of organic matter and carbonate shells, subduction, and volcanic outgassing. This slow cycle takes 100–200 million years and has regulated Earth's climate over its history. Fossil fuels formed when a small fraction of buried organic carbon escaped oxidation over hundreds of millions of years.

Human perturbation

Since the Industrial Revolution, burning fossil fuels and deforestation have transferred carbon to the atmosphere far faster than natural processes remove it. Atmospheric CO₂ has risen from about 280 ppm in 1750 to over 420 ppm today, driving global warming. Roughly half of human emissions are absorbed by the ocean and land biosphere; ocean uptake causes ocean acidification. Understanding and managing the carbon cycle is central to climate science and climate policy.
