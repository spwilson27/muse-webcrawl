Socialist realism

Period: 1934–1980s

Region: Soviet Union and socialist states

Socialist realism was the official cultural doctrine of the Soviet Union and later other socialist states, requiring art and literature to depict reality "in its revolutionary development" — realistic in form, socialist in content, and accessible to the working class as an instrument of education and ideology.

Origins

Proclaimed at the 1934 Congress of Soviet Writers, associated with Maxim Gorky and cultural official Andrei Zhdanov, the doctrine replaced the experimental avant-garde of the 1920s. Its typical hero was the positive, optimistic worker, soldier or collective farmer building communism.

Across the arts

In literature it shaped novels like Gorky's Mother (an early model) and Ostrovsky's How the Steel Was Tempered; in painting, heroic canvases and murals; in architecture, the Stalinist monumental classicism of the planned Palace of the Soviets and Moscow's VDNKh; in music it condemned "formalism", notably in the Zhdanov decrees against Shostakovich and Prokofiev.

Spread and decline

After 1945 the doctrine was exported to Eastern Europe, China (where it merged with revolutionary romanticism) and other communist states. It loosened after Stalin's death and the Thaw under Khrushchev, and collapsed with the socialist states themselves, though its monumental works remain widely studied.
