Proof by contradiction

In logic, proof by contradiction is a form of proof that establishes the truth or the validity of a proposition by showing that assuming the proposition to be false leads to a contradiction. It is also known by its Latin name reductio ad absurdum ("reduction to absurdity").

Method

To prove a statement P, one assumes ¬P (the negation of P) together with the accepted axioms and previously proved results, and derives a statement of the form Q ∧ ¬Q. Since a contradiction cannot be true, the assumption ¬P must be false, so P is true. The final step relies on the law of the excluded middle or double-negation elimination in classical logic.

Examples

Classic examples include Euclid's proof that there are infinitely many prime numbers (assuming finitely many primes, their product plus one yields a new prime or a new prime divisor, contradicting the assumption) and the proof that the square root of 2 is irrational (assuming √2 = a/b in lowest terms leads to both a and b being even). Proofs of impossibility and non-existence, such as the unsolvability results in geometry, are often naturally framed by contradiction.

Status in constructive logic

Intuitionistic and constructive mathematics reject the general principle that ¬¬P implies P. A proof by contradiction of a positive existence claim gives no construction of the object claimed to exist, so constructivists accept proof by contradiction only for negative statements. The mathematician G. H. Hardy praised reductio ad absurdum as one of the mathematician's finest weapons, while L. E. J. Brouwer and his school restricted its use.

Related methods

Proof by contradiction is closely related to proof by contraposition, which proves P → Q by proving ¬Q → ¬P; unlike contradiction, contraposition is valid intuitionistically for the implication itself. A proof of negation — showing P leads to absurdity and concluding ¬P — is valid in constructive logic as well.
