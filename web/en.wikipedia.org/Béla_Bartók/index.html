Béla Viktor János Bartók (25 March 1881 – 26 September 1945) was a Hungarian composer, pianist, and ethnomusicologist, regarded as one of the most important composers of the 20th century. Through his collection and analytical study of folk music, he was a founder of comparative musicology, which later became ethnomusicology. His own music fused folk material with modernist techniques, producing works of striking rhythmic vitality and formal originality.

Life and career

Born in the Banat region, Bartók studied piano at the Royal Academy of Music in Budapest, where he later taught. From 1904 onward, he and Zoltán Kodály undertook field expeditions recording peasant songs across Hungary, Slovakia, Romania, and beyond, eventually transcribing thousands of melodies. These studies transformed his compositional language. Opposed to Nazism, Bartók emigrated to the United States in 1940, where he worked at Columbia University; he died of leukemia in New York in 1945, just as his reputation was rising.

Music

Major works include the opera Duke Bluebeard's Castle (1911), the ballets The Wooden Prince and The Miraculous Mandarin, three piano concertos, the Music for Strings, Percussion and Celesta (1936), the Sonata for Two Pianos and Percussion, the six string quartets, and the Concerto for Orchestra (1943), commissioned by Serge Koussevitzky. The six-volume piano cycle Mikrokosmos remains a staple of teaching. His style combines modal folk melody, percussive rhythm, and sophisticated symmetrical forms.
