Piano concerto

A piano concerto is a type of concerto, a solo composition in the classical music tradition, written for a piano accompanied by an orchestra or other ensemble. Piano concertos are typically cast in the standard three-movement concerto format, with a fast opening movement, a slow middle movement, and a fast finale, often including a cadenza in the first movement where the soloist performs alone.

A solo pianist performs with orchestra.

History

The piano concerto emerged in the mid-18th century as the piano superseded the harpsichord. Early keyboard concertos were written by the sons of J. S. Bach, including C. P. E. Bach. Mozart composed 27 piano concertos, works that established the genre's classical form and remain central to the repertoire.

Romantic era

Beethoven's five piano concertos, especially the "Emperor" Concerto, expanded the genre's scale and drama. In the 19th century, virtuosos such as Chopin, Schumann, Liszt, Brahms, and Tchaikovsky wrote concertos combining technical brilliance with symphonic depth. Rachmaninoff's Second and Third concertos became among the most popular of all.

20th century and beyond

Prokofiev, Bartók, Shostakovich, and Gershwin (whose Rhapsody in Blue blends concerto form with jazz) extended the repertoire in the 20th century. Composers continue to write piano concertos today, often for specific soloists.

Characteristics

The concerto stages a dialogue — sometimes a contest — between soloist and orchestra. The first movement commonly uses sonata form with a double exposition, and the cadenza, once improvised, is usually written out by the composer.
