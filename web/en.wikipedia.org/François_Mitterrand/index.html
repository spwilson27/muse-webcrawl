François Maurice Adrien Marie Mitterrand (1916–1996) was a French politician who served as President of France from 1981 to 1995, the longest-serving president in French history and the first of the left under the Fifth Republic. Under his presidency France abolished the death penalty and pursued European integration, culminating in the Maastricht Treaty.

Early life and career

Born in Jarnac, Charente, Mitterrand studied law and politics in Paris. Taken prisoner in 1940, he escaped and worked in the Vichy administration before joining the Resistance, where he led a network of former prisoners of war under the codename "Morland". After the war he served as a deputy and held eleven ministerial posts under the Fourth Republic, including Minister of the Interior.

Presidency

As leader of the Socialist Party from 1971, Mitterrand lost presidential elections in 1974 before defeating Valéry Giscard d'Estaing in 1981. His first government enacted nationalisations, raised the minimum wage, extended holidays, lowered the retirement age and abolished capital punishment (under Justice Minister Robert Badinter). After an austerity turn in 1983, he survived periods of "cohabitation" with right-wing governments (1986–88, 1993–95) and was re-elected in 1988. Abroad he championed the Franco-German partnership with Helmut Kohl and the single European currency.

Legacy

Mitterrand commissioned the "Grands Projets" that reshaped Paris, including the Louvre Pyramid and the Bibliothèque nationale de France. His later years were marked by controversy over his Vichy past and by illness; he died of prostate cancer in January 1996, months after leaving office.
