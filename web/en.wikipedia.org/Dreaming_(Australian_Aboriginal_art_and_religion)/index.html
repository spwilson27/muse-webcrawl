Dreaming (Australian Aboriginal art and religion)

The Dreaming, also called Dreamtime or Tjukurrpa in some languages, is a term devised by early anthropologists to refer to a religio-cultural worldview attributed to Australian Aboriginal beliefs. It encompasses the time of creation, when ancestral beings journeyed across the land forming its features, laws and customs — and it is understood not as a finished past but as an ongoing reality connecting people, ancestors and country.

Concept

The word "Dreamtime" derives from a rendering of the Arrernte word altyerr ("dream"), popularised by anthropologists Baldwin Spencer and F. J. Gillen; scholars such as W. E. H. Stanner, who coined the phrase "everywhen", noted that European translations are imperfect. In Dreaming belief, ancestral spirits created the world and laid down the moral and social law; each person has a spiritual connection to particular ancestors, species and places, expressed in totems and in the obligation to care for country.

Stories and songlines

Dreaming narratives are preserved in oral stories, ceremony, rock art and songlines — sung routes that map ancestral journeys across the continent and function as mnemonic guides to geography, law and resource knowledge. In art, the acrylic dot-painting movement that began at Papunya in 1971 brought Dreaming imagery to international attention, sometimes deliberately obscuring sacred elements.
