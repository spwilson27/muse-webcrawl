Organoboron chemistry

Organoboron chemistry is the chemistry of compounds containing a carbon–boron bond, known as organoboranes and organoboronates. Although boron is a metalloid, its compounds with carbon behave in many ways like organic compounds, and organoboron reagents are important intermediates in organic synthesis because the C–B bond can be converted stereospecifically into C–C, C–O, C–N and C–halogen bonds.

History

The field dates to Edward Frankland's preparation of triethylborane in 1860. It was transformed by Herbert C. Brown's discovery of hydroboration in 1956 — the addition of B–H bonds across alkenes — for which Brown shared the 1979 Nobel Prize in Chemistry with Georg Wittig.

Principal compound classes

Trialkylboranes (R3B) are strong Lewis acids. Boronic acids, RB(OH)2, and their esters (boronates) are stable, easily handled compounds central to the Suzuki–Miyaura coupling, a palladium-catalysed cross-coupling widely used to form biaryl bonds in pharmaceuticals and materials. Borinic acids, borates, carboranes and frustrated Lewis pairs are other significant classes.

Reactions

Besides hydroboration–oxidation (giving alcohols with anti-Markovnikov selectivity) and Suzuki coupling, notable reactions include the Matteson homologation, allylboration of carbonyls, diboration, and the use of boranes as hydroboration-derived reducing agents such as 9-BBN. Boron-containing drugs include the proteasome inhibitor bortezomib and the antifungal tavaborole.
