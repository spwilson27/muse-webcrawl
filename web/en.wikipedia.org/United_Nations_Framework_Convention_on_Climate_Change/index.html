United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change

The United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) is an international environmental treaty adopted on 9 May 1992 and opened for signature at the Earth Summit in Rio de Janeiro in June 1992. It entered into force on 21 March 1994 and has near-universal membership, with 198 parties. Its objective is to stabilise greenhouse gas concentrations in the atmosphere "at a level that would prevent dangerous anthropogenic interference with the climate system."

Structure and principles

The Convention divides countries into Annex I (industrialised countries and economies in transition), Annex II (developed countries obliged to provide financial and technological support) and non-Annex I (mostly developing) parties. Its principles include common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities, the precautionary principle, and the right to sustainable development. Parties commit to publishing national greenhouse gas inventories and programmes to mitigate climate change, but the Convention itself set no binding emission limits.

Conference of the Parties

The supreme decision-making body is the Conference of the Parties (COP), which has met annually since COP1 in Berlin in 1995. Landmark outcomes include the Kyoto Protocol (COP3, 1997), which set binding reduction targets for developed countries; the Copenhagen Accord (2009); and the Paris Agreement (COP21, 2015), under which parties submit nationally determined contributions (NDCs) aiming to limit warming to well below 2 °C, and preferably 1.5 °C, above pre-industrial levels. The first "global stocktake" concluded at COP28 in Dubai (2023), which included the first COP text calling for "transitioning away from fossil fuels."

Criticism

The UNFCCC process has been criticised for slow progress — global emissions continued to rise for most of its lifetime — for non-binding commitments, and for consensus rules that allow small groups of states to block stronger action. Defenders argue it is the only universal forum for climate diplomacy and that it has bent the projected warming curve substantially from early-century baselines.
