Karl Llewellyn

Karl Nickerson Llewellyn (22 May 1893 – 13 February 1962) was an American legal scholar and a leading figure of the legal realism movement. He taught at Yale, Columbia and the University of Chicago, and as chief reporter for Article 2 of the Uniform Commercial Code he shaped the modern American law of sales.

Life and career

Llewellyn was educated at Yale, interrupting his studies to serve with the German army in the First World War — an experience for which he was decorated with the Iron Cross — before returning to complete his law degrees. He taught at Yale Law School, then at Columbia Law School, and from 1951 at the University of Chicago Law School. With the anthropologist E. Adamson Hoebel he studied dispute resolution among the Cheyenne, producing The Cheyenne Way (1941), a classic of legal anthropology.

Legal realism

Llewellyn argued that law should be understood through what officials — especially judges — actually do, rather than through formal rules alone. His 1930 lectures to first-year students, published as The Bramble Bush, became one of the most widely read introductions to legal study in America. In later work, notably The Common Law Tradition: Deciding Appeals (1960), he examined the "Grand Style" of appellate judging and the leeways and steadying factors that make judicial decisions reasonably predictable.

Uniform Commercial Code

Llewellyn was the driving intellectual force behind the Uniform Commercial Code project begun in the 1940s and served as chief reporter for Article 2 on sales of goods. The Code, eventually enacted in nearly every U.S. state, modernised commercial law with rules informed by actual mercantile practice, including open-price and open-delivery terms and the battle-of-the-forms provision.

Legacy

Llewellyn is remembered as one of the most influential American jurists of the twentieth century: a founder of legal realism, a reformer of commercial law, and a stylist whose writings on the craft of judging remain in print.
