Hamilton's Financial Plan

Hamilton's financial plan was the economic program of Alexander Hamilton, first US Secretary of the Treasury.

Hamilton's financial plan was a series of measures proposed by the first United States Secretary of the Treasury, Alexander Hamilton, in a series of reports between 1790 and 1791. The plan had three interlocking parts: the establishment of the credit of the United States through the funding of the national debt, the federal assumption of the states' Revolutionary War debts, and the creation of a national bank — supported by federal taxation, notably tariffs and an excise on whiskey, and by federal encouragement of manufactures.

The reports

In the First Report on the Public Credit (January 1790), Hamilton proposed that the federal government fund the domestic and foreign debt of the Confederation at par and assume the remaining war debts of the states, arguing that sound public credit and a funded national debt — serviced by dedicated revenues — would bind creditors to the new federal government and stabilise its finances. He opposed "discrimination" between original holders of government securities and speculators who had bought them at depressed prices.

The assumption proposal initially failed in Congress because states such as Virginia had already paid much of their debt. It passed as part of the Compromise of 1790, in which Hamilton's supporters agreed that the permanent national capital would be placed on the Potomac River in the South.

In the Report on a National Bank (December 1790), Hamilton proposed the Bank of the United States, chartered for twenty years, capitalised at $10 million, with the federal government holding one-fifth of the stock. President George Washington signed the bank bill in February 1791 after rejecting Thomas Jefferson's and James Madison's argument that the Constitution gave Congress no power to create a bank; Hamilton's rebuttal laid out the doctrine of implied powers. The Report on Manufactures (December 1791) advocated tariffs and bounties to encourage American industry, but most of it was never enacted.

Funding and opposition

To service the debt, Hamilton relied on import duties and an excise on distilled spirits (1791), which provoked the Whiskey Rebellion of 1794 in western Pennsylvania. The plan's emphasis on federal power, public credit, and close ties between government and finance became the foundation of the Federalist Party's program and provoked the formation of Jefferson and Madison's Democratic-Republican Party in opposition. Despite controversy, the plan restored American credit abroad, created a market in federal securities, and laid the institutional foundations of United States public finance.
