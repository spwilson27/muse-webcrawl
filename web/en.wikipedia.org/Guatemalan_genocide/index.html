Guatemalan genocide

The Guatemalan genocide, also called the Maya genocide or the Silent Holocaust, refers to the mass killing of Maya civilians during the Guatemalan civil war (1960–1996), above all during the counterinsurgency campaigns of the early 1980s under the governments of Romeo Lucas García and Efraín Ríos Montt.

Background

Guatemala's civil war began after the 1954 US-backed coup that overthrew the democratically elected president Jacobo Árbenz. Leftist guerrilla movements later drew support from indigenous Maya communities suffering extreme poverty and land inequality. The military governments responded by treating Maya villagers in contested regions as guerrilla collaborators.

The violence of 1981–1983

Under the scorched-earth campaigns of 1981–1983, the Guatemalan army destroyed hundreds of Maya villages. The UN-backed Commission for Historical Clarification (CEH) documented 626 massacres and concluded in its 1999 report that state forces had committed acts of genocide against Maya groups in several regions, including the Ixil Maya of El Quiché. Overall, the war killed an estimated 200,000 people, the great majority of them indigenous civilians killed by state forces.

Accountability

In 2013 a Guatemalan court convicted former head of state Efraín Ríos Montt of genocide and crimes against humanity — the first time a former head of state was tried for genocide in his own country — though the verdict was annulled on procedural grounds days later and Ríos Montt died in 2018 during retrial proceedings. Other trials, such as the Dos Erres massacre case and the 2016 Sepur Zarco case, have produced further convictions.
