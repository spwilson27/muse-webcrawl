Turbidite

A turbidite is the deposit of a turbidity current — a rapid, sediment-laden underwater flow that travels down continental slopes and spreads across deep-ocean floors. Turbidite beds typically show normal grading, fining upward from coarse sand at the base to silt and mud at the top.

Bouma sequence

The classic vertical succession of structures in a turbidite bed is the Bouma sequence (Ta–Te divisions), from massive or graded sand at the base through parallel and ripple lamination to pelagic mud, reflecting waning flow energy.

Settings

Turbidites accumulate in submarine fans, abyssal plains, and deep lakes, often triggered by earthquakes or slope failures. Successions of turbidites make up much of the geological "flysch" of mountain belts.

Importance

Turbidite sandstones form important hydrocarbon reservoirs, and submarine cables have famously been broken by turbidity currents, as after the 1929 Grand Banks earthquake.
