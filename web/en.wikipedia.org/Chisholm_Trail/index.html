Chisholm Trail

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Chisholm Trail was a trail used in the post-Civil War era to drive cattle overland in the United States, from ranches in Texas to railheads in Kansas. The trail was established by Indian trader Jesse Chisholm, who built trading posts in Indian Territory (present-day Oklahoma) over which drovers later moved their herds, and by cattle merchant Joseph G. McCoy, who promoted Abilene, Kansas, as a shipping point. It was the most important route for cattle drives north from Texas in the late 19th century.

History

After the American Civil War, Texas had an abundance of longhorn cattle but few accessible markets. In 1867 McCoy built stockyards at Abilene, where the Kansas Pacific Railway had reached, and persuaded Texas ranchers to drive their herds north. The route followed Chisholm's wagon road through Indian Territory, crossing the Red River into Texas and continuing south to San Antonio and beyond. Drovers called it the Chisholm Trail, although Chisholm himself never drove cattle over it.

Between the late 1860s and the mid-1880s, millions of cattle and horses were driven up the trail — estimates commonly range from five to ten million head. Successive railheads moved west and south, and the trail's northern terminus shifted to towns such as Ellsworth, Wichita, Dodge City, and Caldwell. Kansas quarantine laws against Texas fever, the spread of homesteads and barbed wire, and the extension of railroads into Texas itself gradually closed the trail; the last major drives took place in the mid-1880s.

Route

The trail ran roughly from the Rio Grande and San Antonio, Texas, northward through Austin and Fort Worth, across the Red River at Red River Station, through Indian Territory past present-day Duncan and Kingfisher, and into Kansas, ending at Abilene and later railheads. Numerous feeder branches joined the main stem from across Texas.

Legacy

The Chisholm Trail became a central element of the mythology of the American West, commemorated in songs such as "The Old Chisholm Trail," in museums, and by historical markers along its route in Texas, Oklahoma, and Kansas. The states along the trail have promoted the Chisholm Trail heritage corridor for tourism.
