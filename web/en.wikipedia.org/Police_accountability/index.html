Police accountability

Police accountability refers to the mechanisms by which law enforcement agencies and individual officers are held responsible for their actions, particularly misconduct such as excessive force, corruption, and violations of civil rights. Because police hold a state-sanctioned monopoly on the legitimate use of force, accountability systems seek to ensure that this power is exercised lawfully and with public consent.

Civilian review and oversight are key instruments of police accountability.

Mechanisms

Internal mechanisms include internal affairs units, professional standards investigations, supervisory review, and early intervention systems that flag officers with patterns of complaints. External mechanisms include civilian review boards, independent police auditors and inspectors general, criminal prosecution, civil litigation, and oversight by elected officials. In the United States, the Department of Justice may investigate departments for a "pattern or practice" of constitutional violations and impose consent decrees; in England and Wales, the Independent Office for Police Conduct investigates serious incidents.

Legal doctrines

In the United States, officers sued for civil rights violations under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 are shielded by qualified immunity unless they violated clearly established law. Criminal prosecution of officers is comparatively rare and often difficult. Legal scholars debate the effects of indemnification, by which governments pay nearly all settlements and judgments against officers.

Transparency tools

Body-worn cameras, dashboard cameras, public complaint databases, and open records laws have expanded public scrutiny of policing. Reform advocates also promote national use-of-force databases and decertification registries so that officers dismissed for misconduct cannot simply be hired by another department.
