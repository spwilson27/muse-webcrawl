School of Salamanca

The School of Salamanca (Spanish: Escuela de Salamanca) was a movement of Spanish theologians, jurists and philosophers, centred on the University of Salamanca in the 16th and early 17th centuries, that renewed scholastic thought and applied it to the new moral, legal and economic problems created by Spain's global empire. Its founder is generally taken to be the Dominican Francisco de Vitoria (c. 1483–1546).

Thought

Working within the Thomist tradition, the Salamanca theologians — among them Domingo de Soto, Martín de Azpilcueta, Luis de Molina, Tomás de Mercado and Francisco Suárez — produced foundational work in several fields:

  • International law: Vitoria's lectures De Indis and De iure belli (c. 1539) defended the natural rights of the Indigenous peoples of the Americas, denied that discovery or papal donation alone gave Spain title to their lands, and developed just-war criteria — work for which he is often called a father of international law.
  • Natural law and rights: The school articulated ideas of subjective natural rights, popular sovereignty (power originates in the community and is transferred to rulers conditionally) and limits on royal authority, influencing later European political thought.
  • Economics: Salamanca thinkers gave early formulations of the quantity theory of money — Azpilcueta noted that American silver inflows raised prices in Spain — defended the legitimacy of interest and markets within moral limits, and developed subjective theories of value; they are sometimes described as forerunners of the Austrian School of economics.

Influence

The Valladolid debate of 1550–51, in which Bartolomé de las Casas argued against the enslavement of Indigenous people before a junta of theologians, was a direct product of the school's controversies. Suárez's Disputationes Metaphysicae (1597) became a standard philosophy text across Europe, Catholic and Protestant alike, and Salamanca ideas fed into Grotius's law of nations and into early modern theories of resistance and constitutionalism.
