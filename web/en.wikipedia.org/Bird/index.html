Bird

Birds are a group of warm-blooded vertebrates constituting the class Aves, characterised by feathers, toothless beaked jaws, the laying of hard-shelled eggs, a high metabolic rate, a four-chambered heart, and a strong yet lightweight skeleton. Birds live worldwide and range in size from the 5.5 cm (2.2 in) bee hummingbird to the 2.8 m (9 ft 2 in) common ostrich. There are about 11,000 living species.

Evolution

Birds are feathered theropod dinosaurs and constitute the only living dinosaurs. They evolved from earlier feathered dinosaurs during the Jurassic period; the early bird Archaeopteryx, from about 150 million years ago, combined feathers and wings with teeth and a long bony tail. Birds survived the mass extinction 66 million years ago that eliminated their non-avian dinosaur relatives and then diversified explosively into the modern orders.

Anatomy and flight

Most birds are adapted for flight: their forelimbs are modified into wings, their bones are hollow and braced by struts, and large flight muscles anchor to a keeled sternum. A highly efficient respiratory system with air sacs supports the oxygen demands of flight and of endothermy. Flightless birds include the ratites (ostriches, emus, kiwis), penguins — which "fly" underwater — and many island species that lost flight in the absence of predators.

Behaviour

Many species undertake long-distance annual migrations. Birds communicate with song and calls, and some — notably corvids and parrots — rank among the most intelligent animals, capable of tool use and vocal learning. Most species are socially monogamous for a breeding season and care for their young in nests.

Relationship with humans

Birds are economically important as domesticated species (chickens, ducks, turkeys) and culturally prominent in art, religion, and sport; birdwatching is a major recreational activity. However, hundreds of species have become extinct through human activity, and many more are threatened by habitat loss and introduced predators.
