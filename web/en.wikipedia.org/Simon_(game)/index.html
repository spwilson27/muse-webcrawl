Simon is an electronic game of short-term memory skill invented by Ralph H. Baer and Howard J. Morrison, with software programming by Lenny Cope. Launched by Milton Bradley in 1978, the device creates a series of tones and lights and requires a player to repeat the sequence; as the game progresses the sequence grows longer and faster until the player makes an error. Simon was a cultural phenomenon of the late 1970s and 1980s and is considered one of the landmark early electronic toys.

Gameplay

The original unit is a round disc divided into four large coloured sections — red, green, blue and yellow — each producing a distinct musical tone when lit. The device plays a random sequence, adding one step each round, and the player must reproduce it by pressing the pads in order. Several game modes vary the difficulty, including a multiplayer "Simon Says" style variant in which players take responsibility for individual colours. The four tones form a harmonious set (commonly described as A, C#, D and E) so that sequences sound musical rather than random.

Development and launch

Baer and Morrison developed the game at the design firm Marvin Glass and Associates, drawing on the earlier Atari arcade game Touch Me. Simon was famously launched at midnight at the Studio 54 nightclub in New York in 1978, an event that helped make it a must-have holiday gift. Its microprocessor-based design used a Texas Instruments chip that kept production costs low.

Legacy and versions

Numerous successors appeared, including Super Simon, pocket editions, and modern versions with touch-sensitive or motion-based controls; Hasbro continues to sell the game. Countless software clones exist, and "Simon" mechanics appear in video games and memory-training apps. A giant version of the game has been exhibited at museums, and the original is preserved in collections of design and technology history.
