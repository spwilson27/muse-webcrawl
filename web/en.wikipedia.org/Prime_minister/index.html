A prime minister, premier, chief of cabinet or chief minister is the head of the cabinet and the leader of the ministers in the executive branch of government, often in a parliamentary or semi-presidential system. A prime minister is not the head of state, but rather the head of government, serving under a monarch in a monarchy or as a subordinate to a president in a republic. The term derives from the Latin primus ("first") and minister ("servant"), the title having been used in Britain from the 18th century.

Origin of the term

The title emerged in Great Britain, where ministers advised the sovereign in the Privy Council and, later, the Cabinet. Robert Walpole (1721–1742), who led the government for over twenty years, is generally regarded as the first prime minister, though the title was initially an insult implying an over-mighty subject, and Walpole himself denied holding it. The office was recognised officially in the 20th century; in the United Kingdom it was given a statutory definition in the Ministers of the Crown Act 1937.

Selection and tenure

In most parliamentary systems, the head of state appoints as prime minister the person most likely to command the confidence of the elected chamber — normally the leader of the majority party or coalition. The prime minister remains in office only while retaining that confidence: a lost vote of no confidence, a lost election, or replacement by their own party can end a premiership at any time. This flexibility contrasts with the fixed terms of a presidential system.

Powers

A prime minister typically chairs the cabinet, selects and dismisses ministers, directs government policy, controls the legislative timetable (where the government commands a majority), and advises the head of state on matters such as dissolving parliament and calling elections. In many countries the prime minister is also the effective commander of the armed forces and controls senior civil-service and judicial appointments. Powers vary greatly: the British and Canadian premierships are among the strongest, while in some systems the prime minister is a mere coordinator among powerful ministers.

Prime ministers around the world

Offices equivalent to a prime minister exist in most of the world's countries. In Germany and Austria the head of government is the Chancellor; in Spain, the President of the Government; in Ireland, the Taoiseach; in Israel and Greece, the title is simply prime minister. In semi-presidential systems such as France, the prime minister shares executive authority with a directly elected president. Long-serving modern prime ministers include Lee Kuan Yew of Singapore (31 years), Hun Sen of Cambodia, and Margaret Thatcher, the longest-serving British prime minister of the 20th century.
