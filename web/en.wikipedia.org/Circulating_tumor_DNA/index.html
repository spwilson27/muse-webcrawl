Circulating tumor DNA

Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) is tumor-derived fragmented DNA that circulates in the bloodstream, released by cancer cells through apoptosis, necrosis, and secretion. It forms a small fraction of cell-free DNA (cfDNA) in patients with cancer and carries the genetic mutations of the tumor.

Clinical applications

Analysis of ctDNA from a blood sample—often called a liquid biopsy—allows non-invasive detection of cancer mutations, selection of targeted therapies, monitoring of treatment response, and detection of minimal residual disease and recurrence. Approved tests can detect mutations such as those in EGFR in non-small cell lung cancer. Sensitivity is limited in early-stage disease, where ctDNA levels are very low.

Biology

ctDNA fragments are typically around 160–180 base pairs long and have a short half-life in circulation, on the order of hours, making ctDNA a near-real-time marker of tumor burden.
