History of Sudan


The Nubian pyramids at Meroë, capital of the Kingdom of Kush

The history of Sudan covers the lands along the middle Nile, from ancient Nubia to the modern republic. Sudan was home to the Kingdom of Kush, which at its height in the 8th century BCE ruled Egypt as its 25th Dynasty. Medieval Christian Nubian kingdoms gave way to Islamised sultanates, Ottoman-Egyptian rule (the "Turkiya"), the Mahdist state, and Anglo-Egyptian condominium rule. Sudan gained independence on 1 January 1956. Its post-independence history has been dominated by civil wars between the Arab-Muslim north and the south, culminating in the secession of South Sudan in 2011, and by a further devastating war between rival military factions from 2023.

Ancient Nubia and Kush

Prehistoric farming and pastoralist cultures flourished along the Nile from the Neolithic. The Kerma culture (c. 2500–1500 BCE) built a powerful kingdom in Nubia before Egypt's New Kingdom conquered the region. After Egypt's decline, an indigenous Kushite kingdom arose at Napata; its king Piye conquered Egypt around 744 BCE, founding the 25th Dynasty of pharaohs. The Kushite capital later moved south to Meroë, a major centre of iron production and trade, which endured until its fall to the Aksumite kingdom in the 4th century CE. Meroitic remains one of Africa's earliest written scripts.

Christian Nubia and Islamisation

In the 6th century CE, three Christian kingdoms — Nobatia, Makuria and Alodia — emerged in Nubia. Makuria, with its capital at Old Dongola, resisted Arab invasions and maintained a treaty (the baqt) with Muslim Egypt for centuries. From the 14th century the kingdoms disintegrated under pressure from Arab migration and Mamluk intervention, and Islam spread gradually through trade and Sufi orders. The Funj Sultanate of Sennar (1504–1821) and the Keira sultanate of Darfur dominated the following centuries, while the slave trade from the south expanded.

Turkiya and Mahdiyya

In 1820–21 the forces of Muhammad Ali of Egypt conquered Sudan, beginning the period known as the Turkiya, marked by heavy taxation and slave raiding but also administrative unification. Resentment fed the revolt of Muhammad Ahmad, who proclaimed himself the Mahdi in 1881. His followers destroyed a relief column at Shaykan (1883), and Khartoum fell in January 1885, its governor Charles Gordon killed. The Mahdist state under the Khalifa Abdallahi ruled until an Anglo-Egyptian army under Kitchener defeated it at Omdurman on 2 September 1898.

Anglo-Egyptian Sudan

From 1899 Sudan was administered as an Anglo-Egyptian condominium, in practice a British colony. The British developed export cotton schemes such as the Gezira Scheme and governed the south separately under the "Southern Policy", restricting northern access and fostering division. Sudanese nationalism grew after the Second World War, and Sudan became independent on 1 January 1956 under Prime Minister Ismail al-Azhari.

Independence and civil wars

Southern grievances had already sparked mutiny in 1955, igniting the First Sudanese Civil War (1955–1972). Politics alternated between parliamentary governments and military regimes: Ibrahim Abboud (1958–64), Jaafar Nimeiri, who seized power in 1969 and signed the Addis Ababa Agreement ending the first war in 1972, and Sadiq al-Mahdi. Nimeiri's imposition of sharia law in 1983 reignited war in the south under John Garang's SPLA. Nimeiri was overthrown in 1985; in 1989 Omar al-Bashir took power in an Islamist-backed coup. The second civil war (1983–2005) killed an estimated two million people. It ended with the Comprehensive Peace Agreement of 2005, which led to a referendum and the independence of South Sudan on 9 July 2011. Meanwhile war in Darfur from 2003 drew international condemnation and an International Criminal Court indictment of al-Bashir for genocide.

Revolution and war (2019–present)

Mass protests forced the military to remove al-Bashir in April 2019. A transitional power-sharing arrangement collapsed with a coup in October 2021. On 15 April 2023 fighting erupted between the Sudanese Armed Forces under Abdel Fattah al-Burhan and the Rapid Support Forces under Mohamed Hamdan Dagalo ("Hemedti"), devastating Khartoum and Darfur and displacing millions in what became one of the world's largest humanitarian crises.
