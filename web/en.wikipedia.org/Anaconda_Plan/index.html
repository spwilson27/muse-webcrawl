Anaconda Plan

The Anaconda Plan was the Union Army's outline strategy for subduing the seceding Confederate States at the start of the American Civil War. Proposed by General-in-Chief Winfield Scott in 1861, it emphasised a naval blockade of Southern ports and an advance down the Mississippi River to cut the Confederacy in two, gradually squeezing it into submission — like an anaconda constricting its prey, as newspaper commentators described it.

The proposal

Scott, an elderly veteran, believed the war should avoid heavy bloodshed and a direct invasion of Southern territory. His plan called for blockading the Atlantic and Gulf coasts, then moving a strong force down the Mississippi with gunboats to sever Texas, Arkansas and Louisiana from the eastern Confederacy. Surrounded and economically strangled, the South would, he hoped, return to the Union with minimal loss of life. The press ridiculed the plan's slow pace, but its main elements were ultimately implemented.

Implementation

The blockade, proclaimed by President Abraham Lincoln in April 1861, steadily tightened despite Confederate blockade runners. Union forces under David Farragut captured New Orleans in 1862, and Ulysses S. Grant's capture of Vicksburg in July 1863 completed the severing of the Confederacy along the Mississippi. Combined with hard-fought land campaigns in Virginia and the interior, the strategy contributed decisively to Union victory in 1865.
