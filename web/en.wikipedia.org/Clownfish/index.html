FamilyPomacentridae (damselfishes)

Clownfish or anemonefish are fishes from the subfamily Amphiprioninae in the family Pomacentridae. Thirty species are recognized: one in the genus Premnas, the rest in the genus Amphiprion. In the wild, they all form symbiotic mutualisms with sea anemones, sheltering among the anemone's stinging tentacles, to which they are protected by a mucus coat. The best-known species, the ocellaris clownfish (Amphiprion ocellaris), is bright orange with three white bars and became famous worldwide through the film Finding Nemo (2003).

Ecology and behaviour

Clownfish live in small groups within a single anemone, with a strict size-based dominance hierarchy. They are protandrous hermaphrodites: all are born male, and the dominant individual becomes female; if she dies, the largest male changes sex and takes her place. Pairs lay eggs on a flat surface near the anemone, and the male guards and fans them until hatching. The fish defend their host vigorously and in return provide the anemone with nutrients and protection from polyp-eating fish; chemicals in the fish's mucus prevent the anemone from firing its nematocysts.

Distribution

Clownfish inhabit shallow tropical reefs from the Red Sea and East Africa across the Indian Ocean to the western and central Pacific, including the Great Barrier Reef. They are absent from the Atlantic. Different species specialize on particular host anemone species and depth ranges.

In aquaria

Clownfish are among the most popular marine aquarium fish and are now widely captive-bred, reducing pressure on wild populations. Demand surged after Finding Nemo, and selective breeding has produced many colour varieties unknown in the wild.
