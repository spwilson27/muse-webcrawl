Code of Hammurabi

Code of Hammurabi
[Image: the basalt stele of Hammurabi in the Louvre]
Created: c. 1755–1750 BC
Location: Louvre Museum, Paris (Sb 8)
Author: Hammurabi, king of Babylon
Medium: Basalt stele, 2.25 m tall
Content: 282 laws in Akkadian cuneiform

The Code of Hammurabi is a Babylonian legal text composed during 1755–1750 BC. It is the longest, best-organised, and best-preserved legal text from the ancient Near East. It is written in the Old Babylonian dialect of Akkadian, purportedly by Hammurabi, sixth king of the First Dynasty of Babylon. The primary copy of the text is inscribed on a basalt stele 2.25 metres (7.4 ft) tall, topped by a relief showing Hammurabi receiving the laws from Shamash, the Babylonian god of justice.

Content

The code lists 282 laws dealing with matters such as contracts, wages, property, family law, inheritance, assault, and professional liability. Punishments were graded by the social status of the parties — the code distinguishes between the awīlum (free man), the muškēnum (dependent), and the wardum (slave). It is famous for the principle of lex talionis, "an eye for an eye", though in practice many offences were settled with fines. It also contains early examples of the presumption of innocence and the use of evidence in trials.

Discovery

The stele was rediscovered in 1901–1902 at Susa in modern Iran, where it had been carried as war booty by the Elamite king Shutruk-Nakhunte in the 12th century BC. It was excavated by a French team under Jacques de Morgan and translated by Jean-Vincent Scheil. It is now displayed in the Louvre Museum in Paris, with replicas in museums and institutions worldwide.

Influence

The code influenced later legal traditions of the Near East and remains an iconic symbol of law, displayed for example in the chamber of the United States House of Representatives among reliefs of history's great lawgivers. Scholars debate whether it functioned as a practical statute book or as royal propaganda demonstrating the king's justice; its provisions were rarely cited in surviving court records.
