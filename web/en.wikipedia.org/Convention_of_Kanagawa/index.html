Convention of Kanagawa

The Convention of Kanagawa, also known as the Kanagawa Treaty, was an agreement between the United States of America and the government of Japan (the Tokugawa shogunate), signed on March 31, 1854, at Kanagawa-juku (now part of Yokohama). It is sometimes called the Perry Convention after its negotiator, Commodore Matthew Perry of the United States Navy. The convention opened the Japanese ports of Shimoda and Hakodate to American ships and began the end of Japan's policy of national seclusion (sakoku), which had lasted more than 200 years.

Terms

Perry first arrived in Uraga, in Edo Bay, in July 1853 with a squadron of American warships — the famous "black ships" — and delivered a letter from President Millard Fillmore requesting trade and humane treatment for shipwrecked sailors. Returning in February 1854 with a larger fleet, he negotiated the treaty. Its twelve articles provided that American vessels could obtain provisions, water, fuel and other necessities at Shimoda and Hakodate; that shipwrecked Americans would be treated humanely and returned; and that an American consul could reside at Shimoda after 18 months. It did not yet provide for general trade.

Consequences

The convention was followed by the Treaty of Amity and Commerce of 1858 (the Harris Treaty), which opened additional ports to actual commerce. Similar treaties were concluded with Britain, Russia, France and the Netherlands. The opening of Japan destabilized the Tokugawa shogunate, contributed to the Boshin War and the Meiji Restoration of 1868, and began Japan's rapid engagement with the wider world.
