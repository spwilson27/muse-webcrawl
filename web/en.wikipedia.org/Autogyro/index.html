Autogyro

A modern autogyro (gyrocopter) in flight.

An autogyro (from Greek αὐτός and γῦρος, "self-turning"), also known as a gyroplane or gyrocopter, is a type of rotorcraft that uses an unpowered rotor in free autorotation to develop lift. While similar to a helicopter rotor in appearance, the autogyro's unpowered rotor disc must have air flowing upward through it to make it rotate. Forward thrust is provided by a separate propeller, similar to that of a fixed-wing aircraft.

History

The autogyro was invented by Spanish engineer Juan de la Cierva in the early 1920s; his C.4 autogiro made successful flights in January 1923. Cierva's designs were built under licence in Britain and the United States, where Harold Pitcairn developed the type further. Although largely eclipsed by the helicopter in the 1940s, small autogyros enjoyed a revival from the 1960s as homebuilt and factory-built recreational aircraft.

Flight characteristics

Because the rotor is driven by the airflow, an autogyro cannot stall in the fixed-wing sense and can take off and land in very short distances, though a true vertical takeoff is not possible except with a powered pre-rotator and jump-takeoff rotor systems. Autogyros fly at modest speeds and are noted for stable, gentle handling in turbulent air.
