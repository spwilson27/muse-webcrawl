Kings of Rome

The kings of Rome (reges Romae) were the rulers of the Roman Kingdom from the legendary founding of Rome in 753 BC until the overthrow of Lucius Tarquinius Superbus in 509 BC and the establishment of the Roman Republic. Roman tradition lists seven kings, whose reigns span 244 years. Modern historians treat the earliest kings as legendary but accept that Rome was genuinely ruled by kings — Latin, Sabine and Etruscan — in its archaic period.

The seven kings

  • Romulus (753–717 BC) — legendary founder and first king; credited with the asylum, the rape of the Sabine women, and the first Senate.
  • Numa Pompilius (717–673 BC) — the Sabine lawgiver-king who founded Rome's religious institutions, calendar and priesthoods.
  • Tullus Hostilius (673–642 BC) — the warlike king who destroyed Alba Longa; the combat of the Horatii and Curiatii belongs to his reign.
  • Ancus Marcius (642–617 BC) — grandson of Numa; builder of the port of Ostia and the first bridge over the Tiber.
  • Lucius Tarquinius Priscus (616–579 BC) — the first Etruscan king; builder of the Circus Maximus and the Cloaca Maxima, and enlarger of the Forum.
  • Servius Tullius (578–535 BC) — credited with the Servian constitution: the census, the centuriate assembly, the tribes and the city wall.
  • Lucius Tarquinius Superbus ("Tarquin the Proud", 535–509 BC) — the tyrant whose son Sextus's rape of Lucretia provoked the revolution led by Brutus and Collatinus, ending the monarchy.

The office

The king (rex) was elected for life by the comitia curiata on nomination of the Senate during an interregnum, and held imperium — combined military, judicial and religious authority, symbolised by the fasces and the curule chair. After 509 BC the Republic preserved religious remnants of the office in the rex sacrorum, while the title rex became anathema in Roman politics.

Historicity

Archaeology confirms an archaic monarchy: 6th-century BC inscriptions such as the Lapis Niger cippus name a rex, and the great temples and drainage works of the Tarquin period are real. The individual biographies, reign lengths and dates, however, come from Livy, Dionysius of Halicarnassus and Plutarch, writing centuries later, and mix myth with fact.
