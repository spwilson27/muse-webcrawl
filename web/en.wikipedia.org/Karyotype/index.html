A karyotype is the general appearance of the complete set of chromosomes in the cells of a species or an individual organism, mainly including their sizes, numbers, and shapes. Karyotyping is the process by which a karyotype is discerned by determining the chromosome complement of an individual, including the number of chromosomes and any abnormalities.

Preparation and analysis

A karyotype is typically prepared from cells arrested in metaphase of mitosis (often white blood cells cultured from a blood sample, or fetal cells from amniocentesis). Chromosomes are stained — classically with Giemsa to produce G-banding patterns — photographed, and arranged in pairs by size and centromere position into a karyogram. The standard human karyotype has 46 chromosomes: 44 autosomes and two sex chromosomes.

Notation

Karyotypes are described using the International System for Human Cytogenomic Nomenclature (ISCN): total chromosome count, sex chromosome complement, then abnormalities — e.g. 47,XY,+21 for a male with trisomy 21 (Down syndrome); 45,X for Turner syndrome; 47,XXY for Klinefelter syndrome.

Applications

Karyotyping is used to detect chromosomal abnormalities in prenatal and postnatal diagnosis, infertility and miscarriage work-ups, and cancer cytogenetics — famously the Philadelphia chromosome translocation t(9;22) in chronic myeloid leukaemia. Comparative karyotyping across species also informs evolutionary biology, since chromosome number and structure vary widely among organisms.
