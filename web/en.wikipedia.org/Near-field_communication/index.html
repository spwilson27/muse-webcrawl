Near-field communication

Near-field communication (NFC) is a set of communication protocols that enable two electronic devices to communicate when they are brought within about 4 cm of each other. Operating at 13.56 MHz in the globally available ISM band, NFC supports data rates of 106 to 848 kbit/s and is standardised in ISO/IEC 18092 and related specifications.

Technology

NFC evolved from radio-frequency identification (RFID) technology and was developed principally by Philips (later NXP Semiconductors) and Sony. One device acts as an initiator and generates a radio-frequency field that can power a passive target, such as a tag or smart card, which therefore needs no battery. NFC devices can operate in three modes: reader/writer mode, card emulation mode, and peer-to-peer mode.

Applications

  • Contactless payments — mobile wallets such as Apple Pay, Google Pay and Samsung Pay emulate payment cards using NFC.
  • Transit and access — fare cards and key fobs based on technologies such as FeliCa and MIFARE interoperate with NFC readers.
  • Tags and pairing — passive NFC tags can encode URLs, Wi-Fi credentials or trigger actions; NFC also simplifies Bluetooth and Wi-Fi pairing.
  • Identity documents — e-passports and national ID cards can be read via NFC interfaces.

Security

NFC's very short range limits casual eavesdropping, though relay attacks and eavesdropping at greater distances with specialised equipment have been demonstrated. Sensitive applications therefore add encryption and secure elements on top of the NFC link.
