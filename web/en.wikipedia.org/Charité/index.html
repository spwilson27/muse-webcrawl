The Charité – Universitätsmedizin Berlin is one of Europe's largest university hospitals, affiliated with Humboldt University and the Free University of Berlin. Founded in 1710 by King Frederick I of Prussia as a plague house outside the city gates, it became a military teaching hospital and, from 1746, bore the name Charité ("charity"). Its medical faculty is among the most highly regarded in Germany, and Charité consistently ranks among the world's leading hospitals.

History

Figures associated with Charité include the pathologist Rudolf Virchow, founder of cellular pathology, who worked in its pathology institute; Robert Koch, who won the Nobel Prize for identifying the tuberculosis bacillus while based in Berlin; and Ferdinand Sauerbruch, a pioneer of thoracic surgery. More than half of all German Nobel Prize winners in Physiology or Medicine, including Emil von Behring and Paul Ehrlich, came from Charité. During the division of Berlin the hospital stood in East Berlin; after reunification it merged with the medical faculties of both universities, formally uniting in 2003.

Today

Charité operates four campuses — Campus Charité Mitte, Campus Benjamin Franklin, Campus Virchow-Klinikum, and Campus Berlin Buch — with around 3,300 beds, some 100 clinics and institutes, and about 8,000 students. Roughly half of Berlin's hospital physicians and around 13,000 nursing staff contribute to its care of approximately 142,000 inpatients and 700,000 outpatients annually. During the COVID-19 pandemic, Charité virologist Christian Drosten's laboratory developed one of the first SARS-CoV-2 PCR diagnostic tests, distributed worldwide through the WHO.
