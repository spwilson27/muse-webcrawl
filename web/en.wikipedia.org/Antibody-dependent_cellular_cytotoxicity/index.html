Antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity

Antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC), also called antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity, is an immune defence mechanism in which an effector cell of the immune system actively lyses a target cell whose surface antigens have been bound by specific antibodies.

Mechanism

Antibodies — principally IgG — coat the target cell, binding it with their Fab regions. Effector cells, above all natural killer (NK) cells but also macrophages, monocytes, neutrophils and eosinophils, attach to the antibodies' exposed Fc regions through Fc receptors (CD16/FcγRIII on NK cells). Cross-linking triggers the effector cell to release cytotoxic granules containing perforin and granzymes, killing the target — for example a virus-infected or tumour cell — without the effector cell needing to recognise the antigen itself.

Medical significance

ADCC is one of the mechanisms by which therapeutic monoclonal antibodies work: rituximab, trastuzumab and cetuximab all recruit NK-cell killing of antibody-tagged cancer cells, and engineering the Fc region to bind CD16 more strongly ("afucosylation") can enhance the effect. Parasites too large to phagocytose are attacked by eosinophil-mediated ADCC, and the process also features in transplant rejection and some viral infections.
