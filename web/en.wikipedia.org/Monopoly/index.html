Monopoly

A monopoly (from Greek monos, "alone" or "single", and polein, "to sell") is a market structure in which a single firm or entity is the sole provider of a particular product or service, dominating the market and facing no competition from other producers. Because the monopolist is the only supplier, it can influence the price of its product by controlling the quantity supplied, although in practice its pricing power is limited by consumer demand, potential substitutes, and government regulation.

A monopolist faces the entire market demand curve for its product.

Characteristics

The defining features of a monopoly include: a single seller serving the whole market; a unique product with no close substitutes available to buyers; significant barriers preventing other firms from entering the market; and substantial control over price, with the firm acting as a price maker rather than a price taker. The monopolist nevertheless faces a downward-sloping demand curve, so raising price reduces the quantity consumers are willing to buy.

Causes and barriers to entry

Monopolies arise from barriers to entry that prevent competitors from entering the market. These include:

  • Control of key resources — a single firm may own an essential input, as when one company historically controlled most of the world's bauxite supply for aluminium production.
  • Government-created monopolies — patents, copyrights, and licenses grant exclusive rights, deliberately creating temporary monopolies to reward innovation.
  • Economies of scale — when one firm can supply the entire market at lower cost than two or more firms, a natural monopoly emerges, common in utilities such as water and electricity distribution.
  • Network effects — a product becomes more valuable as more people use it, entrenching the incumbent.

Economic effects

Standard economic analysis holds that, compared with a competitive market, a monopolist restricts output and charges a higher price, transferring surplus from consumers to the producer and creating a deadweight loss — mutually beneficial trades that never occur. Monopolies may also engage in price discrimination, charging different prices to different buyers. Defenders argue that monopoly profits can fund research and development, and that the prospect of monopoly rents motivates innovation.

Regulation and antitrust

Governments respond to monopoly power through competition (antitrust) law, regulation of prices and service quality, and occasionally public ownership. Landmark cases include the breakup of Standard Oil in 1911 under the United States Sherman Antitrust Act of 1890, and the dissolution of AT&T's telephone monopoly in 1984. Modern competition authorities, such as the European Commission and the U.S. Federal Trade Commission, review mergers and police abuses of dominant positions.

History

State-granted monopolies were a familiar feature of mercantilist economies: monarchs sold exclusive trading rights, exemplified by chartered companies such as the British and Dutch East India Companies. Adam Smith criticized monopolies in The Wealth of Nations (1776), arguing they kept prices artificially high. The industrial era produced vast private monopolies and trusts in oil, steel, railroads, and tobacco, prompting the first modern antitrust statutes.
