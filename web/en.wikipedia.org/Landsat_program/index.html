The Landsat program is the longest-running enterprise for acquisition of satellite imagery of Earth. Jointly managed by NASA and the U.S. Geological Survey, it has flown a series of satellites since 1972 — originally called the Earth Resources Technology Satellites (ERTS) — that image the entire land surface of the planet roughly every 16 days, building an unmatched continuous archive of medium-resolution land imagery.

History

Inspired by handheld photographs from Mercury and Gemini missions, the programme was championed within the Department of the Interior by Secretary Stewart Udall. Landsat 1 carried a Multispectral Scanner (MSS) producing imagery in four bands at about 80 m resolution. Landsats 4 and 5 (1982, 1984) added the Thematic Mapper at 30 m; Landsat 7 (1999) added a 15 m panchromatic band, though a 2003 scan-line corrector failure left gaps in its images. Landsat 6 was lost at launch in 1993. Landsat 8 (2013) and Landsat 9 (2021) carry the Operational Land Imager (OLI) and Thermal Infrared Sensor (TIRS), and together give an 8-day revisit. The next mission, Landsat Next, is planned as a three-satellite constellation with finer resolution and many more spectral bands.

Applications

Landsat data underpin land-cover mapping, deforestation and wildfire monitoring, agricultural yield assessment, glacier and coastline change studies, urban growth analysis, water-resource management and disaster response. The 2008 decision to make the entire USGS archive free transformed usage from tens of thousands of scenes sold per year to millions downloaded annually, and Landsat time series are now a standard input to global environmental science — often combined with the European Sentinel-2 satellites.
