Philippine adobo is a popular Filipino dish and cooking process in Philippine cuisine that involves meat, seafood or vegetables marinated and simmered in vinegar, soy sauce, garlic, bay leaves and black peppercorns, then browned in oil. It is frequently considered the unofficial national dish of the Philippines. The name comes from the Spanish adobar, "to marinate," but the method itself is indigenous: pre-colonial Filipinos cooked and preserved food in vinegar and salt, a technique suited to a tropical climate, well before Spanish colonisation began in 1565.

History

When Spanish colonisers observed Filipinos stewing food in vinegar, they applied their word adobo — used in Spain and Latin America for a marinade of vinegar, oil, paprika and herbs — to the superficially similar Filipino dish. The two preparations are otherwise unrelated. Chinese traders introduced soy sauce, which most modern Filipino versions incorporate, and regional ingredients such as coconut milk (in the Bicol region), turmeric, annatto, pineapple, potatoes and sugar have produced countless local and household variations. Because every Filipino family prepares it differently, adobo is often described as having as many recipes as there are cooks in the Philippines.

Preparation

The classic method marinates chicken, pork, or a combination of both, in vinegar, soy sauce, crushed garlic, bay leaves and whole peppercorns, then simmers the meat in the marinade until tender and either fries it briefly to brown and crisp the edges, reducing the sauce, or serves it in its braising liquid. It is almost always eaten with steamed white rice, which balances the dish's saltiness and acidity. The acid of the vinegar inhibits spoilage, so adobo keeps well without refrigeration and is said to taste better the next day.

Variations

  • Adobong puti ("white adobo") — omits soy sauce, using salt or fish sauce instead; considered a very old form of the dish.
  • Adobo sa gata — adds coconut milk, common in Bicol and southern Luzon, often with chillies.
  • Adobong dilaw ("yellow adobo") — coloured and flavoured with turmeric, typical of Batangas.
  • Adobong pusit — squid cooked in its own ink with vinegar.
  • Adobong kangkong and other vegetable versions; adobong manok (chicken only); adobo flakes, shredded leftover adobo fried crisp and served at breakfast.

Cultural significance

Adobo is a fixture of Filipino home cooking, celebrations and roadside eateries (carinderias) alike, and a touchstone of identity for the Filipino diaspora. Its prominence has prompted periodic debate about "standardising" the recipe — in 2021 the Philippine Department of Trade and Industry proposed developing a national standard for adobo and other dishes for international promotion, a plan that drew criticism from chefs and the public who argued that adobo's diversity is its essence; the department clarified the standard would be voluntary. Adobo should not be confused with Spanish or Mexican adobo sauces or with the Puerto Rican dry rub adobo.
