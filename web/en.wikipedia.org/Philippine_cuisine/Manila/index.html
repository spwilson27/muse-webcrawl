Manila in Philippine cuisine

Manila, the capital of the Philippines, is the meeting point of the country's regional kitchens and the historical gateway through which foreign influences entered Filipino cooking. From the Chinese trading communities of Binondo — the world's oldest Chinatown — to the Spanish colonial tables of Intramuros, the city's food reflects five centuries of exchange layered over Malay foundations.

Signature institutions

Binondo's eateries popularised pancit, lumpia, hopia and other Hokkien-derived dishes now considered Filipino staples. The city's carinderias (small eateries) serve affordable ulam with rice to workers and students, while historic restaurants around Ermita and Malate helped define "classic" Manila dishes such as chicken relleno and kare-kare. The La Loma district is famous as Manila's lechon capital, its streets lined with roasting stalls since the early twentieth century.

Street food

Manila street food is a cuisine in itself: grilled offal skewers (isaw, betamax), fish balls and squid balls with sweet-spicy sauce, kwek-kwek (battered quail eggs), balut, and taho sold by morning vendors. University districts and transport hubs anchor dense clusters of vendors.
