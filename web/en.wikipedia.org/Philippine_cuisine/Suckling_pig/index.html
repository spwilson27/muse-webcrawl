Suckling pig in Philippine cuisine

The suckling pig — a piglet still fed on its mother's milk, typically two to six weeks old — is the original form of the Filipino lechon. The very word lechon derives from the Spanish leche (milk), and the Spanish colonial dish of roast suckling pig (cochinillo) was adopted and transformed in the Philippines with local aromatics and charcoal roasting.

Lechon de leche

In the Philippines the small suckling-pig version is called lechon de leche and is prized for exceptionally tender meat and thin, shatteringly crisp skin. Modern commercial lechon more often uses larger young pigs (20–40 kg) for economy, but de leche remains the prestige choice for weddings and baptisms. Puerto Rican lechón and Balinese babi guling are regional cousins of the same roasting tradition.

Preparation

The piglet is cleaned, stuffed with lemongrass, tamarind leaves, garlic and pepper, sewn shut, and roasted on a spit over charcoal for several hours, basted occasionally with oil or milk to deepen the skin's colour. It is served chopped, with liver sauce or spiced vinegar, alongside rice and sour soups such as sinigang.
