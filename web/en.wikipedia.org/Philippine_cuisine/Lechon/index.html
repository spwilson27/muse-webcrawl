Lechon

Lechon (from Spanish lechón, "suckling pig") is a whole roasted pig dish popular in the Philippines and other former Spanish colonies. In the Philippines it is considered the centerpiece of festive celebrations, fiestas, birthdays, weddings, and holidays such as Christmas and New Year's Eve. The pig is roasted slowly over charcoal until the skin is crisp and golden while the meat remains tender and juicy.

Preparation

A whole pig, usually a young one, is cleaned, seasoned inside with salt, pepper, garlic, lemongrass, bay leaves, and sometimes star anise or spring onions, then sewn shut and skewered on a bamboo pole. It is roasted over charcoal for several hours, turned continuously so the skin cooks evenly. The prized result is the crackling, blistered skin, served with the meat and a liver-based dipping sauce known as sarsa.

Regional varieties

Cebu is especially famed for its lechon, often described as needing no sauce because the aromatics stuffed into the cavity season the meat thoroughly. In Manila, lechon is commonly served with a sweet liver sauce. Balayan and La Loma in Quezon City are well-known lechon hubs. Related dishes include lechon kawali (deep-fried pork belly) and lechon manok (roasted chicken).

Cultural significance

Lechon symbolizes abundance and celebration in Filipino cuisine. The dish has pre-colonial roots in indigenous roasting practices, adapted and named under Spanish rule. Leftover lechon is commonly repurposed into paksiw na lechon, a stew simmered in vinegar and liver sauce.
