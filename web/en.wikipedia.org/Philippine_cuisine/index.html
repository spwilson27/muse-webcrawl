Filipino cuisine

Classic Filipino dishes served with steamed rice.

Filipino cuisine (Lutuing Pilipino/Pagkaing Pilipino) is composed of the cuisines of more than a hundred distinct ethnolinguistic groups found throughout the Philippine archipelago. A majority of mainstream Filipino dishes that compose Filipino cuisine are from the cuisines of the various ethnolinguistic groups and tribes of the archipelago, including the Ilocano, Pangasinan, Kapampangan, Tagalog, Bicolano, Visayan, Chavacano and Maranao ethnolinguistic groups.

The style of food making and the food associated with it have evolved over centuries from Austronesian origins (shared with Malaysian and Indonesian cuisines) to a mixed cuisine of Japanese, Chinese (especially Hokkien), Spanish, and American influences, adapted to indigenous ingredients and the local palate.

Characteristics

Filipino cuisine is characterised by a contrast of flavours — salty, sour, sweet and spicy, sometimes in a single dish. Vinegar (suka) and soy sauce form the base of adobo, often called the unofficial national dish, in which meat is braised in vinegar, soy sauce, garlic, bay leaves and black pepper. Sour soups such as sinigang (flavoured with tamarind) and Bicolano and Kapampangan dishes made with coconut milk are other staples. Rice accompanies nearly every meal, and communal eating — including the boodle fight (kamayan, eating with the hands from banana leaves) — is traditional.

Popular dishes

  • Adobo — meat or seafood braised in vinegar and soy sauce
  • Sinigang — sour tamarind soup with pork, shrimp or fish
  • Lechon — whole roasted pig, centrepiece of festivals
  • Kare-kare — oxtail stew in peanut sauce
  • Pancit — noodle dishes of Chinese origin, e.g. pancit Malabon, pancit palabok
  • Lumpia — spring rolls, fresh or fried
  • Sisig — sizzling chopped pork from Pampanga
  • Halo-halo — shaved-ice dessert with sweet beans, jellies, leche flan and ube

Historical influences

Chinese traders introduced noodles, soy sauce, tofu and spring rolls; over 300 years of Spanish colonization (1565–1898) added tomatoes, garlic fried in oil (gisa), relleno, afritada and many festival dishes, as well as vocabulary — most Filipino dish names of Spanish origin describe cooking methods rather than Spanish dishes. American colonization (1898–1946) brought canned goods, hot dogs, hamburgers and sweet spaghetti, while regional cuisines in the Muslim south preserve older Malay and spice-trade traditions largely untouched by Spain.
