Philippine cuisine

Philippine cuisine is the traditional food culture of the Philippines, shaped by Austronesian foundations and by Malay, Chinese, Spanish, and American influences. Its celebratory dishes — above all lechón, the whole roasted pig served at fiestas and family gatherings, and sinigang, the sour soup that anchors everyday meals — are frequently cited as the twin emblems of Filipino cooking.

Signature dishes

Sinigang is a soup soured most often with tamarind, into which pork, shrimp, or fish is simmered with tomatoes, onions, labanos (radish), kangkong (water spinach), and sitaw (long beans). Variants use guava, calamansi, kamias, or green mango as the souring agent, and regional versions appear throughout Luzon and the Visayas.

Lechón is a suckling or adult pig roasted whole over charcoal, stuffed with lemongrass and aromatics, producing crisp skin and tender meat. It descends from Spanish cochinillo traditions but is prepared and seasoned in a distinctly Filipino manner, served with liver-based sauce and eaten at weddings, baptisms, and town fiestas. The lechón of Cebu is particularly celebrated.

Place in the cuisine

Together with adobo, kare-kare, and lumpia, sinigang and lechón form the core repertoire of dishes by which Philippine cuisine is known abroad. Their contrasting profiles — sour and soupy against rich and roasted — illustrate the Filipino palate's love of contrast between the sour (asim), salty, and fatty.
