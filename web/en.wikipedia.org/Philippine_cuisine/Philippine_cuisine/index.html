Philippine cuisine

Philippine cuisine (Filipino: lutuing Pilipino/pagkaing Pilipino) is composed of the cuisines of more than a hundred distinct ethnolinguistic groups spread across the Philippine archipelago. It has evolved over centuries from its Austronesian origins to a mixed cuisine of Malay, Spanish, Chinese, and American influences, adapted to local ingredients and tastes.

History and influences

Pre-colonial cooking relied on rice, root crops, seafood, coconut, and souring agents such as vinegar and tamarind. Trade with China introduced noodles, soy products, and lumpia; three centuries of Spanish colonization brought tomatoes, garlic, onions, and dishes such as adobo, lechón, and afritada, though these were transformed by local methods. American rule added canned goods, sandwiches, and fast-food habits, and the postwar period saw Filipino cuisine become a point of national pride at home and abroad.

Characteristic dishes

Staple dishes include adobo (meat braised in vinegar and soy sauce), sinigang (a sour tamarind soup), lechón (whole roasted pig), kare-kare (oxtail in peanut sauce), lumpia (spring rolls), pancit (noodles), and sisig (chopped seasoned pork). Rice is eaten at essentially every meal, often with a dip of soy sauce, calamansi, or patis (fish sauce). Desserts and snacks — merienda — feature kakanin rice cakes, halo-halo, and bibingka.

Eating customs

Meals traditionally emphasize a balance of salty, sour, and sweet flavors, and many Filipinos still eat kamayan-style, with the hands, on special occasions. Regional cuisines vary widely: Bicol is known for chili and coconut milk, the Ilocos region for bitter and fermented flavors, and Muslim Mindanao for turmeric-rich dishes influenced by Malay cooking.
