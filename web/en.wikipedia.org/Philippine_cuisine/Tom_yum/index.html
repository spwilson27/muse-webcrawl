Tom yum and Philippine cuisine

Tom yum, the Thai hot-and-sour soup, is the closest Southeast Asian relative of the Filipino sinigang, and comparisons between them are a staple of writing on Philippine cuisine. Both are sour seafood or meat broths built on local aromatics, reflecting the shared Austronesian and maritime heritage of the region's food (see Philippine cuisine in ASEAN).

Similarities and differences

Tom yum is soured with lime juice and flavoured with lemongrass, galangal, kaffir lime leaves and chilli; sinigang is soured with tamarind or other fruits and flavoured with ginger, tomatoes and fish sauce, with chilli optional. Sinigang is typically heartier and more vegetable-laden, eaten as a main dish with rice, while tom yum is often a starter. Tom yum's global fame has led some Filipino restaurants abroad to market sinigang as "Filipino tom yum," a comparison many Filipino cooks resist because of the dishes' distinct souring profiles.

Reception in the Philippines

Thai restaurants in Manila serve tom yum widely, and fusion versions — sinigang with lemongrass, or tom yum with tamarind — appear on modern menus in both countries.
