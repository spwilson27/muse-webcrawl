The Philippines in Philippine cuisine

The Philippines — an archipelago of more than 7,600 islands — is both the homeland and the defining context of Philippine cuisine. Geography explains much of the cuisine's character: a maritime Austronesian base of rice, fish, coconut and fermentation, enriched by Chinese trade, overlaid by three centuries of Spanish colonisation and a half-century of American rule, and diversified by more than a hundred ethnolinguistic groups.

Archipelagic diversity

Each island group developed its own pantry. Luzon contributed Kapampangan fiesta cooking and Ilocano fermented-fish dishes; the Visayas are famed for lechon and chicken inasal; Mindanao preserves older Malay-Islamic foodways centred on turmeric, palapa and coconut. Inter-island migration and Manila's pull as capital have nationalised many regional dishes — adobo, sinigang, kare-kare and lumpia are eaten everywhere, each with local variations.

Rice and the meal structure

Rice is the non-negotiable centre of the Filipino meal; viands (ulam) are judged by how well they accompany it, and the phrase kanin at ulam (rice and viand) describes the basic unit of eating. Fermentation — of rice, fish, shrimp and coconut sap — is a preservation technology suited to the tropical archipelago and a signature of its flavour profile.
