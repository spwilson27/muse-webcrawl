Wired Equivalent Privacy

Wired Equivalent Privacy (WEP) is a security algorithm for IEEE 802.11 wireless networks, introduced in 1997 as part of the original Wi-Fi standard. It was designed to give wireless links confidentiality comparable to a wired connection, but serious cryptographic flaws made it breakable within minutes, and it was formally deprecated in 2004 in favor of WPA and WPA2.

Introduced1997 (IEEE 802.11)
CipherRC4 stream cipher
Key sizes40-bit ("64-bit WEP") or 104-bit ("128-bit")
StatusDeprecated 2004; insecure

Design

WEP encrypts each packet with the RC4 stream cipher, using a key formed by concatenating a static shared secret with a 24-bit initialization vector (IV) sent in cleartext. A CRC-32 checksum provided data integrity. Export restrictions of the era encouraged the short 40-bit version of the key.

Weaknesses

The tiny 24-bit IV space guarantees reuse after a few thousand packets, and RC4 key-scheduling weaknesses — publicized by the Fluhrer, Mantin, and Shamir attack in 2001 — let passive listeners recover the key from captured traffic. Later improvements reduced cracking to under a minute with tools such as Aircrack-ng. The CRC-32 integrity check is linear and permits undetected packet modification. The Wi-Fi Alliance retired WEP in 2004, and its use is prohibited under the PCI DSS payment-card standard.

Legacy

WEP remains a canonical classroom example of cryptographic design failure: short IVs, key reuse, unauthenticated encryption, and reliance on secrecy of a widely shared static key. It was replaced by WPA (TKIP as an interim measure) and then WPA2 with AES.
