Yungang Grottoes

Yungang Grottoes
Location: Datong, Shanxi, China
Built: c. 460 – 525 CE, Northern Wei dynasty
Caves: 253 grottoes, c. 51,000 statues
Status: UNESCO World Heritage Site (2001)

The Yungang Grottoes (Chinese: 雲岡石窟) are ancient Chinese Buddhist temple grottoes carved into the sandstone cliffs near Datong in Shanxi province. With about 51,000 statues in 253 caves, they are one of China's great cave-temple complexes, alongside Mogao and Longmen.

History

Carving began around 460 CE under the Northern Wei dynasty, after Emperor Wencheng restored Buddhism following its persecution under Emperor Taiwu. The monk Tanyao proposed five imperial caves (caves 16–20), each dominated by a colossal Buddha identified with a Northern Wei emperor — the tallest about 17 metres. A second phase under Emperor Xiaowen added richly sculpted paired caves as the capital remained at Pingcheng (Datong) until its move to Luoyang in 494; smaller caves by nobles and commoners continued into the 520s.

Art

Yungang sculpture fuses Indian Gupta forms, Gandharan and Central Asian motifs travelling the Silk Road, and emerging Chinese style — visible in the shift from massive, foreign-featured Buddhas of the Tanyao caves to the slender, Chinese-garbed figures of later grottoes. Cave 6 is famed for its relief cycle of the Buddha's life around a central pagoda pillar; caves 5–6 and the "Five Magnificent Caves" preserve traces of original pigment and (in some) gilding.

Preservation

Weathering, water seepage, and coal-dust pollution from Datong's mines long threatened the stone; major conservation and a tourist-centre relocation were undertaken before and after UNESCO listing in 2001.
