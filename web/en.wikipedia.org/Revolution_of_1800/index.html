The Revolution of 1800 is the name Thomas Jefferson gave to the United States presidential election of 1800, in which his Democratic-Republican Party defeated the incumbent Federalist administration of John Adams. It is regarded as the first peaceful transfer of power from one political party to another under the US Constitution, and a landmark in the history of democracy.

Background

The election took place against the backdrop of the Quasi-War with France, bitter partisan conflict, and the Federalist Alien and Sedition Acts of 1798, which Jeffersonians condemned as unconstitutional repression of opposition speech. The campaign of 1800 was notoriously vicious: Federalists warned that Jefferson was an atheist and a Jacobin, while Republicans accused Adams of monarchism.

The electoral crisis

Under the original Constitution, electors cast two undifferentiated votes, and Jefferson and his running mate Aaron Burr each received 73 electoral votes, ahead of Adams (65) and Charles Cotesworth Pinckney (64). Because the tie was between the two Republicans, the choice fell to the outgoing, Federalist-controlled House of Representatives. The House deadlocked through 35 ballots in February 1801 before Federalist abstentions on the 36th ballot elected Jefferson. The crisis directly produced the Twelfth Amendment (ratified 1804), requiring separate electoral ballots for president and vice president.

Significance

Jefferson called the result "as real a revolution in the principles of our government as that of 1776 was in its form," effected "by the rational and peaceful instruments of reform, the suffrage of the people." His administration reversed Federalist policies — letting the Sedition Act expire, pardoning those convicted under it, cutting taxes and the military, and reducing the national debt — and the Federalists never regained the presidency. Historians emphasise the deeper precedent: the defeated party surrendered power peacefully, demonstrating that constitutional government could survive partisan change.
