Lie detection

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Lie detection is the attempt to determine, through observation or measurement, whether a person is telling the truth or deliberately deceiving. Because no single physiological response is uniquely associated with lying, all lie-detection methods are probabilistic assessments of behaviour, physiological arousal, or verbal content, and their accuracy is a subject of continuing scientific controversy.

Polygraph

The best-known technique is the polygraph, which records respiration, skin conductance (sweating), blood pressure, and pulse while an examiner asks questions, typically using a comparison-question format: responses to relevant questions are compared with responses to "control" questions intended to provoke a reaction in truthful subjects. A 2003 review by the U.S. National Research Council concluded that polygraph testing, especially in screening settings with low base rates of deception, has insufficient accuracy to justify its use for security screening, a view shared by many scientific bodies; polygraph results are generally inadmissible in courts in many jurisdictions, though the United States Supreme Court left the question to the states in United States v. Scheffer (1998).

Other approaches

  • Voice stress analysis: claims to detect microtremors in the voice; controlled studies have found performance near chance.
  • Brain-based methods: fMRI studies report brain regions associated with deception tasks, and EEG-based "brain fingerprinting" (P300 responses) attempts to detect recognition of concealed information; these remain research tools with debated real-world validity.
  • Concealed information test: rather than lying per se, tests whether a subject recognizes crime-relevant details known only to investigators and the perpetrator; it is used in some countries, notably Japan, and is considered by researchers to have a stronger theoretical basis than the comparison-question polygraph.
  • Behavioural and verbal analysis: methods such as statement validity analysis, criteria-based content analysis, and observation of behaviour; meta-analyses find that unaided human lie judgments average only slightly above chance (about 54%).
  • Automated systems: thermal imaging, eye-tracking, and machine-learning analysis of facial micro-expressions have been explored, with claims of high accuracy in laboratory settings that often do not replicate in the field.

Criticism

Critics argue that arousal-based methods confuse anxiety with deception, producing false positives for nervous truth-tellers and false negatives for calm liars, and that trained countermeasures can defeat the tests. The scientific consensus is that there is no "Pinocchio response" — no known unique physiological signature of lying.
