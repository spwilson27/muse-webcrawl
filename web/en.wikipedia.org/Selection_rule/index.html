Selection rule

In physics and chemistry, a selection rule, or transition rule, formally defines the probability of a transition of a system from one state to another. Selection rules have been derived for electromagnetic transitions in molecules, atoms, atomic nuclei, and other systems, based on symmetry and conservation laws.

Atomic transitions

For electric dipole transitions in atoms, the selection rules require that the spin does not change (ΔS = 0), the orbital angular momentum changes by one unit (ΔL = ±1), and the total angular momentum changes by at most one (ΔJ = 0, ±1, except J = 0 to J = 0, which is forbidden). Transitions violating these rules are "forbidden" — not absolutely impossible, but far weaker, occurring through magnetic dipole or electric quadrupole radiation or over longer timescales.

Molecular spectroscopy

In vibrational spectroscopy, a vibration is infrared-active only if it changes the molecular dipole moment, and Raman-active only if it changes the polarizability. For centrosymmetric molecules, the rule of mutual exclusion holds: no vibration can be both infrared- and Raman-active.

Origin

Selection rules follow from the requirement that the transition moment integral does not vanish by symmetry, and from conservation of angular momentum carried by the emitted or absorbed photon.
