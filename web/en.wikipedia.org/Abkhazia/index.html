Abkhazia

Abkhazia is a partially recognised state in the South Caucasus, on the eastern coast of the Black Sea. It considers itself an independent republic, but most of the international community regards it as part of Georgia. Only a handful of states, including Russia, recognise its independence, declared after the 1992–93 war with Georgia and reaffirmed following the 2008 Russo-Georgian War. Its capital and largest city is Sukhumi.

Geography

Abkhazia covers about 8,600 square kilometres between the Greater Caucasus mountains and the Black Sea. The climate is subtropical along the coast, which was famous in the Soviet era for resorts such as Gagra and Pitsunda and for tea and citrus cultivation. The population is roughly a quarter of a million, comprising Abkhaz, Armenians, Russians, Georgians, and others; the Abkhaz language belongs to the Northwest Caucasian family.

History

The region hosted Greek colonies in antiquity and the medieval Kingdom of Abkhazia, which helped unify Georgia in the 10th century. It later came under Ottoman influence and was annexed by the Russian Empire in the 19th century, when many Muslim Abkhaz emigrated. In the Soviet period Abkhazia was first an SSR, then an autonomous republic within the Georgian SSR (from 1931).

Conflict and status

As the USSR dissolved, tensions between the Abkhaz and Georgia escalated into war in 1992–93. Abkhaz forces, backed by North Caucasian volunteers and elements of the Russian military, took control of the territory; most ethnic Georgians — over 200,000 people — fled or were expelled. A ceasefire held uneasily until the August 2008 war, after which Russia recognised Abkhazia and stationed troops there. Georgia classifies Abkhazia as occupied territory. The unresolved status restricts Abkhazia's economy and international links, and the right of return of displaced Georgians remains a central dispute.
