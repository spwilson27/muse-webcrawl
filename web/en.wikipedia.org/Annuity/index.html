Annuity

Annuity
TypeFinancial / insurance product
ProvidersLife insurance companies
PurposeConvert a sum of money into a regular income, often for retirement

An annuity is a contract sold by financial institutions, usually life insurance companies, that provides a series of regular payments over a specified period, in exchange for an initial premium or series of contributions. Annuities are most commonly used to provide a guaranteed income in retirement: the purchaser (annuitant) hands over a lump sum and receives payments for a fixed term or for the rest of their life, thereby transferring the risk of outliving one's savings (longevity risk) to the insurer.

Types

  • Immediate vs deferred — payments begin at once, or after an accumulation phase during which contributions earn interest or investment returns.
  • Fixed vs variable — fixed annuities pay a guaranteed rate; variable annuities invest in sub-accounts (funds) so payments vary with performance; indexed annuities link returns to a market index.
  • Life annuity vs term-certain — payments last for the annuitant's lifetime (possibly with a guarantee period or a joint-life option for a spouse), or for a fixed number of years.

How annuities work

Pricing is based on mortality tables and interest rates: the insurer pools the funds of many annuitants, and those who die early subsidise those who live long — a mechanism known as mortality credits. The value of a series of equal payments is calculated using annuity formulae based on the time value of money (present value of an annuity), a standard topic in actuarial science and finance. Payouts depend on age, sex (where permitted), health (impaired-life or enhanced annuities pay more), interest rates and chosen options.

History and criticism

Annuity-like contracts date to Roman times (annua), and tontines and state annuities financed European governments in the 17th and 18th centuries; Edmond Halley's 1693 life table was devised for pricing them. Annuities have been criticised for complexity, high fees (especially variable annuities), inflexibility, and poor value when interest rates are low; conversely, economists note that guaranteed lifetime income is difficult to replicate with ordinary investments. In the United Kingdom, the 2015 "pension freedoms" ended compulsory annuity purchase and sales fell sharply.
