The Troglodytidae are a family of small, mostly brown passerine birds, the wrens. The family comprises roughly 96 species in 19 genera, almost entirely confined to the New World; the sole Old World representative is the Eurasian wren (Troglodytes troglodytes), widespread in Europe, Asia, and North Africa. The family name derives from the Greek trōglodytēs, "cave-dweller", referring to the birds' habit of creeping into crevices and building domed nests.

Description and behaviour

Wrens are small (10–22 cm), stocky insectivores with slender, slightly downcurved bills, short rounded wings, and tails often held cocked upright. They are renowned for songs of extraordinary power and complexity for their size; several tropical species sing duets, and males of some species build multiple "dummy" nests. Well-known members include the house wren, Carolina wren, winter wren, Bewick's wren, and the cactus wren of the American Southwest — the state bird of Arizona.

Distribution and evolution

The family's great diversity lies in Central and South America, where forest understory and montane species proliferate; only a handful of species breed in North America. Molecular studies suggest the lineage arose in the Americas, with the Eurasian wren crossing Beringia into the Old World. Many island and mountain populations are threatened by habitat loss, and a few species, such as the Zapata wren of Cuba, are endangered.
