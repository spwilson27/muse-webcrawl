Fair Housing Act

The Fair Housing Act is a United States federal law, enacted as Title VIII of the Civil Rights Act of 1968, that prohibits discrimination in the sale, rental and financing of housing. It was signed by President Lyndon B. Johnson on 11 April 1968, one week after the assassination of Martin Luther King Jr., whose death helped break a congressional deadlock over the bill.

Provisions

As originally enacted, the Act prohibited discrimination on the basis of race, colour, religion and national origin. Sex was added in 1974, and the Fair Housing Amendments Act of 1988 extended protection to people with disabilities and families with children, and strengthened enforcement. The Act makes it unlawful to refuse to sell or rent, to impose different terms, to advertise a preference, or to engage in practices such as blockbusting, steering and redlining that deny housing on a protected basis. It covers most housing, with limited exemptions for owner-occupied small buildings and single-family homes sold without a broker.

Enforcement

The Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD) administers the Act, investigates complaints and can refer cases to the Department of Justice. Aggrieved persons may also file suit in federal court. Enforcement was considered weak until the 1988 amendments added administrative law judge hearings and meaningful penalties. In 2015 the Supreme Court held in Texas Department of Housing and Community Affairs v. Inclusive Communities Project that disparate-impact claims are cognisable under the Act, allowing challenges to practices that disproportionately harm protected groups even without proof of intentional discrimination.
