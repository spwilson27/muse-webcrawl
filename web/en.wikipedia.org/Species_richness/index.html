Species richness

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Species richness is the number of different species represented in an ecological community, landscape or region. Species richness is simply a count of species, and it does not take into account the abundances of the species or their relative abundance distributions. Species richness is often used as a criterion when assessing the relative conservation value of habitats or landscapes, and it is a fundamental component of biodiversity measurement.

Sampling considerations

Because the observed richness depends on sampling effort, ecologists use species accumulation curves and rarefaction to compare sites fairly. The more individuals or samples examined, the more species are typically found, especially rare ones. Estimators such as the Chao index attempt to infer the true richness of a community from incomplete samples.

Patterns of richness

Species richness varies enormously across the globe. It generally decreases from the equator toward the poles — the latitudinal diversity gradient — and is highest in tropical rainforests and coral reefs. Richness also tends to increase with area, a relationship described by the species–area curve, and with habitat heterogeneity, productivity, and evolutionary history.

Richness versus diversity

Species richness differs from species diversity indices such as the Shannon index or Simpson index, which combine richness with evenness — the relative abundances of species. A community can be rich in species yet dominated by a few abundant ones, giving it lower diversity than its richness alone suggests.

Applications

Richness is widely used in conservation planning, environmental impact assessment, and monitoring of ecosystem change, including responses to climate change, habitat fragmentation, and invasive species.
