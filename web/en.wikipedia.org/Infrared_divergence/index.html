Infrared divergence

An infrared divergence is a situation in quantum field theory in which an integral — typically arising from the emission or exchange of very low-energy ("soft") virtual particles — diverges when the momentum of those particles approaches zero. The term infrared refers to the low-energy, long-wavelength end of the spectrum, by analogy with infrared light.

Origin

In quantum electrodynamics, calculations involving photons of arbitrarily small energy lead to divergent integrals. Physically, this reflects the fact that any scattering process involving charged particles is accompanied by the emission of an unlimited number of extremely soft photons, which no real detector can fully resolve.

Resolution

Infrared divergences cancel when one computes physically observable quantities. Felix Bloch and Arnold Nordsieck showed in 1937 that the divergence in the probability of emitting soft photons cancels against divergent virtual corrections when the finite energy resolution of a detector is taken into account. More generally, the Kinoshita–Lee–Nauenberg theorem guarantees that suitably inclusive transition rates — summed over degenerate initial and final states — are free of infrared divergences.

Related phenomena

Closely related are collinear (mass) divergences, which occur when massless particles are emitted parallel to one another or to an external leg. Infrared divergences should be distinguished from ultraviolet divergences, which arise from high-energy, short-distance behaviour and are handled by renormalisation.
