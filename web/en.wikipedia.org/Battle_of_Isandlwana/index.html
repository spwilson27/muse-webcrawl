The Battle of Isandlwana on 22 January 1879 was the first major encounter in the Anglo-Zulu War. A Zulu army of about 20,000 warriors, under the overall command of Ntshingwayo kaMahole, attacked and annihilated a British column camped beneath the hill of Isandlwana in Zululand. Of some 1,800 British, colonial and native troops in the camp, more than 1,300 were killed, including nearly all the regular infantry. It was one of the worst defeats ever inflicted on a British army by an indigenous force, and it shocked Victorian Britain.

Background

In December 1878 the British High Commissioner, Sir Henry Bartle Frere, presented King Cetshwayo with an ultimatum designed to be rejected, and in January 1879 Lord Chelmsford invaded Zululand in three columns. The centre column, accompanied by Chelmsford himself, camped at Isandlwana on 20 January. On 22 January Chelmsford took about half the column away to pursue a reported Zulu force, leaving Lieutenant Colonel Henry Pulleine in command of the camp, with orders to defend it. Colonel Anthony Durnford arrived later with mounted troops and rocket battery. The camp was neither entrenched nor formed into a wagon laager, contrary to standing practice.

The battle

The main Zulu army, advancing to attack the camp the following day, was discovered by a British mounted patrol shortly after midday. The Zulus immediately attacked in their classic "horns of the buffalo" formation. The British firing line, extended too far in front of the camp, inflicted heavy casualties with Martini-Henry rifles and artillery, but problems with ammunition resupply and the sheer momentum of the attack allowed the Zulu left horn to work around the flank. The line collapsed back into the camp, where fighting became hand-to-hand. Pulleine and Durnford were both killed. A desperate group of fugitives fled toward the Buffalo River at Fugitives' Drift; only a handful of Europeans and some African auxiliaries escaped. A partial solar eclipse occurred during the battle, remembered in Zulu oral tradition.

Aftermath

The Zulu victory was not exploited strategically: a detached Zulu force attacked the mission station at Rorke's Drift the same evening and was repulsed. Chelmsford's invasion was halted and had to be reorganised with massive reinforcements; the war ended with the British victory at the Battle of Ulundi in July 1879 and the destruction of the Zulu kingdom as an independent state. Isandlwana remains a landmark in military history — a case study in underestimating an opponent and in the failure of firepower unsupported by secure logistics and field fortification.
