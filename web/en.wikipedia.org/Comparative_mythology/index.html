Comparative mythology

FieldMythology, anthropology, religious studies
SubjectShared themes and structures across mythologies
Key figuresMax Müller, James Frazer, Georges Dumézil, Claude Lévi-Strauss, Joseph Campbell

Comparative mythology is the comparison of myths from different cultures in an attempt to identify shared themes, structures, and origins. Scholars in the field ask why strikingly similar stories — creation from chaos or water, great floods, dying-and-rising gods, dragon-slayers, tricksters, and heroic quests — appear in widely separated societies, and whether such parallels reflect common ancestry, diffusion through contact, or universal features of the human mind.

Approaches

  • Nature mythology — Nineteenth-century scholars such as Max Müller interpreted myths as allegories of natural phenomena, especially the sun and storms, often using comparative linguistics to link divine names across Indo-European languages.
  • Ritualism — James Frazer's The Golden Bough (1890) and the Cambridge Ritualists argued that myths originated as explanations of ritual practices.
  • Functionalism and structuralism — Bronisław Malinowski treated myth as a social charter; Claude Lévi-Strauss analyzed myths as systems of oppositions mediating universal cognitive structures.
  • Archetypal psychology — Carl Jung proposed archetypes of a collective unconscious; Joseph Campbell popularized the "monomyth" or hero's journey in The Hero with a Thousand Faces (1949).
  • Historical reconstruction — Georges Dumézil and others have reconstructed Proto-Indo-European mythology from the traditions of its descendant peoples, identifying a trifunctional ideology of sovereignty, force, and fertility.

Recurrent motifs

Comparatists catalog flood myths (from Mesopotamian and Hebrew tradition to Mesoamerica and China), creation myths, axis mundi and world trees, culture heroes who steal fire, and eschatological battles. The Aarne–Thompson–Uther index performs a similar cataloguing task for folktales. Modern scholarship is generally more cautious than its nineteenth-century predecessors, stressing local context and demanding evidence for claims of diffusion or inheritance rather than assuming that similarity implies connection.
