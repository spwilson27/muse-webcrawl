The Quran (Arabic: ٱلْقُرْآن, al-Qurʾān, "the Recitation"), also romanized Qur'an or Koran, is the central religious text of Islam, believed by Muslims to be a revelation directly from God (Allah). It is organized in 114 chapters (surah, pl. suwar) consisting of verses (ayah, pl. ayat). In addition to its religious significance, the Quran is widely regarded as the finest work in classical Arabic literature.

Muslims believe that the Quran was orally revealed by God to the final Islamic prophet Muhammad through the angel Gabriel (Jibril) incrementally over a period of some 23 years, beginning on the Night of Decree (Laylat al-Qadr), when Muhammad was 40, and concluding in 632, the year of his death. Muslims regard the Quran as Muhammad's most important miracle, a proof of his prophethood, and the culmination of a series of divine messages starting with those revealed to Adam, including the Torah (Tawrat), the Psalms (Zabur) and the Gospel (Injil).

History and compilation

According to Islamic tradition, several of Muhammad's companions served as scribes, recording the revelations during his lifetime. Shortly after the prophet's death, the Quran was compiled by his companions, who had written down or memorized parts of it. Caliph Uthman established a standard version, now known as the Uthmanic codex, which is generally considered the archetype of the Quran known today. Variants arising from regional readings (qira'at) were largely standardised, though a canonical system of ten readings survives.

Content and themes

The Quranic text discusses subjects including the nature of God, resurrection and final judgment, ethics, law, and narratives of earlier prophets such as Noah, Abraham, Moses and Jesus. It instructs moral and legal conduct and describes itself as a guide. The opening chapter, Al-Fatiha ("The Opening"), is recited in every unit of the daily prayers (salah). Chapters are arranged roughly from longest to shortest and are classified as Meccan or Medinan according to where their verses were revealed.

Interpretation

Exegesis of the Quran is called tafsir; major classical commentators include al-Tabari and Ibn Kathir. A related body of literature, the Hadith — reports of Muhammad's words and deeds — supplements the Quran as a source of Islamic law and practice, since many details of ritual and jurisprudence are not spelled out in the Quranic text itself.

Translation and transmission

The Quran has been translated into most African, Asian and European languages. It is traditionally memorized in full by some Muslims, known as hafiz. Manuscripts such as the early Birmingham fragment, radiocarbon-dated to the seventh century, are among the oldest witnesses to the text.
