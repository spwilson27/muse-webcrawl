Hadith (Arabic: حديث, plural أحاديث, aḥādīth) is the corpus of reports recording the words, actions, and tacit approvals of the Islamic prophet Muhammad, as transmitted through chains of narrators. For most Muslims, hadith literature is the second foundational source of guidance after the Quran: it supplies the practical example (the Sunnah) by which Quranic commands are understood and applied, and it underpins Islamic jurisprudence, ritual, and ethics.

Structure and transmission

Each hadith consists of two parts: the isnād, the chain of transmitters reaching back to a Companion of Muhammad who witnessed the report, and the matn, the text itself. Because hadith were transmitted orally for generations before being systematically written down, Muslim scholars developed an elaborate science of criticism (ʿilm al-ḥadīth) to authenticate reports — scrutinising the reliability, memory, and moral character of every narrator and the continuity of each chain. Reports are graded as ṣaḥīḥ (sound), ḥasan (good), or ḍaʿīf (weak), among other categories.

Collections

The great compilations were assembled in the 9th century CE. For Sunni Islam, six collections are canonical, headed by the Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, regarded as the most authoritative books after the Quran, followed by the Sunan works of Abu Dawud, al-Tirmidhi, al-Nasa'i, and Ibn Majah; other revered collections include Malik's Muwaṭṭaʾ (the earliest surviving large compilation) and the vast Musnad of Ahmad ibn Hanbal. Shia Islam maintains its own corpus, the "Four Books" — including al-Kulayni's Al-Kāfī — which include reports from the Prophet's family and the Imams and differ in criteria of acceptance.

Role in Islamic thought

Hadith explain and supplement the Quran: the method of prayer, the rates of zakat, and the details of pilgrimage all derive principally from hadith rather than the Quranic text. Collections are organised by legal topic or by narrator, and vast commentaries gloss them. In the modern era the authenticity and authority of hadith have been debated — by reformers who stress the Quran alone, by traditionalists who defend the classical science, and by academic scholars who subject the transmission history to historical-critical analysis.
