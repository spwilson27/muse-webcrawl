The Hijrah (Arabic: الهجرة, "migration") was the journey of the Islamic prophet Muhammad and his followers from Mecca to the oasis town of Yathrib — renamed Madīnat an-Nabī, "City of the Prophet", or simply Medina — in 622 CE. The migration marks the founding of the first Muslim community (ummah) as a political body, and it is so pivotal that the Islamic lunar calendar begins its count from this year: dates are given as AH (Anno Hegirae, "in the year of the Hijrah").

Background

After Muhammad began preaching in Mecca around 610 CE, his followers — drawn largely from outside the powerful clans — faced escalating persecution from the Quraysh, Mecca's ruling tribe: boycott, violence, and the deaths of vulnerable converts. Some Muslims had earlier emigrated to Abyssinia (c. 613–615) for refuge. In 620 and 621, delegations from Yathrib, a town torn by feuding between its Aws and Khazraj tribes, pledged allegiance at al-Aqaba and invited Muhammad to come as arbiter and leader. Through the summer of 622, Muslims slipped away to Yathrib in small groups.

The journey

When the Quraysh resolved to kill Muhammad, he departed secretly by night with his closest companion, Abu Bakr. In Islamic tradition, the two hid for three days in the cave of Thawr south of Mecca while search parties scoured the area — a spider's web and a dove's nest at the cave mouth are said to have concealed them — then travelled by a little-used coastal route with a guide, arriving first at Quba, where the first mosque was founded, and entering Yathrib on 12 Rabi' al-awwal (24 September 622 in the traditional dating). Letting his camel choose the stopping place, Muhammad settled where the Prophet's Mosque now stands.

Aftermath

In Medina, Muhammad united the Emigrants (Muhajirun) from Mecca with the Helpers (Ansar) of Medina in bonds of brotherhood, promulgated the Constitution of Medina defining relations among Muslims, Jews, and pagans, and established the congregational mosque as the community's centre. From this secure base, the early battles with Mecca (Badr, Uhud, the Trench) unfolded over the following years, ending with the peaceful conquest of Mecca in 630.

Significance

Beyond its historical role, hijrah became a theological concept: migration from unbelief and oppression to faith and safety, invoked throughout Islamic history — by communities fleeing persecution, by jurists debating the duty to emigrate, and in modern discourse both literally and metaphorically. The Hijri New Year (1 Muharram) commemorates the migration, though observance is generally subdued compared with other religious festivals.
