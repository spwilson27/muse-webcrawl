Sahih al-Bukhari (Arabic: صحيح البخاري), formally titled al-Jami' al-Sahih ("The Authentic Collection"), is a collection of hadith — reports of the words, actions, and tacit approvals of the prophet Muhammad — compiled by the Persian scholar Muhammad ibn Ismail al-Bukhari (810–870 CE). Sunni Muslims regard it as the most authentic (sahih) book after the Quran, and it heads the six major hadith collections known as the Kutub al-Sittah.

Compilation

Al-Bukhari spent about sixteen years travelling through the Abbasid world — to Mecca, Medina, Egypt, Syria, and Iraq — gathering reports. According to his own account he examined some 600,000 narrations and included only those he judged to meet his stringent criteria: an unbroken chain (isnad) of narrators each of proven memory and upright character, who demonstrably met one another. The collection is organised into books (kutub) arranged by topic — from revelation, faith, and prayer to commerce, marriage, and warfare — with each report given with its full chain of transmission.

Contents and reception

Depending on edition, the work contains roughly 7,500 hadiths including repetitions (about 2,600 distinct reports), preceded by chapter headings in which al-Bukhari embedded his own legal reasoning. Generations of scholars wrote commentaries on it, the most famous being Ibn Hajar al-Asqalani's Fath al-Bari. Muslim tradition celebrates al-Bukhari's prodigious memory and holds the collection's completions (khatm) as communal events in times of crisis. Modern academic historians debate the dating and attribution of hadith generally, while within Islamic scholarship Sahih al-Bukhari retains its canonical pre-eminence.
