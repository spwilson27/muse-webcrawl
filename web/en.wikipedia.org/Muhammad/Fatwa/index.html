Fatwa

A fatwa (Arabic: فتوىٰ; plural fatāwā فتاوىٰ) is a nonbinding legal opinion or ruling in Islamic law (sharia) issued by a qualified jurist or mufti in response to a question posed by a private individual, judge, or government. The person who issues a fatwa is called a mufti, and the person who asks for one is a mustafti. Fatwas are not court judgments: they are advisory, though they carry moral and social weight for those who accept the mufti's authority.

History

The institution developed in the early centuries of Islam as scholars answered questions from believers seeking guidance on ritual, commerce, family life, and governance. Collections of fatwas became an important literary genre and a mechanism by which Islamic law adapted to new circumstances. In the Ottoman Empire, the office of Shaykh al-Islam headed a hierarchy of official muftis whose fatwas carried state significance.

Procedure and qualifications

A mufti is expected to master the Qur'an, hadith, the methodology of jurisprudence (usul al-fiqh), and the precedents of his school of law (madhhab). A fatwa typically sets out the question, the evidence from scripture and precedent, and the resulting ruling. Because fatwas are non-binding, a questioner may consult another mufti, and differing fatwas can coexist.

Fatwas in the modern world

State fatwa councils (such as Egypt's Dar al-Ifta, founded 1895) and independent scholars issue fatwas on everything from banking and medicine to technology. The internet has multiplied "online fatwas", raising debates about qualifications and authority. In Western media the word is often misused to mean a death sentence; in fact fatwas overwhelmingly concern everyday religious and legal questions, and a ruling of death is a court matter, not a mufti's decree.

Famous fatwas

Well-known examples include the 1989 fatwa by Iran's Ayatollah Khomeini calling for the death of author Salman Rushdie over The Satanic Verses — politically exceptional rather than typical — and the 2004 Amman Message, which defined orthodoxy and forbade declarations of apostasy among recognised schools of Islam.
