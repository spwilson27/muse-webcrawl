Madhhabs systematised the derivation of Islamic law from scripture.

A madhhab (Arabic: مَذْهَب, plural madhahib) is a school of thought within fiqh, Islamic jurisprudence: a tradition of interpreting sharia with its own methods and body of rulings, usually associated with a founding jurist. Muslims historically followed the rulings of a madhhab in matters of ritual, family law, commerce and other fields.

Sunni schools

Four Sunni schools survive and are mutually recognised as valid: the Hanafi school (after Abu Hanifa), the largest, predominant in Turkey, Central and South Asia; the Maliki school (after Malik ibn Anas), strong in North and West Africa; the Shafi'i school (after al-Shafi'i), common in East Africa, Yemen and Southeast Asia; and the Hanbali school (after Ahmad ibn Hanbal), historically centred in Arabia.

Shia and other schools

The main Shia school is the Ja'fari (Twelver) school, alongside Zaydi jurisprudence; Ismailis historically followed Fatimid codifications. Extinct schools include the Zahiri, once influential in al-Andalus. The 2004 Amman Message formally recognised eight madhhabs as legitimate.

Approach

Madhhabs differ in the weight given to sources beyond the Quran and sunnah — consensus (ijma), analogy (qiyas), juristic preference, local custom and public interest — while sharing the same ultimate sources.
