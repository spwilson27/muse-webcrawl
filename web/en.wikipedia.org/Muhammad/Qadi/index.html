Qadi

A qadi (Arabic: قاضي, qāḍī; also spelled kadi or cadi) is a judge in Islamic law (sharia) responsible for administering justice in a sharia court. Historically, qadis were appointed by the ruler or caliph and adjudicated civil, criminal and family matters according to the Quran, the Sunna and the jurisprudence (fiqh) of one of the recognised schools of law.

History

The office arose in the first Islamic century; under the Umayyad and Abbasid caliphates the chief qadi (qāḍī al-quḍāt) in the capital supervised provincial judges. The four Sunni schools (Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i and Hanbali) produced distinct bodies of procedure and precedent, and the ruler's court of complaints (maẓālim) supplemented the qadi's jurisdiction. Classical legal theory prescribed the qadi's qualifications: adult, Muslim, of sound mind and upright character, and learned in the law.

Modern role

With the adoption of European-style legal codes in most Muslim-majority states during the 19th and 20th centuries, qadis' jurisdiction narrowed largely to personal-status matters — marriage, divorce, inheritance and religious endowments — or disappeared into unified judiciaries. In some countries, including Israel, sharia courts staffed by state-appointed qadis continue to serve Muslim communities in family law, and kadis historically served in the Balkans and British colonial courts.
