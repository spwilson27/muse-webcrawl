Fiqh (Arabic: فقه, "deep understanding") is Islamic jurisprudence — the human understanding and elaboration of the Sharia, the divine law of Islam as revealed in the Quran and the Sunnah (the teachings and practices of the prophet Muhammad). Whereas Sharia is regarded by Muslims as God's immutable law, fiqh is the fallible human effort to interpret and apply it, developed by jurists (fuqaha) over the centuries.

Methodology

The classical theory of the sources of law (usul al-fiqh) ranks the Quran first, then the Sunnah as transmitted in hadith, then the consensus (ijma) of scholars, and then analogical reasoning (qiyas). Jurists also employ subsidiary principles such as juristic preference (istihsan), public interest (maslaha), and local custom ('urf). A qualified jurist who exercises independent reasoning is a mujtahid; his considered legal opinion is a fatwa when issued in answer to a question.

Schools of law

Fiqh crystallised into schools of law (madhhabs). The four surviving Sunni schools are the Hanafi school (the largest, dominant in Turkey, Central and South Asia), the Maliki school (North and West Africa), the Shafi'i school (East Africa, Yemen, Southeast Asia), and the Hanbali school (Saudi Arabia and Qatar). Shia Islam has its own principal school, the Ja'fari, alongside Zaydi and Ismaili traditions. The schools differ in their weighting of the sources and in detailed rulings, while recognising one another as legitimate.

Scope

Classical fiqh manuals cover ritual law — purification, prayer, fasting, almsgiving, pilgrimage — and then transactions and social law: marriage, divorce, inheritance, commerce, crime and punishment, and governance. In the modern period most Muslim-majority states have codified selected fiqh rules, especially in personal-status law, within national legal systems.
