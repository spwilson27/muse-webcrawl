The Islamic calendar, also known as the Hijri calendar, is a lunar calendar consisting of 12 lunar months in a year of 354 or 355 days. It is used to determine the proper days of Islamic holidays and rituals, such as Ramadan and the Hajj, and in several Muslim-majority countries as a civil or religious calendar. The epoch of the calendar is the Hijra — the migration of the prophet Muhammad and his followers from Mecca to Medina in 622 CE — and years are counted as AH (Anno Hegirae, "in the year of the Hijra").

Months

The twelve months are: Muharram, Safar, Rabi' al-Awwal, Rabi' al-Thani, Jumada al-Awwal, Jumada al-Thani, Rajab, Sha'ban, Ramadan, Shawwal, Dhu al-Qa'dah, and Dhu al-Hijjah. Four of them (Muharram, Rajab, Dhu al-Qa'dah, and Dhu al-Hijjah) are traditionally "sacred months" in which warfare was forbidden. Each month begins with the sighting of the new crescent moon (hilal), so dates can vary by a day between countries depending on observation or astronomical calculation.

Relation to the solar year

Because the Islamic year is about 11 days shorter than the solar year, Islamic dates drift through the seasons, completing a full cycle roughly every 33 years. The Quran prohibits intercalation (adding a leap month to realign the calendar with the seasons), so unlike lunisolar calendars such as the Hebrew and Chinese calendars, the Islamic calendar is purely lunar.

Tabular calendar

For conversion and long-range planning, a tabular Islamic calendar uses a fixed 30-year cycle with 11 leap years in which the final month has 30 days instead of 29. Conversion between Hijri and Gregorian dates is approximate by hand but is handled by standard algorithms; a rough rule is that 33 Islamic years equal about 32 Gregorian years.
