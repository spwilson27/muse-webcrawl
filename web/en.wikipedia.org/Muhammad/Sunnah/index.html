The Sunnah (Arabic: سنة, "habitual practice" or "customary norm") is the body of teachings, practices, and examples set by the Islamic prophet Muhammad — his words (qawl), deeds (fi'l), and tacit approvals (taqrir) — as transmitted in reports called hadith. Alongside the Quran, it constitutes the primary scriptural source of Islamic law (Sharia) and of Muslim belief and practice.

Role in Islam

Muslims regard Muhammad not only as the messenger who conveyed the Quran but as its living exemplar — the Quran describes him as "an excellent model" for believers. The Sunnah therefore explains how Quranic commands are to be carried out: the Quran enjoins prayer and almsgiving, for example, while the Sunnah supplies their detailed forms. Devotional acts that follow the Prophet's example but are not strictly obligatory are also themselves commonly called "sunnah" in everyday usage, as in "sunnah prayers."

Transmission

The Sunnah was preserved first through the practice and memory of Muhammad's companions and was later compiled in written hadith collections, each report accompanied by a chain of transmitters (isnad) that scholars scrutinised for reliability. Major canonical collections include Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim for Sunnis, and the Four Books for Twelver Shia, whose conception of Sunnah also embraces the teachings of the Imams. The science of hadith criticism grew up to grade reports as authentic, good, or weak.

Debates

The authority and authenticity of the Sunnah have been debated within Islam and by academic historians: movements sometimes grouped as "Quranists" argue that the Quran alone is binding, while mainstream Sunni and Shia scholarship holds the Sunnah to be an indispensable and divinely protected complement to it. Classical legal theory (usul al-fiqh) treats Sunnah as the second source of law after the Quran, capable of specifying, qualifying, and supplementing it.
