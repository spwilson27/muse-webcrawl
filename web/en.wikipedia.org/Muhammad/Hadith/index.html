Hadith (Arabic: حديث, romanized: ḥadīth, plural aḥādīth) is the Arabic word for "speech", "report" or "account". In Islamic terminology, a hadith is a report attributing to the Islamic prophet Muhammad a statement, action or tacit approval. Hadith are regarded by most Muslims as, after the Quran, the most important source of guidance in Islam, forming, together with Muhammad's biography (sīra), the basis of the Sunnah — the normative practice of the prophet that believers are encouraged to emulate.

Each hadith consists of two parts: the isnād, the chain of narrators who transmitted the report, and the matn, the text of the report itself. Because hadith were transmitted orally for generations before being written down, Muslim scholars developed an elaborate science of hadith criticism ('ilm al-ḥadīth) to evaluate the reliability of chains of transmission and the consistency of texts.

History

During Muhammad's lifetime, his companions memorised and, in some cases, recorded his sayings and deeds. After his death in 632 CE, the expanding Muslim community relied increasingly on these reports in legal and theological disputes. Systematic collection accelerated in the second and third Islamic centuries, as reports proliferated — including, critics charged, fabricated ones — and scholars travelled widely (the riḥla fī ṭalab al-ḥadīth, "journey in search of hadith") to hear reports directly from transmitters and to verify chains of narration.

The most famous early compilations include the Muwaṭṭaʾ of Malik ibn Anas (8th century) and the great ninth-century collections of al-Bukhari and Muslim ibn al-Hajjaj, whose works came to be ranked as the most authoritative in Sunni Islam.

Classification

Hadith are classified by the strength of their transmission:

  • Ṣaḥīḥ (sound) — an unbroken chain of reliable, precise narrators, with a text free of anomalies and defects.
  • Ḥasan (good) — largely sound, but with narrators of slightly lesser precision.
  • Ḍaʿīf (weak) — a chain or text failing one or more conditions of soundness.

Reports transmitted by so many narrators at every stage that fabrication is considered inconceivable are termed mutawātir; the great majority of hadith are āḥād, transmitted by a limited number of chains.

Major collections

Sunni Islam recognises six canonical collections, the Kutub al-Sittah: Sahih al-Bukhari, Sahih Muslim, Sunan Abu Dawud, Jami' al-Tirmidhi, Sunan al-Nasa'i and Sunan Ibn Majah. Shia Islam relies principally on its own four books (al-Kutub al-Arbaʿa), including al-Kulayni's al-Kafi, which contain reports from the Prophet and the Imams.
