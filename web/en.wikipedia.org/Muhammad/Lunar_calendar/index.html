A lunar calendar reckons months by the phases of the Moon.

A lunar calendar is a calendar based on the monthly cycles of the Moon's phases (synodic months). Each month begins with a phase such as the new moon or first crescent and lasts about 29.5 days, so lunar months alternate between 29 and 30 days. Twelve lunar months total about 354 days — roughly 11 days shorter than a solar year — so a purely lunar calendar drifts through the seasons.

Pure lunar vs lunisolar calendars

The Islamic (Hijri) calendar is a purely lunar calendar: its months, including Ramadan, migrate through the solar year. Lunisolar calendars — such as the Hebrew, Chinese and Hindu calendars — insert an extra (intercalary) month periodically, the Metonic cycle being one scheme, to keep months aligned with the seasons while still beginning on lunar phases.

History

Lunar reckoning is among the oldest forms of timekeeping; tally sticks from the Upper Palaeolithic have been interpreted as lunar counts, and lunar or lunisolar schemes underpinned Babylonian, Egyptian and many other ancient calendars before solar calendars like the Julian and Gregorian became dominant for civil purposes.
