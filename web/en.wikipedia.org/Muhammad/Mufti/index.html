Mufti

A mufti (Arabic: muftī) is an Islamic jurist qualified to issue a nonbinding legal opinion (fatwa) on a point of Islamic law (sharia). The person who asks a mufti for a ruling is called a mustafti. Fatwas issued by muftis are opinions based on the Qur'an, the hadith, and the methodology of the jurist's school of law (madhhab); unlike the judgment of a qadi (judge), they are not formally binding, though they may carry great moral and social authority.

The role developed in the first centuries of Islam as private scholars responded to questions from the public. With time, muftis became attached to states: the Ottoman Empire institutionalized the office, at whose apex stood the Shaykh al-Islam in Istanbul, whose fatwas could legitimate imperial acts, including the deposition of sultans. Colonial-era and modern governments created official mufti posts; a Grand Mufti — such as the Grand Mufti of Egypt, of Saudi Arabia, or of Jerusalem — is typically the highest or state-appointed mufti of a country or region.

Qualifications

Classically, a mufti was expected to be a mature, upright Muslim jurist trained in Arabic, Qur'anic exegesis, hadith, legal theory (usul al-fiqh), and the substantive law of a madhhab, licensed through an ijaza from a teacher. The discipline of issuing fatwas has its own literature, adab al-fatwa, covering the mufti's qualifications, the questioner's duties, and the proper form of opinions.

Modern era

Today, state bodies such as Egypt's Dar al-Ifta, Saudi Arabia's Council of Senior Scholars, and Turkey's Diyanet issue official rulings, while satellite television and the internet have enabled independent and sometimes controversial "online muftis" to reach global audiences, fragmenting traditional authority. The term also survives in British military slang: "mufti" meaning civilian dress derives from the robes of muftis, once stereotypically worn off-duty by officers.
