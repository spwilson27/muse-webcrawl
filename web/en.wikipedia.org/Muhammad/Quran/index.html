The Quran (Arabic: القرآن‎, romanized: al-Qurʾān, lit. "the recitation") is the central religious text of Islam, believed by Muslims to be a revelation from God (Allah) to the prophet Muhammad through the angel Gabriel (Jibril) over a period of about 23 years, beginning in 610 CE. It is the foundational scripture of Islam and is regarded in its Arabic original as the literal word of God.

Structure

The Quran consists of 114 chapters (surahs) of varying length, comprising more than 6,000 verses (ayat). With the exception of the short opening chapter, Al-Fatiha, the surahs are arranged roughly from longest to shortest rather than in the order of revelation. Each surah has a name, often drawn from a distinctive word within it, such as Al-Baqarah ("The Cow") or Yusuf ("Joseph").

Compilation

According to Islamic tradition, the text was memorised and written down by Muhammad's companions and was standardised into a single codex under the third caliph, Uthman, around the mid-7th century. The resulting Uthmanic recension became the basis of the Quran as it exists today.

Themes

Central themes include the oneness of God (tawhid), guidance for personal conduct and community life, narratives of earlier prophets shared with the Bible, the Day of Judgment, and exhortations to charity and justice. Muslims regard the Quran's inimitability (iʿjāz) as evidence of its divine origin.
