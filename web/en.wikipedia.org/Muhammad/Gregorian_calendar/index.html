The Gregorian calendar is the calendar used in most of the world today. It was proclaimed in 1582 by Pope Gregory XIII as a modification of, and replacement for, the Julian calendar. The principal adjustment corrected the drift of the Julian leap-year rule against the tropical year, so that the date of Easter — tied to the March equinox — would stay near its traditional season.

The reform

In October 1582, Catholic countries skipped ten days to restore the equinox to 21 March. The new leap rule — a year is a leap year if divisible by 4, except century years, which must be divisible by 400 — gives an average year of 365.2425 days, very close to the tropical year. Protestant and Orthodox countries adopted the calendar later; Britain and its colonies in 1752, Russia in 1918, and Greece in 1923.

Structure

The calendar has twelve months of 28–31 days and a seven-day week. Years are numbered from the traditional year of the birth of Jesus (AD/CE system). Dates before 1582 computed in the Gregorian system are called proleptic Gregorian dates.
