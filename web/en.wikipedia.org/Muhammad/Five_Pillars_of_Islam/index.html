Five Pillars of Islam
PillarsShahada, Salah, Zakat, Sawm, Hajj
SourceHadith of Gabriel; Quran
Applies toSunni Islam (with counterparts in Shia practice)

The Five Pillars of Islam (arkān al-Islām) are fundamental practices in Islam, considered to be obligatory acts of worship for all Muslims. They are summarised in the famous hadith of Gabriel: the Shahada (declaration of faith), Salah (prayer), Zakat (almsgiving), Sawm (fasting), and Hajj (pilgrimage to Mecca).

Shahada

The Shahada is the declaration of faith: "I bear witness that there is no god but God, and I bear witness that Muhammad is the Messenger of God." Reciting it with conviction is the act by which one becomes a Muslim, and it forms part of the call to prayer and of daily worship.

Salah

Salah is the ritual prayer performed five times daily — at dawn (Fajr), noon (Dhuhr), afternoon (Asr), sunset (Maghrib), and night (Isha) — preceded by ritual washing (wudu). Prayers consist of set cycles of standing, bowing, and prostration accompanied by recitations from the Quran, above all the opening chapter, al-Fatiha. On Fridays the noon prayer is performed congregationally at the mosque with a sermon.

Zakat

Zakat is the obligatory giving of a fixed proportion of one's wealth — classically 2.5% of monetary savings above a threshold (nisab) held for a lunar year — to specified categories of recipients, including the poor and indebted. It is understood as purifying wealth and redistributing it within the community.

Sawm

Sawm is fasting, obligatory during the month of Ramadan, when healthy adult Muslims abstain from food, drink, and marital relations from dawn to sunset, breaking the fast with the iftar meal. The fast ends with the festival of Eid al-Fitr.

Hajj

The Hajj is the pilgrimage to Mecca, required once in a lifetime of every Muslim who is physically and financially able. Performed during the month of Dhu al-Hijjah, its rites — circumambulating the Kaaba, walking between the hills of Safa and Marwah, and standing at Arafat — commemorate Abraham, Hagar, and Ishmael, and conclude with Eid al-Adha.
