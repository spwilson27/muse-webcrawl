Sharia (Arabic: شريعة, romanized: sharīʿa) is the body of religious law that forms part of the Islamic tradition. It is derived from the scriptural sources of Islam — principally the Quran and the Sunnah, the teachings and practices of Muhammad as preserved in the hadith literature — and from scholarly interpretation (fiqh, "understanding") developed over the centuries by jurists (fuqahāʾ). Strictly, many scholars distinguish sharīʿa, God's ideal law, from fiqh, the fallible human attempt to discern it.

Scope

Classical Islamic law addresses worship (prayer, fasting during Ramadan, almsgiving, pilgrimage), family law (marriage, divorce, inheritance), commercial law, criminal law, dietary rules and personal conduct, as well as matters such as the conduct of war and relations between rulers and ruled. Muslim jurisprudence classifies human actions into five categories: obligatory (farḍ), recommended (mustaḥabb), permitted (mubāḥ), disliked (makrūh) and forbidden (ḥarām).

Schools of law

Distinct schools of jurisprudence (madhhabs) crystallised in the eighth and ninth centuries. Sunni Islam recognises four surviving schools — Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i and Hanbali — while Shia Islam developed its own traditions, principally the Ja'fari school. The schools differ in the weight they give to sources such as local custom, juristic preference and public interest, but historically coexisted within a plural legal culture administered by qadis (judges) and muftis.

Modern era

From the nineteenth century, most Muslim-majority states codified or replaced parts of classical law with European-style legal codes; today sharia plays widely varying roles, from a symbolic source of legislation to the basis of personal-status law or, in a few states, a more comprehensive legal framework. Its interpretation and place in public life remain subjects of vigorous debate among Muslims and in global politics.
