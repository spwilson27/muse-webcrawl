Grand Mufti

A Grand Mufti is the chief mufti — the senior official interpreter and expounder of Islamic law (sharia) — of a country or major community. A mufti issues fatwas, non-binding legal opinions in response to questions; the Grand Mufti is typically the head of an official fatwa-issuing body such as Egypt's Dar al-Ifta, or the recognised supreme religious authority for Sunni Muslims in a state.

History

The institution developed within the Ottoman Empire, where the Şeyhülislam of Istanbul stood at the apex of the religious hierarchy. As Ottoman provinces and later nation-states created their own religious administrations, titles such as Grand Mufti of Egypt (established 1895), Grand Mufti of Saudi Arabia, and Grand Mufti of Jerusalem became prominent. Notable holders include Muhammad Abduh in Egypt, Abd al-Aziz ibn Baz in Saudi Arabia, and Mohammed Amin al-Husseini, the controversial Grand Mufti of Jerusalem under the British Mandate.

Role today

Grand Muftis are usually appointed by the state or elected by a council of scholars. Their authority varies: in some countries their fatwas merely advise, while elsewhere official fatwa bodies shape public policy and personal-status law. Shia Islam has a different structure, centred on senior marja' scholars rather than a single state mufti.
