Icelanders' sagas

The sagas of Icelanders (Icelandic: Íslendingasögur), also known as family sagas, are a group of Old Icelandic prose narratives written mainly in the 13th and 14th centuries. They are set principally in the period of Iceland's settlement and Commonwealth, roughly 870 to 1050, and recount the lives, feuds, journeys and lawsuits of early Icelandic settlers and their descendants.

Characteristics

The sagas are written in a distinctive laconic, objective prose style, mixing genealogy, historical tradition and fictional artistry. Their subjects are ostensibly historical, but scholars debate how much derives from oral tradition and how much is literary invention. About forty sagas and nearly fifty shorter tales (þættir) survive.

Major examples

Among the most celebrated are Njáls saga (the longest and generally considered the greatest), Egils saga, Laxdæla saga, Gísla saga, Eyrbyggja saga and the Vinland sagas (Grœnlendinga saga and Eiríks saga rauða), which describe Norse voyages to North America. Together with the other saga genres they constitute the best-known body of medieval Icelandic literature and a major influence on later Scandinavian writing.
