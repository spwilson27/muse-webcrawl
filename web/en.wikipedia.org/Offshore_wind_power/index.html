Offshore wind power

Offshore wind power refers to the generation of electricity by wind turbines installed in bodies of water, usually at sea, where winds are stronger and more consistent than on land. The first offshore wind farm, Vindeby, was installed off Denmark in 1991. Since then capacity has grown rapidly, led by China and the United Kingdom, with global installed capacity exceeding 70 GW by the mid-2020s.

Technology

Most offshore turbines stand on fixed foundations — monopiles or steel jackets — driven into the seabed in waters up to about 60 m deep. For deeper waters, floating platforms moored to the seabed are used, pioneered commercially by Hywind Scotland (2017). Modern offshore turbines are far larger than onshore models, with rotor diameters exceeding 220 m and ratings of 14–16 MW. Power is collected by inter-array cables to an offshore substation and exported to shore, increasingly by high-voltage direct current (HVDC) links for distant farms.

Economics and policy

Costs fell sharply during the 2010s as turbines grew and supply chains matured, with auction prices in Europe dropping to near-subsidy-free levels by the late 2010s, though inflation and interest rates pushed costs up again in the early 2020s. Offshore wind is a central element of decarbonisation plans in the UK, the European Union, China, and the United States.

Environmental considerations

Concerns include noise during pile-driving (affecting marine mammals), effects on birds and fishing grounds, and seabed disturbance; benefits include low lifecycle emissions and reef-like habitat creation on foundations.
