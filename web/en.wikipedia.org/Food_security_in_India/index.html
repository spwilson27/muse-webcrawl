Food security in India

India operates one of the world's largest food distribution systems to support food security.

Food security in India concerns the availability, accessibility, and affordability of food for the population of India, the world's most populous country. India has been broadly self-sufficient in staple foodgrains since the Green Revolution of the late 1960s and 1970s, and it operates one of the largest food safety-net systems in the world. Nevertheless, undernutrition — particularly child stunting, wasting, and anaemia — remains a persistent challenge, and India has ranked in the lower half of the Global Hunger Index in recent years.

History

The Bengal famine of 1943, which killed an estimated two to three million people, profoundly shaped Indian food policy after independence in 1947. The government adopted food rationing, price controls, and public procurement, and established the Food Corporation of India in 1965. The Green Revolution introduced high-yielding wheat and rice varieties, irrigation, and fertilizer use, transforming India from a food-importing country dependent on shipments such as US PL-480 wheat into a net exporter of rice and wheat.

Policy framework

India's food security system rests on three pillars: procurement of wheat and rice from farmers at minimum support prices; storage and movement of grain in the central pool managed mainly by the FCI; and distribution to consumers through the public distribution system (PDS) of fair price shops. The system was reoriented by the Targeted PDS in 1997, and greatly expanded by the National Food Security Act 2013 (NFSA), which gave legal entitlements to subsidized foodgrains — 5 kg per person per month at highly subsidized prices — to up to 75% of the rural and 50% of the urban population, together covering about 810 million people. From 2023 the government made NFSA grain distribution free of charge under the Pradhan Mantri Garib Kalyan Anna Yojana framework.

Malnutrition

Despite adequate aggregate grain supplies, surveys such as the National Family Health Survey (NFHS-5, 2019–21) have found that roughly 35% of children under five are stunted and about 19% are wasted, while anaemia affects a majority of women of reproductive age. Analysts attribute this "Indian puzzle" to factors including poor sanitation, low dietary diversity, women's health and status, and weaknesses in complementary programmes such as the Integrated Child Development Services and the midday meal scheme (PM-POSHAN).

Challenges

Current challenges include the fiscal cost of the food subsidy, storage losses and grain stocks above buffer norms, the environmental costs of paddy cultivation in water-stressed regions, and the effects of climate change on yields. Reform debates concern diversifying procurement toward pulses, oilseeds, and millets; improving the nutritional content of the food basket; and the use of direct benefit transfers in place of physical grain distribution in some areas.
