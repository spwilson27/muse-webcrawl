The United States secretary of the treasury is the head of the United States Department of the Treasury, and is the chief financial officer of the federal government of the United States. The secretary of the treasury is a member of the Cabinet and is fifth in the presidential line of succession. The secretary serves as the principal advisor to the president on economic policy and is responsible for formulating and recommending domestic and international financial, economic, and tax policy; participating in the formulation of broad fiscal policies; and managing the public debt.

The Treasury Department's bureaus include the Internal Revenue Service (IRS), the Bureau of the Fiscal Service, the Bureau of Engraving and Printing, the United States Mint, the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency, the Alcohol and Tobacco Tax and Trade Bureau, and the Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN). The secretary's signature appears on US paper currency, and the secretary administers economic sanctions through the Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC).

History and notable secretaries

The first secretary was Alexander Hamilton (1789–1795), who established the nation's financial system: federal assumption of state debts, the First Bank of the United States, a mint, and a system of tariffs and excise taxes. His reports on public credit, manufactures and a national bank remain foundational documents of American economic policy. Other notable secretaries include Albert Gallatin (1801–1814), the longest-serving; Salmon P. Chase (1861–1864), who financed the Civil War and created the greenback and national banking systems; Andrew Mellon (1921–1932); Henry Morgenthau Jr. (1934–1945), second-longest-serving and architect of war finance and the Bretton Woods conference; Robert Rubin (1995–1999); Henry Paulson (2006–2009), who led the response to the 2008 financial crisis; Timothy Geithner (2009–2013); and Janet Yellen (2021–2025), the first woman to hold the post, having previously chaired the Federal Reserve.
