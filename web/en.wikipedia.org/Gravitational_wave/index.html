Gravitational waves are waves of the intensity of gravity generated by the accelerated masses of an orbital binary system, propagating outward from their source at the speed of light. They were first proposed by Oliver Heaviside in 1893 and then by Henri Poincaré in 1905 as waves similar to electromagnetic waves but for gravity, and were predicted by Albert Einstein in 1916 on the basis of his general theory of relativity.

Detection

Indirect evidence came from the Hulse–Taylor pulsar (discovered 1974), whose orbit decays exactly as predicted for energy carried away by gravitational waves. Direct detection was achieved on 14 September 2015, when the LIGO observatories recorded GW150914, the merger of two black holes about 1.3 billion light-years away; the discovery was announced in February 2016 and earned the 2017 Nobel Prize in Physics for Rainer Weiss, Barry Barish and Kip Thorne. In 2017 the merger of two neutron stars (GW170817) was observed in both gravitational waves and light, opening multi-messenger astronomy.

Properties and sources

Gravitational waves alternately stretch and squeeze space in perpendicular directions as they pass. Ground-based interferometers (LIGO, Virgo, KAGRA) detect merging stellar-mass binaries; pulsar timing arrays reported evidence in 2023 for a background of very-long-wavelength waves from supermassive black hole binaries, and the planned space detector LISA will open further frequency bands.
