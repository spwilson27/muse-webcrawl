Philippe de Vitry (31 October 1291 – 9 June 1361) was a French composer, music theorist, poet, diplomat, and bishop. He is credited as the inventor of the Ars nova ("new art") style of music of the late medieval era, and was one of the most admired musicians and intellectuals of his day, praised by Petrarch as a great poet and thinker.

Life

Vitry was born in Vitry-en-Artois in northern France and was educated in Paris, probably at the Sorbonne. He served the French court as a clerk, secretary, and diplomat under several kings, traveled on missions to Avignon and Italy, and in 1351 became Bishop of Meaux. His career exemplifies the union of learning, music, and statecraft in fourteenth-century France.

Music and theory

The treatise Ars nova notandi (c. 1322), traditionally attributed to Vitry, described innovations in rhythmic notation — including the use of duple as well as triple divisions of note values and the mensuration signs that allowed far greater rhythmic flexibility. His surviving compositions are chiefly motets, many preserved in the Roman de Fauvel manuscript; they pioneered the technique of isorhythm, in which repeating rhythmic and melodic patterns structure a work. These methods set the course for the music of Guillaume de Machaut and the rest of the century.

Legacy

Vitry's name became attached to the entire Ars nova movement, the "new art" that succeeded the Ars antiqua of the Notre Dame school. His motets remain central works of medieval music.
