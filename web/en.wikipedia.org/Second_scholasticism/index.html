Second scholasticism

Second scholasticism, also known as late scholasticism or Baroque scholasticism, is the revival of scholastic philosophy and theology that flourished roughly from the mid-16th to the mid-18th century, chiefly in Spain, Portugal, and their empires, and also among Protestant universities in Germany and the Netherlands. It is distinguished from medieval ("first") scholasticism by its engagement with humanism, the Reformation, and the new global questions raised by European overseas expansion.

Principal figures and centres

The movement centred on the School of Salamanca, founded in the tradition of Francisco de Vitoria and continued by Domingo de Soto, Melchor Cano, and Martín de Azpilcueta, and developed by later Jesuits such as Luis de Molina and Francisco Suárez, whose Disputationes Metaphysicae (1597) became a standard metaphysics text across Europe. Portuguese Coimbra and Roman colleges were also major centres, producing commentaries on Aristotle and Aquinas of great systematic ambition.

Contributions

Second scholastics made foundational contributions to international law (Vitoria's lectures on the Indies and the law of war), natural rights theory, economics (analyses of money, value, and just price sometimes credited as anticipating marginalism), probability theory, and metaphysics (theories of being, causation, and possible worlds taken up by Descartes, Leibniz, and Wolff). Protestant scholasticism developed in parallel in Lutheran and Reformed orthodoxy.
