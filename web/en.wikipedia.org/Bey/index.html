Bey (Ottoman Turkish: بك, bey; from Turkic beg) is a Turkic title for a chieftain, and a royal and noble title in various historical Turkic and Mongolic states and societies, later used widely in the Ottoman Empire and the wider Islamic world. Strictly speaking, the equivalent title for a woman is begum, used especially in South Asia.

Usage

In early Turkic societies a beg was a clan or tribal chief. In the Ottoman system, bey denoted rulers of smaller provinces and districts: a sanjak-bey governed a sanjak, while the higher-ranking beylerbey ("bey of beys") governed an eyalet, a major province. The semi-independent principalities of medieval Anatolia are known as beyliks, each ruled by a bey. In Ottoman Egypt and in Tunisia, "Bey" became the title of the ruler himself — the Bey of Tunis reigned until the monarchy's abolition in 1957. The title also survived as a polite form of address in Turkish (placed after a man's name, much like "Mr." or "Sir"), and in compounds such as beyefendi ("gentleman").

Related titles

Related forms include beg in Central Asia, bek, the Persian-influenced usage in Mughal India (where Mirza and Beg appear in personal names), and Azerbaijani bəy. In the Arab world under Ottoman rule, beys formed part of the provincial military-administrative elite, notably the Mamluk beys of Egypt.
