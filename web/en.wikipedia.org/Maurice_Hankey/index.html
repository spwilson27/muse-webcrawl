Maurice Hankey, 1st Baron Hankey

Maurice Pascal Alers Hankey, 1st Baron Hankey (1 April 1877 – 26 January 1963) was a British civil servant who gained prominence as the first Cabinet Secretary, serving under five prime ministers from 1916 to 1938. Often called the inventor of the modern British cabinet system, he institutionalized cabinet minutes, agendas, and the circulation of papers, and was a central figure in British war administration in both world wars.

Career

A former Royal Marine officer, Hankey became secretary of the Committee of Imperial Defence in 1912. During the First World War he served as secretary to the War Council and War Cabinet, and in December 1916 Lloyd George made him the first Secretary to the Cabinet. He attended the Paris Peace Conference and helped organize the machinery of interwar imperial conferences.

Later life

Raised to the peerage in 1939, Hankey served in Chamberlain's and Churchill's early wartime governments as Chancellor of the Duchy of Lancaster and Paymaster General. After the Second World War he campaigned against the war-crimes trials of German leaders, a controversial stance that coloured his later reputation. His diaries are a major source for British high politics.
