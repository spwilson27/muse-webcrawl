Property

Property is a system of rights that gives people legal control of valuable things and the ability to exclude others from them. The objects of property rights may be tangible — land, buildings, goods — or intangible, such as shares, patents and copyrights. Property is a central concept in law, economics and political philosophy: legal theorists often describe ownership not as a single right but as a "bundle of rights", including the rights to possess, use, manage, derive income from, transfer and exclude others from a thing.

Types

Real property (real estate) comprises land and things permanently attached to it, while personal property (chattels) covers movable things. Private property is owned by individuals or firms; public property by the state; and common property by a defined group, as in common land. Intellectual property grants time-limited exclusive rights over creations of the mind. Ownership may be individual, joint, or corporate, and interests in land can be split into estates such as freehold and leasehold.

Theory and history

Philosophers have long contested the justification of property. John Locke argued that individuals acquire property by "mixing their labour" with nature; Jean-Jacques Rousseau and later Karl Marx treated private property as a source of inequality and alienation, while others defend it as essential to liberty and prosperity. Legal systems worldwide protect property: the right appears in the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (Article 17) and many constitutions, which also typically allow expropriation for public purposes with compensation (eminent domain). Economists stress the role of secure, clearly defined property rights in enabling investment, trade and the efficient use of resources, while the "tragedy of the commons" describes over-exploitation where access is open and unmanaged.
