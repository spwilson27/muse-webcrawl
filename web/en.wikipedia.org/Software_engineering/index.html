Software engineering

Software engineering is a branch of both computer science and engineering focused on developing software systems. It applies engineering principles and computer programming expertise to the development and maintenance of software systems, with the aim of producing software that is reliable, efficient, maintainable, and delivered on time and within budget.

History

The term "software engineering" was popularized by the NATO Software Engineering Conferences held in 1968 and 1969, convened in response to the "software crisis" of the 1960s, when projects frequently ran over budget, missed deadlines, and produced error-prone software. Margaret Hamilton, who led the software team for the Apollo program, is credited with helping to establish the discipline as a legitimate engineering practice.

During the 1970s and 1980s, structured programming, modular design, and process models such as the waterfall model shaped practice. The 1990s saw the rise of object-oriented methods and the Unified Modeling Language (UML). The publication of the Agile Manifesto in 2001 shifted emphasis toward iterative development and customer collaboration, and practices such as DevOps and continuous delivery later blurred the boundary between development and operations.

The software development life cycle

Software engineering organizes work into phases commonly called the software development life cycle (SDLC):

  • Requirements engineering — eliciting and documenting what the system must do
  • Design — defining architecture, components, interfaces, and data models
  • Implementation — writing and integrating code
  • Testing — verifying and validating the software against requirements
  • Deployment — releasing the software to users
  • Maintenance — fixing defects and evolving the system over time

Methodologies

Development methodologies structure how teams work. The waterfall model proceeds through phases sequentially. Agile methods such as Scrum and Kanban emphasize short iterations, feedback, and adaptability. Other approaches include the spiral model, extreme programming (XP), and lean software development. Modern teams commonly combine these with practices like test-driven development, code review, and continuous integration.

Quality and testing

Software quality attributes include correctness, reliability, usability, performance, security, and maintainability. Testing spans unit tests of small components, integration tests of combined modules, system tests, and acceptance tests with users. Formal methods and static analysis can provide stronger guarantees for critical systems.

Education and profession

Software engineering is taught as a distinct degree subject at many universities, and bodies of knowledge such as SWEBOK (Software Engineering Body of Knowledge) codify the field's core areas. Practitioners are known as software engineers, and professional societies including the IEEE and ACM support standards and codes of ethics.

Criticism

Some critics argue that software construction differs fundamentally from classical engineering because software is intangible and does not wear out, and that overly rigid processes can stifle creativity. Nevertheless, the engineering framing is widely accepted for large and safety-critical systems.
