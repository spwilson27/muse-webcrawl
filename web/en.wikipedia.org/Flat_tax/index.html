Flat tax

A flat tax (also called a single tax rate or proportional tax in its simplest form) is a tax system with a constant marginal rate, usually applied to individual or corporate income. It stands in contrast to a progressive tax, in which the rate rises with the amount taxed. Many systems described as "flat" retain a tax-free personal allowance, which introduces some progressivity in average rates while keeping a single marginal rate above the threshold.

Design

The best-known modern proposal is the Hall–Rabushka flat tax, developed by economists Robert Hall and Alvin Rabushka of the Hoover Institution: a single rate applied to business income (with immediate expensing of investment) and to wages above a generous family allowance, with most deductions and credits abolished. Supporters argue such a system is simpler, reduces compliance and avoidance costs, and encourages work and investment. Critics respond that moving from graduated rates to a single rate tends to shift the tax burden from high earners toward the middle class, unless the rate and allowance are set to prevent it.

Adoption

Flat income taxes attracted wide attention after several post-communist states adopted them: Estonia introduced a flat tax in 1994, followed by Lithuania, Latvia, Russia (a 13% rate in 2001), Slovakia, Ukraine, Romania, Georgia and others, often with the aim of simplifying collection and curbing evasion. Some later moved back toward progressive schedules — for example Slovakia added a second rate in 2013 and Russia introduced a higher top rate in 2021 and a fuller progressive scale from 2025. In the United States, flat-rate proposals have featured in presidential campaigns (notably Steve Forbes in 1996 and 2000), and several US states levy a single-rate state income tax.

Related concepts

A pure proportional tax applies the same rate to all income with no allowance. Consumption taxes such as a uniform VAT are proportional to spending rather than income. Economists evaluate flat taxes by revenue yield, distributional impact, simplicity and effects on labour supply and savings; assessments differ sharply by assumptions and by the details of each country's reform.
