The zebrafish (Danio rerio) is a small freshwater fish of the minnow family native to streams and rice paddies of the Ganges region in South Asia. Named for the five horizontal blue stripes along its body, it is a popular aquarium fish and, since the 1970s, one of the most important model organisms in biological research.

Description and habitat

Adults reach about 4–5 cm in length and live in slow-moving or standing fresh water, feeding on insects, crustaceans, and zooplankton. They spawn frequently, producing hundreds of eggs per mating, and the transparent embryos develop externally and hatch within about three days.

Model organism

Pioneered by George Streisinger and championed by Christiane Nüsslein-Volhard's large-scale mutagenesis screens, the zebrafish combines vertebrate anatomy with optical transparency, rapid development, and easy genetics. Around 70% of human genes have a zebrafish counterpart, and some 84% of human disease-causing genes have zebrafish orthologues. Zebrafish are used to study development, cancer, heart disease, and drug screening, and they are notable for regenerating fins, spinal cord, and heart tissue. The genetically modified fluorescent GloFish sold in the pet trade were derived from zebrafish.
