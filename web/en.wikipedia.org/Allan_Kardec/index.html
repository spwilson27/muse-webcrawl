Allan Kardec

Allan Kardec is the pen name of the French educator Hippolyte Léon Denizard Rivail (3 October 1804 – 31 March 1869), the founder and systematizer of Spiritism, a spiritualist doctrine whose core works he published in the mid-nineteenth century.

Life

Rivail was born in Lyon and educated at Johann Heinrich Pestalozzi's institute at Yverdon, Switzerland. He became a teacher and author of textbooks on grammar, arithmetic, and pedagogy, and founded schools and courses for poor students in Paris. In the 1850s he began investigating the "table-turning" phenomena then fashionable, attending séances and compiling questions he put to mediums.

Works

Adopting the name Allan Kardec — which he believed to have been his name in a previous incarnation as a Druid — he published The Spirits' Book (1857), a catechism-style compilation of teachings attributed to spirits, followed by The Mediums' Book (1861), The Gospel According to Spiritism (1864), Heaven and Hell (1865), and The Genesis (1868). Together these five works constitute the basic codification of Spiritist doctrine, centred on belief in God, the immortality of the soul, reincarnation, and moral progress through charity.

Legacy

Kardec founded the Parisian Society of Spiritist Studies in 1858 and edited the monthly Revue Spirite. Spiritism spread widely in France, Spain, and especially Brazil, where Kardec's books remain best-sellers and his maxim "Without charity there is no salvation" is widely quoted. He is buried at Père Lachaise Cemetery in Paris, where his dolmen-style tomb bears the inscription "To be born, to die, to be reborn yet again, and constantly progress — that is the Law."
