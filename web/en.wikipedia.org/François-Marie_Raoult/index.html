François-Marie Raoult

François-Marie Raoult (1830–1901), French chemist known for Raoult's law.

François-Marie Raoult (10 May 1830 – 1 April 1901) was a French chemist best known for his research on the properties of solutions, which laid much of the foundation of physical chemistry. His name is attached to Raoult's law, which describes the vapour pressure of an ideal solution.

Life and career

Raoult was born in Fournes-en-Weppes, in northern France. He began his career teaching in lycées while pursuing his doctorate, which he obtained in Paris in 1863 with work on the electromotive forces of voltaic cells. In 1867 he took up a post at the University of Grenoble, where he became professor of chemistry in 1870 and remained for the rest of his career, despite opportunities elsewhere.

Scientific work

From the late 1870s Raoult turned to the study of freezing points and vapour pressures of solutions. He showed that the lowering of the freezing point of a solvent by a dissolved substance is proportional to the amount dissolved, a generalisation (the method of cryoscopy) that allowed chemists to determine molecular weights of solutes. In 1887 he published the relationship now called Raoult's law: the partial vapour pressure of each component of an ideal mixture of liquids is equal to the vapour pressure of the pure component multiplied by its mole fraction in the mixture.

Raoult's measurements provided crucial early support for the emerging theory of solutions associated with van 't Hoff and Arrhenius. He received the Davy Medal of the Royal Society in 1892 and was made a corresponding member of numerous academies.
