Mount Sinai

Mount Sinai (Hebrew: Har Sinai; Arabic: Jabal Musa, "Mountain of Moses") is the mountain at which the Ten Commandments were given to Moses by God, according to the Book of Exodus in the Hebrew Bible. In the biblical narrative, Moses ascends the mountain and receives the tablets of the Law after the Exodus of the Israelites from Egypt. The mountain is a sacred site in Judaism, Christianity, and Islam.

The exact location of the biblical Mount Sinai is a matter of long-standing debate. Since the 4th century CE, Christian tradition has identified it with a peak (2,285 metres, or 7,497 feet) in the southern Sinai Peninsula of Egypt, near Saint Catherine's Monastery. This peak, Jebel Musa, rises above the surrounding granite mountains and is ascended by pilgrims via two routes: the Steps of Repentance, some 3,750 stone steps, and a longer, gentler camel path.

Biblical account

According to Exodus, the Israelites camped at the foot of the mountain "in the third month" after leaving Egypt. Amid thunder, lightning, and a thick cloud, God descended upon the mountain in fire, and Moses received the Ten Commandments and the rest of the Torah. While Moses was on the mountain for forty days, the people below fashioned and worshipped the Golden Calf. Sinai is also called Mount Horeb in parts of the Bible, and in Islamic tradition Moses (Musa) is a major prophet; the mountain is known in Arabic as Jabal Musa and the surrounding area as Tur Sinai.

Saint Catherine's Monastery

At the foot of the mountain stands Saint Catherine's Monastery, built between 548 and 565 CE by order of the Byzantine emperor Justinian I. It is one of the oldest continuously inhabited Christian monasteries in the world and is renowned for its library of early manuscripts and icons. The site is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Alternative identifications

Scholars and explorers have proposed other candidates, including Jebel Serbal in the Sinai, Mount Hermon, sites in the Negev, and Jabal al-Lawz in northwestern Saudi Arabia. No consensus exists, and many scholars treat the biblical itinerary as theological rather than strictly geographic.
