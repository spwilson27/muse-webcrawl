Open seat

In United States politics, an open seat is an election where an incumbent officeholder does not seek re-election. Open seats occur for many reasons: the incumbent may retire, be term-limited, die in office, resign, seek a different office, or (in the case of legislative districts) be displaced by redistricting. Because an incumbent's name recognition, fundraising network, and record typically confer a large electoral advantage, open-seat races are generally far more competitive than races in which an incumbent stands for re-election.

Competitiveness

Political scientists and campaign strategists regard open seats as prime pickup opportunities for the party not holding the office. Open-seat primaries tend to attract larger and more diverse fields of candidates, and general elections for open seats draw disproportionate spending from national party committees. In the U.S. House of Representatives, only a small number of seats are open in any given cycle, yet they account for a large share of party changes in control.

Causes

  • Retirement of the incumbent
  • Term limits, especially in state legislatures
  • The incumbent running for another office
  • Death or resignation
  • Redistricting merging two incumbents into one district
