Corporate social responsibility (CSR) is a model of corporate self-regulation in which a business aims to contribute to societal goals — such as environmental protection, social equity, and community welfare — beyond its legal obligations and the pursuit of profit. It is closely related to stakeholder theory, sustainable development, and the environmental, social, and governance (ESG) criteria used by investors.

Development of the concept

Although business philanthropy is older, the modern concept dates from the mid-twentieth century, notably Howard Bowen's Social Responsibilities of the Businessman (1953). In the 1970s Milton Friedman influentially argued the contrary position — that a firm's only social responsibility is to increase its profits within the rules of the game. Archie Carroll's "pyramid of CSR" (1991) organised responsibilities into economic, legal, ethical, and philanthropic layers, while John Elkington's "triple bottom line" (1994) proposed measuring performance by people, planet, and profit.

Practices

CSR encompasses environmental initiatives such as emissions reduction and sustainable sourcing; labour practices and supply-chain standards; charitable giving and employee volunteering; ethical marketing; and reporting under frameworks such as the Global Reporting Initiative. Some jurisdictions require CSR activity or disclosure: India, for example, mandates that large companies spend two percent of average net profits on CSR.

Criticism

Critics argue that CSR can function as public relations or "greenwashing" that masks harmful practices, diverts attention from regulation, and lacks accountability because it is voluntary. Supporters respond that genuine CSR aligns long-term business interests with social welfare and that market and investor pressure increasingly reward it.
