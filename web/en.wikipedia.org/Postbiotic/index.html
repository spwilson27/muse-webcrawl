Postbiotic

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Postbiotics are preparations of inanimate microorganisms that confer a health benefit.

A postbiotic is defined by the International Scientific Association for Probiotics and Prebiotics (ISAPP), in its 2021 consensus statement, as a "preparation of inanimate microorganisms and/or their components that confers a health benefit on the host". The definition deliberately requires that the microorganisms be deliberately inactivated — killed — and that a health benefit be demonstrated; it excludes purified microbial metabolites alone, such as isolated short-chain fatty acids, which the panel considered a separate category.

Background

The concept gathered earlier terms such as "paraprobiotics", "ghost probiotics", and "tyndallised probiotics", which referred to non-viable microbial cells that nonetheless retain biological activity. Because the microbes in a postbiotic are dead, such products offer practical advantages over live probiotics: longer shelf life, easier standardisation, no risk of infection from live organisms in vulnerable patients, and no possibility of the transferred genes for antibiotic resistance spreading from living bacteria.

Mechanisms and evidence

Proposed mechanisms include interaction of microbial cell-wall components with the gut immune system, production of beneficial metabolites during fermentation before inactivation, and strengthening of the intestinal barrier. Studied examples include heat-killed strains of Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium species investigated for irritable bowel syndrome, infant colic, respiratory infections, and diarrhoea. The evidence base remains smaller than that for established probiotics, and health claims vary by jurisdiction; regulators generally treat each preparation on its own data rather than as a class-wide benefit.
