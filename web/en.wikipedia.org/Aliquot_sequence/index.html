Aliquot sequence

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In mathematics, an aliquot sequence is a sequence of positive integers in which each term is the sum of the proper divisors of the previous term. If the sequence reaches the number 1, it ends, since the sum of the proper divisors of 1 is 0. Aliquot sequences are a classical topic in number theory, closely related to perfect numbers, amicable pairs and sociable numbers.

Behaviour

An aliquot sequence can behave in several ways. It may terminate by reaching a prime (whose proper-divisor sum is 1) and then 1; it may reach a perfect number such as 6 or 28, which maps to itself and so forms a cycle of length 1; it may enter an amicable pair such as 220 and 284, a cycle of length 2; or it may cycle through a longer sociable chain. For example, the aliquot sequence starting at 12 runs 12, 16, 15, 9, 4, 3, 1, 0 and terminates.

Open problems

Many aliquot sequences have never been resolved: it is unknown whether they terminate or eventually cycle. The smallest starting number whose fate remains unknown despite extensive computation is famously 276, whose sequence grows to numbers with hundreds of digits. The Catalan–Dickson conjecture asserts that every aliquot sequence is bounded — either terminating or eventually periodic — while Guy and Selfridge conjectured the opposite for many even-numbered starting points. Aliquot sequences have become a popular target for distributed computing searches.
