Posterior probability

In Bayesian statistics, the posterior probability of a random event or an uncertain proposition is the conditional probability assigned after taking into account the relevant evidence or background information. It is the probability of a hypothesis given the observed data, computed from the prior probability and the likelihood using Bayes' theorem.

Definition

For a hypothesis (or parameter) H and data D, the posterior probability P(H|D) is given by Bayes' theorem:

P(H | D) = P(D | H) · P(H) / P(D)

where P(H) is the prior probability, P(D|H) is the likelihood, and P(D) is the marginal likelihood (evidence), which acts as a normalising constant. In the context of parameter estimation, the posterior is a probability distribution over the parameter: posterior ∝ likelihood × prior.

Example

Suppose a disease affects 1% of a population and a test detects it with 99% sensitivity and 95% specificity. A positive result updates the prior probability of 0.01 to a posterior of about 0.01×0.99 / (0.01×0.99 + 0.99×0.05) ≈ 0.167 — roughly a 17% chance that a positively tested person has the disease, illustrating how the posterior balances prior belief against evidence.

Applications

Posterior distributions are the central output of Bayesian inference and are used in Bayesian model comparison (via Bayes factors), credible intervals, medical diagnosis, spam filtering, machine learning, and decision theory. When the posterior cannot be computed analytically, it is approximated by methods such as Markov chain Monte Carlo sampling.
