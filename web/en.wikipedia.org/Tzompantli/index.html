Tzompantli

A tzompantli relief at Chichen Itza
TypeSkull rack / platform
CulturesAztec (Mexica), Toltec, Maya and other Mesoamerican cultures
PurposePublic display of sacrificial victims' skulls

A tzompantli was a type of wooden rack or platform used for the public display of human skulls, typically those of war captives or other sacrificial victims, in several Mesoamerican civilizations, most famously the Aztecs of Tenochtitlan. The name comes from the Nahuatl words tzontecomatl ("skull") and pantli ("row" or "banner").

Rows of skulls were threaded through holes pierced in the sides of the crania onto horizontal wooden poles supported by vertical posts. The principal tzompantli of Tenochtitlan, the Hueyi Tzompantli, stood before the Templo Mayor; Spanish conquistadors such as Andrés de Tapia reported tens of thousands of skulls on display, and excavations by Mexico's National Institute of Anthropology and History since 2015 have uncovered towers and racks of hundreds of skulls, including those of women and children.

Stone versions with carved skulls survive at sites such as Chichen Itza. The tzompantli was closely associated with human sacrifice and with the Mesoamerican ballgame, and its imagery survives today in Day of the Dead sugar skulls and popular art.
