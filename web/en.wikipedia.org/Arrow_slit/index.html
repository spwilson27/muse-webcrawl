Arrow slit

An arrowslit (often also referred to as an arrow loop, loop hole, or loop, and sometimes a balistraria if intended for a crossbow) is a narrow vertical aperture in a fortification through which an archer can launch arrows or a crossbowman can launch bolts.

The interior walls behind an arrow loop are often cut away at an oblique angle so that the archer has a wide field of view and field of fire. Arrow slits come in a remarkable variety. A common and recognizable form is the cross, accommodating the use of both the longbow and the crossbow. The narrow vertical aperture permits the archer large degrees of freedom to vary the elevation and direction of their bowshot but makes it difficult for attackers to harm the archer since there is only a small target at which to aim.

History

The arrowslit is believed to have been invented by Archimedes during the siege of Syracuse in 214–212 BC, when narrow openings in the city walls allowed defenders to shoot at Roman attackers. Arrowslits became widespread in medieval castles and fortifications across Europe from the late 12th century onwards, particularly under the influence of Crusader fortification design.

Forms

Arrow slits vary in design: simple vertical slits for longbows, cross-shaped openings (crossloops) permitting use of both bows and crossbows, slits with circular openings at the base (oil­lets) to increase the field of view or fire, and horizontal or fish-tail variants adapted to early firearms (gun loops). The internal splay is crucial: the opening widens on the inside of the wall so the defender can move and aim while presenting only a narrow slit to the enemy.
