Templo Mayor

The Templo Mayor (Great Temple) was the main temple of the Mexica people of Tenochtitlan, the capital of the Aztec Empire, located in what is now central Mexico City. It was a massive step pyramid, approximately 45 metres high, crowned by two shrines dedicated to the gods Huitzilopochtli, god of war and the sun, and Tlaloc, god of rain and agriculture.

History

According to tradition, the temple marked the spot where the Mexica found an eagle on a cactus — the sign their god had foretold. The pyramid was rebuilt six times as the empire grew, each version encasing the earlier one. After the Spanish conquest of 1521, Hernán Cortés ordered the temple demolished, and a cathedral was eventually built nearby using its stones. The ruins were largely forgotten until 1978, when electrical workers uncovered the great Coyolxauhqui monolith, prompting a major excavation programme.

Archaeology and museum

The excavations, led by Eduardo Matos Moctezuma, revealed enormous caches of offerings — sculptures, masks, shells and sacrificial remains — reflecting the temple's role as the ritual centre of the empire, including ceremonies of human sacrifice. The Templo Mayor Museum, opened in 1987 beside the site, displays the finds, including the 8-ton Coyolxauhqui stone.
