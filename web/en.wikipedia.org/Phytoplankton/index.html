Phytoplankton

Phytoplankton are the autotrophic (self-feeding) components of the plankton community and a key part of ocean and freshwater ecosystems. The name comes from the Greek words phyton, meaning "plant", and planktos, meaning "wanderer" or "drifter". Phytoplankton obtain energy through photosynthesis and so must live in the well-lit surface layer (the euphotic zone) of an ocean or body of water.

Ecology

Phytoplankton form the base of almost every aquatic food web. They are agents for primary production, creating organic compounds from carbon dioxide dissolved in the water, and are estimated to produce about half of the world's oxygen. Major groups include diatoms, dinoflagellates, cyanobacteria such as Prochlorococcus, coccolithophores, and green algae.

Their growth depends on the availability of nutrients such as nitrate, phosphate, silicate, and iron, as well as sunlight and water temperature. When conditions are favorable, populations can explode into visible blooms; some blooms produce toxins harmful to marine life and humans.

Climate role

Through photosynthesis, phytoplankton draw down carbon dioxide from the atmosphere, and the "biological pump" exports a fraction of that carbon to the deep ocean as organisms die and sink. Changes in phytoplankton communities are therefore important indicators and drivers of climate change.
