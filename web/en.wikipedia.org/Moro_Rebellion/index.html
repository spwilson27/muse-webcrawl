Moro Rebellion

Date1899–1913
LocationMindanao and the Sulu Archipelago, Philippines
CombatantsUnited States vs. Moro peoples
ResultAmerican victory; Moro territories incorporated into the Philippine government

The Moro Rebellion (1899–1913) was an armed conflict between the Moro peoples — the Muslim inhabitants of Mindanao and the Sulu Archipelago — and the United States military during the American occupation of the Philippines following the Philippine–American War. The Moros had resisted Spanish colonisation for over three centuries and continued their resistance against the new American rulers.

Background

Under the Bates Treaty of 1899, the United States initially recognised the authority of the Sultan of Sulu in return for neutrality. When the U.S. abrogated the treaty in 1904 and moved to administer the Moro Province directly — taxing, registering land, and banning slavery — armed resistance spread.

Major engagements

The conflict saw repeated campaigns in the Lake Lanao region and on Jolo. Notorious episodes include the First Battle of Bud Dajo (1906), where U.S. troops under Leonard Wood killed some 600–1,000 Moros, including women and children, sheltering in a volcanic crater, and the Second Battle of Bud Dajo (1911). The final major engagement, the Battle of Bud Bagsak in June 1913, fought under General John J. Pershing, broke organised resistance. The U.S. Army's adoption of the .45-calibre Colt M1911 pistol is often popularly linked to the difficulty of stopping Moro juramentado swordsmen.

Aftermath

With the creation of the Department of Mindanao and Sulu in 1914, the region passed to civil administration, but Moro grievances over land, Christian settlement, and political marginalisation persisted and fuelled later insurgencies in the southern Philippines.
