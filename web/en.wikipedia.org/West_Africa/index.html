West Africa, as defined by the United Nations geoscheme.

West Africa, or Western Africa, is the westernmost region of Africa. The United Nations defines Western Africa as the 16 countries of Benin, Burkina Faso, Cape Verde, The Gambia, Ghana, Guinea, Guinea-Bissau, Ivory Coast, Liberia, Mali, Mauritania, Niger, Nigeria, Senegal, Sierra Leone, and Togo, as well as Saint Helena, Ascension and Tristan da Cunha (United Kingdom Overseas Territory). The population of West Africa is estimated at 460 million people as of the 2020s, 54.2% of whom are urban.

Geography

The region stretches from the Atlantic coast eastward to roughly the Chad basin and from the Sahara in the north to the Gulf of Guinea in the south. Major geographical features include the Niger River, the Sahel belt between desert and savanna, the Guinea highlands, and coastal rainforests. Climate ranges from arid in the north to humid tropical on the coast.

History

West Africa was home to a succession of great empires and kingdoms — Ghana, Mali, and Songhai in the savanna; the forest states of Ashanti, Dahomey, Benin, and the Yoruba city-states — enriched by trans-Saharan trade in gold and salt. From the 15th century, European coastal trade, including the Atlantic slave trade, reshaped the region; by 1900 nearly all of it was colonized, mainly by France and Britain. Independence came mostly between 1956 (Ghana under Kwame Nkrumah led the way in 1957) and the mid-1970s. Fifteen states cooperate through the Economic Community of West African States (ECOWAS), founded in 1975.

Culture

The region is renowned for griot oral traditions, kora and djembe music, highlife and Afrobeat (pioneered by Nigeria's Fela Kuti), bronze casting of Benin, and the religious traditions described in West African Vodun and the Yoruba and Akan faiths, alongside widespread Islam in the north and Christianity in the south.
