The forty-seven rōnin (Japanese: 四十七士, Shijūshichishi) were a band of rōnin (leaderless samurai) in 18th-century Japan. They took part in the Akō incident, in which they avenged the death of their lord, Asano Naganori, by killing Kira Yoshinaka, and were then ordered by the shogunate to commit ritual suicide (seppuku). The story is a famous example of the samurai code of honour known as bushidō, and one of the most celebrated tales in Japanese history and theatre.

The incident

In 1701, Asano Naganori, daimyō of Akō, was assigned to receive envoys of the Emperor in Edo. Under the tutelage of the shogunal official Kira Yoshinaka, Asano felt insulted and drew his sword, wounding Kira. Drawing a weapon within Edo Castle was a grave offence; Asano was ordered to commit seppuku the same day, his domain was confiscated, and his retainers became rōnin.

Led by Ōishi Kuranosuke, 47 of the retainers waited nearly two years, dissembling to avoid suspicion, before attacking Kira's mansion in Edo on a snowy night in early 1703 (1702 by the Japanese calendar). They killed Kira, carried his head to Asano's grave at Sengaku-ji, and surrendered. After deliberation, the shogun ordered them to commit seppuku; they were buried beside their lord, where their graves remain a place of pilgrimage.

Legacy

The vendetta was rapidly dramatized in puppet theatre and kabuki as Chūshingura, and has been retold in countless plays, novels, films, and television dramas. Debate over the morality and legality of the rōnin's actions began immediately and continues today.
