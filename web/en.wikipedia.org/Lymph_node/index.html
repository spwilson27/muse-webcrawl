Lymph node

Structure of a lymph node

System: Lymphatic system / immune system

Number in humans: ~500–600

Drains: Lymph from regional tissues

A lymph node, or lymph gland, is a kidney- or oval-shaped organ of the lymphatic system and the adaptive immune system. Lymph nodes are widely distributed throughout the body, linked by lymphatic vessels, and act as filters: lymph percolates through them, bringing foreign particles and antigens into contact with the lymphocytes and macrophages stationed inside. Humans have roughly 500 to 600 lymph nodes, clustered in the neck, armpits, chest, abdomen, and groin.

Structure

Each node is enclosed in a fibrous capsule and divided into an outer cortex — containing follicles rich in B cells — a paracortex dominated by T cells, and an inner medulla with antibody-secreting plasma cells. Lymph enters through afferent vessels, flows through sinuses lined with macrophages, and leaves by efferent vessels at the hilum, which also admits blood vessels and nerves.

Clinical significance

Swollen, tender lymph nodes (lymphadenopathy) are a common sign of infection, as immune cells proliferate in response to pathogens. Persistent painless enlargement may indicate lymphoma or metastatic cancer: the first node draining a tumour, the "sentinel node", is routinely biopsied in cancers such as breast cancer and melanoma to stage the disease. Lymph-node examination is a standard part of the physical examination.
