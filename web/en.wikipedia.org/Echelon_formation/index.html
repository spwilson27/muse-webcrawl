Echelon formation

An echelon formation is a military formation in which units — soldiers, vehicles, ships or aircraft — are arranged diagonally, each offset behind and to one side of the unit ahead, like steps. The word comes from the French échelle ("ladder"). Formations are described as echelon right or echelon left depending on the direction in which successive units are stepped back.

Land warfare

Echelon arrangements have been used since antiquity; the Theban general Epaminondas advanced in an oblique, echeloned order at Leuctra (371 BC). An echelon attack commits successive lines one after another to sustain pressure or to exploit a breakthrough, a concept developed in depth in modern doctrine, where formations attack "in echelon" with reserves following to maintain momentum. Armored units also advance in echelon to cover a flank while maximising frontal firepower.

Air and naval use

In aviation, echelon is a basic parade and tactical formation in which aircraft fly at the same altitude stepped back at roughly 45 degrees from the leader, on either side. Migrating birds such as geese fly in an echelon-like V formation for aerodynamic advantage. Naval fleets have used echeloned lines ahead in fleet manoeuvres.

Other uses

The term is also applied to supplies and organizations deployed "in echelon", and gives its name to the ECHELON signals-intelligence network, a reference to layered surveillance.
