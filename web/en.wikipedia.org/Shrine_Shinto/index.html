Shrine Shinto (神社神道, Jinja Shintō) is a category of Shinto that takes the form of the Shinto faith as spread via shrines. It is the most popular form of Shinto in present-day Japan. It contrasts with Sect Shinto, folk Shinto, and the former State Shinto. The arrival of Buddhism in Japan starting in the 6th century caused Shinto to evolve significantly, with many practices and shrine rituals shaped by interaction with Buddhist institutions.

After the Second World War, shrines were separated from state control, and the Association of Shinto Shrines (Jinja Honcho) was established in 1946 to serve as an umbrella organisation for most of Japan's roughly 80,000 shrines.

Beliefs and practice

Shrine Shinto centres on the worship of kami — deities, spirits, and ancestral presences — enshrined at particular shrines (jinja). Worship typically involves purification, offerings, prayers (norito), and festivals (matsuri). Visitors to a shrine pass beneath a torii gate, purify their hands and mouth at a water basin (temizuya), and before the hall of worship bow twice, clap twice, and bow once more when offering prayers or coins. Shrine priests (kannushi) and female shrine attendants (miko) conduct rituals.

Life-cycle observances associated with shrines include the first shrine visit of a newborn (hatsumiyamairi), the Shichi-Go-San festival for children, shrine weddings, and New Year visits (hatsumōde), which draw millions of worshippers in the first days of January. Charms (omamori) and fortunes (omikuji) are sold at shrines.

Shrines and hierarchy

Most shrines belong to the Association of Shinto Shrines, with the Ise Grand Shrine — dedicated to the sun goddess Amaterasu — at their apex. Other major shrines include Izumo-taisha, Fushimi Inari-taisha, Itsukushima Shrine, and the thousands of branch shrines of popular cults such as Inari, Hachiman, and Tenjin. The sacred object of a shrine, the shintai, is housed in the inner sanctuary (honden), while worshippers pray at the haiden (hall of worship).

History

Shrines developed from early Japanese ritual sites at sacred natural features such as Mount Miwa. From the Meiji period (1868–1912), the state reorganised shrine worship as State Shinto, ranking shrines and funding major ones. The Shinto Directive of 1945, issued under the Allied occupation, disestablished State Shinto, after which shrines became private religious institutions.
