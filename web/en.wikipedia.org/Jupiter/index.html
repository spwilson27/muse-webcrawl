Jupiter is the fifth planet from the Sun and the largest in the Solar System. It is a gas giant with a mass more than two and a half times that of all the other planets in the Solar System combined, and slightly less than one one-thousandth the mass of the Sun.

Structure and atmosphere

Jupiter is composed primarily of hydrogen, with a quarter of its mass being helium. It probably has a dense core of heavier elements surrounded by a layer of liquid metallic hydrogen. The visible atmosphere is organised into colourful horizontal bands of ammonia clouds, with turbulent storms at their boundaries. The Great Red Spot is a persistent anticyclonic storm, observed since at least the 17th century, larger than Earth. Jupiter has the shortest day of any planet, rotating in just under 10 hours, which produces an equatorial bulge.

Moons and rings

Jupiter has a large system of moons. The four Galilean moons — Io, Europa, Ganymede, and Callisto — were discovered by Galileo Galilei in 1610. Ganymede is the largest moon in the Solar System, bigger than the planet Mercury, and Europa is believed to harbour a global subsurface ocean. Jupiter also has a faint planetary ring system discovered by Voyager 1 in 1979.

Exploration

Robotic spacecraft have visited Jupiter since Pioneer 10 in 1973. The Galileo orbiter studied the system from 1995 to 2003, and NASA's Juno spacecraft has been in polar orbit since 2016, probing the interior, magnetosphere, and poles. ESA's JUICE mission, launched in 2023, will study the icy moons and arrive in 2031.

Observation and cultural history

Known since prehistoric times as a bright "wandering star", Jupiter is named after the Roman king of the gods. It is usually the fourth-brightest object in the sky and has been an object of religious and astrological significance in many cultures.
