The North Pacific Gyre (NPG), located in the northern Pacific Ocean, is one of the five major oceanic gyres — large systems of circular ocean currents formed by global wind patterns and the rotation of the Earth. It occupies most of the northern Pacific, extending roughly from the equator to 50° N and from Asia to North America, and is the largest ecosystem on the planet.

Circulation

The gyre circulates clockwise in the Northern Hemisphere. It is bounded by four major currents: the Kuroshio Current along its western edge off Japan, the North Pacific Current to the north, the cold California Current to the east, and the North Equatorial Current to the south. The centre of the gyre, near the horse latitudes, is a vast region of high atmospheric pressure, weak winds, and slowly descending water, notable for its biological calm and clear, nutrient-poor waters.

Great Pacific garbage patch

The gyre is best known for the accumulation of marine debris in its interior, especially within the eastern garbage patch — the concentration of floating plastics and other refuse popularly called the Great Pacific garbage patch, publicised by Charles J. Moore after he crossed the gyre in 1997. Much of the material consists of microplastics suspended in the upper water column rather than a solid island of trash. A 2018 study estimated the patch covered about 1.6 million square kilometres and contained tens of thousands of tonnes of plastic, much of it derelict fishing gear.

Ecology

Despite low nutrient levels in its centre, the gyre's edges support abundant marine life where currents upwell and mix. The interior subtropical waters are dominated by phytoplankton adapted to scarce nitrogen, including nitrogen-fixing cyanobacteria. Debris in the gyre has created a novel "plastic" habitat colonised by coastal species far from shore, a phenomenon researchers have termed the neopelagic community.
