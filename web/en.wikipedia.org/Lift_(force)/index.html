Lift (force)

Lift acts perpendicular to the oncoming flow around a wing.

Lift is the component of the aerodynamic force on a body that is perpendicular to the direction of the oncoming flow. It is most familiar as the force that supports an aircraft in flight, produced mainly by its wings. Lift contrasts with drag, the component parallel to the flow, and with weight and thrust in the standard four forces of flight.

How lift is generated

A wing generates lift by deflecting air downward: the shape and angle of attack of the aerofoil turn the flow, and the reaction to this downward deflection (and the associated pressure difference — lower pressure above the wing, higher below) pushes the wing upward. Explanations based solely on Bernoulli's principle or on equal transit time of air parcels are incomplete; rigorous treatments use circulation and the Kutta–Joukowski theorem, which relates lift per unit span to air density, flow speed and circulation.

The lift equation

Lift is commonly calculated as L = ½ρv²S CL, where ρ is air density, v is true airspeed, S is wing area and CL is the coefficient of lift, which depends mainly on the angle of attack and aerofoil shape. Lift therefore grows with the square of speed, which is why aircraft need a minimum takeoff speed and why wings can support far more weight at cruise speed.

Limitations

As the angle of attack increases, lift rises until the critical angle, beyond which the flow separates from the upper surface and the wing stalls, with an abrupt loss of lift. Flaps and slats increase the maximum lift coefficient for takeoff and landing. Lift also produces a by-product, induced drag, associated with wingtip vortices.

Other applications

Lift acts on sails, hydrofoils, helicopter rotors, wind turbine blades, propellers and the control surfaces and fins of missiles and racing cars — where it is often generated downward ("downforce") to increase grip.
