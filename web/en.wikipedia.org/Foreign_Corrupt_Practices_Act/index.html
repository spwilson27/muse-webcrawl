Foreign Corrupt Practices Act

EnactedDecember 19, 1977
Signed byPresident Jimmy Carter
Codification15 U.S.C. §§ 78dd-1 et seq.
Enforced byDOJ and SEC

The Foreign Corrupt Practices Act of 1977 (FCPA) is a United States federal law that prohibits US citizens and entities from bribing foreign government officials to benefit their business interests. Enacted in the wake of Watergate-era revelations that American corporations had made questionable payments abroad (including the Lockheed bribery scandals), the FCPA was the first law of its kind and later inspired the OECD Anti-Bribery Convention of 1997.

The FCPA is enforced jointly by the Department of Justice and the Securities and Exchange Commission.

Provisions

The Act has two main parts. The anti-bribery provisions prohibit giving or offering anything of value, directly or through intermediaries, to a foreign official for the purpose of influencing official action to obtain or retain business; narrow exceptions exist for "facilitating payments" for routine governmental action. The accounting provisions require companies listed in the US to maintain accurate books and records and a system of internal accounting controls. Amendments in 1998 extended the Act's reach to foreign companies and persons acting while in US territory.

Enforcement

Enforcement, shared between the Department of Justice (criminal) and the Securities and Exchange Commission (civil), expanded dramatically after 2004, producing multi-hundred-million-dollar settlements, including Siemens (2008, US$800 million in US penalties) and Goldman Sachs (2020, over US$2.9 billion globally in the 1MDB case). Penalties can include fines, disgorgement, and debarment; individuals face imprisonment.
