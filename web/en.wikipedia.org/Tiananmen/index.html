The Tiananmen (simplified Chinese: 天安门; traditional Chinese: 天安門; pinyin: Tiān'ānmén), also spelled Tian'anmen, is a monumental gate in the city center of Beijing, China, the capital of the People's Republic of China. It is widely used as a national symbol of China. Located to the north of Tiananmen Square, it was the southern entrance to the Imperial City and leads north to the Forbidden City.

History

The gate was first built in 1417 during the Ming dynasty and was originally named Chengtianmen (“Gate of Bearing Heaven”). It was damaged and rebuilt several times; in 1651, under the Qing dynasty, it was rebuilt and given its present name, meaning “Gate of Heavenly Peace”. During the Ming and Qing dynasties, imperial edicts were proclaimed from the gate, including the announcement of each new emperor's accession.

Role in modern China

On 1 October 1949, Mao Zedong proclaimed the founding of the People's Republic of China from the rostrum of Tiananmen. A large portrait of Mao Zedong hangs on the gate's south face, a feature added in 1949; earlier, portraits of Sun Yat-sen and Chiang Kai-shek had hung there at different times. The gate appears in the national emblem of the People's Republic of China. Flanking banners read “Long live the People's Republic of China” and “Long live the great unity of the world's peoples”.

Architecture

The gate is 66 metres long, 37 metres wide and 34.7 metres high, built on a red platform pierced by five arched openings, with a double-eaved hip roof of yellow glazed tiles. Two stone lions and two ornamental columns (huabiao) stand before it, and the Golden Water Bridges cross the moat in front.
