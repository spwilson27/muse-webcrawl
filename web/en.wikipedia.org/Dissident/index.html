Dissident

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A dissident is a person who actively challenges an established political system, doctrine, belief or institution. In a religious context, the word has been used since the 18th century for those who oppose the established church; in politics, it came into widespread use in the 20th century for opponents of authoritarian regimes, particularly in the Soviet Union and its satellite states, who were often subjected to imprisonment, internal exile, psychiatric confinement or forced emigration.

Soviet and Eastern Bloc dissidents

Prominent dissidents in the USSR included the physicist Andrei Sakharov, the writer Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn, and human rights activists of the Moscow Helsinki Group. In Czechoslovakia, the Charter 77 movement, associated with Václav Havel, pressed the communist government to honour its human rights commitments; in Poland, dissent coalesced around the Solidarity trade union. Dissident activity typically relied on samizdat — the clandestine copying and distribution of banned literature — and on appeals to the regime's own laws and international agreements.

Wider usage

The term is also applied to dissenters within organisations, to whistleblowers, and to opponents of governments in many other countries, including China, Iran, Cuba and Saudi Arabia. Notable examples include Chinese activist Liu Xiaobo, awarded the Nobel Peace Prize in 2010 while imprisoned. Political scientists distinguish dissidents, who use nonviolent and often legalistic tactics, from revolutionaries who seek the violent overthrow of a regime.
