Chandler wobble

The Chandler wobble or Chandler variation of latitude is a small deviation in the Earth's axis of rotation relative to the solid Earth, with a period of about 433 days, discovered by the American astronomer Seth Carlo Chandler in 1891. It causes the points of the Earth's geographic poles to move in an irregular circle with a radius of up to about 9 metres (0.2 arcseconds) as measured at the surface.

Discovery and theory

Leonhard Euler had predicted that a rigid Earth would exhibit a free nutation with a period of about 305 days. Chandler's analysis of latitude observations found two periodic components — an annual term and a longer one of about 427 days (later refined to about 433 days) — revealing that the real Earth is elastic, which lengthens the period, and that the oceans and fluid core modify the response. The discrepancy between Euler's period and the observed one became an important probe of Earth's internal structure.

Excitation

Unlike the free oscillation of a rigid body, the Chandler wobble would damp out within decades unless continuously excited. Studies, notably a 2000 analysis by Richard Gross using ocean models, showed that fluctuations in ocean-bottom pressure, combined with atmospheric pressure variations, supply most of the excitation needed to maintain the wobble; earthquakes, once thought important, contribute negligibly. Tidal and loading effects also play minor roles.

Monitoring

The wobble is tracked continuously by the International Earth Rotation and Reference Systems Service (IERS) using very-long-baseline interferometry, satellite laser ranging, GPS and other techniques. Knowledge of polar motion is essential for precise navigation, satellite positioning and astronomy, as it changes the coordinates of points on Earth relative to inertial space by metres.
