Lobes of the brain

The lobes of the brain are the major identifiable zones of the cerebral cortex. Each cerebral hemisphere is conventionally divided into four lobes, named after the bones of the skull that overlie them.

Frontal lobe

The frontal lobe, separated from the parietal lobe by the central sulcus, contains the primary motor cortex and the prefrontal cortex. It is involved in planning, decision-making, personality, expressive language (including Broca's area in the dominant hemisphere), and voluntary movement.

Parietal lobe

The parietal lobe integrates sensory information, particularly touch, proprioception and spatial awareness, and contains the primary somatosensory cortex behind the central sulcus.

Temporal lobe

The temporal lobe, below the lateral sulcus (Sylvian fissure), processes auditory information and contains the primary auditory cortex, Wernicke's area for language comprehension, and structures important for memory formation in its medial portion, including the hippocampus.

Occipital lobe

The occipital lobe at the back of the brain is devoted largely to vision, containing the primary and association visual cortices around the calcarine sulcus.

Additional regions

Some classifications also recognise the insular cortex, hidden within the lateral sulcus, and the limbic lobe as further lobes.
