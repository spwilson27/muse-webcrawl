Euphonium

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The euphonium is a medium-sized, three- or four-valve, often compensating, brass instrument in the tenor range. It is a conical-bore instrument — its tubing gradually increases in diameter throughout its length — which gives it a mellow, dark tone distinct from the cylindrical-bore baritone horn and trombone, with which it shares approximately the same range.

History

The euphonium was invented by Ferdinand Sommer of Weimar in 1843 as the Sommerhorn, although the name euphonium derives from the Greek euphōnos, "sweet-voiced". It developed from the ophicleide and serpent family and quickly became the principal tenor-voiced solo instrument of the British-style brass band, a role it retains today.

Characteristics

Most euphoniums are pitched in B♭ with a written range roughly from B♭1 upward for three and a half to four octaves. Professional models use a four-valve compensating system, patented by David Blaikley in 1874, which adds extra tubing when valve combinations are used, improving intonation in the low register. The instrument is typically played with a deep cup mouthpiece and is read in treble clef (transposing) in brass bands and in bass clef (concert pitch) in many wind band parts.

Repertoire

The euphonium is a featured solo instrument in brass bands and concert bands, with original concertos by composers such as Joseph Horovitz, Edward Gregson, and Philip Sparke. Orchestral parts occasionally calling for the instrument include Holst's The Planets (tenor tuba part), Janáček's Sinfonietta, and Strauss's Ein Heldenleben and Don Quixote. Noted virtuosos include Steven Mead, David Childs, and Simone Mantia.
