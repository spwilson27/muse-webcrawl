Possessory credit

A possessory credit (also called a possessory security or, in older usage, credit on pledge) is a form of secured lending in which the creditor takes physical possession of an asset belonging to the debtor and retains it until the debt is repaid. The transfer of possession both secures the loan and publicises the creditor's interest, so that third parties are on notice that the debtor no longer has free control of the property.

Legal basis

The classic possessory security is the pledge (Latin pignus), one of the oldest forms of security in Roman law and a fixture of both civil-law and common-law systems. A valid pledge generally requires a contract of loan, delivery of the asset to the creditor (or an agreed third-party holder), and continued possession for the duration of the security. Because possession is the foundation of the right, a creditor who surrenders the asset ordinarily loses the security, except where the return is temporary or obtained by fraud.

Examples

  • Pawnbroking — a customer leaves goods with a pawnbroker as security for a small loan, redeeming them on repayment with interest.
  • Field warehousing and documents of title — commodities such as grain or metal are held by a warehouse keeper who issues receipts pledged to a lender.
  • Lombard lending — banks lend against securities physically deposited with them, historically named after the Lombard merchants of medieval Italy.
  • Liens of artisans and carriers — a repairer or carrier in possession of goods may retain them until charges are paid, a possessory lien arising by operation of law.

Comparison with non-possessory security

Possessory credit contrasts with non-possessory devices such as mortgages, charges, and hypothecation, in which the debtor keeps the asset and the creditor's interest depends on registration or formal documentation for its publicity. Possessory security is simple and self-enforcing but inconvenient where the debtor needs the asset to earn the income from which the loan will be repaid; for that reason modern commercial law has largely shifted toward registered, non-possessory security interests, reserving possessory credit for consumer pawnbroking, commodity finance, and certain capital-market lending.
