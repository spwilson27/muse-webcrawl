Short tandem repeat


STRs consist of a short motif repeated head-to-tail a variable number of times.

A short tandem repeat (STR), also known as a microsatellite, is a region of DNA in which a motif of two to six base pairs is repeated in tandem — for example, (CA)n or (CAG)n — with the number of repeats varying between individuals. STRs occur abundantly throughout most genomes, including the human genome, and because their repeat counts are highly polymorphic and inherited in a Mendelian fashion, they are powerful markers in genetics and forensics.

Variation and mutation

STR repeat numbers change mainly through replication slippage, in which the newly synthesised strand mispairs with the template during DNA replication, adding or deleting one or more repeat units. Mutation rates are high by DNA standards (roughly 10−3 to 10−5 per generation), producing many alleles at each locus and high heterozygosity, which is exactly what makes STRs informative for distinguishing individuals.

Forensic and genetic applications

Analysis of a standard panel of STR loci — the CODIS core loci in the United States and corresponding sets elsewhere — by PCR amplification and capillary electrophoresis generates the profiles used in DNA profiling for criminal identification, paternity testing, and disaster victim identification. STRs are also used in linkage analysis, population genetics, conservation biology, and the authentication of cell lines.

STR expansion diseases

When STRs within or near genes expand beyond a threshold length, they can cause disease. Trinucleotide repeat expansions underlie Huntington's disease (CAG in HTT), fragile X syndrome (CGG in FMR1), and myotonic dystrophy, and the phenomenon of anticipation — worsening severity in successive generations — reflects further expansion during transmission.
