Glycated hemoglobin


An HbA1c blood test.

Glycated hemoglobin (also called hemoglobin A1c, HbA1c, or simply A1c) is a form of hemoglobin chemically linked to a sugar. Glucose in the blood reacts non-enzymatically with hemoglobin inside red blood cells; because red cells live for roughly 120 days, the amount of HbA1c reflects average blood glucose over the preceding two to three months.

Measurement

HbA1c is reported either as a percentage of total hemoglobin (NGSP/DCCT units) or in mmol/mol (IFCC units). Typical values are below 5.7% in people without diabetes; an HbA1c of 6.5% or higher on two occasions is a diagnostic criterion for diabetes, and 5.7–6.4% indicates prediabetes. An HbA1c of 7% corresponds to an estimated average glucose of about 154 mg/dL (8.6 mmol/L).

Clinical use

The landmark Diabetes Control and Complications Trial (DCCT) and UK Prospective Diabetes Study (UKPDS) showed that keeping HbA1c lower reduces microvascular complications such as retinopathy, nephropathy and neuropathy. Most guidelines recommend a target below 7% for many adults with diabetes, individualised for age, comorbidity and hypoglycaemia risk.

Limitations

Conditions that alter red-cell turnover — anaemia, haemoglobin variants, pregnancy, recent transfusion or blood loss — can make HbA1c misleading, in which case fructosamine or continuous glucose monitoring metrics may be used instead.
