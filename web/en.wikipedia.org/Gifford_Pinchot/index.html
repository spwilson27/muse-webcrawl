Gifford Pinchot (11 August 1865 – 4 October 1946) was an American forester and politician. He was the first chief of the United States Forest Service (1905–1910) under President Theodore Roosevelt, and twice served as governor of Pennsylvania. Pinchot is known for reforming the management and development of forests in the United States and for advocating the conservation of the nation's reserves by planned use and renewal — a philosophy summed up in his maxim of "the greatest good for the greatest number in the long run."

Early life and forestry career

Pinchot was born into a wealthy family in Simsbury, Connecticut, and educated at Yale University. Encouraged by his father, he studied forestry in France at the French National School of Forestry in Nancy, since no American school then offered the subject. Returning home, he began managing the forests at George Vanderbilt's Biltmore Estate in North Carolina, the first large-scale scientific forestry project in the United States. In 1898 he became head of the federal Division of Forestry, which under his leadership became the Forest Service in 1905, managing the national forests transferred into its care.

Conservation politics

Pinchot was Roosevelt's closest adviser on conservation and helped expand the national forests to some 200 million acres. His feud with Secretary of the Interior Richard Ballinger led President William Howard Taft to dismiss him in 1910, an episode that split the Republican Party and fed the Progressive movement. Pinchot's utilitarian conservation contrasted with John Muir's preservationism, most famously in their clash over the damming of Hetch Hetchy Valley in Yosemite National Park.

Later life

Pinchot ran unsuccessfully for the US Senate and served two terms as a reforming, prohibition-supporting Republican governor of Pennsylvania (1923–1927 and 1931–1935). His family home, Grey Towers in Milford, Pennsylvania, is now a National Historic Site. He died in 1946.
