Optical resolution

Optical resolution describes the ability of an imaging system — a telescope, microscope, camera or the human eye — to distinguish small details of an object, producing a distinct image. It is quantified as the smallest angular or spatial separation at which two points can still be recognised as separate, or as the finest resolvable spacing in a test pattern.

Diffraction limit

Even a perfect lens produces a blurred spot, the Airy disk, because of diffraction at the aperture. The Rayleigh criterion states that two point sources are just resolvable when the principal maximum of one diffraction pattern coincides with the first minimum of the other, giving an angular resolution θ ≈ 1.22 λ/D, where λ is the wavelength and D the aperture diameter. Larger apertures and shorter wavelengths therefore resolve finer detail. The Dawes limit gives a similar empirical rule for telescopes.

Other factors

Real systems are also limited by aberrations, atmospheric turbulence (seeing) for ground-based telescopes, sensor pixel sampling (per the Nyquist criterion), noise and motion blur. Techniques to exceed the classical diffraction limit include interferometry, adaptive optics, deconvolution and the super-resolution fluorescence microscopy methods (such as STED and PALM/STORM) recognised by the 2014 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Resolution is commonly specified in line pairs per millimetre, in arcseconds, or by the modulation transfer function of the system.
