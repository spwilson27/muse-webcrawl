The Kingdom of Dahomey was a West African kingdom located within present-day Benin that existed from approximately 1600 until 1904. It was established by the Fon people and developed on the Abomey Plateau. A significant regional power, Dahomey grew through conquest and through trade — including the Atlantic slave trade — with Europeans, especially the Portuguese, through its coastal port of Whydah (Ouidah).

History

According to tradition, the kingdom was founded around 1600 by the Fon, with Houegbadja (c. 1645–1685) often regarded as its true founder, who built the royal palaces at Abomey and codified laws. Under Agaja (1718–1740), Dahomey conquered the coastal kingdoms of Allada (1724) and Whydah (1727), gaining direct access to European trade. The kingdom later paid tribute to the Oyo Empire from 1730 to 1823 before reasserting independence under Ghezo (1818–1858), who reformed the military and administration.

Society and military

Dahomey was a highly centralized monarchy: the king owned all land in principle, censuses and taxes were organized, and royal authority was reinforced by the Annual Customs, ceremonies honoring royal ancestors. The kingdom is famous for its female military regiment, the Minon ("our mothers"), known to Europeans as the Dahomey Amazons, an all-women force that by the mid-19th century numbered in the thousands and fought with notable discipline. Dahomey's economy involved palm oil production increasingly after the suppression of the slave trade.

Conquest and aftermath

Under Béhanzin, Dahomey resisted French expansion in the Franco-Dahomean Wars (1890 and 1892–1894). The second war ended with French victory; Béhanzin was exiled, and Dahomey became a French colony and later part of French West Africa. The Republic of Dahomey became independent in 1960 and renamed itself Benin in 1975. The royal palaces of Abomey are a UNESCO World Heritage Site, and in 2021 France returned 26 royal treasures looted in 1892.
