Black powder, also known as gunpowder, is the earliest known chemical explosive. It consists of a mixture of sulfur, charcoal, and potassium nitrate (saltpeter). Invented in China during the 9th century, it was used for centuries as a propellant in firearms, as an explosive in mining and artillery, and in fireworks.

History

Chinese alchemists searching for an elixir of immortality discovered the mixture's flammability; by the 10th century it was used in fire arrows and bombs, and metal-barreled guns appeared by the late 13th century. The technology spread westward along the Silk Road and through the Mongol conquests, transforming warfare in Eurasia. For over six centuries it remained the only practical explosive and propellant.

Properties

Black powder burns rapidly (deflagrates) rather than detonating, producing a large volume of hot gas and dense white smoke. It is classified by grain size for uses from fine priming powder to coarse blasting powder. It is sensitive to friction and static sparks and leaves corrosive fouling in gun barrels.

Replacement and modern use

In the late 19th century, smokeless powders and high explosives such as dynamite displaced black powder in military and industrial use. It remains in use in fireworks, pyrotechnics, model rocketry, quarrying of dimension stone, and for historic and replica firearms.
