Constitution of Turkey

The Constitution of the Republic of Turkey (Turkish: Türkiye Cumhuriyeti Anayasası), also known as the Constitution of 1982, is Turkey's fundamental law. It establishes the organization of the government and sets out the principles and rules of the state's exercise of sovereignty, together with the rights and duties of citizens. The current constitution was ratified during the military rule of the National Security Council following the 1980 coup d'état, and it succeeded the Constitution of 1961.

History

The first constitution of the Ottoman Empire, the Kanun-ı Esasi, was adopted in 1876. The Republic of Turkey adopted constitutions in 1921, 1924, 1961 and 1982. The 1982 text was drafted by a Consultative Assembly appointed under the military government and approved by referendum on 7 November 1982, in a vote held together with the confirmation of Kenan Evren as president. It has been amended many times, most notably in 2010 and 2017.

Principles

The constitution defines Turkey as a secular, democratic and unitary republic. Its preamble emphasizes nationalism, secularism and the reforms of Mustafa Kemal Atatürk. The first three articles — on the form of the state, the characteristics of the Republic, and the integrity of the state, its official language, flag, national anthem and capital — are entrenched and their amendment is prohibited.

Rights and freedoms

Part Two sets out fundamental rights and duties, including equality before the law, personal liberty and security, freedom of expression, freedom of the press and freedom of association. Amendments in 2001 and 2004, adopted as part of Turkey's European Union accession process, expanded several of these protections and abolished the death penalty in all circumstances.

Amendments

The constitution has been amended on numerous occasions. The 2017 referendum approved a package of 18 amendments that abolished the office of prime minister and introduced an executive presidency, a change that came into force after the 2018 general elections. Debates over an entirely new, civilian constitution have recurred in Turkish politics.
