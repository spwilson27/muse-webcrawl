DDT

Dichlorodiphenyltrichloroethane, commonly known as DDT, is a colourless, crystalline, tasteless, and almost odourless organochlorine insecticide. First synthesised in 1874, its insecticidal action was discovered by the Swiss chemist Paul Hermann Müller in 1939. It was used extensively in the Second World War to control malaria and typhus among soldiers and civilians, and after the war for agricultural pest control.

Environmental concerns

DDT is highly persistent, accumulating in soil and in the fatty tissues of animals, and biomagnifying up food chains. Its metabolite DDE causes eggshell thinning in birds of prey, and populations of peregrine falcons, bald eagles, and pelicans crashed. Rachel Carson's 1962 book Silent Spring publicised these harms and helped launch the modern environmental movement.

Bans and current use

The United States banned DDT in 1972, and the Stockholm Convention (2004) restricts it globally to disease-vector control under WHO guidelines: indoor residual spraying remains an important tool against malaria in parts of Africa and Asia, where its use is credited with saving many lives and where the malaria burden is weighed against environmental costs.

Health

DDT is a probable human carcinogen (IARC Group 2A) and an endocrine disruptor; acute toxicity to humans is relatively low, which contributed to its once-enthusiastic acceptance.
