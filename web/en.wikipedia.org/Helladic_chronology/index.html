Helladic chronology

Helladic chronology is the system of relative dating used by archaeologists for the Bronze Age cultures of mainland Greece. It divides the period into Early, Middle and Late Helladic phases, each subdivided into numbered sub-phases (I, II, III, with further lettered divisions such as IIIA, IIIB and IIIC), based mainly on changes in pottery styles identified at key sites. The scheme is the mainland counterpart of the Minoan chronology used for Crete and the Cycladic chronology for the Aegean islands.

Early Helladic (c. 3200–2000 BC)

The Early Helladic period covers the first farming and early metal-using cultures of the mainland after the Neolithic. Early Helladic II (c. 2700–2200 BC) saw the rise of substantial settlements with monumental buildings such as the House of the Tiles at Lerna, long-distance exchange and specialised crafts. The period ends with destructions and cultural changes in Early Helladic III, sometimes linked in older scholarship to the arrival of Greek speakers.

Middle Helladic (c. 2000–1550 BC)

Middle Helladic culture is characterised by grey Minyan ware and matt-painted pottery, small settlements and intramural burials. Its final phase saw increasing contact with Minoan Crete and the Cyclades ("Minoanisation"), setting the stage for the sudden flowering of wealth at Mycenae, where the Shaft Graves (Grave Circles B and A) mark the transition to the Late Helladic world.

Late Helladic (c. 1550–1050 BC)

The Late Helladic period is the age of Mycenaean Greece: palatial citadels at Mycenae, Tiryns, Pylos and Thebes, Linear B administration, warrior elites and wide Mediterranean trade. Late Helladic IIIA–IIIB (c. 1400–1200 BC) represents the palatial peak; the palaces were destroyed around 1200 BC amid the wider Late Bronze Age collapse. Late Helladic IIIC was a period of regional survival and gradual decline, after which the sequence gives way to the Submycenaean and Protogeometric phases of the early Iron Age.

Dating

Because Helladic chronology is relative, absolute calendar dates are established by cross-dating with historically dated Egyptian and Near Eastern contexts, imported objects, and scientific methods such as radiocarbon dating. The precise placement of some horizons — most famously the eruption of Thera (Santorini) in relation to the Late Helladic IA phase — remains debated between "high" and "low" chronologies.
