Scientometrics

Scientometrics is the quantitative study of science, communication in science, and science policy. Among the broader topics investigated are the analysis of scientific publications and their citations, the mapping of scientific fields, and the use of indicators in research management and policy contexts. Scientometrics overlaps with bibliometrics, which focuses on the quantitative analysis of documents, and with informetrics, the quantitative study of information in general.

History

The term scientometrics was coined in the Russian language (naukometriya) by Vasily V. Nalimov and Z. M. Mul'chenko in 1969. The field grew with the founding of the journal Scientometrics by Tibor Braun in 1978 and with the widespread availability of citation databases such as the Science Citation Index, created by Eugene Garfield and the Institute for Scientific Information.

Methods and indicators

Common scientometric indicators include the impact factor of journals, citation counts for individual articles and researchers, and composite measures such as the h-index. Co-citation and bibliographic coupling are used to map the intellectual structure of research fields, while co-authorship analysis reveals collaboration networks.

Applications

Scientometric measures are used by universities, funding agencies, and governments to evaluate research performance, allocate resources, and compare institutions. University rankings such as the Academic Ranking of World Universities rely heavily on bibliometric data.

Criticism

Critics argue that citation-based indicators can be gamed, vary widely between fields, and may distort research behaviour by rewarding quantity over quality. The San Francisco Declaration on Research Assessment (DORA) and the Leiden Manifesto for research metrics call for responsible use of quantitative indicators alongside qualitative judgement.
