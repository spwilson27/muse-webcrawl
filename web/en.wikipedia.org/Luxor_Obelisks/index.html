Luxor Obelisks

The western obelisk, now in Paris

Material: red granite (Aswan)
Height: ~23 m (Paris obelisk)
Date: reign of Ramesses II, 13th century BC

The Luxor Obelisks (French: Obélisques de Louxor) are a pair of ancient Egyptian obelisks, more than 3,000 years old, carved during the reign of Ramesses II. They originally stood on either side of the portal of the Luxor Temple in the Egyptian city of Luxor. The right (western) obelisk was moved to the Place de la Concorde in Paris in the 1830s; the left (eastern) obelisk remains in its original position in Egypt.

History

The obelisks were erected at the entrance of the temple built largely under Amenhotep III and Ramesses II, flanked by colossal statues of the king. Their shafts are inscribed with hieroglyphs celebrating Ramesses' military victories, including the Battle of Kadesh.

Removal to Paris

In 1829 Muhammad Ali Pasha, the Ottoman viceroy of Egypt, offered both obelisks to France. Although both were given, only the western obelisk was transported: engineer Apollinaire Lebas lowered it using a purpose-built apparatus, and the specially constructed ship Louxor carried it down the Nile and across the Mediterranean to Paris. It was erected in the Place de la Concorde on 25 October 1836, in the presence of King Louis-Philippe, on the spot where the guillotine had stood during the Revolution. France formally returned the second, unmoved obelisk to Egypt in 1981 under President François Mitterrand.

Description

The Paris obelisk stands about 23 metres high and weighs over 200 tonnes, quarried from the red granite of Aswan. Its pedestal depicts baboons, and its tip is capped with a gold-leafed pyramidion added in 1998. The obelisk remaining at Luxor is slightly taller and stands beside the first pylon of the temple.
