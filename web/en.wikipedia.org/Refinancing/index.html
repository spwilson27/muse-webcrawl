Refinancing

Refinancing is the replacement of an existing debt obligation with another debt obligation under different terms, typically to obtain a lower interest rate, change the loan duration, or extract equity. The most common context is residential mortgages, but refinancing applies to auto loans, student loans, credit card balances, and corporate and sovereign debt.

Refinancing replaces an old loan with a new one on different terms.

Motives

  • Rate-and-term refinancing: obtaining a lower interest rate or switching between adjustable and fixed rates, or shortening the term to build equity faster.
  • Cash-out refinancing: borrowing more than the remaining balance and taking the difference in cash, usually against accumulated home equity.
  • Cash-in refinancing: paying down principal at closing to qualify for better terms or remove mortgage insurance.
  • Consolidation: combining several debts into a single loan.

Costs and break-even analysis

Refinancing involves closing costs — origination fees, appraisal, title insurance, and possibly prepayment penalties on the old loan — commonly 2–6% of the loan amount. The decision hinges on the break-even point: the time at which cumulative monthly savings exceed the costs. Because refinancing resets the amortisation schedule, a borrower who refinances into a new 30-year loan late in the old loan's life may pay more total interest despite a lower rate.

Risks

Cash-out refinancing increases the secured debt against the home and can raise the risk of foreclosure. Serial refinancing ("loan flipping") that strips equity through repeated fees is a predatory lending practice restricted by laws such as the U.S. Home Ownership and Equity Protection Act. At the macroeconomic level, waves of mortgage refinancing during falling-rate periods (as in 2003 and 2020–21) are a major channel through which monetary policy reaches households.
