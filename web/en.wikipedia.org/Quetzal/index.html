Quetzal

Resplendent quetzal (Pharomachrus mocinno)
KingdomAnimalia
PhylumChordata
ClassAves
OrderTrogoniformes
FamilyTrogonidae
GeneraPharomachrus, Euptilotis

Quetzals are strikingly coloured birds in the trogon family, Trogonidae, found in forests, especially in montane cloud forests of Central America. The name quetzal derives from the Nahuatl word quetzalli, meaning "large brilliant tail feather".

The best-known species is the resplendent quetzal (Pharomachrus mocinno), famous for the male's iridescent green tail streamers, which can reach 65 cm (26 in) in length. It is the national bird of Guatemala, and its image appears on the country's flag and coat of arms; the Guatemalan currency is likewise called the quetzal.

Species

The quetzals comprise two genera: Pharomachrus, with five species including the resplendent quetzal, the crested quetzal, the white-tipped quetzal, the golden-headed quetzal and the pavonine quetzal; and Euptilotis, containing the single species known as the eared quetzal.

Diet and behaviour

Quetzals feed on fruits, berries, insects and small vertebrates such as frogs. The resplendent quetzal is a specialised fruit-eater, swallowing wild avocados whole and regurgitating the pits, which makes it an important seed disperser in cloud forest ecosystems. Quetzals are weak fliers and typically perch upright in the forest canopy.

Cultural significance

In Mesoamerican cultures, the quetzal was associated with the feathered serpent deity Quetzalcoatl. Its green tail feathers were prized in royal headdresses, and killing a quetzal was traditionally forbidden; birds were captured, plucked, and released.

Conservation

The resplendent quetzal is listed as Near Threatened by the IUCN, principally because of habitat loss from deforestation of Central American cloud forests.
