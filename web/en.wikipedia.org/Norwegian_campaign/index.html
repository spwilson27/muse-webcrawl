The Norwegian campaign (9 April – 10 June 1940) was the German invasion of Norway during the Second World War, launched under the codename Operation Weserübung. Germany simultaneously invaded neutral Denmark. The campaign pitted German forces against Norwegian troops and Allied (British, French and Polish) expeditionary forces sent to Norway's aid.

Background

Both sides had strategic reasons to act. Germany depended on Swedish iron ore shipped through the Norwegian port of Narvik, and sought naval and air bases on Norway's long Atlantic coast. Britain and France, for their part, planned to mine Norwegian waters and contemplated landings to cut the ore route and support Finland. The German plan, an audacious combined operation, struck first.

Course of the campaign

On 9 April 1940 German forces seized Oslo, Bergen, Trondheim, Narvik and other ports in a single stroke. The sinking of the heavy cruiser Blücher by Oscarsborg Fortress in the Oslofjord delayed the occupation of the capital long enough for the King, government and gold reserves to escape. Allied forces landed at Namsos, Åndalsnes and Narvik; the first two expeditions were evacuated in early May, while at Narvik Allied troops captured the town on 28 May — the first significant Allied land victory of the war — before withdrawing in June as France fell.

Aftermath

King Haakon VII and his government went into exile in London. Norway remained under German occupation, administered by Reichskommissar Josef Terboven with Vidkun Quisling's collaborationist regime, until May 1945. The Kriegsmarine suffered crippling losses of surface ships during the campaign, while politically the Allied failure contributed to the fall of Neville Chamberlain and the appointment of Winston Churchill as British prime minister.
