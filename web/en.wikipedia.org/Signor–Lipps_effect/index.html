Signor–Lipps effect

The Signor–Lipps effect is a principle in palaeontology proposed by Philip W. Signor and Jere H. Lipps in 1982, stating that, because the fossil record is inherently incomplete, neither the first nor the last fossil occurrence of a taxon coincides with its true origination or extinction. The observed stratigraphic range of a species is therefore always shorter than its real range.

Consequences

The most important consequence concerns mass extinctions. If many species actually disappeared simultaneously at a boundary, incomplete sampling would spread their last appearances backwards through the strata below the boundary, making the extinction look gradual rather than abrupt. The effect is therefore central to debates over whether extinctions — such as the Cretaceous–Palaeogene extinction event — were sudden or prolonged.

Related concepts

The effect underlies several statistical methods for placing confidence intervals on true extinction times from sighting records, both for fossils and for recently extinct species (sometimes called the "Lazarus effect" when a taxon reappears after an apparent absence, and reflected in debates about "dead clades walking"). The reverse problem — first appearances that make an origination look later than it was — is also recognised.
