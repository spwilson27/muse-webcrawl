Healthcare in the United States

Healthcare in the United States is provided by many distinct organizations, made up of insurance companies, healthcare providers, hospital systems, and independent providers. Healthcare facilities are largely owned and operated by private sector businesses. The United States spends more on healthcare per capita than any other country — roughly 17–18% of GDP — yet its system is not universal: health insurance is principally provided through employers, purchased privately, or supplied through government programs such as Medicare (for those 65 and older) and Medicaid (for low-income individuals).

History

Employer-sponsored insurance expanded during World War II, when wage controls made health benefits an attractive form of compensation. Medicare and Medicaid were established in 1965 under President Lyndon B. Johnson. The Patient Protection and Affordable Care Act (2010), commonly called Obamacare, expanded Medicaid eligibility, created health insurance marketplaces, and prohibited insurers from denying coverage for pre-existing conditions, reducing the uninsured rate substantially.

Coverage and outcomes

According to the Census Bureau, roughly 92% of Americans had health insurance in the early 2020s, leaving around 26 million uninsured. The U.S. performs well in areas such as cancer survival and medical innovation, but has lower life expectancy and higher infant mortality than peer nations, outcomes attributed to factors including cost barriers, administrative overhead, chronic disease, and social determinants of health. Health care prices in the U.S. are the highest in the world, and medical debt remains a leading cause of personal bankruptcy.

Providers and regulation

Care is delivered by a mix of private for-profit and nonprofit hospitals, clinics, and physician practices. The federal government operates the Veterans Health Administration for military veterans and the Indian Health Service for Native Americans. Regulation is shared between federal agencies such as the Food and Drug Administration and the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services, and state licensing boards and insurance commissioners.
