Square root

The square root function and its geometric interpretation.

In mathematics, a square root of a number x is a number y such that y² = x; in other words, a number y whose square (the result of multiplying the number by itself, or y · y) is x. For example, 4 and −4 are square roots of 16, because 4² = 16 and (−4)² = 16.

Every nonnegative real number x has a unique nonnegative square root, called the principal square root, denoted √x, where the symbol √ is called the radical sign or radix. For example, the principal square root of 9 is 3, denoted √9 = 3, because 3² = 9 and 3 is nonnegative. The term whose root is being considered is known as the radicand.

History

Square roots were known to ancient civilizations. The Old Babylonian clay tablet YBC 7289 (c. 1800–1600 BC) gives an approximation of √2 in four sexagesimal figures, accurate to about six decimal digits. The ancient Egyptians computed square roots using an inverse proportion method, and the Indian mathematician Aryabhata described methods for extracting roots in the Aryabhatiya. The symbol √ was introduced by Christoff Rudolff in 1525.

Properties

The principal square root function f(x) = √x maps the set of nonnegative real numbers onto itself. Its graph is the upper half of a sideways parabola. Important identities include √(xy) = √x √y for nonnegative x and y, and √(x²) = |x|. The square root of 2 is irrational, a fact proved in ancient Greece; √2 ≈ 1.41421356.

Computation

Square roots can be computed by the Babylonian method (also called Heron's method), an iterative algorithm that repeatedly averages a guess with the quotient of the number divided by the guess. Modern calculators and computers use Newton's method or digit-by-digit algorithms. See also standard deviation, which relies on square roots in statistics.

Square roots of negative and complex numbers

Negative numbers have no real square roots, since the square of any real number is nonnegative. In the complex number system, every nonzero number has two square roots; the square roots of −1 are the imaginary units i and −i.
