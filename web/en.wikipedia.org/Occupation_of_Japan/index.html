The occupation of Japan was the period from the end of the Second World War in 1945 until 1952 during which Japan was governed indirectly by the victorious Allies — in practice overwhelmingly by the United States — through the office of the Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers (SCAP), General Douglas MacArthur. It was the first and only time Japan has been occupied by a foreign power, and it reshaped the country's political, social and economic order profoundly.

Reform era, 1945–1947

Acting under the Potsdam Declaration, SCAP demobilised the Japanese military, purged wartime leaders from public life, and presided over the International Military Tribunal for the Far East in Tokyo. Emperor Hirohito was retained but renounced his divinity. Reformers dismantled the great zaibatsu conglomerates (in part), legalised trade unions, extended the vote to women, freed political prisoners, and carried out a sweeping land reform that turned millions of tenant farmers into owners. The new Constitution of Japan, drafted under SCAP in 1946 and effective from May 1947, made the emperor a symbol, established popular sovereignty and fundamental rights, and — in the famous Article 9 — renounced war as a sovereign right of the nation.

The "reverse course"

With the onset of the Cold War, American priorities shifted from democratisation to economic recovery and anti-communism: union activism was curbed, the purge was lifted from many wartime figures, and Japan was rebuilt as a key US ally and logistics base during the Korean War. A National Police Reserve, forerunner of the Self-Defense Forces, was created in 1950 despite Article 9.

End of the occupation

The Treaty of San Francisco, signed in September 1951 and effective 28 April 1952, restored Japanese sovereignty over the main islands, while Okinawa remained under US administration until 1972. The occupation left a durable legacy: the postwar constitution has never been amended, and the reforms of 1945–47 still structure Japanese politics and society.
