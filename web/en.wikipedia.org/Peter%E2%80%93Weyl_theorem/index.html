Peter–Weyl theorem

The Peter–Weyl theorem is a fundamental result in the theory of harmonic analysis, named after Hermann Weyl and his student Fritz Peter. It describes the structure of unitary representations of compact topological groups and is the noncommutative analogue of the theory of Fourier series.

Statement

In its basic form, the theorem states that for a compact group G, the matrix coefficients of finite-dimensional irreducible unitary representations of G form an orthonormal basis for the Hilbert space L²(G) of square-integrable functions on G. Consequently, every unitary representation of a compact group decomposes as a direct sum of finite-dimensional irreducible representations.

History

Peter and Weyl published the result in 1927. It built on Weyl's work applying group theory to quantum mechanics and on the earlier theory of representations of finite groups developed by Frobenius and Schur.

Applications

The theorem underlies the decomposition of functions on spheres into spherical harmonics, the theory of Fourier series on the circle group (where it reduces to the classical completeness of trigonometric functions), and much of the modern representation theory of compact Lie groups such as SU(2) and SO(3).
