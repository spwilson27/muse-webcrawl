Victor Hugo

Victor-Marie Hugo (26 February 1802 – 22 May 1885) was a French Romantic author, poet, essayist, playwright and politician, regarded as one of the greatest figures in French literature. His most famous works are the novels The Hunchback of Notre-Dame (1831) and Les Misérables (1862), the latter a sweeping epic of social injustice in post-Napoleonic France that has been adapted countless times for stage and screen. Hugo was a leader of the Romantic movement in France and a vocal campaigner for the abolition of the death penalty; his nearly two decades of exile under Napoleon III made him a symbol of republican resistance.

Early career and Romanticism

The son of a Napoleonic general, Hugo published his first poems as a teenager and won royal patronage. His preface to the verse drama Cromwell (1827) became the manifesto of French Romanticism, and the premiere of Hernani (1830) provoked a celebrated theatrical riot between Romantics and classicists. The Hunchback of Notre-Dame (1831), written partly to draw attention to the neglected Gothic cathedral, revived French interest in medieval architecture and preservation.

Politics and exile

Elected to the Académie française in 1841 and made a peer of France, Hugo moved from royalism toward republicanism. After Louis-Napoléon's coup d'état of December 1851 — which Hugo denounced as a crime — he fled to Brussels, then Jersey, and finally Guernsey, refusing amnesties until the regime fell in 1870. In exile he wrote his masterpieces: the poetry collections Les Châtiments (a savage attack on Napoleon III) and Les Contemplations (1856), and the novels Les Misérables (1862), Toilers of the Sea (1866) and The Man Who Laughs (1869). His daughter Léopoldine's drowning in 1843 haunted his later poetry.

Return and death

Hugo returned to Paris in 1870 to a hero's welcome, served as a senator under the Third Republic, and remained France's most revered living writer. When he died in 1885, more than two million people followed his funeral cortège; he was buried in the Panthéon. The avenue where he lived was renamed for him in his lifetime — an almost unheard-of honour.

Legacy

Hugo's influence extends far beyond France: Les Misérables alone has inspired films, a long-running stage musical and continuing debate about poverty and justice. His drawings and ink works, once a private pursuit, are now exhibited in museums, and his Guernsey home, Hauteville House, is preserved as a museum.
