Bitwise operation

In computer programming, a bitwise operation operates on a bit string, a bit array or a binary numeral at the level of its individual bits. It is a fast and simple action, basic to the higher-level arithmetic operations and directly supported by the processor.

Bitwise operators

The basic bitwise operators are NOT, AND, OR, and XOR. NOT (~) inverts each bit. AND (&) yields 1 where both corresponding bits are 1. OR (|) yields 1 where at least one bit is 1. XOR (^) yields 1 where the bits differ. For example, 1010 AND 1100 = 1000, and 1010 XOR 1100 = 0110.

Bit shifts

Bit shifts move all bits in a word left or right. A left shift by n positions multiplies an unsigned value by 2n; a right shift divides by 2n. Arithmetic right shifts preserve the sign bit of signed integers, while logical right shifts fill with zeros. Shifts are used in multiplication and division by powers of two, in packing data, and in graphics and cryptography.

Applications

Bitwise operations are used for bit masks and flags, low-level device control, checksums and hash functions, image processing, and optimisations where individual bits encode settings or permissions — as in Unix file permission bits.
