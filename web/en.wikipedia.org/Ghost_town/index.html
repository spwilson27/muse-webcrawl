Ghost town
DefinitionAn abandoned settlement with substantial visible remains
Common causesResource depletion, economic collapse, disaster, war, infrastructure bypass
Notable examplesBodie (USA), Pripyat (Ukraine), Kolmanskop (Namibia), Hashima (Japan), Centralia (USA)

Abandoned buildings are the hallmark of a ghost town.

A ghost town, or alternatively an abandoned city or town, is a deserted settlement that usually contains substantial visible remains. A town often becomes a ghost town because the economic activity that supported it — commonly mining, milling or a transport route — has failed or ceased, or because of natural or human-caused disasters such as floods, droughts, war, pollution or nuclear accidents. The term can also denote settlements that are greatly reduced in population but not wholly empty.

Causes

The classic ghost towns of the American West arose when a mine's ore ran out: camps that had grown to thousands of residents within a few years emptied almost as quickly. Bodie, California, a gold-mining town that peaked around 1879 with perhaps 10,000 inhabitants, is preserved by the state in a condition of "arrested decay." Other causes include railroads being rerouted away from a town, highways bypassing it, the closure of a single dominant employer, agricultural decline and soil exhaustion, flooding by new reservoirs, and government-ordered evacuation — as at Pripyat, abandoned in 1986 after the Chernobyl disaster, and Centralia, Pennsylvania, emptied by an underground coal-seam fire burning since 1962.

Around the world

In Namibia, the diamond town of Kolmanskop was abandoned in the 1950s and is now partly buried in desert sand. Japan's Hashima Island (Gunkanjima), a dense undersea coal-mining city, was evacuated in 1974 and later inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage site. In Europe, villages were abandoned after both World Wars and by rural depopulation; Spain alone has thousands of deserted hamlets. China's rapid urbanization has produced modern under-occupied districts sometimes loosely called ghost cities, though many later filled in.

Preservation and tourism

Some ghost towns are maintained as heritage sites or open-air museums and attract tourism, photography and film production. Preservationists balance public access against vandalism, looting and the natural decay of wooden structures in harsh climates. A few ghost towns have been revived by new industries, counterculture communities or their conversion into art colonies.
