Neural plate

The neural plate folds upward during neurulation to form the neural tube.

The neural plate is a key developmental structure that serves as the basis for the nervous system. Opposite the primitive streak in the embryo, ectodermal tissue thickens and flattens to become the neural plate. In humans, the neural plate appears as a slipper-shaped plate of thickened ectoderm around the middle of the third week of development.

Development

During the third week of embryogenesis, signals from the notochord and surrounding mesoderm induce the overlying ectoderm to form the neural plate. The cells of the plate are columnar and are known as neuroepithelial cells. The lateral edges of the plate rise as neural folds, and the depressed midregion forms the neural groove. Gradually the folds approach each other and fuse, converting the plate into the neural tube in the process called neurulation. Closure begins in the cervical region and proceeds cranially and caudally; the open ends, the neuropores, close by about the fourth week.

Signaling

Formation of the neural plate is inhibited by bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs); organizer tissues such as the node secrete BMP antagonists including noggin, chordin, and follistatin, permitting the ectoderm to adopt its "default" neural fate. Retinoic acid and Wnt signals pattern the plate along its anteroposterior axis, while sonic hedgehog from the notochord patterns the ventral midline, establishing the floor plate.

Clinical significance

Failure of the neural plate to close properly produces neural tube defects, including spina bifida and anencephaly. Adequate folate intake before and during early pregnancy substantially reduces their incidence. Cells at the border of the neural plate give rise to the neural crest, sometimes called the fourth germ layer.
