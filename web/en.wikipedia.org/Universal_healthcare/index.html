Universal healthcare

Universal healthcare (also called universal health coverage) is a health care system in which all residents of a country or region are assured access to health care services. It is generally organised around providing either all residents, or only those who cannot afford it, access to either the full range of health services or a core set of services, usually financed through taxation, social insurance contributions or a combination of both. The World Health Organization defines universal health coverage as ensuring that all people have access to the health services they need without suffering financial hardship.

Models

Universal systems take several forms. In the Beveridge model, exemplified by the United Kingdom's National Health Service, care is provided by public institutions funded through general taxation. The Bismarck model, used in Germany, France and Japan, relies on regulated non-profit "sickness funds" financed by employers and employees. The national health insurance model, as in Canada and Taiwan, combines public payment with largely private delivery. Some countries use mixed systems with both public and private insurance.

History

Germany under Chancellor Otto von Bismarck introduced the first national social health insurance legislation in 1883. The United Kingdom's National Health Service was created in 1948 following the Beveridge Report. Over the twentieth century most industrialised democracies adopted some form of universal coverage; the United States remains a notable exception among wealthy nations, relying on a mixture of private insurance and public programmes such as Medicare and Medicaid.

Debate

Supporters argue universal systems achieve lower administrative costs, better population health outcomes and protection from medical bankruptcy. Critics point to waiting times, fiscal burdens and limits on choice under some systems. Economists compare per-capita spending, which is markedly higher in the United States than in countries with universal coverage, while noting wide variation in outcomes and satisfaction across systems.
