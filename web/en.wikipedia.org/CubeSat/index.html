CubeSat

A CubeSat is a class of miniaturized satellite built from standardized cubic units. One unit (1U) measures 10 × 10 × 10 centimetres and has a mass of no more than 2 kilograms; common configurations include 1U, 2U, 3U, 6U, and 12U. The standard was developed in 1999 by Jordi Puig-Suari of California Polytechnic State University and Bob Twiggs of Stanford University, originally to give students hands-on experience designing, building, and operating satellites.

Design and deployment

CubeSats use a standard deployer, the Poly Picosatellite Orbital Deployer (P-POD), a spring-loaded tube that ejects the satellites from a launch vehicle as secondary payloads. Commercial off-the-shelf components, standardized interfaces, and rideshare launches have driven costs down dramatically, making orbit accessible to universities, startups, and developing nations. The first CubeSats were launched in June 2003 on a Russian Eurockot vehicle.

Applications

Uses range from Earth observation and remote sensing to technology demonstration, communications, astronomy, and biological experiments. Notable missions include MarCO, the twin 6U CubeSats that flew by Mars in 2018 relaying data from the InSight lander — the first interplanetary CubeSats — and large commercial constellations such as Planet's Dove satellites for daily global imaging.

Challenges

Limited volume and power constrain CubeSat capabilities, and the growing population of small satellites raises concerns about orbital debris and space traffic management. Most CubeSats are placed in low Earth orbit, where atmospheric drag eventually deorbits them, but operators are increasingly required to demonstrate disposal plans.
