WHIP


Sport: Baseball
Category: Pitching statistic
Meaning: Walks plus hits per inning pitched
Coined by: Daniel Okrent (1979)

In baseball statistics, WHIP — walks plus hits per inning pitched — measures how many baserunners a pitcher has allowed via hits and walks per inning of work. It is one of the most commonly used statistics for evaluating the effectiveness of a pitcher.

Formula

WHIP = (Walks + Hits) / Innings Pitched

Only walks and hits count against the pitcher; batters who reach on errors, by being hit by a pitch, or through catcher's interference are not included, even if they eventually score.

Interpretation

Lower is better. A WHIP of 1.00 or below is outstanding; league-average figures in Major League Baseball typically fall around 1.25–1.35, while a WHIP above 1.50 generally indicates a struggling pitcher. Hall of Fame reliever Mariano Rivera holds the modern career record at approximately 1.000, and Pedro Martínez's 0.737 WHIP in 2000 is widely regarded as the finest single-season mark of the modern era.

History

The statistic was devised in 1979 by writer Daniel Okrent, one of the founders of Rotisserie (fantasy) baseball, who originally called it "innings pitched ratio". It was popularised through the 1980s by the fantasy game and the writings of Bill James, entered mainstream baseball coverage in the 1990s, and is now published by MLB as an official statistic. While WHIP does not account for the quality or timing of hits (a home run counts the same as a single) and correlates imperfectly with run prevention, it remains a staple of sabermetric analysis.
