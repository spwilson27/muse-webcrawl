East Syriac Rite

The East Syriac Rite (also called the East Syrian Rite, Chaldean Rite, Assyrian Rite, or Persian Rite) is a Christian liturgical rite that employs the Divine Liturgy of Saints Addai and Mari and the East Syriac dialect as its liturgical language. It originated in Edessa, Mesopotamia, and was historically used in the Church of the East, the largest branch of Christianity in the Middle East for centuries, whose influence extended along the Silk Road as far as China and India.

History

The rite developed within the Church of the East, the church of the Sasanian Persian Empire, which organized its hierarchy under the Catholicos of Seleucia-Ctesiphon. Cut off from the Roman Empire's church by political frontiers and later by theological disputes after the Council of Ephesus (431), the Church of the East developed its own liturgical tradition in Aramaic, the language closest to that spoken by Jesus and the apostles. Its liturgy is traditionally traced to the apostles Addai (Thaddeus of Edessa) and Mari, disciples of the apostle Thomas.

The Liturgy of Addai and Mari

The central Eucharistic service of the East Syriac Rite is the Holy Qurbana celebrated with the Anaphora of Addai and Mari, notable among ancient anaphoras because, in its oldest form, it lacks an explicit institution narrative recounting the Last Supper. The liturgy includes preparatory services, readings from the Old and New Testaments, the signing of the cross, the fraction and consignation of the host, and communion. The liturgical year is divided into seasons called shawu'i, each of seven weeks.

Modern usage

Today the East Syriac Rite is used principally by the Chaldean Catholic Church and the Assyrian Church of the East, both centered historically in northern Iraq, as well as the Ancient Church of the East. The Syro-Malabar Church of India used the rite for most of its history, with varying degrees of latinization, and a restored form of its traditional liturgy has been promoted since the late twentieth century. In 2001 the Holy See formally recognized the validity of the Anaphora of Addai and Mari despite its lack of an institution narrative, facilitating limited Eucharistic sharing between Chaldean Catholics and Assyrian Christians.
