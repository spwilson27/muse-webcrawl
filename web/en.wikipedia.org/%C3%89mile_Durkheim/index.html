Émile Durkheim

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Born: 15 April 1858, Épinal, France
Died: 15 November 1917, Paris, France
Known for: Social facts, collective consciousness, anomie, functionalism

David Émile Durkheim (15 April 1858 – 15 November 1917) was a French sociologist. He is widely regarded as one of the principal founders of modern social science and formally established the academic discipline of sociology, helping to secure its place in French universities.

Life and career

Durkheim was born in Épinal in Lorraine into a rabbinical family, but led a largely secular life. He studied at the École Normale Supérieure in Paris and taught at the University of Bordeaux, where he founded one of the first sociology departments in Europe, and later at the Sorbonne. In 1898 he founded the journal L'Année sociologique. The death of his son André in the First World War deeply affected him, and he died in 1917.

Major works

His major works include The Division of Labour in Society (1893), which analysed the shift from mechanical to organic solidarity; The Rules of Sociological Method (1895), which argued that "social facts" should be studied as things; Suicide (1897), a landmark statistical study identifying egoistic, altruistic, anomic and fatalistic suicide; and The Elementary Forms of the Religious Life (1912), which treated religion as a product of society and developed the ideas of the sacred and profane and collective effervescence.

Legacy

Durkheim's functionalist approach influenced generations of sociologists and anthropologists, and concepts such as anomie and collective consciousness remain central to the discipline.
