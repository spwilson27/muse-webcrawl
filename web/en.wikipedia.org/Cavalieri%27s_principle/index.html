Cavalieri's principle

Cavalieri's principle, named for the Italian mathematician Bonaventura Cavalieri (1598–1647), states that if two solids are included between two parallel planes, and every plane parallel to these planes intersects both solids in cross-sections of equal area, then the two solids have equal volume. An analogous statement holds for areas in two dimensions.

History

Cavalieri presented the principle in his Geometria indivisibilibus (1635), using the method of "indivisibles" — an early precursor of integral calculus. Similar ideas appeared much earlier: the Chinese mathematician Zu Gengzhi stated an equivalent result in the 5th–6th century CE, and it is known in China as Zu's principle.

Applications

The principle gives elegant proofs of the volume of a sphere (by comparing a hemisphere with a cylinder minus a cone), the volume of cones and pyramids regardless of base shape, and results such as the napkin ring problem, where the volume of a drilled sphere depends only on the height of the remaining ring.

Relation to calculus

In modern terms, Cavalieri's principle follows from Fubini's theorem and the fact that volume is the integral of cross-sectional area.
