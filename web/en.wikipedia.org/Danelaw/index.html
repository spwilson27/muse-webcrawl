The Danelaw (from Old English Dena lagu, "Danes' law") was the part of England in the ninth, tenth and eleventh centuries where the laws of the Danish Viking settlers held sway in place of the laws of the Anglo-Saxons. The term also refers to the territory itself, roughly the northern and eastern counties from the River Tees to the Thames.

Origins

Following the invasion of the Great Heathen Army from 865 and Alfred the Great's victory over Guthrum at the Battle of Edington in 878, the Treaty of Wedmore and the subsequent treaty between Alfred and Guthrum fixed a boundary running along the Thames, up the Lea, to Bedford and along the Ouse to Watling Street. Land north and east of the line was recognised as under Danish control.

Settlement and society

Danish armies settled as farmers, and the region's place names preserve their presence in hundreds of names ending in -by (village), -thorpe (secondary settlement) and -thwaite (clearing). The Five Boroughs — Derby, Leicester, Lincoln, Nottingham and Stamford — formed a confederation of fortified towns, while York (Jórvík) became a major trading centre. The Danelaw's legal customs, including its system of wapentakes instead of hundreds and its land units, survived in local administration for centuries.

Reconquest

Alfred's successors Edward the Elder and Æthelstan systematically reconquered the Danelaw in the early tenth century; Æthelstan's victory at Brunanburh (937) confirmed English rule, and the last Scandinavian king of York, Eric Bloodaxe, was expelled in 954. Danish law and settlers were nevertheless absorbed into the English kingdom, and the word "Danelaw" continued to be used in legal texts into the twelfth century.
