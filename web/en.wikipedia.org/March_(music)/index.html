March (music)

A march is a piece of music with a strong regular rhythm, originally written for marching soldiers and now often played by a military band, marching band or concert band. Marches are typically in a duple or quadruple metre — 2/4, 4/4 (common time) or cut time (2/2) — with a tempo of about 100–120 beats per minute corresponding to a marching pace, although funeral marches are slower and concert marches may be faster. The march form is one of the most durable genres of band music and has influenced orchestral music, musical theatre and popular song.

Form

The classic march, as codified by John Philip Sousa (1854–1932), the American "March King", follows a fixed multi-strain form:

  • A short introduction, often four bars, in the tonic key
  • Two or three contrasting strains (themes), the second usually beginning in the tonic and moving toward the dominant or subdominant
  • A trio — a lyrical strain in the subdominant key, traditionally played more softly, sometimes followed by a "break strain" or "dogfight" of energetic interlude
  • A final reprise of the trio, often with added countermelodies and greater volume (the "stinger" ending)

Sousa's best-known marches include "The Stars and Stripes Forever" (1897) — the national march of the United States — "Semper Fidelis", "The Washington Post", "King Cotton" and "El Capitan".

History and types

Military music for marching date to antiquity, but the march as a composed genre developed in the 16th and 17th centuries alongside professional standing armies. The funeral march became an orchestral genre through works such as the slow movement of Beethoven's Eroica Symphony and Chopin's famous funeral march sonata. Operatic marches include the triumphal march from Verdi's Aida and the bridal chorus from Wagner's Lohengrin. British march tradition runs from Kenneth J. Alford ("Colonel Bogey") to Edward Elgar's Pomp and Circumstance marches; Germany produced composers such as Julius Fučík, whose "Entrance of the Gladiators" became famous as circus music. Related forms include the pasodoble of Spain, the wedding march, and the processional and recessional marches used in ceremonies.
