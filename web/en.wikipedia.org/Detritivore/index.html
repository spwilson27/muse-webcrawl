Detritivores, also known as detritophages, are heterotrophs that obtain nutrients by consuming detritus—dead particulate organic material, including decomposing plants and animals as well as feces. By feeding on this material, detritivores play a central role in decomposition and in the cycling of nutrients through ecosystems.

Role in ecosystems

Detritivores break large pieces of dead organic matter into smaller fragments, greatly increasing the surface area available to decomposer bacteria and fungi. In the process they release nutrients such as nitrogen and phosphorus in forms that plants can use, contributing to soil formation and fertility. In many ecosystems, especially forests and the deep sea where sunlight is absent, detritus-based food webs carry most of the energy flow.

Examples

Classic terrestrial detritivores include earthworms, millipedes, woodlice, dung beetles, and many springtails and mites. Aquatic detritivores include sea cucumbers, fiddler crabs, and numerous polychaete worms that process sediment on the seabed. Some fungi and bacteria perform similar functions, though they are usually classified separately as decomposers or saprotrophs.

Detritivores and other feeders

Detritivores are often confused with scavengers, which feed on larger pieces of dead animals, and with decomposers such as bacteria and fungi, which absorb nutrients at the molecular level. Detritivores, by contrast, ingest the material and digest it internally, producing feces that further feed the detrital food web.
