North Yemen is the geographic and political term for the northern part of Yemen, which from 1962 to 1990 existed as the Yemen Arab Republic (YAR). Before 1962, northern Yemen was ruled by the Zaydi imams as the Mutawakkilite Kingdom of Yemen, which had emerged from Ottoman rule after the First World War.

History

On 26 September 1962, shortly after the death of Imam Ahmad, army officers led by Abdullah al-Sallal deposed the new imam, Muhammad al-Badr, and proclaimed the Yemen Arab Republic. Royalist forces, supported by Saudi Arabia and Jordan, fought the republicans — who were backed militarily by Egypt — in the North Yemen Civil War (1962–1970). A republican victory and a compromise peace left the YAR in place, recognised by Saudi Arabia in 1970.

Later republic

The 1970s brought coups and instability, including the assassinations of Presidents Ibrahim al-Hamdi (1977) and Ahmad al-Ghashmi (1978). Ali Abdullah Saleh became president in 1978 and held the office for more than a decade, consolidating power and improving relations with the south. The discovery of oil near Marib in 1984 strengthened the state.

Unification

Following agreements reached as the Cold War ended, the Yemen Arab Republic united with the People's Democratic Republic of Yemen (South Yemen) on 22 May 1990 to form the Republic of Yemen, with Sanaa as capital and Saleh as president. A brief civil war in 1994 ended with northern forces victorious and unification preserved.
