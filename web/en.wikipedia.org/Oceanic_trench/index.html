Oceanic trench

Oceanic trenches are prominent, long, narrow topographic depressions of the ocean floor. They are typically 50 to 100 kilometers (30 to 60 mi) wide and 3 to 4 km (1.9 to 2.5 mi) below the level of the surrounding oceanic floor, but can be thousands of kilometers in length. There are about 50,000 km (31,000 mi) of oceanic trenches worldwide, mostly around the Pacific Ocean, but also in the eastern Indian Ocean and a few other locations.

Formation

Trenches mark the position where the flexed, subducting slab begins to descend beneath another lithospheric slab. They form at convergent plate boundaries where one oceanic plate subducts beneath another oceanic plate or beneath a continental plate. The descending slab creates a deep, V-shaped depression, often paralleled on the overriding plate by a volcanic island arc or mountain belt.

List of notable trenches

  • Mariana Trench — the deepest oceanic trench on Earth, east of the Mariana Islands, reaching about 10,935 m at Challenger Deep.
  • Tonga Trench — the second deepest, south of Tonga.
  • Japan Trench — off northeastern Japan, site of the 2011 Tōhoku earthquake and tsunami.
  • Peru–Chile Trench — along the western coast of South America, where the Nazca Plate subducts beneath the South American Plate.
  • Middle America Trench — off the Pacific coast of Mexico and Central America.
  • Java (Sunda) Trench — in the eastern Indian Ocean, source of the 2004 Indian Ocean earthquake.

Geological significance

Trenches are the surface expression of subduction zones, where oceanic lithosphere is recycled into the mantle. Subduction at trenches drives much of Earth's volcanism and generates the largest earthquakes, including megathrust events capable of producing devastating tsunamis. Material scraped off the downgoing plate may accumulate as an accretionary prism on the trench's landward slope.

Life in trenches

Despite extreme pressure and darkness, trenches host specialized ecosystems. Hadal zone fauna such as snailfish, amphipods, and xenophyophores have been observed at depths exceeding 8,000 metres.
