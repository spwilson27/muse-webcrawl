Waterspout

A waterspout is an intense columnar vortex, usually appearing as a funnel-shaped cloud, that occurs over a body of water and connects to the surface. Waterspouts are similar in character to tornadoes over land and are most common in tropical and subtropical waters, including the Florida Keys and the Gulf of Mexico.

Types

Meteorologists distinguish two main types. Tornadic waterspouts are true tornadoes that form over water or move from land to water; they are associated with severe thunderstorms and can be dangerous. Fair-weather waterspouts, the more common type, form along the surface beneath developing cumulus clouds, typically in light wind conditions; they usually develop through five stages — dark spot, spiral pattern, spray ring, mature funnel, and decay — and dissipate quickly if they move over land.

Hazards

Although fair-weather waterspouts are generally weaker than tornadoes, they can capsize small boats and pose risks to swimmers and coastal areas; mariners are advised to keep well clear. Waterspouts have occasionally been reported to lift fish and frogs, a proposed explanation for rains of animals.
