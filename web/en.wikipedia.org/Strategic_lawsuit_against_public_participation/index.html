Strategic lawsuit against public participation

A strategic lawsuit against public participation (SLAPP) is a lawsuit intended to censor, intimidate, and silence critics by burdening them with the cost and stress of a legal defence until they abandon their criticism or opposition. Typically, a SLAPP plaintiff does not expect to win the case; the goal is to achieve a chilling effect on speech through the litigation process itself, most often using claims such as defamation, conspiracy, or interference with business.

Characteristics

The term was coined in the 1980s by University of Denver professors Penelope Canan and George W. Pring. SLAPPs are commonly brought by corporations, developers, or public officials against citizens, journalists, activists, and community groups who have spoken out on matters of public concern — for example, opposing a development project or reporting on pollution. The lawsuits convert a political or public debate into a legal one, where the critic is placed on the defensive.

Anti-SLAPP laws

Many jurisdictions have enacted anti-SLAPP legislation allowing defendants to file a special motion to strike or dismiss at an early stage, often with a stay of discovery, a shifting of the burden to the plaintiff to show a probability of prevailing, and recovery of attorneys' fees if the motion succeeds. In the United States, more than thirty states — notably California and Texas — have anti-SLAPP statutes, and uniform and federal proposals have been debated. The European Union adopted an anti-SLAPP directive in 2024 to protect journalists and human rights defenders in cross-border cases.
