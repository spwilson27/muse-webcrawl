Sources of international law

Key textArticle 38(1), Statute of the ICJ
Primary sourcesTreaties, customary international law, general principles
Subsidiary meansJudicial decisions, scholarly writings

Sources of international law are the materials and processes from which the rules and principles governing relations between states and other international actors derive. The classic enumeration is Article 38(1) of the Statute of the International Court of Justice.

Article 38 sources

The Court is directed to apply: (a) international conventions (treaties), whether general or particular; (b) international custom, as evidence of a general practice accepted as law; (c) the general principles of law recognised by civilised nations; and, as subsidiary means for determining the rules, (d) judicial decisions and the teachings of the most highly qualified publicists.

Treaties and custom

Treaties create obligations by consent and are governed by the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties (1969). Customary international law arises from widespread state practice combined with opinio juris — the belief that the practice is legally required. Some norms, such as the prohibitions of genocide and slavery, are peremptory (jus cogens) and admit no derogation.

Modern debate

Scholars debate whether resolutions of international organisations, "soft law", and unilateral acts constitute additional sources, and how the traditional list accommodates the growing role of non-state actors.
