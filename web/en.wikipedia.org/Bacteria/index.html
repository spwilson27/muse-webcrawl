Bacteria (sing. bacterium) are a large domain of prokaryotic microorganisms. Typically a few micrometres in length, bacteria were among the first life forms to appear on Earth, and are present in most of its habitats: soil, water, deep within the Earth's crust, acidic hot springs, radioactive waste, and the bodies of plants and animals. There are approximately 5×1030 bacteria on Earth, forming a biomass exceeding that of all plants and animals.

Structure

Bacterial cells lack a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles. Their DNA lies in a single circular chromosome in the cytoplasm, often accompanied by small plasmid rings. A peptidoglycan cell wall surrounds the cell membrane; the presence or absence of an outer membrane distinguishes Gram-negative from Gram-positive bacteria, the basis of the Gram stain. Many bacteria bear flagella for motility, pili for adhesion and conjugation, and capsules against desiccation and phagocytosis; some form highly resistant endospores.

Metabolism and ecology

Bacteria display almost every known metabolic lifestyle: photosynthesis (cyanobacteria), chemosynthesis, aerobic and anaerobic respiration, and fermentation. They are indispensable to global nutrient cycles — nitrogen fixation, nitrification, decomposition — and to human physiology: the gut microbiota aid digestion and immunity. A minority are pathogens, causing diseases such as tuberculosis, cholera, syphilis and pneumonia; these are treated with antibiotics, against which resistance evolves readily, partly through horizontal gene transfer.

History of study

Bacteria were first observed by Antonie van Leeuwenhoek in 1676 with his single-lens microscope ("animalcules"). Louis Pasteur and Robert Koch established the germ theory of disease in the 19th century, and Carl Woese's molecular phylogeny (1977) recognised Bacteria as one of the three great domains of life, alongside Archaea and Eukarya.
