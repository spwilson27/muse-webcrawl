Simulated annealing searches for the global maximum while sometimes accepting worse states to escape local optima.

Simulated annealing (SA) is a probabilistic technique for approximating the global optimum of a given function. It is a metaheuristic for optimization problems in a large search space, used when approximate solutions are acceptable in reasonable time. The name comes from annealing in metallurgy, in which a material is heated and then slowly cooled to reduce defects and settle into a low-energy crystalline state.

Algorithm

The algorithm explores candidate solutions step by step. At each step, a neighbouring state is proposed; if it is better than the current state, it is accepted. If it is worse, it may still be accepted with a probability that depends on the difference in quality and on a "temperature" parameter, typically following the Boltzmann form exp(−ΔE/T). The temperature starts high — allowing wide exploration and escape from local optima — and is gradually decreased according to a cooling schedule, so the search settles into a good optimum.

History

The method was described by Scott Kirkpatrick, C. Daniel Gelatt, and Mario Vecchi in 1983, drawing on the Metropolis algorithm for simulating the behaviour of atoms at a given temperature, an idea developed independently by Nicholas Metropolis and others, and by Vladimír Černý.

Applications

Simulated annealing has been applied to the travelling salesman problem, circuit placement and routing in VLSI design, scheduling, protein folding models, and many other combinatorial optimization problems.
