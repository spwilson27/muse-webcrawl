Runes are the letters of a set of related alphabets used by Germanic peoples from about the 2nd century AD. The alphabets are known as runic alphabets or futharks, after their first six letters (F, U, Þ, A, R, K). Runes were used to write various Germanic languages before the adoption of the Latin alphabet, and survived longest in Scandinavia, where thousands of runestones — most in Sweden — still stand.

Origins and variants

The earliest runic inscriptions, dating to around 150 AD, appear on weapons, combs, and ornaments across northern Europe. The original 24-letter Elder Futhark developed into the 33-letter Anglo-Saxon Futhorc in England and Frisia, and the reduced 16-letter Younger Futhark in Viking Age Scandinavia. The letter shapes, composed of straight strokes, were well suited to carving in wood, bone, and stone. In Norse mythology, the god Odin was said to have won the runes through self-sacrifice, hanging on the world tree Yggdrasil for nine nights, and runes were also used for charms and memorial formulas.

Use and decline

Runestones typically commemorate the dead, mark inheritance, or boast of deeds such as bridge-building or expeditions. With Christianisation and the spread of Latin literacy, runes gradually gave way to the Latin alphabet, though they persisted in parts of Scandinavia — for example in the Swedish province of Dalarna — into the early 20th century, and were used in calendars and merchants' marks.

Modern interest

Runes were revived in romantic nationalism, occult movements of the 19th and 20th centuries, and modern popular culture, notably in fantasy literature such as J. R. R. Tolkien's works. Runology, the scholarly study of runic inscriptions, remains an active field within Germanic philology.
