Collateral (finance)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In lending agreements, collateral is a borrower's pledge of a specific property to a lender, to secure repayment of a loan. The collateral serves to protect the lender against a borrower's default — that is, any failure to pay the principal and interest under the terms of a loan obligation. If a borrower does default on a loan (due to bankruptcy or other event), that borrower loses the property pledged as collateral, with the lender then becoming its owner.

Concept

Collateral can take many forms: real estate, vehicles, equipment, inventory, receivables, securities, or cash. Loans secured by collateral typically carry lower interest rates than unsecured loans, because the lender's risk is reduced. A loan-to-value (LTV) ratio expresses the size of the loan relative to the value of the collateral; lenders commonly apply a "haircut", valuing the collateral below its market price to allow for price fluctuations.

Secured lending

The legal mechanism that attaches the lender's interest in the collateral is generally called a security interest, lien, mortgage, or pledge, depending on the type of property and jurisdiction. Upon default, the lender may seize and sell the collateral through foreclosure or repossession to satisfy the debt.

Marketable collateral and repo

In financial markets, collateral underpins repurchase agreements (repos), margin lending, and derivatives clearing. High-quality liquid assets such as government bonds are commonly posted as collateral. Rehypothecation — the reuse of posted collateral by the recipient — is permitted under many agreements and increases leverage in the system, a feature scrutinised after the financial crisis of 2007–2008.

Collateral in central banking

Central banks lend to commercial banks against eligible collateral, applying haircuts according to asset quality. The breadth of a central bank's collateral framework influences credit conditions throughout the economy.
