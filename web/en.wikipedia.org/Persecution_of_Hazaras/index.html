Persecution of Hazaras

Hazarajat, the Hazara homeland in the central highlands of Afghanistan.

The persecution of Hazaras refers to the long history of discrimination, massacres, enslavement and displacement suffered by the Hazara people, a predominantly Twelver Shia ethnic group native to the Hazarajat region of central Afghanistan. Hazaras have been targeted both for their religious identity and their distinct ethnic and physical features.

Nineteenth century

The most catastrophic episodes occurred under the Emir Abdur Rahman Khan, whose campaigns of 1891–1893 against the independent Hazara chiefdoms of Hazarajat killed or displaced large proportions of the population — estimates commonly cited hold that more than half the Hazara population perished or was enslaved — and confiscated Hazara lands, many of which were granted to Pashtun Kuchis. Slavery of Hazaras was abolished only in the early twentieth century.

Civil war and Taliban rule

During the Afghan Civil War, Hazaras suffered massacres including the 1993 Afshar operation in Kabul. Under the Taliban regime (1996–2001), mass killings of Hazara civilians were documented in Mazar-i-Sharif in 1998, in Yakawlang and Bamyan, and elsewhere, which Human Rights Watch characterised as ethnic cleansing; the Taliban also enforced blockades that brought Hazarajat to famine.

Recent decades

After 2001 Hazaras achieved greater political representation and educational advancement, but Shia Hazaras have remained targets of sectarian terrorism in both Afghanistan and Pakistan. The Islamic State Khorasan Province and affiliates have repeatedly bombed Hazara schools, mosques, hospitals and demonstrations in Kabul, including the 2016 Dehmazang protest bombing and the 2021 Sayed al-Shuhada school attack. In Quetta, Pakistan, Lashkar-e-Jhangvi and Islamic State affiliates killed thousands of Hazara Shia in attacks from the 2000s onward. Since the Taliban's return to power in August 2021, Hazara communities have faced continued attacks, forced evictions from contested land, and killings in Daykundi, Uruzgan and Kabul, drawing widespread concern from the United Nations and human rights organisations.
