Addison's disease

Synonyms: Primary adrenal insufficiency
Symptoms: Fatigue, weight loss, abdominal pain, darkening of skin, low blood pressure, salt craving
Causes: Autoimmune adrenalitis (most common in developed world), tuberculosis, other adrenal destruction
Treatment: Glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid replacement

Addison's disease, also called primary adrenal insufficiency, is a rare, long-term endocrine disorder in which the adrenal glands do not produce enough steroid hormones, principally cortisol and aldosterone. It is named after Thomas Addison, who described the condition in 1855.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms develop gradually and include fatigue, muscle weakness, weight loss, nausea, abdominal pain, low blood pressure, salt craving, and hyperpigmentation of the skin and mucous membranes. An acute worsening, an adrenal (Addisonian) crisis, can cause shock and is life-threatening.

Causes and diagnosis

In developed countries the most common cause is autoimmune destruction of the adrenal cortex; worldwide, tuberculosis remains a major cause. Diagnosis rests on blood tests showing low cortisol with high ACTH, confirmed by an ACTH stimulation test.

Treatment

Treatment is lifelong replacement with hydrocortisone (or another glucocorticoid) and fludrocortisone, with dose increases during illness and emergency injectable hydrocortisone for crisis.
