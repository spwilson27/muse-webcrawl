The Golden Ass

The Golden Ass (Latin: Asinus aureus), also known as the Metamorphoses of Apuleius, is the only ancient Roman novel in Latin to survive in its entirety. It was written in the late 2nd century AD by Apuleius of Madauros, a Numidian philosopher and rhetorician.

Plot

The narrator, Lucius, travelling in Thessaly, is obsessed with magic. Through a mishap with a witch's ointment he is transformed not into an owl, as intended, but into a donkey. In animal form — unable to obtain the roses that will restore him — he passes through a succession of owners, witnessing and suffering cruelty, crime and absurdity, and overhearing dozens of stories. At last, at Cenchreae, the goddess Isis appears to him in a dream; he eats roses from a priest's garland in her procession, regains human form, and is initiated into her mysteries.

Interpolated tales

The novel contains many inset stories, most famously the tale of Cupid and Psyche, spread across Books 4–6 — an allegory of the soul's trials that has inspired art and literature for centuries. Other episodes, such as the Tale of the Tub and the story of the miller's wife, mix farce with dark violence.

Significance

Apuleius's picaresque, stylistically virtuosic narrative influenced Boccaccio, Cervantes and the development of the European novel. The title "Golden Ass" reflects the work's precious, entertaining quality — golden meaning excellent.
