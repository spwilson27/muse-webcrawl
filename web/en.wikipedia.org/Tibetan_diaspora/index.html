The Tibetan diaspora is the community of Tibetan people living in exile outside Tibet. Its origins lie in the flight of the 14th Dalai Lama and tens of thousands of Tibetans to India in 1959, following the failure of the 1959 Tibetan uprising against Chinese rule. The exile community, centred on Dharamshala in northern India, maintains the Central Tibetan Administration, an administration that seeks autonomy or independence for Tibet.

History

After the Dalai Lama crossed the Himalayas into India in March 1959, some 80,000 Tibetans followed over the ensuing years, travelling on foot over mountain passes into India, Nepal, and Bhutan. The Indian government granted asylum and, with international aid, established agricultural settlements and camps, mainly in Karnataka (such as Bylakuppe and Mundgod), Himachal Pradesh, and Arunachal Pradesh. A second wave of migration followed in the 1980s and 1990s, and since the 1990s many Tibetans have resettled in North America and Europe.

Communities

India hosts the largest exile population (roughly 85,000–100,000), followed by Nepal (about 13,000–20,000) and Bhutan. In the West, significant communities exist in the United States (notably New York City), Canada (Toronto), and Switzerland, which accepted Tibetan refugees from the 1960s. Settlements maintain monasteries, schools, and cultural institutions aimed at preserving Tibetan language and Tibetan Buddhism in exile.

Politics and culture

The exile community elects a parliament and a political leader (Sikyong) under the Central Tibetan Administration in Dharamshala. Since the Dalai Lama devolved his political authority in 2011, the elected leadership has directed the exile administration's policy of seeking genuine autonomy for Tibet within China (the “Middle Way Approach”). Cultural preservation — through institutions such as the Library of Tibetan Works and Archives, the Tibetan Institute of Performing Arts, and Tibetan Children's Villages schools — is a central concern, alongside tensions over assimilation and declining refugee numbers.
