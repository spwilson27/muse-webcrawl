Taft-Hartley Act

The Taft-Hartley Act (officially the Labor Management Relations Act, 1947) is a United States federal statute that amended the National Labor Relations Act of 1935, restricting labor unions and expanding the list of unfair labor practices to include certain union conduct.

Key features

Named for Senator Robert A. Taft of Ohio and Representative Fred A. Hartley Jr. of New Jersey, the law banned closed shops, allowed states to prohibit union shops through right-to-work statutes, barred secondary boycotts and jurisdictional strikes, and gave presidents power to seek 80-day injunctions against strikes deemed a national emergency.

Enactment

The Republican 80th Congress passed the act in 1947 amid postwar strike waves; President Truman's veto was overridden on June 23, 1947. Unions campaigned against it for years, but full repeal efforts repeatedly failed, and it continues to structure American labor relations.
