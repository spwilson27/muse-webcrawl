Model act

A model act, also known as a model law or model legislation, is a draft statute prepared by a body other than a legislature — typically an expert organisation, government agency, or advocacy group — and offered as a template that legislatures may adopt in whole or in part. Model acts promote uniformity of law across jurisdictions while leaving each legislature free to modify the text.

United States

In the United States, the Uniform Law Commission (National Conference of Commissioners on Uniform State Laws) drafts "uniform acts" intended for adoption by all states; influential examples include the Uniform Commercial Code (drafted jointly with the American Law Institute) and the Model Penal Code produced by the American Law Institute. Federal agencies and professional organisations also publish model laws — for example, the NAIC's model insurance regulations — and advocacy groups such as the American Legislative Exchange Council circulate model bills to state legislators.

International examples

Internationally, the United Nations Commission on International Trade Law (UNCITRAL) produces model laws, such as the Model Law on International Commercial Arbitration and the Model Law on Electronic Commerce, which countries adapt into national legislation. Model legislation is particularly common in federal systems and in fields requiring cross-border consistency.

Criticism

Critics note that model bills drafted by private interests may be enacted with little scrutiny, and that uniformity can come at the expense of local democratic deliberation, while supporters emphasise the value of expert drafting and legal consistency.
