The vihuela (Spanish: [biˈwela]) is a 15th- and 16th-century plucked string instrument that flourished in Spain, Portugal and Italy. It had a guitar-like figure-eight body, a flat back, and five to seven double courses of gut strings, and was tuned much like a lute. The vihuela occupied the social and artistic role in Iberian court culture that the lute held elsewhere in Europe.

History

Three types of vihuela are described in period sources: the vihuela de arco (played with a bow), the vihuela de péñola (played with a quill), and the vihuela de mano (played with the fingers). By the early 16th century the de mano type predominated, and a substantial printed repertory appeared between 1536 and 1576.

Repertoire

Seven books of vihuela music survive, by composers including Luis de Milán (El maestro, 1536), Luis de Narváez (Los seys libros del Delphín, 1538), Alonso Mudarra, Enriquez de Valderrábano, Diego Pisador, Miguel de Fuenllana and Esteban Daça. The repertoire includes fantasias, dance music, intabulations of vocal polyphony, and songs for voice and vihuela. Narváez's Diferencias sobre "Guárdame las vacas" is among the earliest published sets of variations.

Decline and revival

The vihuela fell out of use by the end of the 16th century as the five-course guitar gained popularity. Interest revived in the 20th century with the early music movement; notable modern players include Hopkinson Smith and members of ensembles devoted to Renaissance Spanish music.
