The Battle of Goose Green (28–29 May 1982) was the first major land battle of the Falklands War. The 2nd Battalion, Parachute Regiment (2 Para), commanded by Lieutenant Colonel H. Jones, attacked the Argentine garrison holding the settlements of Darwin and Goose Green on East Falkland. Although outnumbered, the British force defeated the defenders after a hard night-and-day fight; Jones was killed leading an assault and was posthumously awarded the Victoria Cross.

Background

After the British landings at San Carlos on 21 May 1982, political and public pressure in Britain built for a visible land victory. The Argentine force at Goose Green — principally the 12th Infantry Regiment under Lieutenant Colonel Ítalo Ángel Piaggi, with air force personnel manning the nearby airfield (Condor Air Base) — threatened the flank of any advance on Stanley and was ordered to be attacked. British intelligence underestimated the garrison's size, which totalled over a thousand personnel of all services.

The battle

2 Para advanced south from San Carlos on 27 May. The attack began in darkness early on 28 May, supported by naval gunfire from HMS Arrow and three light guns. The battalion fought its way down the narrow isthmus against well-dug-in positions, taking Darwin Hill after a costly struggle during which Colonel Jones was killed charging a trench; his successor, Major Chris Keeble, took command. By nightfall the paratroopers had surrounded Goose Green. The following morning, after negotiations conducted through captured islanders and the psychological pressure of continued fire, Piaggi surrendered. Around a thousand Argentine prisoners were taken, and 112 islanders confined in the community hall were freed unharmed.

Aftermath

The victory gave British forces a major morale boost and secured the southern flank of the San Carlos bridgehead, enabling the advance across East Falkland toward Stanley. The battle remains controversial in some accounts because of questions over whether the attack was operationally necessary and criticism of the BBC World Service's premature broadcast of the operation. Goose Green was later used as a helicopter base and prisoner-of-war camp.
