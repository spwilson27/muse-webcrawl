Topos

In mathematics, a topos (plural topoi or toposes) is a category that behaves like the category of sheaves of sets on a topological space, or like the category of sets itself. Topoi provide a unified framework for geometry, logic and set theory: every topos has an internal language in which one can reason intuitionistically, and every topos defines a notion of "universe of sets".

History

Topos theory grew out of Alexander Grothendieck's work in algebraic geometry in the 1960s, when he introduced Grothendieck topologies on categories — generalising the notion of covering from open sets to families of morphisms — in order to define sheaf cohomology for schemes. F. William Lawvere and Myles Tierney subsequently axiomatised the concept of an elementary topos around 1970, isolating the categorical properties (finite limits, exponentials and a subobject classifier) that make set-like reasoning possible.

The subobject classifier

A signature feature of a topos is the subobject classifier, an object Ω that plays the role of the two-element Boolean algebra {true, false} in sets but which in general topoi is a richer Heyting algebra. Truth in a topos is therefore naturally multi-valued and intuitionistic, a fact that links topos theory closely with logic: each topos determines a higher-order intuitionistic type theory, and geometric morphisms between topoi correspond to interpretations between theories.

Applications

Topoi are used in algebraic geometry, mathematical logic, foundations of mathematics, and increasingly in physics and computer science, where they model context-dependent truth and varying sets.
