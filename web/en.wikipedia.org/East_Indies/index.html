East Indies

The East Indies is a historical term for the lands of South and Southeast Asia reached by European traders via the sea route around Africa — in its narrowest and most common use, the islands of the Malay Archipelago, including present-day Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines, Brunei, Timor-Leste and Papua New Guinea. It was contrasted with the "West Indies" (the Caribbean), a distinction born of Columbus's mistaken belief that he had reached Asia.

Usage and scope

In loose usage the East Indies could encompass India and mainland Southeast Asia as well. The term is embedded in the names of the great chartered trading companies: the English East India Company (1600) and the Dutch East India Company (VOC, 1602), as well as French, Danish, Swedish and Austrian counterparts. The Dutch colonial empire in the archipelago was formally the Dutch East Indies, which became Indonesia after the Second World War.

Trade

The region drew European commerce for its spices — nutmeg and mace from the Banda Islands, cloves from the Maluku Islands, pepper from Sumatra and Malabar — as well as textiles, tea, coffee, sugar and later rubber and oil. Trading posts grew into colonial capitals such as Batavia (Jakarta), Malacca, Manila and Singapore.

Modern usage

"East Indies" survives today mainly in historical, biogeographical contexts (for example, the flora of the East Indies) and in names of companies, dishes and institutions, having been replaced in geography by Southeast Asia and Maritime Southeast Asia.
