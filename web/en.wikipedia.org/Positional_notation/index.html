Positional notation

Positional notation (or place-value notation, or positional numeral system) is a method of representing or encoding numbers in which the value of a digit depends on its position within the number. In the familiar decimal system, the numeral 352 means 3×10² + 5×10¹ + 2×10⁰, using a base (or radix) of ten.

Principles

A positional system with base b uses b distinct digit symbols, including zero, and interprets a string of digits dk…d0 as the sum of di × bi. A radix point extends the system to fractions with negative powers of the base. Common bases include decimal (10), binary (2), octal (8), and hexadecimal (16).

History

The Babylonian sexagesimal (base-60) system was positional but initially lacked a true zero. The fully developed decimal place-value system with zero arose in India by the early centuries AD and was transmitted westward through Arabic mathematics — hence "Hindu–Arabic numerals" — reaching Europe through works such as Fibonacci's Liber Abaci (1202). The Maya independently developed a vigesimal positional system with a zero glyph. Positional notation hugely simplifies arithmetic compared with additive systems such as Roman numerals, and makes algorithms for multiplication, division, and fractions straightforward.
