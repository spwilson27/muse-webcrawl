Northeast megalopolis

The Northeast megalopolis, also known as the Northeast Corridor, BosWash, Boston–Washington corridor, or Megalopolis, is the most heavily urbanized agglomeration in the United States, running mainly along the Atlantic coast from the northern suburbs of Boston to the southern suburbs of Washington, D.C. It includes the metropolitan areas of Boston, New York City, Philadelphia, Baltimore, and Washington, D.C., along with many smaller cities such as Providence, Hartford, and Richmond.

Concept

The French geographer Jean Gottmann popularised the concept in his 1961 book Megalopolis: The Urbanized Northeastern Seaboard of the United States. He described the region as a nearly continuous urban and suburban belt, though in practice it also contains substantial rural areas and greenbelts. The region occupies only about 2 percent of the land area of the United States but houses roughly one-sixth of its population and produces a disproportionate share of its economic output, academic institutions, and media.

Transportation

The megalopolis is bound together by Interstate 95 and U.S. Route 1, and by the Northeast Corridor railway, the busiest passenger rail line in the United States, served by Amtrak's Acela and Northeast Regional trains. A chain of major airports — Boston Logan, New York's JFK, LaGuardia, and Newark, Philadelphia, Baltimore/Washington, and Washington Dulles and Reagan National — serves the region.

Significance

The corridor contains the headquarters of many Fortune 500 companies, the federal government in Washington, the financial centre of New York, and a dense concentration of universities and research hospitals. Gottmann's concept has since been applied to other urbanised belts worldwide, from Japan's Tōkaidō corridor to the European "Blue Banana".
