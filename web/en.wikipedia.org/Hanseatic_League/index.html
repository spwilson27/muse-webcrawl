The Hanseatic League (German: Deutsche Hanse) was a commercial and defensive confederation of merchant guilds and market towns in northern Europe. Growing from a few north German towns in the late 12th century, the league between the 13th and 15th centuries came to dominate Baltic maritime trade for three centuries, with established trading posts (Kontore) from London and Bruges in the west to Novgorod in the east.

History

Lübeck, refounded in 1159, became the hub of a network of merchants; the word Hanse originally denoted a convoy or guild of merchants travelling together. In 1356 the league's towns held their first general Diet (Hansetag), marking the league as a confederation of cities. At its height it included some 200 towns directly or indirectly. The league's privileges gave it a near-monopoly on Baltic trade in goods such as grain, timber, furs, herring, amber, wax, cloth and salt.

Trade and power

The Hanse maintained its own armed forces and could impose blockades and embargoes; it defeated Denmark in war in 1370, securing favourable terms in the Treaty of Stralsund. Four great Kontore organised foreign trade: the Peterhof in Novgorod, the Tyske Brygge in Bergen, the Steelyard in London, and the Hanseatic presence in Bruges.

Decline

From the late 15th century the league declined as territorial states consolidated, Dutch and English competitors rose, the herring shoals shifted, and trade routes moved toward the Atlantic. The last Hansetag met in Lübeck in 1669; Lübeck, Hamburg and Bremen retained the honorary title of Hanseatic cities. The term survives in names such as the airline Lufthansa and German city titles ("Hansestadt").
