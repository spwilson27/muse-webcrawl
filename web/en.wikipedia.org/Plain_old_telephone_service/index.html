Plain old telephone service (POTS) is a retronym for the analog voice-grade telephone service that dominated the public switched telephone network (PSTN) for most of the twentieth century. It was the service delivered to homes and small businesses over copper twisted-pair local loops from a central office or remote concentrator, providing a single voice channel of roughly 300–3,400 Hz.

Characteristics

POTS lines are powered from the telephone exchange, typically at −48 volts DC, so a corded telephone continues to work during a local power failure. Signaling is in-band: the exchange detects the off-hook condition by loop current, accepts dial pulses or dual-tone multi-frequency (DTMF) digits, and applies an AC ringing voltage to alert the called party. Voice is carried as an analog signal; long-distance transport was historically frequency-division multiplexed and later digitized, while the last-mile loop remained analog.

Features

Beyond basic local and long-distance calling, POTS supported supplementary services such as call waiting, call forwarding, three-way calling, and Caller ID. The same copper pair also carried dial-up Internet access and, later, DSL broadband, which shared the line through frequency splitting.

Decline and retirement

From the 2000s onward, mobile telephony, cable voice, and Voice over IP displaced POTS for most users. Regulators in several countries have permitted carriers to retire copper networks; the United Kingdom's PSTN switch-off and similar programs elsewhere mark the end of POTS as the default residential service, though it persists in alarms, elevators, fax lines, and rural areas.
