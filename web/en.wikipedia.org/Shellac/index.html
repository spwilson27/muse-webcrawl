Shellac

Shellac is a resin secreted by the female lac bug on trees in the forests of India and Thailand. It is processed and sold as dry flakes and dissolved in alcohol to make liquid shellac, which is used as a brush-on colorant, food glaze and wood finish. The term "shellac" comes from "shell" and "lac", the latter being a unit of one hundred thousand in Indian numbering, reflecting the huge numbers of insects involved in its production.

Production

Female lac bugs (Kerria lacca) colonize branches of host trees such as kusum, ber and palas, secreting a resinous encrustation. The encrusted twigs are harvested, crushed and sieved to make sticklac, which is then washed, heated and stretched into thin sheets that are broken into flakes. Refined shellac may be bleached to produce lighter grades.

Uses

Shellac has been used since ancient times as a dye and, from the 19th century, as the classic French polish finish for fine furniture. It was the material of early phonograph records. In food and pharmaceuticals it is used as a confectioner's glaze (additive E904) and to coat pills and candies. It is also used as an electrical insulator and in adhesives, inks and cosmetics.

Properties

Shellac is soluble in alkaline solutions and in alcohol, forming tough, glossy films, but it is not waterproof and can blush white with heat or moisture. Compared with synthetic finishes it is considered non-toxic once the solvent has evaporated, and it remains popular in restoration because repairs blend easily into existing coats.
