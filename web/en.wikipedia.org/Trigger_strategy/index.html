Trigger strategy

In game theory, a trigger strategy is a strategy employed in a repeated non-cooperative game. A player using a trigger strategy initially cooperates but punishes the opponent if a certain level of defection (i.e., the trigger condition) is observed. The level of punishment and the sensitivity of the trigger vary with the strategy. Trigger strategies are central to results such as the folk theorem, which shows that cooperation can be sustained as an equilibrium in indefinitely repeated games such as the repeated prisoner's dilemma, provided players are sufficiently patient.

Examples

In the grim trigger strategy, a player cooperates until the opponent defects once, and thereafter defects forever — the harshest possible punishment. In tit for tat, introduced by Anatol Rapoport and the winner of Robert Axelrod's famous tournaments, a player cooperates on the first move and then copies the opponent's previous move, punishing a defection for exactly one round. Variants such as "tit for two tats" forgive an isolated defection and punish only two consecutive defections.

Applications

Trigger strategies model how firms sustain tacit collusion in oligopolies (threatening price wars if a rival undercuts), how trading partners enforce agreements without legal contracts, and arms-control arrangements based on retaliation threats.
