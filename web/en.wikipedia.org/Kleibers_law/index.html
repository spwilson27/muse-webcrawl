Kleibers law

Basal metabolic rate plotted against body mass follows a power law with exponent near 3/4.

Kleiber's law, named after the Swiss-American biologist Max Kleiber, states that for most animals the basal metabolic rate scales to the 3/4 power of the animal's body mass: q ∝ M3/4. Kleiber presented the relationship in 1932 from measurements across species ranging from rats to cattle, and published it in full in his 1961 book The Fire of Life.

Formulation

If an animal's mass is quadrupled, its metabolic rate rises by a factor of about 2.83 rather than 4, so large animals use less energy per unit of body mass than small ones. A common formulation gives metabolic rate in watts as approximately 3.4 × M0.75 with mass in kilograms. The regularity holds remarkably well across more than twenty orders of magnitude, from unicellular organisms to the largest mammals, though the precise exponent remains debated and may vary between groups and conditions.

Explanations and debate

The earlier "surface law" of Max Rubner predicted an exponent of 2/3, based on heat loss through body surface. In 1997 Geoffrey West, James Brown and Brian Enquist proposed a model deriving the 3/4 exponent from the fractal-like branching of distribution networks such as the circulatory system, sparking a large literature of support and criticism. Kleiber's law underlies allometric scaling used throughout physiology, ecology, pharmacology and the study of life-history traits.
