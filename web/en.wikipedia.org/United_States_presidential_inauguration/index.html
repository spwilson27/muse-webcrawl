United States presidential inauguration

The inauguration of the president of the United States is a ceremony marking the commencement of a new four-year term of the president. The day a presidential inauguration occurs is known as Inauguration Day. The only constitutionally required element is the presidential oath of office, prescribed by Article II of the Constitution, which the president-elect takes before assuming executive power.

Inauguration ceremonies are held on the west front of the United States Capitol.

The ceremony

Modern inaugurations center on the swearing-in at noon, followed by the inaugural address, a luncheon in the Capitol hosted by Congress, and a parade down Pennsylvania Avenue to the White House. The outgoing president customarily attends and departs after the ceremony. Inaugural balls and prayer services surround the official events. George Washington delivered the first inaugural address in New York City in 1789; the shortest was his second address, at 135 words, and the longest was William Henry Harrison's in 1841.

Date and succession

The Twentieth Amendment, ratified in 1933, moved the start of the presidential term from March 4 to January 20. When January 20 falls on a Sunday, the oath is often taken privately that day and repeated publicly the following Monday. When a president dies or resigns mid-term, the successor takes the oath in whatever setting is available — Lyndon B. Johnson was sworn in aboard Air Force One in Dallas in 1963 — without the full ceremonial program.
