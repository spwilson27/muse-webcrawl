Double-slit experiment

The double-slit experiment, sometimes called Young's experiment, is a demonstration that light and matter can satisfy both definitions of a wave and of a particle. When coherent light passes through two closely spaced slits, it produces an interference pattern of bright and dark bands on a screen, a result explicable only if the light behaves as a wave. Yet when the experiment is performed one photon at a time, individual photons still land as discrete points, gradually building up the same interference pattern — behaviour at the heart of wave–particle duality and quantum mechanics.

History

Thomas Young first performed a version of the experiment in 1801 and presented his results to the Royal Society in 1803, providing strong evidence for the wave theory of light against the corpuscular theory favoured since Newton. Augustin-Jean Fresnel later put the wave theory on a firm mathematical footing.

In 1961 Claus Jönsson performed the experiment with electrons, and in 1974 Pier Giorgio Merli and collaborators demonstrated interference with single electrons. A 2002 poll of readers of Physics World voted the single-electron double-slit experiment "the most beautiful experiment" in physics.

Overview

In the basic version, a light source such as a laser illuminates a plate pierced by two parallel slits, and the light passing through them is observed on a screen behind the plate. The wave nature of light causes the waves passing through the two slits to interfere, producing bright and dark bands: where peaks align, the light is bright (constructive interference); where a peak meets a trough, it cancels out (destructive interference).

If light consisted strictly of classical particles, each would pass through either one slit or the other and the pattern would simply be the sum of the two single-slit patterns. Instead, the observed pattern differs, demonstrating that each photon interferes with itself.

Variations and interpretation

Richard Feynman called the double-slit experiment a phenomenon "impossible, absolutely impossible, to explain in any classical way" and said it contains "the only mystery" of quantum mechanics. Variants include the delayed-choice experiment proposed by John Archibald Wheeler and quantum eraser experiments, in which information about which slit a particle passed through can be created or erased after the fact, correspondingly destroying or restoring the interference pattern.

When detectors are placed at the slits to observe which path each photon takes, the interference pattern disappears — an outcome closely connected to the uncertainty principle and to the principle of complementarity articulated by Niels Bohr.
