Seed funding

Seed funding, or seed capital, is the earliest official equity funding stage for a new company. It finances the first steps of a business — product development, market research and building an initial team — typically before the company has revenue or a proven business model. The name derives from the metaphor of planting a seed from which the company may grow.

Sources

Seed money comes from founders themselves, friends and family, angel investors, seed-stage venture capital funds, startup accelerators, and increasingly from equity crowdfunding platforms. Government grants and competitions may also contribute. Amounts vary widely: informal friends-and-family rounds may be tens of thousands of dollars, while institutional seed rounds in major technology hubs commonly reach several million.

Instruments

Because valuing a very young company is difficult, seed investment is often made through convertible instruments that delay pricing until a later round. Convertible notes are debt that converts to equity at a discount or valuation cap. The SAFE (Simple Agreement for Future Equity), introduced by Y Combinator in 2013, dispenses with debt features such as interest and maturity. Priced seed rounds, in which shares are sold at an agreed valuation, have also become more common as seed rounds have grown.

Place in the funding sequence

Seed funding follows pre-seed capital and precedes Series A venture funding, at which investors typically expect evidence of product–market fit and a repeatable growth model. The period a seed round finances — often twelve to eighteen months of "runway" — is meant to let the company reach milestones that justify the next, larger round. Most seed-funded companies do not reach that stage, making seed investment the riskiest part of the venture market.
