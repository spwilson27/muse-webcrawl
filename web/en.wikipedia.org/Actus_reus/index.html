Actus reus

Actus reus (/ˈæktəs ˈreɪəs/), sometimes called the external element or the objective element of a crime, is the Latin term for the "guilty act" which, when proved beyond a reasonable doubt in combination with the mens rea, the "guilty mind", produces criminal liability in common law–based criminal law jurisdictions such as England and Wales, Canada, Australia, India and the United States.

Elements

The actus reus of an offence generally consists of three components: a voluntary act (or, in limited circumstances, an omission where a legal duty to act exists), attendant circumstances, and consequences specified in the definition of the offence. A defendant whose bodily movement is involuntary — for example, a reflex action, a spasm, or an act performed while unconscious — does not perform a voluntary act and so lacks actus reus.

Omissions

As a general rule there is no liability for a mere omission, but exceptions arise where a duty of care exists: duties imposed by statute, by contract, by a special relationship such as parent and child, by the voluntary assumption of care for another, or where the defendant created a dangerous situation and then failed to act, as in R v Miller [1983].

Causation

Where an offence requires a particular result, the prosecution must prove that the defendant's conduct caused that result. Factual causation is tested by the "but for" test, while legal causation asks whether the defendant's act was an operating and substantial cause of the result, subject to intervening acts (novus actus interveniens) that may break the chain of causation.

Coincidence with mens rea

The actus reus and mens rea must coincide in time. Courts have overcome apparent gaps through doctrines such as the "continuing act" (Fagan v Metropolitan Police Commissioner) and the "single transaction" principle (Thabo Meli v R).
