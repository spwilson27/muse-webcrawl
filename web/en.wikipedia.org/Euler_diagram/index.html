Euler diagram

An Euler diagram is a diagrammatic means of representing sets and their relationships, typically using overlapping closed curves (often circles or ellipses) inside a rectangle representing the universal set. Unlike a Venn diagram, an Euler diagram shows only the intersections that actually exist: disjoint sets are drawn apart and subsets are drawn nested.

History

The diagrams are named for Leonhard Euler, who popularised such circle diagrams in his Letters to a German Princess (1768), though earlier antecedents exist. John Venn extended the idea in 1880 by requiring all possible intersections to be shown, producing the stricter Venn diagram.

Usage

Euler diagrams are used in logic teaching to test syllogisms, in computer science for visualising class hierarchies and database groupings, and in information design where omitting empty regions reduces clutter. For example, a diagram of animals might show 'birds' entirely inside 'animals with wings' overlapping 'flying animals', while a Venn of the same sets would require every combination region to appear.

Formalisation

Research on diagrammatic reasoning defines well-formedness conditions (such as no disconnected zones) and algorithms for automatically drawing Euler diagrams from set data. See also Venn diagram, Set theory and Leonhard Euler.
