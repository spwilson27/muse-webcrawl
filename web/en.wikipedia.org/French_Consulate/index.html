The Consulate (French: Consulat) was the top-level government of France from the fall of the Directory in the coup of 18 Brumaire (9 November 1799) until the proclamation of the First French Empire on 18 May 1804. Formally a triumvirate of three consuls, it was dominated from the start by its First Consul, Napoleon Bonaparte.

Establishment

The coup replaced the Directory with three provisional consuls — Bonaparte, Emmanuel Joseph Sieyès, and Roger Ducos — and the Constitution of the Year VIII then installed Bonaparte as First Consul alongside Cambacérès and Lebrun, whose roles were largely advisory. In 1802, following the Peace of Amiens, Napoleon was made Consul for life by plebiscite.

Reforms

The Consulate carried out some of the most durable state-building of the Revolutionary era: the Napoleonic Code (Civil Code of 1804), the Bank of France (1800), the prefectural system of centralized administration, the lycées, the Légion d'honneur (1802), and the Concordat of 1801 with the Papacy, which reconciled the state with the Catholic Church. Amnesties encouraged émigrés to return, and public finances were stabilized.

Wars and end

Victories at Marengo (1800) and the Treaties of Lunéville (1801) and Amiens (1802) briefly pacified Europe, though war with Britain resumed in 1803. After the discovery of royalist plots and the execution of the duc d'Enghien, the regime moved to a hereditary empire: the Senate proclaimed Napoleon Emperor on 18 May 1804, ending the Consulate.
