Omega-6 fatty acid

Omega-6 fatty acids (also written ω-6 or n-6 fatty acids) are a family of polyunsaturated fatty acids whose first double bond, counted from the methyl (omega) end of the carbon chain, lies at the sixth carbon. The principal dietary member is linoleic acid (18:2 n-6), an essential fatty acid abundant in seed oils such as soybean, corn, sunflower and safflower oil.

Biochemistry

Humans cannot synthesise linoleic acid and must obtain it from food. In the body it is desaturated and elongated to arachidonic acid (20:4 n-6), a precursor of eicosanoids — prostaglandins, thromboxanes and leukotrienes — which mediate inflammation, blood clotting, vascular tone and many other signalling processes. Gamma-linolenic acid (GLA), found in evening primrose and borage oils, is an intermediate in the same pathway.

Health

Linoleic acid is essential for skin barrier function and growth; deficiency is rare but causes scaly dermatitis and poor wound healing. Because omega-6-derived eicosanoids include both inflammatory and resolving mediators, the once-popular claim that dietary omega-6 is simply "pro-inflammatory" is now regarded as oversimplified. Nutrition authorities generally advise replacing saturated fats with polyunsaturated fats, including omega-6 oils, to lower LDL cholesterol and cardiovascular risk, while also eating sources of omega-3 fatty acids. Debate continues over the ideal ratio of omega-6 to omega-3 in modern diets, which has risen with the spread of refined seed oils.
