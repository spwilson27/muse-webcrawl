The Danevirke (Danish: Danevirke; German: Danewerk) is a system of Danish fortifications in present-day Schleswig-Holstein, northern Germany. Consisting of earthen ramparts, ditches, and associated works stretching across the base of the Jutland Peninsula between the Schlei inlet and the marshes of the Eider and Treene rivers, it protected Denmark's southern frontier and the trading town of Hedeby for centuries.

History

Construction began in the early medieval period, around 650 AD, and the works were expanded in several phases — notably under King Gudfred around 808, during the Viking Age around 968, and by Valdemar the Great in the 12th century with a brick-faced wall. The Danevirke was intended to defend against Saxons, Franks, Slavs, and later German expansion from the south.

The fortifications saw military use as late as the Second Schleswig War (1864), when the Danish army withdrew from the position during a severe winter. In 2018 the Danevirke, together with the neighbouring Viking trading centre of Hedeby, was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
