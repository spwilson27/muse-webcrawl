Friedrich Ratzel (30 August 1844 – 9 August 1904) was a German geographer and ethnographer, founder of anthropogeography — the study of the influence of the physical environment on human societies. His two-volume Anthropogeographie (1882–1891) and his Politische Geographie (1897) laid foundations for human and political geography, and his essay on Lebensraum ("living space", 1901) introduced a term later taken up, in distorted form, by Nazi ideology.

Life

Ratzel studied natural sciences at Heidelberg, Jena and Berlin, and travelled widely as a journalist, reporting from North America, Mexico and Cuba in the 1870s — journeys that fed his multi-volume Völkerkunde (The History of Mankind). He became professor of geography at Leipzig in 1886 and remained there until his death.

Ideas

Influenced by Darwin and by his teacher Moritz Wagner, Ratzel emphasised the migration and diffusion of cultural traits and treated the state as an organism bound to its soil (Boden), requiring space to grow. His ideas were transmitted to Anglophone geography by his student Ellen Churchill Semple and were reshaped by Karl Haushofer's school of Geopolitik. Modern geographers read Ratzel more critically, distinguishing his diffusionist ethnography from the expansionist uses made of his "living space" concept after his death.
