Waveguide

TypesMetallic, dielectric, optical
Typical useMicrowave and optical signals
First analysed1890s–1930s (Rayleigh; Southworth; Carson)

A waveguide is a structure that guides waves by restricting their transmission to one direction, with minimal loss of energy. Examples of waves guided in this way include electromagnetic waves (microwaves and light) and sound waves. Without the physical constraint of a waveguide, wave amplitudes typically fall off according to the inverse-square law as they expand into three-dimensional space.

Principles

Metallic waveguides are usually hollow rectangular or circular tubes of conductive material. Waves propagate inside by successive reflections from the walls, and only certain patterns — called modes — can propagate at a given frequency. Each mode has a cut-off frequency below which it cannot propagate, determined by the waveguide's cross-section; this makes a waveguide behave as a high-pass filter. Dielectric waveguides, such as optical fibres, guide light by total internal reflection at the boundary between materials of differing refractive index.

History

The theory of waves in hollow tubes was analysed by Lord Rayleigh in 1897, but practical development came in the 1930s at Bell Telephone Laboratories under George C. Southworth and at MIT, driven by the needs of radar. Waveguides became standard plumbing for microwave systems, where they carry power with lower loss than coaxial cable at high frequencies.

Applications

Waveguides feed radar antennas, link satellite communication equipment, and form the magnetron feed in microwave ovens. Optical waveguides — above all the optical fibre — carry the world's telecommunications traffic. Acoustic waveguides appear in musical instruments, hearing tubes and architectural acoustics.
