Food vs. fuel

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Food versus fuel is the dilemma regarding the risk of diverting farmland or crops for biofuels production to the detriment of the food supply. The debate concerns the balance between producing fuel from biomass — such as corn ethanol, sugarcane ethanol, and biodiesel from vegetable oils — and maintaining adequate food production for a growing world population.

Background

Biofuel mandates and subsidies, particularly in the United States (the Renewable Fuel Standard) and the European Union, expanded sharply in the 2000s. In the United States, roughly a third or more of the corn crop has been used for ethanol production, though much of it returns to the feed supply as distillers grains. In Brazil, sugarcane ethanol is a major transport fuel; in Europe and Southeast Asia, biodiesel is made from rapeseed and palm oil.

The debate

Critics argue that biofuel demand contributed to the food price rises of 2007–2008 and subsequent spikes, because land, water, and crops devoted to fuel are unavailable for food, and because higher crop prices raise costs for livestock feed and staple foods. They also argue that clearing land for biofuel crops can release stored carbon and harm biodiversity, offsetting climate benefits. Defenders respond that food prices are driven mainly by oil prices, weather, trade policies, and speculation; that by-products feed livestock; and that advanced ("second-generation") biofuels made from cellulose, waste, and algae avoid direct competition with food.

Policy responses

Policy responses have included caps on food-based biofuels (the European Union limits their share of renewable transport energy), sustainability criteria, incentives for advanced biofuels, and periodic reviews of mandates in light of food price volatility. The issue remains central to debates over land use, energy security, and climate policy.
