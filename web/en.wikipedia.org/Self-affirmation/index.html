Self-affirmation

Self-affirmation theory is a psychological theory developed by Claude Steele in 1988, holding that people are motivated to maintain a perception of themselves as adequate, moral and capable — a sense of self-integrity. When one domain of the self is threatened, people can restore self-integrity by affirming their values or strengths in another, unrelated domain.

Mechanism

According to the theory, affirming an important value (for example, by writing about a time when a core value mattered) broadens a person's perspective and reduces defensiveness when they face threatening information, such as health warnings or evidence that a group they belong to performs poorly. The affirmation does not remove the threat; instead it reminds the person that their overall self-worth does not depend on the threatened domain.

Applications and research

Values-affirmation exercises have been tested in education, where brief writing interventions improved the grades of students subject to negative stereotypes, and in health communication, where affirmed participants were more open to risk information and more likely to change behaviour. Meta-analyses find small but reliable effects, although replication and boundary conditions remain active topics of research.
