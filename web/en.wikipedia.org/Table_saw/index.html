Table saw

A table saw (also known as a sawbench or bench saw in England) is a woodworking tool consisting of a circular saw blade, mounted on an arbor, that is driven by an electric motor (either directly, by belt, or by gears). The blade protrudes through the surface of a table, which provides support for the material, usually wood, being cut. Table saws are a staple of most workshops and are used primarily for making long straight cuts, known as rip cuts, and crosscuts with the aid of a mitre gauge.

Types

Table saws range from small benchtop models, designed to sit on a workbench and be transported between job sites, through contractor saws mounted on legs or stands, to heavy cabinet saws with enclosed bases and powerful motors used in professional shops. Hybrid saws combine features of contractor and cabinet designs.

Components and use

The blade's height and tilt angle are adjustable; most blades tilt for bevel cuts between 0 and 45 degrees. A rip fence guides the workpiece for cuts parallel to the blade, while a mitre gauge slides in slots in the table to guide crosscuts. Modern saws include a riving knife or splitter behind the blade to prevent the workpiece from binding and kicking back, as well as a blade guard and dust extraction port.

Safety

Table saws are responsible for tens of thousands of injuries each year in the United States, including amputations caused by contact with the spinning blade and injuries from kickback. Safety practices include using push sticks, keeping the guard in place and avoiding freehand cuts. Flesh-sensing technology, marketed as SawStop, detects electrical contact with skin and stops the blade within milliseconds, dramatically reducing injury severity.

History

Circular saw blades mounted in tables were used in shipyards in the late 18th century, and the table saw developed alongside steam and then electric power. Portable models emerged in the 20th century as electric motors became compact, making the tool a centrepiece of both professional and home workshops.
