Test Act

The Test Acts were a series of English penal laws that served as a religious test for public office and imposed various civil disabilities on Roman Catholics and nonconformist Protestants. The underlying principle was that only people taking communion in the established Church of England were eligible for public employment, and the severe penalties pronounced against recusants — whether Catholic or nonconformist — were intended to promote public safety.

The Acts of 1673 and 1678

The Test Act 1673 required all holders of civil or military office under the Crown to take the oaths of supremacy and allegiance, to receive the sacrament of the Lord's Supper according to the usage of the Church of England within three months of taking office, and to make a declaration against transubstantiation. The requirement famously forced the king's brother and heir, James, Duke of York, to resign as Lord High Admiral when he refused. A second act in 1678 extended a declaration against Catholic doctrine to members of both Houses of Parliament; it kept the Duke of York's exclusion from Parliament as well, and a clause in it eventually barred Catholics from the Commons until 1829.

Effects and repeal

The Acts excluded Catholics and, in practice, Protestant dissenters from office, although annual Indemnity Acts from 1727 shielded nonconformists who accepted office without qualifying. The sacramental requirement was repealed by the Sacramental Test Act 1828, and Catholic emancipation followed with the Roman Catholic Relief Act 1829. Remaining university tests were abolished by the Universities Tests Act 1871.
