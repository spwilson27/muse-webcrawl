Constitutional republic

A constitutional republic is a state in which the head of state and other officials are representatives of the people and must govern according to an existing constitution. In a constitutional republic, executive, legislative, and judicial powers are separated into distinct branches, and no branch may exercise the powers properly belonging to another.

Meaning and usage

The phrase is especially common in American political discourse. The United States, for example, is a republic — sovereignty rests with the people and their elected representatives rather than a monarch — and a constitutional one, because the Constitution limits the government's powers and protects individual rights. Similar descriptions apply to other states combining republican government with constitutional limits.

Distinction from direct democracy

A constitutional republic is a form of representative democracy but differs from direct democracy, in which citizens vote on laws themselves. Constitutional limits may also restrain majority rule itself — for instance, enumerated rights cannot normally be removed by a simple legislative majority.

History

The idea has roots in classical models such as the Roman Republic and in Enlightenment thinkers including Montesquieu, whose theory of the separation of powers deeply influenced the framers of the United States Constitution.
