National Wildlife Refuge

National Wildlife Refuge
Designation: United States
Administered by: U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service
First refuge: Pelican Island (1903)

A National Wildlife Refuge is a designation for certain protected areas of the United States managed by the United States Fish and Wildlife Service. Together these lands and waters form the National Wildlife Refuge System, the world's largest network of lands dedicated to wildlife conservation. The system comprises more than 560 national wildlife refuges and dozens of wetland management districts, encompassing roughly 95 million acres, plus several marine national monuments covering hundreds of millions of acres of ocean.

History

The first refuge, Pelican Island National Wildlife Refuge in Florida, was established by executive order of President Theodore Roosevelt on March 14, 1903, to protect egrets and other shorebirds from plume hunters. The National Wildlife Refuge System Improvement Act of 1997 established the mission of the system and identified six priority public uses: hunting, fishing, wildlife observation, photography, environmental education, and interpretation, to be permitted when compatible with conservation purposes.

Management

Refuges are managed to conserve native fish, wildlife, plants, and their habitats. Unlike many other federal lands, refuges are managed primarily for wildlife rather than multiple use; activities such as grazing, farming, or energy development may occur only where compatible with refuge purposes. The system protects habitat for endangered species, migratory birds, and other wildlife across all 50 states, and hosts tens of millions of visitors annually.

Notable refuges

Well-known refuges include the Arctic National Wildlife Refuge in Alaska (the largest in the system), the Okefenokee National Wildlife Refuge in Georgia and Florida, the Wichita Mountains Wildlife Refuge in Oklahoma, and the J. N. "Ding" Darling National Wildlife Refuge in Florida.
