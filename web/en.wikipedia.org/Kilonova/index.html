A kilonova (also called a macronova) is a transient astronomical event that occurs when two neutron stars, or a neutron star and a black hole, merge. Its light is powered mainly by the radioactive decay of heavy r-process nuclei synthesised in the neutron-rich material ejected during the merger.

Physics

During the merger, a few percent of a solar mass of extremely neutron-rich matter is flung out. Free neutrons are captured rapidly onto seed nuclei (the r-process), building elements heavier than iron — including gold, platinum, and the lanthanides and actinides — whose radioactive decay heats the expanding ejecta and makes it glow. Because lanthanides have enormous optical opacity, much of the emission emerges in the red and infrared, and kilonovae evolve from blue to red over days as different ejecta components dominate.

Observations

The term was introduced by Metzger and collaborators in 2010. A candidate event followed the short gamma-ray burst GRB 130603B in 2013. The first confirmed kilonova, AT 2017gfo, was observed in the galaxy NGC 4993 in August 2017 as the optical counterpart of the gravitational-wave event GW170817 — the first detection of gravitational waves from a neutron star merger — accompanied by the short burst GRB 170817A. Its spectra directly confirmed the production of heavy elements, establishing neutron star mergers as a major cosmic source of the heaviest elements in the universe.

Significance

Kilonovae links gravitational-wave astronomy, gamma-ray bursts, and the origin of the elements, and their brightness can be used as standard sirens to measure the expansion rate of the universe.
