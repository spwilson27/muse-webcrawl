Apuleius (c. 124 – c. 170 AD) was a Latin-language prose writer, Platonist philosopher and rhetorician from Numidia in Roman North Africa. He is best known for the Metamorphoses, commonly called The Golden Ass, the only Latin novel to survive complete from antiquity, in which the narrator Lucius is transformed into a donkey and undergoes a series of adventures before being restored by the goddess Isis.

Life

Apuleius was born at Madauros, a Roman colony in Numidia (modern M'Daourouch, Algeria), to a prosperous family; his father was a provincial magistrate. He studied at Carthage and later in Athens, where he immersed himself in Platonic philosophy, and travelled widely in the eastern Mediterranean. On a journey to Alexandria he fell ill at Oea (modern Tripoli), married a wealthy widow, Pudentilla, and was prosecuted by her relatives on a charge of winning her by magic. His defence speech, the Apologia, survives and is a major source for his life.

Works

The Metamorphoses in eleven books combines a picaresque main narrative with numerous inset tales, the most famous being the story of Cupid and Psyche. Its final book describes Lucius's initiation into the mysteries of Isis and Osiris, which many readers connect with Apuleius's own religious interests. His other surviving works include the Florida, a collection of rhetorical excerpts, and philosophical treatises transmitted under his name, including De Platone et eius dogmate and De deo Socratis.

Legacy

Apuleius was celebrated in his lifetime as a philosopher and public speaker, with statues erected in his honour at Carthage and elsewhere. Saint Augustine discussed him at length in The City of God. The Golden Ass influenced later European fiction, from Boccaccio to the picaresque tradition, and remains the most complete window into popular narrative and religious life in the Roman Empire of the second century.
