Louis Néel

Louis Eugène Félix Néel (22 November 1904 – 17 November 2000) was a French physicist who received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1970, jointly with Hannes Alfvén, for his fundamental work on the magnetic properties of solids. His theories of antiferromagnetism and ferrimagnetism underpin modern magnetic materials and data-storage technology.

Work

Néel proposed that in certain materials, neighbouring atomic magnetic moments align antiparallel on two sublattices. When the sublattice moments cancel exactly, the result is antiferromagnetism; when they cancel only partially, it is ferrimagnetism — the behaviour of ferrites used in electronics. The characteristic temperatures at which such ordering disappears (the Néel temperature) and domain-wall structures (Néel walls) bear his name. He also explained the magnetism of rocks, contributing to paleomagnetism and the evidence for continental drift and seafloor spreading.

Career

Based in Grenoble from 1940, Néel built that city into a major centre of magnetism research, founding laboratories that later became part of the CNRS Grenoble institutes and advocating for the Institut Laue–Langevin. During the Second World War he developed methods to protect French naval vessels from magnetic mines.
