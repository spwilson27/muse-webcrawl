Analgesic

An analgesic drug, also simply called a painkiller, is any member of the diverse group of drugs used to achieve relief from pain (that is, analgesia). Analgesic drugs act in various ways on the peripheral and central nervous systems; they differ fundamentally from anesthetics, which temporarily affect, and in some cases eliminate, sensation entirely.

Classes

  • Paracetamol (acetaminophen) — a widely used analgesic and antipyretic with weak anti-inflammatory activity.
  • NSAIDs — such as ibuprofen, aspirin, and naproxen; they inhibit cyclooxygenase enzymes and prostaglandin synthesis, giving analgesic, antipyretic, and anti-inflammatory effects. Side effects include gastrointestinal irritation and kidney injury.
  • Opioids — such as morphine, codeine, oxycodone, and fentanyl; they act on opioid receptors and are used for moderate to severe pain, with risks of sedation, respiratory depression, tolerance, dependence, and addiction.
  • Adjuvants — antidepressants (such as amitriptyline and duloxetine), anticonvulsants (gabapentin, pregabalin), and others used especially for neuropathic pain.

Use

The World Health Organization's analgesic ladder originally proposed stepwise escalation from non-opioids to weak then strong opioids according to pain severity, a framework long used in cancer pain. Choice of agent depends on the type and severity of pain, patient factors, and side-effect profiles; combination and multimodal regimens are common.
