Ancient Greece was a civilisation belonging to a period of Greek history that lasted from the Greek Dark Ages of the 12th–9th centuries BC to late antiquity (c. AD 600). At its centre lay a loose collection of culturally and linguistically related city-states (poleis) and territories around the Aegean Sea and across the Mediterranean, politically unified only under the kingdom of Macedon in the 4th century BC and later absorbed into the Roman world. Despite this political fragmentation, the Greeks shared a language, religion and cultural identity — expressed in the epics of Homer, common sanctuaries such as Olympia and Delphi, and a sense of distinction from "barbarians".

Periods

Greek history is conventionally divided into the Archaic period (c. 800–480 BC), which saw the rise of the polis, colonisation around the Mediterranean and Black Sea, the invention of the alphabet from Phoenician script, coinage, and the first Olympic Games (traditionally 776 BC); the Classical period (480–323 BC), beginning with the Greek victories over Persia at Salamis and Plataea and marked by Athenian democracy, the Peloponnesian War between Athens and Sparta, and the flowering of drama, history, philosophy and classical art; and the Hellenistic period (323 BC–31 BC), from the death of Alexander the Great to the Roman conquest, when Greek culture spread across Egypt and the Near East under the Ptolemaic, Seleucid and Antigonid kingdoms. Roman rule followed, though Greek language and culture continued to dominate the eastern Mediterranean.

Politics and society

The polis combined a city with its surrounding countryside and was governed under constitutions ranging from Athenian direct democracy — in which male citizens voted in the assembly and served by lot on juries and councils — to Spartan oligarchy with its dual kingship and militarised social system, and tyrannies in many states. Society rested heavily on slavery and excluded women from political life. Greek religion was polytheistic, centred on the Olympian gods headed by Zeus, and was practised through sacrifice, festivals and oracles rather than scripture; see Ancient Greek religion.

Culture and legacy

Ancient Greece is often described as the cradle of Western civilisation. Its achievements include the philosophy of Socrates, Plato and Aristotle; the histories of Herodotus and Thucydides; tragedy and comedy by Aeschylus, Sophocles, Euripides and Aristophanes; the mathematics of Euclid and Archimedes; the medicine of Hippocrates; and the orders of classical architecture embodied in the Parthenon. Transmitted through Rome, the Islamic world and the Renaissance, Greek thought has shaped modern science, politics, literature and art.
