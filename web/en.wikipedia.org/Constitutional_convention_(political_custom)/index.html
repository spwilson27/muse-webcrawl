Constitutional convention (political custom)

A constitutional convention is an informal and uncodified tradition that is followed by the institutions of a state. In some states, notably those with uncodified constitutions such as the United Kingdom, Canada, and New Zealand, conventions form an important part of the constitutional framework. They are distinct from laws: conventions are not enforced by courts, but are regarded as binding because political actors accept an obligation to follow them, and breach may provoke political consequences or constitutional crisis.

Nature and identification

Constitutional theorists such as A. V. Dicey distinguished conventions — the "conventions of the constitution" — from laws enforced by courts. Sir Ivor Jennings proposed a three-part test for identifying a convention: there must be precedents; the actors in those precedents must have believed they were bound by a rule; and there must be a constitutional reason for the rule.

Examples

  • In the United Kingdom, the monarch is conventionally bound to give royal assent to bills passed by Parliament (last refused in 1708), to appoint as Prime Minister the person able to command the confidence of the House of Commons, and to act on ministerial advice.
  • Caretaker conventions govern the behaviour of governments during election periods in Westminster systems.
  • In the United States, conventions include the two-term presidential tradition followed until Franklin D. Roosevelt (later codified by the Twenty-second Amendment) and aspects of Senate practice such as senatorial courtesy.
  • In Canada, the Patriation Reference (1981) famously held that a convention required substantial provincial consent for constitutional amendment, even though the law alone did not.

Breach and enforcement

Because conventions are not law, courts may recognise their existence but will not enforce them. Enforcement is political: criticism, loss of legitimacy, retaliation in kind, or, in some cases, the codification of the convention into statute or written rules, as with the UK's Cabinet Manual (2011) recording many conventions.
