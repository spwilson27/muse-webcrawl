Sol Invictus

Sol Invictus ("Unconquered Sun") was a religious epithet applied to several solar deities in the later Roman Empire, and the name of the official sun god of the late Roman state. The cult of Sol Invictus was promoted to prominence by the emperor Aurelian, who, after his victory over Zenobia of Palmyra, raised a great temple to the god in Rome in 274 CE and made his festival an empire-wide celebration.

Background

Sun worship had old roots in Rome in the cult of Sol Indiges, and Syrian influences — especially the cult of Elagabal of Emesa, promoted by the emperor Elagabalus — helped shape the later cult. Aurelian's Sol, however, was presented as a supreme, universal deity, patron of the emperor and of the empire, whose invincibility guaranteed Rome's own.

Festival

The birthday of Sol Invictus, Dies Natalis Solis Invicti, was celebrated on 25 December, near the winter solstice, with games in the Circus Maximus. The proximity of this feast to Christmas has often been noted in discussion of the date chosen for the Christian celebration of the Nativity; scholars debate whether the Christian feast was set to coincide with, or independently of, the solar festival.

Decline

The cult flourished through the third and early fourth centuries; Constantine the Great depicted Sol on his coinage before his conversion to Christianity. Under Theodosius I, pagan cults including that of Sol Invictus were suppressed in the late fourth century.
