Incendiary device

An incendiary device is a weapon or munition designed to start fires or destroy sensitive equipment using intense heat generated by a chemical reaction. Incendiary materials include napalm, thermite, white phosphorus, and gelled fuels; such devices have been used since ancient times, from Greek fire to the firebombing campaigns of the twentieth century.

History

Incendiary warfare is ancient: flaming arrows, pots of pitch, and Byzantine Greek fire all served to burn ships and fortifications. In the Second World War, incendiary bombs were used on a massive scale in the strategic bombing of Germany and Japan — the raids on Hamburg (1943) and Tokyo (March 1945) created firestorms that killed tens of thousands. Small incendiary devices were also dropped by Axis balloon bombs and used by resistance movements for sabotage.

Regulation

Protocol III of the 1980 Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons prohibits making civilians and civilian objects the object of attack by incendiary weapons and restricts their use near concentrations of civilians; it does not ban incendiary weapons outright against military targets. Improvised incendiaries such as the Molotov cocktail fall under domestic arson and explosives law in most jurisdictions.
