Fix (position)

Fix
PurposeDetermining the position of a vessel, aircraft, or vehicle
MethodsVisual bearings, celestial sights, radio navigation, satellite navigation (GNSS)
Related conceptsDead reckoning, estimated position, line of position

In nautical and aviation navigation, a fix is the determination of a vessel's or aircraft's position at a given time from external reference points or signals, as opposed to a position estimated by dead reckoning alone. A fix is obtained by intersecting two or more lines of position, or by direct measurement with an electronic navigation system.

Methods

Visual fix

A navigator takes compass bearings on two or more charted landmarks — headlands, lighthouses, buoys — and plots the bearing lines on the chart; their intersection is the fix. A running fix uses a single object observed at two different times, advancing the first line of position by the course and distance run in between.

Celestial fix

Using a sextant, the navigator measures the altitudes of celestial bodies, reduces the sights to lines of position, and plots their intersection. Celestial fixes were the mainstay of ocean navigation before electronic aids and are still taught as a backup.

Electronic fix

Radio direction finding, hyperbolic systems such as Decca and Loran-C, radar ranges and bearings, and satellite navigation systems such as GPS produce fixes automatically and continuously. A modern GPS fix is accurate to a few metres; the plotted fix remains the standard against which the dead-reckoning track is compared, since discrepancies reveal the effect of current, leeway, and steering error.

Use in practice

Fixes are taken at regular intervals — frequently in pilotage waters, less often in the open ocean — and logged with the time. In aviation, fixes also denote named reporting points on airways, defined by coordinates or by the intersection of radio navigation aids, which aircraft use for position reporting and flight planning.
