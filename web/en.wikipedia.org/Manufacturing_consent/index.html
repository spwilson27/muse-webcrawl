Manufacturing consent

Concept: Political communication / media studies
Origin: Walter Lippmann, Public Opinion (1922)
Related: Propaganda, agenda-setting, hegemony

Manufacturing consent is a concept in political science and media studies describing how public agreement with government policies and elite interests can be produced through the management of information and opinion, rather than arising spontaneously from open deliberation.

Origins

The phrase derives from Walter Lippmann's Public Opinion (1922), in which he wrote that the "manufacture of consent" had become a regular organ of popular government, allowing a specialised class to shape the pictures in citizens' heads through the press. Lippmann saw the public as largely unable to grasp complex issues directly, and treated managed opinion as a fact — and partly a necessity — of modern democracy.

Development

The phrase was later taken up critically by Edward S. Herman and Noam Chomsky in their 1988 book Manufacturing Consent, whose propaganda model explained consent as an outcome of structural features of commercial media: ownership, advertising, sourcing, flak and ideology. Related ideas appear in Antonio Gramsci's theory of cultural hegemony and in agenda-setting and framing research, which study how media influence what the public thinks about, even when they do not dictate conclusions.

Criticism

Scholars debate how far consent is "manufactured" as opposed to contested: audiences interpret media actively, alternative and social media can challenge dominant frames, and governments often fail to secure public support despite favourable coverage.
