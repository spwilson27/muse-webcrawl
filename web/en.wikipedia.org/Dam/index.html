Dam

A dam is a barrier that stops or restricts the flow of surface water or underground streams. Reservoirs created by dams not only suppress floods but also provide water for activities such as irrigation, human consumption, industrial use, aquaculture, and navigability. Hydropower is often used in conjunction with dams to generate electricity. A dam can also be used to collect or store water which can be evenly distributed between locations.

Generally, a dam serves the primary purpose of retaining water within a defined area, while a diversionary structure such as a levee serves to prevent water from flooding a specific area. Dams are among the oldest of human structures: the Jawa Dam in Jordan dates to about 3000 BC, and Egypt's Sadd el-Kafara to about 2900 BC.

Types

  • Gravity dams resist the horizontal pressure of water entirely by their own weight (e.g., the Grand Coulee Dam).
  • Arch dams transfer water pressure to the canyon walls; curved and thin, they use far less concrete (e.g., Hoover Dam, an arch-gravity design).
  • Embankment dams are built of compacted earth or rock fill (e.g., the Tarbela Dam); they are the most common type.
  • Buttress dams support a water-facing slab with a series of triangular buttresses.

Notable dams

The world's tallest dam is the Nurek Dam in Tajikistan (an embankment dam, 300 m). The Three Gorges Dam in China has the largest generating capacity of any power station (22,500 MW) and the greatest reservoir of any concrete gravity dam by structural volume. The Itaipu Dam on the Paraná River long held the generation record. Among the oldest still in use is the Lake Homs Dam in Syria, dating to Roman times.

Failure and safety

Dam failures can be catastrophic: the 1975 Banqiao Dam failure in China, caused by Typhoon Nina, killed an estimated 171,000 people. Common failure modes include overtopping, foundation defects, piping (internal erosion), and earthquake damage. Most countries now have dam safety legislation requiring periodic inspection, surveillance instrumentation, and emergency planning.

Environmental impact

Dams fragment river ecosystems, block fish migration (partially mitigated by fish ladders), trap sediment, and displace human communities through reservoir flooding. Reservoirs can emit methane from decomposing vegetation. These costs have led to dam removal projects, particularly of smaller, obsolete dams in North America and Europe.
