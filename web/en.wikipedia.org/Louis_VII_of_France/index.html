Louis VII (1120 – 18 September 1180), called the Young (le Jeune), was King of France from 1137 to 1180. He was the second son of Louis VI and was originally intended for the Church, but became heir on the death of his elder brother Philip. His reign saw the beginning of the long rivalry between the Capetian kings of France and the Angevin kings of England, and he led the unsuccessful Second Crusade to the Holy Land.

Marriage to Eleanor of Aquitaine

Shortly before his accession, Louis married Eleanor, heiress to the vast Duchy of Aquitaine, greatly enlarging the territory under Capetian control. The marriage was unhappy, and Louis's burning of Vitry-en-Perthois during a war with Champagne — in which townspeople sheltering in a church perished — troubled his conscience deeply and helped prompt his crusading vow.

The Second Crusade

In 1147 Louis and Conrad III of Germany led the Second Crusade, preached by Bernard of Clairvaux after the fall of Edessa. The expedition suffered heavy losses crossing Anatolia, and its attack on Damascus in 1148 failed. Eleanor accompanied Louis to the Holy Land; their marriage deteriorated further and was annulled in 1152 on grounds of consanguinity.

Rivalry with Henry II

Within weeks of the annulment, Eleanor married Henry Plantagenet, Count of Anjou and Duke of Normandy, who became King Henry II of England in 1154. The Angevin lands — England, Normandy, Anjou and (through Eleanor) Aquitaine — dwarfed the French royal domain. Louis spent much of the rest of his reign scheming against Henry, supporting rebellions by Henry's sons in 1173–74, though with limited success.

Domestic rule and legacy

At home Louis consolidated royal administration, granted charters to towns, protected trade and the Church, and began construction of Notre-Dame Cathedral in Paris (1163). His patronage helped make Paris a centre of learning and Gothic architecture. By his third marriage, to Adèle of Champagne, he finally had a son, the future Philip II Augustus, born in 1165. Louis died in Paris in 1180.
