Pan

In Greek mythology, Pan (Greek: Πάν) is the god of the wild, of shepherds and flocks, of nature and rustic music, and a companion of the nymphs. He has the hindquarters, legs, and horns of a goat, in the same manner as a faun or satyr. Associated with fertility, spring, and untouched wilderness, Pan is one of the oldest Greek gods, with origins in Arcadia, the mountainous region that remained his principal cult centre.

Mythology

Pan's parentage varies by source; he is most often called the son of Hermes, with a mother who is usually a nymph or the goat Amalthea in some traditions. He is credited with inventing the panpipes (syrinx), fashioned, in the famous myth, from the reeds into which the nymph Syrinx was transformed to escape him. Pan's sudden, frightening shout was said to inspire the irrational terror called panic (panikos), which he used to aid the gods and, in legend, the Athenians at the Battle of Marathon. He also challenged Apollo to a musical contest, judged by the mountain god Tmolus.

Cult and worship

Unlike most Olympians, Pan was worshipped chiefly in caves and wild places rather than temples; the Cave of Pan on the Athenian Acropolis and the sanctuary at the source of the Cephissus were notable sites. After a runner reported meeting Pan before Marathon, Athens adopted his cult with sacrifices and a torch race.

Later influence

Pan's image — goat-legged, piping, and lustful — influenced the iconography of the Devil in Christian art. The famous report in Plutarch that a voice announced "the great god Pan is dead" during the reign of Tiberius was long discussed by theologians. In the 19th and 20th centuries Pan was revived in literature and art as a symbol of nature and pagan freedom, appearing in works by Kenneth Grahame (The Wind in the Willows), Arthur Machen, and others.
