Straw poll

A straw poll, straw vote, or straw ballot is an ad hoc or unofficial vote. It is used to show the popular opinion on a certain matter, and can be used to help politicians know the majority opinion and help them decide what to say in order to gain votes. Straw polls provide dialogue among movements within large groups. In meetings subject to rules of order, impromptu straw polls often are taken to see if there is a consensus for a particular motion, for the same reason some motions to table are taken in committees.

The idiom may allude to the practice of throwing straw into the air to see which way the wind blows, a metaphor for testing public sentiment. In the United States, the term is particularly associated with early, non-binding votes in presidential nomination contests — most famously the Iowa Straw Poll held by the Republican Party of Iowa in Ames from 1979 until its abolition in 2015.

History

The term was in use in American politics by the early 19th century; John Quincy Adams recorded in his diary a reference to a "straw vote" in 1824. Newspapers in the 19th and early 20th centuries conducted large straw polls by mail — the Literary Digest polls being the most famous — until the Digest's catastrophic misprediction of the 1936 presidential election discredited unrepresentative sampling, despite an enormous sample size of over two million returned ballots.

Famous straw polls

  • Iowa Straw Poll (Ames Straw Poll): held in the summer before a contested Republican caucus; won by candidates including George H. W. Bush, George W. Bush, Mitt Romney and Michele Bachmann. Abolished in June 2015.
  • CPAC straw poll: taken at the Conservative Political Action Conference, often seen as a barometer of conservative activist sentiment.
  • Straw polls at U.S. state party conventions and county fairs, used to gauge activist support early in election cycles.

Binding vs. non-binding

A straw poll is not binding and typically does not award delegates or determine an outcome; its value is in publicity, fundraising momentum, and as a test of campaign organisation. Some straw polls do, however, formally influence later stages — for example, early rounds of voting in some leadership elections. Because participants are self-selected (typically the most motivated supporters), straw poll results are not scientifically representative of the broader electorate.
