Tungsten oxide refers to the oxides of tungsten, of which the most important are tungsten(VI) oxide (tungsten trioxide, WO₃) and tungsten(IV) oxide (tungsten dioxide, WO₂). Several intermediate, non-stoichiometric oxides also exist between these compositions, related to the family of Magnéli phases and tungsten bronzes.

Tungsten trioxide

Tungsten trioxide is a yellow solid obtained industrially by calcining ammonium paratungstate or by roasting tungsten ores and intermediates derived from scheelite and wolframite. It is the principal intermediate in the production of tungsten metal: WO₃ is reduced with hydrogen at high temperature to tungsten powder, which is used for lamp filaments, electrical contacts, and cemented carbides. Structurally, WO₃ consists of corner-sharing WO₆ octahedra and undergoes several phase transitions with temperature; it is a semiconductor and is slightly soluble in alkalis, forming tungstates such as sodium tungstate.

WO₃ is notable for electrochromism: thin films change colour reversibly when ions such as H⁺ or Li⁺ are intercalated under an applied voltage, a property exploited in smart windows and displays. It is also used as a catalyst and in gas sensors, photochromic materials, pigments and fireproofing fabrics.

Tungsten dioxide and other oxides

Tungsten dioxide (WO₂) is a brown, metallic-conducting solid obtained by partial reduction of WO₃; it has a distorted rutile structure. Intermediate oxides such as W₁₈O₄₉ (WO₂.₇₂) form needle-like crystals studied for optical and sensing applications. Hydrated forms of tungsten oxide, such as tungstic acid (H₂WO₄), occur naturally as rare minerals (tungstite, hydrotungstite).
