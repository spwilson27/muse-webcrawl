Just war theory

Just war theory is a doctrine of military ethics that studies the conditions under which warfare may be morally justified and the limits that should govern its conduct. Developed by thinkers including Cicero, Augustine of Hippo, Thomas Aquinas, Francisco de Vitoria and Hugo Grotius, it seeks a middle path between pacifism and unrestricted realism.

Jus ad bellum

The criteria for justly going to war traditionally include: (1) just cause — usually defence against aggression; (2) legitimate authority — war must be declared by a proper authority; (3) right intention — the aim must be peace and justice, not revenge or gain; (4) last resort — peaceful alternatives must be exhausted; (5) probability of success; and (6) proportionality — the good expected must outweigh the harm caused.

Jus in bello

The criteria for just conduct in war include discrimination — non-combatants must not be deliberately targeted — and proportionality in the use of force. These ideas underlie much of modern international humanitarian law, including the Geneva Conventions. A third category, jus post bellum, concerning justice after war, has been developed more recently.

Criticism

Pacifists argue no war can be just; realists argue morality has no place in state conflict; and revisionist philosophers such as Jeff McMahan have challenged traditional assumptions about the moral equality of combatants. The theory remains central to military ethics education and legal debate about interventions.
