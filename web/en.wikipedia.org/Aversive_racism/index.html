Aversive racism

Aversive racism is a theory proposed by Samuel L. Gaertner and John F. Dovidio in 1986, proposing a form of prejudice characterizing the attitudes of many white Americans who endorse egalitarian values and regard themselves as nonprejudiced, yet discriminate in subtle, rationalizable ways.

Framework

According to the theory, aversive racists experience discomfort, uneasiness, or fear in interracial interactions rather than overt hostility. Because their prejudice conflicts with a consciously egalitarian self-image, discrimination emerges mainly when behavior can be justified on non-racial grounds — for example, in ambiguous hiring decisions — and is avoided when norms for appropriate behaviour are clear.

Research

Experimental studies by Gaertner, Dovidio and collaborators found, for instance, that white participants were less likely to help a Black victim in an emergency when other bystanders were present (allowing responsibility to be diffused), but helped at equal or higher rates when they were the only witness. The concept relates to broader work on implicit bias and modern, symbolic racism.
