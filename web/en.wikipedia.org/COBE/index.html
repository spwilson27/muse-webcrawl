The Cosmic Background Explorer (COBE), also known as Explorer 66, was a NASA satellite launched on 18 November 1989 to measure the cosmic microwave background (CMB) radiation of the universe. Its measurements confirmed that the CMB spectrum is an almost perfect blackbody and, in 1992, revealed tiny temperature fluctuations (anisotropies) — the primordial "seeds" of galaxies — providing landmark support for the Big Bang theory.

Instruments and findings

COBE carried three instruments. FIRAS (Far-Infrared Absolute Spectrophotometer), led by John Mather, measured the CMB spectrum and showed it matched a blackbody at 2.725 K to extraordinary precision. The DMR (Differential Microwave Radiometer), led by George Smoot, mapped temperature differences of about one part in 100,000 across the sky — the anisotropies announced in April 1992 to wide acclaim. DIRBE surveyed infrared background light from stars and dust.

Legacy

The results earned Mather and Smoot the 2006 Nobel Prize in Physics. COBE's findings were later refined by the WMAP and Planck missions, but its detections remain a foundation of precision cosmology, confirming the hot Big Bang model and the gravitational-instability theory of structure formation.
