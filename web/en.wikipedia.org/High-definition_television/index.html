High-definition television

High-definition television (HDTV) describes a television system that provides an image resolution substantially higher than that of standard-definition television (SDTV). HDTV is usually transmitted digitally; earlier analog versions were also developed. The term has been used since 1936, but in modern times refers to a generation of systems including 720p (1280×720), 1080i (1920×1080 interlaced), and 1080p (1920×1080 progressive), typically with a 16:9 widescreen aspect ratio and multichannel digital audio.

History

Japan's NHK began research into high-definition systems in the 1960s, leading to the analog Hi-Vision/MUSE system demonstrated in the 1980s. In the United States, the FCC's advisory process in the late 1980s and 1990s pivoted toward digital transmission, culminating in the ATSC standards adopted in 1996 and the first digital HDTV broadcasts in 1998. Europe adopted the DVB family of standards. The transition from analog to digital broadcasting was completed in the US in 2009 and in most other countries during the following decade.

Technical details

HDTV signals are compressed using codecs such as MPEG-2 and, increasingly, MPEG-4 AVC/H.264 and HEVC, and delivered by terrestrial broadcast, cable, satellite, and internet streaming. Display technologies shifted from CRT and plasma to LCD, LED-backlit LCD, and OLED panels. HDTV has since been succeeded at the high end by ultra-high-definition television (UHDTV), including 4K and 8K resolutions, though HD formats remain dominant in broadcasting.

Adoption

By the early 2010s, HD channels and receivers had become standard in most developed markets, and virtually all new televisions sold were HD or UHD capable. Sports broadcasting was a major driver of adoption, as the wider aspect ratio and finer detail suited fast-moving events.
