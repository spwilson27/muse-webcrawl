The Virtual Boy is a 32-bit tabletop video game console developed and manufactured by Nintendo. Released in 1995, it was marketed as the first console capable of displaying stereoscopic 3D graphics. The player looks into a binocular eyepiece mounted on a stand and sees a monochrome red image; a second display produces the parallax effect that creates depth. It was designed by Gunpei Yokoi and his R&D1 team.

Development and technology

Nintendo licensed reflective display technology developed by the US company Reflection Technology. To keep costs down, the display used red LEDs — the cheapest available — scanning a column of pixels across an oscillating mirror 50 times per second to form a 384 × 224 image for each eye. Development was long and difficult, and Yokoi reportedly did not consider the machine ready for market; Nintendo released it to focus resources on the Nintendo 64.

Reception and failure

The Virtual Boy was a commercial failure, selling only about 770,000 units and being discontinued within a year. Critics faulted its high price, uncomfortable form factor, monochrome graphics, and reports of headaches and eye strain, which led Nintendo to include prominent health warnings and a recommended auto-pause feature. Only 22 games were released for it, including Mario's Tennis, Wario Land, Teleroboxer, and Jack Bros. Yokoi left Nintendo in 1996, the year after launch, in the aftermath of the product's failure — though he stated his departure had long been planned — and founded his own company, Koto Laboratory, before his death in a traffic accident in 1997.

Legacy

The Virtual Boy is regularly cited among the worst-selling and most ill-conceived game systems, yet it remains historically significant as an early consumer attempt at virtual reality graphics, anticipating later 3D and VR efforts. Its library, especially Virtual Boy Wario Land, has been re-evaluated more favourably by collectors.
