A D-pad (short for directional pad) is a flat, typically thumb-operated, directional control on a game controller, usually laid out as a plus sign (+) pivoting on a central axis, with one switch under each of the four arms. It allows digital (on/off) control in four directions — up, down, left, and right — and, by pressing two arms at once, diagonal directions.

History

The D-pad was designed by Nintendo engineer Gunpei Yokoi for the 1982 Game & Watch handheld version of Donkey Kong. The compact cross design replaced the bulkier joystick used on other handhelds, allowing the clamshell unit to fold flat without a protruding stick. Nintendo patented the design and used it to great acclaim on the Nintendo Entertainment System (NES) controller in 1983/1985, after which it became a standard feature of gamepads across the industry, even as analogue sticks became the primary means of control in 3D games.

Nintendo's patent on the specific pivoting十字 design expired in 2005, and rival manufacturers adopted numerous variants: circular discs (Sega), segmented crosses, and cross-in-circle layouts (PlayStation's separated four-button design was itself partly a way to avoid the patent).

Design and use

Each arm of the pad rests over a contact switch. Rocking the pad presses one or two switches at a time. Because it gives precise, unambiguous digital input, the D-pad remains preferred for 2D platformers, fighting games (for special-move inputs), puzzle games, and menu navigation. On modern controllers such as the DualSense and Xbox Wireless Controller, the D-pad survives alongside analogue sticks and is often used for weapon or item selection.

Outside games

The D-pad layout has appeared on television remote controls, DVD players, and other devices for navigating on-screen menus.
