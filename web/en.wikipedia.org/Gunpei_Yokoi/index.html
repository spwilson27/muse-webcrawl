Gunpei Yokoi (横井 軍平, Yokoi Gunpei, 10 September 1941 – 4 October 1997), sometimes transliterated as Gumpei Yokoi, was a Japanese video game designer and inventor. A long-time Nintendo employee, he was best known as the creator of the Game & Watch handheld system, the inventor of the cross-shaped control pad (the D-pad), and the designer of the Game Boy, one of the best-selling video game consoles of all time. He also produced the Metroid series and mentored Shigeru Miyamoto. His design philosophy of "Lateral Thinking with Withered Technology" — using mature, inexpensive technology in novel ways — became a defining influence on Nintendo's hardware design.

Early life and Nintendo

Yokoi was born in Kyoto and graduated from Doshisha University with a degree in electronics. He was hired by Nintendo in 1965, when the company was still primarily a manufacturer of hanafuda playing cards, to maintain the assembly line machines used to make the cards. Nintendo president Hiroshi Yamauchi noticed a toy Yokoi had built in his spare time — an extendable arm — and asked him to develop it as a product for the Christmas rush. The result, the Ultra Hand (1966), sold over a million units and launched Yokoi's career as a product designer. He subsequently developed other successful toys, including the Ultra Machine (a baseball pitching machine), the Ultra Scope, and the Love Tester.

Inventions and Game & Watch

Yokoi headed Nintendo's Research & Development 1 division (R&D1), the company's first games development team. Watching a bored businessman playing with an LCD calculator on a Shinkansen train, he conceived a small handheld game that could double as a watch. The Game & Watch line, launched in 1980, sold some 43.4 million units worldwide across 60 titles. For the dual-screen Donkey Kong Game & Watch (1982), Yokoi devised the cross-shaped directional pad — the D-pad — to control movement compactly; the design was patented and became a standard feature of game controllers.

As head of R&D1, Yokoi oversaw or produced numerous arcade and console titles. He mentored the young designer Shigeru Miyamoto, whose first game, Donkey Kong (1981), Yokoi helped supervise. He produced the Metroid series (from 1986) and was involved with Kid Icarus and the Fire Emblem series, as well as hardware such as R.O.B. and the Game Boy's link features.

Game Boy and later career

Yokoi's greatest commercial success was the Game Boy (1989). Applying his philosophy of lateral thinking with withered technology, he chose a modest monochrome dot-matrix display and a relatively weak processor in exchange for long battery life, durability, small size, and low price. Against technically superior colour rivals such as the Atari Lynx and Sega Game Gear, the Game Boy and its successor the Game Boy Color sold over 118 million units combined. The pack-in game Tetris became one of the best-selling video games of all time.

Virtual Boy and departure

Yokoi also designed the Virtual Boy (1995), a tabletop console using red LED stereoscopic displays to create a 3D effect. Released before the technology was mature — in part due to concerns that rivals would seize the VR market — the Virtual Boy was a commercial failure, discontinued within a year after selling about 770,000 units, and is often cited as Nintendo's greatest hardware failure. Yokoi left Nintendo in August 1996, insisting (and being publicly supported by Nintendo) that his departure was not because of the Virtual Boy's failure but a long-planned move toward independence. He founded his own company, Koto Laboratory, and worked on the WonderSwan handheld for Bandai.

Death and legacy

On 4 October 1997, Yokoi was killed in a traffic accident on the Hokuriku Expressway in Ishikawa Prefecture: after a minor collision, he and his driver stopped and were standing on the road when Yokoi was struck by a passing car. He was 56. The WonderSwan, completed under Bandai after his death, was dedicated in his memory in some releases. Yokoi's philosophy — that fun comes from novel uses of cheap, proven technology rather than brute processing power — continued to shape Nintendo's later hardware, notably the Wii and Nintendo DS. He received a posthumous Lifetime Achievement Award at the Game Developers Choice Awards in 2003.
