Nuclear winter

Nuclear winter is a severe and prolonged global climatic cooling effect hypothesised to occur after widespread firestorms following a large-scale nuclear war. The hypothesis is based on the assumption that the resulting fires would inject so much soot and smoke into the stratosphere — where, above the weather, it cannot rain out — that direct sunlight reaching the Earth's surface would be drastically reduced for years, causing crop failures and mass starvation.

History of the hypothesis

The idea was first seriously examined in the early 1980s; the influential 1983 "TTAPS" study (by Turco, Toon, Ackerman, Pollack, and Carl Sagan) modelled temperature drops of 15–20 °C or more in continental interiors after a full-scale exchange. The concept entered public debate and is credited by some historians with influencing late Cold War arms control.

Modern modelling

Twenty-first-century climate models, including work by Alan Robock and colleagues, continue to find severe cooling even for a regional war involving about 100 Hiroshima-sized weapons: global temperatures could fall by more than 1 °C for a decade, shortening growing seasons at mid-latitudes. A full-scale war could lower global average temperatures by several degrees for years, with precipitation declines of tens of percent.

Debate

Critics in the 1980s argued the early models overstated soot production and lofting, dubbing milder scenarios "nuclear autumn". Debate continues over the amount of soot cities would generate, its removal rate, and agricultural consequences, but the broad conclusion that smoke-driven cooling would be climatically significant remains widely supported in the literature.
