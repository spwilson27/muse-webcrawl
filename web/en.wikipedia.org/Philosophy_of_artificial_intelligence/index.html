Philosophy of artificial intelligence

The philosophy of artificial intelligence is a branch of the philosophy of technology that explores artificial intelligence and its implications for knowledge and understanding of intelligence, ethics, consciousness, and free will. It asks whether machines can genuinely think, understand, and be conscious, or only simulate these — and what follows for how such machines should be built, used, and treated.

Can machines think?

Alan Turing's 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" sidestepped definitions of thinking by proposing the imitation game, now the Turing test. The strong AI hypothesis — that a suitably programmed computer literally has a mind — was challenged by John Searle's Chinese room argument (1980), which holds that manipulating symbols by rule does not produce understanding. Hubert Dreyfus criticized the assumptions of symbolic AI from a phenomenological standpoint, while functionalists replied that mental states are defined by their causal organization, realizable in silicon as in neurons.

Consciousness and minds

Philosophers debate whether AI systems could be phenomenally conscious, whether large language models and other architectures have genuine understanding or mere pattern-matching, and how the hard problem of consciousness bears on machine minds. Questions of machine moral status — whether advanced AIs could deserve rights or moral consideration — and of moral agency in autonomous systems form bridges to ethics.

Ethical and social issues

The field also covers algorithmic bias, accountability for AI decisions, superintelligence and existential risk (raised by Nick Bostrom and others), the alignment problem, and the effects of automation on work and society.
