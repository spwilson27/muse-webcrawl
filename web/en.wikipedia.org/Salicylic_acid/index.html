Salicylic acid

Salicylic acid is an organic compound with the formula C7H6O3. A colourless (or white), bitter-tasting solid, it is a precursor to and a metabolite of aspirin (acetylsalicylic acid). It is a plant hormone, and has been listed by the EPA Toxic Substances Control Act's chemical inventory. The name derives from Salix, the willow genus, whose bark is a traditional source. Salicylic acid occurs naturally in many plants and serves in defence signalling; synthetically it is manufactured on a large scale by the Kolbe–Schmitt reaction. It is best known today for its use in skincare products for treating acne and for removing warts, calluses, and dandruff.

Occurrence and history

Willow bark preparations were used for pain and fever from ancient Egypt and Sumer onward; Hippocrates described such remedies, and the Cherokee and other Native American peoples used willow infusions similarly. The active bitter principle, salicin, was isolated in the 1820s, and salicylic acid was first prepared from it and from meadowsweet (Spiraea) in the 1830s–1850s. Its harsh effects on the stomach prompted Felix Hoffmann at Bayer to synthesise the acetylated derivative, aspirin, in 1897.

Production

Industrial production uses the Kolbe–Schmitt reaction: sodium phenoxide is heated with carbon dioxide under pressure to give sodium salicylate, which is acidified to the free acid. Salicylic acid is the feedstock for aspirin synthesis and for dyes, preservatives, and other salicylate esters, including methyl salicylate (oil of wintergreen).

Uses

Topically, salicylic acid is a keratolytic: it softens and sheds the outer layer of skin. It is a standard ingredient in anti-acne and anti-dandruff preparations, typically at 0.5–2%, and at higher concentrations (up to about 40%) in treatments for warts, corns, and psoriasis plaques. Taken orally, salicylates relieve pain, fever, and inflammation but irritate the stomach lining; salicylism (salicylate poisoning) can result from overdose. In plants, salicylic acid is a key signalling molecule in systemic acquired resistance to pathogens.

Safety

Concentrated solutions can irritate or burn skin, and topical products carry warnings against use on large areas or by children in certain conditions. Salicylates should be used cautiously by people allergic to aspirin and are associated, like aspirin, with Reye's syndrome risk in children with viral infections when taken systemically.
