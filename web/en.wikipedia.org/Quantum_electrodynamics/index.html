Quantum electrodynamics

In particle physics, quantum electrodynamics (QED) is the relativistic quantum field theory of electrodynamics. In essence, it describes how light and matter interact and is the first theory where full agreement between quantum mechanics and special relativity is achieved. QED mathematically describes all phenomena involving electrically charged particles interacting by means of exchange of photons and represents the quantum counterpart of classical electromagnetism, giving a complete account of matter and light interaction.

History

QED was developed in the 1940s by Sin-Itiro Tomonaga, Julian Schwinger, Richard Feynman, and Freeman Dyson, building on the earlier work of Paul Dirac. The problem of infinities in calculations was resolved by renormalization. Feynman introduced his eponymous diagrams, a graphical representation of particle interactions. The three founders shared the 1965 Nobel Prize in Physics.

Predictions and accuracy

QED famously predicts the anomalous magnetic moment of the electron and the Lamb shift in the hydrogen spectrum. The agreement between its prediction of the electron's g-factor and experimental measurements — to more than ten significant figures — makes QED one of the most accurate physical theories ever devised.

Lagrangian and structure

The QED Lagrangian couples a Dirac field for the electron to the electromagnetic field, with the interaction term proportional to the elementary charge and the fine-structure constant α ≈ 1/137, which sets the strength of the interaction and allows perturbative calculations as series of Feynman diagrams.
