Whole tone scale

In music and music theory, a whole tone scale is a scale in which each note is separated from its neighbours by the interval of a whole tone, or whole step. The whole tone scale has no leading tone and because all tones are the same distance apart, no single tone stands out; instead, the scale creates a blurred, indistinct sensation.

Structure

There are only two possible whole tone scales in 12-tone equal temperament: one beginning on C (C, D, E, F♯, G♯, A♯, C) and one beginning on D♯ (or equivalently on B). Each whole tone scale divides the octave into six equal parts, and each contains the intervals of the major second, major third, tritone, minor sixth, and minor seventh.

History and usage

The scale was used extensively by the French composer Claude Debussy, notably in pieces such as Voiles from the first book of Préludes, giving his music a dreamy, floating quality. Earlier, Mikhail Glinka used a descending whole tone scale in Ruslan and Lyudmila to depict the villain Chernomor. Franz Liszt and Nikolai Rimsky-Korsakov also employed the scale, and it later became a staple of jazz improvisation and film scoring, where its ambiguous sound often signals dreams or the supernatural.
