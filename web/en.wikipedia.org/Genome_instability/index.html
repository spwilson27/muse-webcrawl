Genome instability

Genome instability (also genomic instability or genetic instability) is an increased tendency of the genome to acquire mutations and other alterations. It refers to a high frequency of changes in the genetic material of a cell lineage, ranging from single-nucleotide substitutions and small insertions and deletions to gross chromosomal rearrangements, aneuploidy, and loss of heterozygosity. Genomic instability is a hallmark of cancer and a feature of several inherited disorders and of ageing.

Forms and causes

Three broad classes are recognised. Nucleotide instability involves elevated rates of base substitution and small indels. Microsatellite instability arises when DNA mismatch repair is defective, so errors at short repetitive sequences go uncorrected, as in Lynch syndrome and many colorectal cancers. Chromosomal instability involves gains, losses, and rearrangements of large chromosome segments or whole chromosomes, driven by defects in DNA repair, replication stress, telomere dysfunction, or errors in chromosome segregation during mitosis. DNA damage from endogenous processes — replication errors, reactive oxygen species, spontaneous hydrolysis — and from exogenous agents such as radiation and mutagenic chemicals continually threatens genome integrity, and cells rely on checkpoints and repair pathways (including those involving the BRCA1, BRCA2, ATM, and p53 proteins) to hold instability in check.

Consequences

In cancer, instability accelerates the accumulation of the driver mutations that produce uncontrolled growth and allows tumours to evolve resistance to therapy; it also creates vulnerabilities exploited by treatments such as PARP inhibitors in repair-deficient tumours. In the germline, instability underlies inherited cancer-predisposition and premature-ageing syndromes such as Bloom syndrome, Fanconi anaemia, and ataxia telangiectasia.
