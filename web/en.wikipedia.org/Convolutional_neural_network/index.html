A convolutional neural network (CNN) is a class of artificial neural network most commonly applied to analyze visual imagery. CNNs use a mathematical operation called convolution in place of general matrix multiplication in at least one of their layers. They are regularized versions of multilayer perceptrons, designed to require minimal preprocessing by exploiting the spatial structure of data such as images.

Typical CNN architecture: convolutional layers extract features, pooling layers reduce dimensions, and fully connected layers classify.

Architecture

A CNN typically consists of an input layer, hidden layers, and an output layer. The hidden layers include convolutional layers, which convolve learned filters (kernels) with the input to produce feature maps; pooling layers, which downsample feature maps, commonly by max pooling; and fully connected layers, which perform classification based on the extracted features. Nonlinear activation functions such as ReLU are applied between layers.

Two key properties give CNNs their power: local connectivity, in which each neuron connects only to a small region of the input, mirroring the receptive fields of neurons in the visual cortex; and parameter sharing, in which the same filter weights are used across the whole image, greatly reducing the number of parameters and providing translation equivariance.

History

The design of CNNs was influenced by the work of Hubel and Wiesel on the visual cortex and by Kunihiko Fukushima's neocognitron (1980). Yann LeCun and colleagues developed LeNet in the late 1980s and 1990s, applying backpropagation-trained convolutional networks to handwritten digit recognition for reading zip codes and checks.

In 2012, AlexNet, developed by Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever, and Geoffrey Hinton, won the ImageNet Large Scale Visual Recognition Challenge by a large margin, sparking the deep learning boom. Later architectures include VGGNet (2014), GoogLeNet/Inception (2014), and ResNet (2015), which introduced residual ("skip") connections enabling networks over 100 layers deep.

Applications

CNNs are used in image and video recognition, image classification, medical image analysis, object detection (e.g., R-CNN, YOLO), semantic segmentation, natural language processing, and recommender systems. In the 2020s, vision transformers began to rival CNNs on some benchmarks, but convolutional architectures remain widely used, especially where efficiency matters.
