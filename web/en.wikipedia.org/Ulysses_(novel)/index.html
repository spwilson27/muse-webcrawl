Ulysses is a modernist novel by the Irish writer James Joyce. Partially serialised in the American journal The Little Review from 1918 to 1920, it was published in its entirety in Paris by Sylvia Beach of Shakespeare and Company on 2 February 1922, Joyce's 40th birthday. It is considered one of the most influential works of modernist literature.

Overview

The novel chronicles the passage of Leopold Bloom through Dublin during the course of an ordinary day, 16 June 1904 — now celebrated as Bloomsday. The title alludes to Odysseus (Ulysses in Latin), hero of Homer's Odyssey, and the book's 18 episodes correspond loosely to episodes of the epic, with Bloom as Odysseus, Stephen Dedalus as Telemachus and Molly Bloom as Penelope.

Style

Joyce uses stream of consciousness, parody, puns and a vast range of styles, each episode adopting a distinct technique — from the newspaper headlines of "Aeolus" to the hallucinatory drama of "Circe" and Molly Bloom's unpunctuated closing monologue in "Penelope".

Reception and legal history

Ulysses was banned for obscenity in the United States and the United Kingdom for years. In the landmark 1933 case United States v. One Book Called Ulysses, Judge John M. Woolsey ruled the book not obscene, and it was published in the US in 1934. It consistently ranks among the greatest novels in English, though its difficulty remains legendary; the text contains roughly 265,000 words.
