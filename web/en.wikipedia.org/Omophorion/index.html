Omophorion

The omophorion (Greek: ὠμοφόριον, "that which is borne on the shoulders") is the distinctive liturgical vestment of a bishop in the Eastern Orthodox Church and Eastern Catholic Churches. It is a broad band of cloth, usually white wool embroidered with crosses, worn about the neck and shoulders, with one end hanging down in front and the other behind.

Symbolism

The omophorion symbolizes the bishop's role as shepherd: its wool recalls the lost sheep carried on the shoulders of the Good Shepherd, and it signifies the pastoral responsibility undertaken by the bishop for his flock. In Byzantine practice, the bishop wears the omophorion for the principal parts of the Divine Liturgy, removing or exchanging it at prescribed moments.

Forms

Two forms are in use. The great omophorion is a wide band that wraps around the shoulders and hangs front and back; it is worn at the beginning of the Liturgy. The small omophorion is simpler, hanging down in front like an epitrachelion, and is worn for later parts of services and lesser occasions. The Western equivalent, in origin, is the pallium worn by metropolitan archbishops in the Latin Church.

Historical notes

Depictions of bishops wearing the omophorion appear in early Byzantine art. Because it is the mark of episcopal office, a deposed bishop was traditionally stripped of his omophorion, and its restoration signified reinstatement.
