Coat of arms of Russia

The coat of arms of the Russian Federation is a red heraldic shield bearing a golden double-headed eagle with raised, outspread wings. The eagle wears three crowns connected by a ribbon, holds a sceptre in its right talon and an orb in its left, and on its breast is a smaller red shield depicting a silver horseman — Saint George — slaying a dragon with a spear. The arms were adopted on 30 November 1993 by decree of President Boris Yeltsin and confirmed in federal constitutional law in 2000, restoring a symbol whose origins lie five centuries earlier.

Origins

The double-headed eagle first appeared as a Russian emblem on the seal of Ivan III in 1497, reflecting both Byzantine influence — Ivan had married Sophia Palaiologina, niece of the last Byzantine emperor — and the empire's self-image as heir to Rome and Byzantium. The horseman (yezdetz) was an older symbol of the Moscow princes, gradually identified with Saint George, patron saint of Moscow, whose arms appear on the capital's own coat of arms. Under Peter the Great the eagle's colours were standardised as black on gold, and through the imperial period the state arms accumulated the shields of subordinate kingdoms and principalities on the eagle's wings.

Soviet period and restoration

After the 1917 revolutions the imperial arms were abolished; the RSFSR and later the USSR adopted emblems of wheat, rising sun, hammer and sickle, and red star. Following the dissolution of the Soviet Union in 1991, the new Russian Federation used the double-headed eagle without crowns or breast shield as an interim emblem, until the full arms, designed by artist Yevgeny Ukhnalev, were formally adopted in 1993. The design largely follows the state arms of the Russian Empire in its post-1857 form, minus the territorial shields.

Symbolism

The two heads are commonly interpreted as looking east and west, symbolising Russia's position spanning Europe and Asia; the three crowns have been explained historically as representing the conquered khanates of Kazan, Astrakhan, and Siberia, and in modern official commentary as sovereignty and the unity of the federation. The sceptre and orb denote state power, while Saint George's victory over the dragon symbolises the defence of the homeland and the triumph of good over evil.

Use

The arms appear on the presidential standard, state seals, coinage (including the rouble coins minted since the 1990s redesign), passports, and government buildings. Their use is regulated by the 2000 Federal Constitutional Law "On the State Coat of Arms of the Russian Federation".
