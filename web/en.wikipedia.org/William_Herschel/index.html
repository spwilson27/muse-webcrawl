Frederick William Herschel (15 November 1738 – 25 August 1822) was a German-British astronomer and composer. He built hundreds of telescopes, discovered the planet Uranus in 1781 — the first planet found in recorded history that was unknown to the ancients — and discovered infrared radiation.

Life and work

Herschel emigrated from Hanover to England in 1757 and worked as a music teacher and organist in Bath. Turning to astronomy, he ground his own mirrors and built reflectors of unprecedented size, culminating in the 40-foot telescope at Slough (1789), briefly the largest in the world. Working systematically with his sister Caroline Herschel — herself a noted astronomer — he catalogued thousands of nebulae and double stars, mapped the shape of the Milky Way, and discovered two moons of Saturn and two of Uranus.

On 13 March 1781 he noticed an object that proved to be the planet Uranus, earning him a royal pension from George III and election to the Royal Society. In 1800, experimenting with sunlight passed through a prism, he found heating beyond the red end of the spectrum, discovering infrared radiation. He was knighted in 1816 and became the first president of the Astronomical Society of London (later the Royal Astronomical Society) in 1820. His son John Herschel extended the surveys to the southern hemisphere.
