Producer gas

Producer gas is a fuel gas of low calorific value manufactured by blowing air, or air and steam, through a bed of incandescent carbonaceous fuel — coke, coal, charcoal or wood — in a reactor called a gas producer. The gas consists mainly of carbon monoxide, hydrogen, methane and a large proportion of inert nitrogen (when air is the oxidant), and had a typical heating value of only about 3.7–5.6 MJ per cubic metre, far below natural gas. When generated from wood it is commonly called wood gas.

Production and chemistry

In the producer, partial combustion at the grate converts carbon to carbon dioxide, which is then reduced by hot carbon higher in the bed to carbon monoxide (the Boudouard reaction). Added steam reacts with carbon to form additional carbon monoxide and hydrogen (water gas reactions), moderating temperatures and enriching the gas. Raw producer gas carries tar, ash and particulates that must be filtered and cooled before use in engines.

Uses

From the late nineteenth century producer gas powered industrial furnaces, gas engines and early power stations, and formed the basis of the Mond and Siemens processes. It gained a second life during the Second World War, when petrol rationing led hundreds of thousands of vehicles in Europe — and many in Asia and Australia — to be fitted with wood or charcoal gasifiers. Interest has revived in biomass gasification for rural electrification and combined heat and power.

Hazards

Producer gas contains roughly 20–30% carbon monoxide and is therefore highly poisonous; leaks in poorly ventilated spaces caused many accidents, and wartime users were warned to start and refuel gasifiers outdoors.
