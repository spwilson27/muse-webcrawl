Rocket artillery is artillery that uses rocket explosives as the projectile. The use of rocket artillery dates back to medieval China, where devices such as fire arrows were used, although modern rocket artillery came into widespread use during the Second World War. Unlike tube artillery, rocket systems are typically lighter, simpler to manufacture, and capable of delivering a very large payload over a short period of time, though they are less accurate and place greater demands on logistics.

Rocket artillery is typically categorized by the type of rocket used. Multiple launch rocket systems (MLRS) fire unguided or guided rockets from a mobile vehicle, saturating a target area with submunitions or high-explosive warheads. Rocket artillery may also be employed as a single-shot weapon, such as man-portable anti-tank rockets and surface-to-air missiles fired from tubes or rails.

History

The first documented use of rocket artillery was by the Chinese in 1232 during the Mongol siege of Kaifeng, where fire arrows — gunpowder tubes attached to arrows — were fired at the attackers. Rocket weapons spread through Asia and Europe over the following centuries. In India, the iron-cased Mysorean rockets developed under Hyder Ali and Tipu Sultan in the late 18th century were used against British forces and directly inspired the Congreve rockets used by British forces in the Napoleonic Wars, including the attack on Fort McHenry in 1814 that produced the phrase "the rockets' red glare" in the United States national anthem.

Modern rocket artillery emerged in the 1930s and 1940s. The Soviet Katyusha multiple rocket launcher, mounted on trucks and firing salvos of 82 mm or 132 mm rockets, became one of the most feared weapons of the Eastern Front. Germany fielded the Nebelwerfer series of multi-barrel rocket launchers, while the United States employed ship-mounted rocket barrages to support amphibious landings in the Pacific.

Postwar development

After 1945, rocket artillery became a standard component of most armies. The Soviet BM-21 Grad (1963), a 40-tube 122 mm system on a truck chassis, was exported worldwide and remains in service in dozens of countries. Western counterparts include the American M270 MLRS and the lighter wheeled HIMARS, which fire guided rockets such as the GMLRS with GPS guidance, giving rocket artillery a precision capability once reserved for missiles.

Characteristics

The principal advantages of rocket artillery are its ability to deliver a massive volume of fire in a single salvo, the simplicity and low cost of unguided rockets compared with guided missiles, and the mobility of truck-mounted launchers, which can fire and relocate before counter-battery fire arrives. Its disadvantages include lower accuracy than tube artillery, the large smoke and flame signature at launch, and the heavy logistical burden of reloading bulky rocket pods.
