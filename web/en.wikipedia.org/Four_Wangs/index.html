Four Wangs

The Four Wangs (Chinese: 四王; pinyin: Sì Wáng) were four Chinese landscape painters of the early Qing dynasty, all surnamed Wang, who dominated orthodox landscape painting in the late 17th and early 18th centuries. Following the theories of the Ming-dynasty master Dong Qichang, they codified a scholarly, tradition-centred style that was favoured by the Qing court and defined the "Orthodox School" of Chinese painting.

The painters

  • Wang Shimin (1592–1680), the eldest, a pupil of Dong Qichang's circle, famed for landscapes in the manner of the Yuan master Huang Gongwang.
  • Wang Jian (1598–1677), a friend of Wang Shimin, known for meticulous imitations of Song and Yuan masters.
  • Wang Hui (1632–1717), pupil of both elder Wangs, the most versatile of the group, who directed the production of the monumental handscroll series The Kangxi Emperor's Southern Inspection Tour.
  • Wang Yuanqi (1642–1715), grandson of Wang Shimin, a court official and painter whose abstract, structurally analysed landscapes strongly influenced later orthodox painting.

Style and influence

The Four Wangs practised fang — creative imitation of ancient masters — holding that true innovation lay in synthesising the brush methods of the Song and Yuan dynasties. Their works favour complex, layered mountain compositions built from texture strokes (cun) and washes, often deliberately dry and scholarly in mood. Together with Wu Li and Yun Shouping they are also grouped as the Six Masters of the early Qing. The orthodox style they established dominated Chinese landscape painting for two centuries; modern critics, including reformers of the early 20th century, sometimes blamed it for stagnation, though later scholarship has reassessed the group's technical achievements and individual differences.
