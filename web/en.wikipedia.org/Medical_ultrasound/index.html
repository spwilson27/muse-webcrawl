Medical ultrasound

Medical ultrasound (also known as diagnostic sonography or ultrasonography) is a diagnostic imaging technique based on the application of ultrasound. It is used to create an image of internal body structures such as tendons, muscles, joints, blood vessels, and internal organs, most famously to examine a developing foetus during pregnancy. Because it uses sound waves rather than ionising radiation, it is considered very safe, portable and inexpensive relative to other medical imaging methods.

Principles

A transducer containing piezoelectric crystals emits pulses of high-frequency sound and receives the returning echoes. The time delay and intensity of echoes from tissue boundaries are converted into a two-dimensional brightness-mode (B-mode) image. Doppler ultrasound measures frequency shifts from moving blood to depict flow direction and velocity; modern systems add three- and four-dimensional rendering, elastography and contrast-enhanced techniques using microbubble agents.

Applications

  • Obstetric ultrasound — confirming pregnancy, dating, screening for anomalies and monitoring growth.
  • Echocardiography — imaging heart structure and function.
  • Abdominal and pelvic imaging — liver, gallbladder, kidneys, uterus and ovaries.
  • Vascular ultrasound — detecting deep-vein thrombosis and arterial stenosis.
  • Point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) — rapid bedside assessment in emergency and critical care, including the FAST exam in trauma.
  • Procedural guidance — for biopsies, injections and line placement.

History

Medical ultrasound grew out of wartime sonar and industrial flaw detection. In Glasgow in the 1950s, Ian Donald and colleagues pioneered obstetric applications; Japanese and American groups developed Doppler and echocardiographic methods in parallel. Real-time grey-scale scanning in the 1970s made ultrasound a routine clinical tool.

Limitations and safety

Ultrasound is blocked by gas and bone, is operator-dependent, and has lower soft-tissue contrast than MRI. Diagnostic ultrasound has no confirmed harmful biological effects at clinical output levels, though professional bodies recommend prudent use and adherence to output indices (mechanical and thermal indices).
