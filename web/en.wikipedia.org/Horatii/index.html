Horatii

The Horatii were, in Roman legend, triplet brothers of the Horatia gens who fought a ritual combat against the Curiatii, triplet brothers of Alba Longa, to decide the war between Rome and Alba during the reign of Tullus Hostilius (traditionally dated to the seventh century BC). The story is told by Livy in Book 1 of Ab Urbe Condita.

The combat

To spare the armies a full battle, the two cities agreed that the triplets would fight as champions. In the duel the three Curiatii were wounded but killed two Horatii. The surviving Horatius, feigning flight, drew his pursuers apart — they could run only as fast as their wounds allowed — and killed them one by one, winning the war for Rome.

The sister's death

Returning in triumph, Horatius found his sister, who had been betrothed to one of the Curiatii, weeping over her fiancé's spoils. In rage he killed her, crying "So perish any Roman woman who mourns an enemy." Tried for treason, he was acquitted after appealing to the people, but performed expiatory rites. The tale became a byword for patriotic duty overriding private grief, most famously in Jacques-Louis David's 1784 painting Oath of the Horatii, a landmark of Neoclassicism.
