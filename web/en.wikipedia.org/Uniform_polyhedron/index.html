Uniform polyhedron

A uniform polyhedron has regular polygon faces and is vertex-transitive: there is an isometry that maps any vertex to any other. It may be regular, quasi-regular or semi-regular. The faces need not be convex, so many uniform polyhedra are star polyhedra.

Classification

Apart from the infinite families of prisms and antiprisms, there are 75 uniform polyhedra (or 76 if the Skilling figure is counted). The convex forms comprise the 5 Platonic solids, the 13 Archimedean solids, and the prisms and antiprisms. The non-convex forms include the 4 Kepler–Poinsot polyhedra (the regular star polyhedra) and 53 others, plus the great dirhombicosidodecahedron known as the Skilling figure, which has doubled edges.

History

The convex uniform polyhedra were known since antiquity. Johannes Kepler systematically studied star polyhedra in his Harmonices Mundi (1619). The complete enumeration of uniform polyhedra was published by H. S. M. Coxeter and collaborators in 1954, with the final non-convex forms proved by John Skilling in 1975.
