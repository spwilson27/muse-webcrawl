Gastric inhibitory polypeptide

Gastric inhibitory polypeptide (GIP), also called glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide, is an inhibiting hormone of the secretin family of hormones. It is a 42-amino-acid peptide secreted by K cells of the mucosa of the duodenum and jejunum in response to nutrients, especially glucose and fat.

Function

GIP was named for its ability, at pharmacological doses, to inhibit gastric acid secretion and motility. Its principal physiological role is now recognised as incretin activity: it stimulates glucose-dependent insulin secretion from pancreatic beta cells after a meal. GIP also promotes glucagon secretion at low glucose levels, influences fat metabolism in adipose tissue, and has effects on bone remodelling.

Clinical significance

The incretin effect is blunted in type 2 diabetes, in part because target tissues become resistant to GIP. Nevertheless, dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonists such as tirzepatide, approved in 2022, produce powerful glucose-lowering and weight loss, demonstrating that pharmacological GIP receptor activation is therapeutically valuable. GIP is rapidly degraded by the enzyme dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4).

History

GIP was isolated from intestinal extracts by John Brown and colleagues around 1970 and sequenced as a 42-residue peptide; its insulinotropic action, discovered later, led to the second expansion of the acronym.
