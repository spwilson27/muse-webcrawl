Panama Canal locks

The Panama Canal locks are the lock systems that lift ships up 26 metres (85 feet) to the main elevation of the Panama Canal and lower them back down. The original canal, opened in 1914, has two lanes of locks at three sites: the two-stage Gatun Locks and one-stage Pedro Miguel Locks on either side of Gatun Lake, and the two-stage Miraflores Locks. A third, larger set of locks — the Neopanamax locks at Agua Clara (Atlantic) and Cocolí (Pacific) — opened in 2016 as part of the Panama Canal expansion project.

Original locks

The original lock chambers are 110 feet (33.5 m) wide and 1,000 feet (305 m) long, defining the "Panamax" ship size. Ships are guided through by electric locomotives ("mules") running on rails alongside the chambers. The gates are pairs of steel mitre gates, each up to 82 feet high and weighing up to 730 tons, yet balanced so they can be moved by small motors. Filling and emptying are by gravity through culverts in the lock walls; a chamber fills in roughly 8–15 minutes, and no pumps are used. Gatun Lake's water is consumed with each transit, making rainfall in the canal watershed critical to operations.

Construction

The locks were among the largest concrete structures of their era, designed under chief engineer John Frank Stevens and built under George Washington Goethals. Gatun alone used about 1.66 million cubic metres of concrete. The lock machinery and control systems were supplied largely by General Electric and introduced pioneering interlocked controls to prevent operator error.

Neopanamax locks

The expansion locks, built by the consortium Grupo Unidos por el Canal, use rolling gates and water-saving basins that reuse about 60% of each chamber's water. Their chambers are 427 m long and 55 m wide, accommodating ships of up to about 14,000 TEU and much larger bulk and gas carriers. Ships are positioned by tugboats rather than locomotives. The new locks opened on 26 June 2016 with the transit of the container ship COSCO Shipping Panama.
