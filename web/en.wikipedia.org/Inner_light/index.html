Inner light

TraditionReligious Society of Friends (Quakers)
Also calledInward light, Light of Christ, that of God in everyone
Associated figuresGeorge Fox, Robert Barclay, Rufus Jones

The inner light (also called the inward light or Light of Christ) is a central concept in the theology of the Religious Society of Friends (Quakers). It refers to the direct, inward experience of God — "that of God in everyone" — which guides, convicts, and comforts each person and is available to all, regardless of creed, clergy, or scripture.

History

George Fox, the founder of Quakerism in 17th-century England, preached that Christ had come to teach his people himself and that true worship required attention to the inward light rather than outward forms, "steeple-houses", or a paid ministry. The apologist Robert Barclay systematised the doctrine in his Apology (1676), placing the light as the primary rule of faith, with Scripture as a secondary and subordinate rule. In the early 20th century, Rufus Jones popularised the phrase "inner light" itself and emphasised its mystical character.

Practice and interpretation

Belief in the inner light underlies distinctive Quaker practices: unprogrammed worship in expectant silence, waiting for the light to prompt spoken ministry; decision-making by the "sense of the meeting" rather than votes; and testimonies such as the refusal of oaths, plain speech, and pacifism. Friends differ in interpretation — liberal Friends often understand the light in universal, experiential terms, while evangelical and conservative Friends stress its identity with the historic Jesus Christ — but the expectation of continuing, direct revelation remains characteristic of the tradition.
