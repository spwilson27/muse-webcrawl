Discount window

The discount window is an instrument of monetary policy (usually controlled by central banks) that allows eligible institutions to borrow money from the central bank, usually on a short-term basis, to meet temporary shortages of liquidity caused by internal or external disruptions. The interest rate charged on such loans is the discount rate.

In the United States

In the U.S., depository institutions may borrow from their district Federal Reserve Bank under Regulation A through three programs: primary credit for generally sound institutions (usually overnight, at a rate set above the federal funds rate target), secondary credit for institutions not eligible for primary credit, and seasonal credit for smaller institutions with recurring funding needs. Loans must be fully collateralised.

Stigma

Banks have historically been reluctant to use the discount window for fear that borrowing signals financial weakness — so-called discount window stigma — a concern the Federal Reserve has sought to reduce by setting the primary credit rate as a backstop above market rates and by emphasising that borrowing is routine and confidential. Usage rose sharply during the financial crisis of 2007–2008 and in March 2023, when the Fed created the Bank Term Funding Program alongside discount window lending.

Lender of last resort

The discount window is a principal channel through which the central bank acts as lender of last resort, following the principles associated with Walter Bagehot: lend freely, at a penalty rate, against good collateral.
