Fixture (property law)

In property law, a fixture is a chattel (item of personal property) that has been attached to real property in such a way that it becomes part of the realty and passes with it, for example on a sale or mortgage. The distinction matters in conveyancing, landlord–tenant disputes, and secured transactions: once an item is a fixture, removing it can constitute damage to the property of the landowner rather than the owner's removal of their own goods.

Built-in fittings are fixtures; freestanding furniture generally is not.

The tests

English law, from cases such as Holland v Hodgson (1872), applies two criteria: the degree of annexation (how firmly the item is attached — items resting on their own weight are presumed chattels, items fixed are presumed fixtures) and the purpose of annexation (whether attachment was for the permanent improvement of the land or merely for the better enjoyment of the chattel itself; purpose is generally the controlling test). In Elitestone Ltd v Morris (1997), a bungalow resting on concrete pillars was held part of the land because it could not be removed without demolition. The United States applies similar multi-factor tests, often codified in the Uniform Commercial Code for "fixture filings" governing competing claims of mortgagees and equipment financiers.

Trade fixtures and agricultural fixtures

Exceptions allow tenants to remove trade fixtures — equipment installed by a tenant for their business, such as shop fittings and machinery — and agricultural fixtures, provided removal occurs before the tenancy ends and any damage is made good. Contracts of sale commonly list included and excluded fixtures and fittings to avoid disputes.
