German mediatisation

The German mediatisation (German: Reichsdeputationshauptschluss) was the mass restructuring of the political map of Germany between 1802 and 1814. In the process, the great majority of the states of the Holy Roman Empire lost their status as immediate subjects of the Emperor — that is, their Reichsunmittelbarkeit — and were absorbed ("mediatised") into a small number of larger neighbouring states, or secularised in the case of ecclesiastical territories. The number of German states fell from several hundred to a few dozen.

Background

Under the Treaty of Lunéville (1801), France annexed the west bank of the Rhine. German princes who lost territory there were promised compensation east of the Rhine, to be found from the Empire's ecclesiastical principalities — the archbishoprics, bishoprics and abbeys that covered large parts of Germany — and from the free imperial cities. The compensation plan was negotiated with heavy French and Russian involvement.

The Reichsdeputationshauptschluss of 1803

The Imperial Deputation's final recess (Reichsdeputationshauptschluss), adopted on 25 February 1803 and ratified by the Emperor, secularised virtually all ecclesiastical states: the archbishoprics of Cologne and Trier and most prince-bishoprics disappeared, their lands going to Prussia, Bavaria, Baden, Württemberg, Hanover and others. Of fifty-one free imperial cities, all but six (Augsburg, Lübeck, Nuremberg, Frankfurt, Bremen and Hamburg) were annexed by their neighbours. Over 100 small secular territories also lost immediacy, and some ten million people acquired new rulers.

Further mediatisation, 1806–1814

In 1806 Napoleon organised the Confederation of the Rhine; its founding act mediatised most of the remaining small principalities, counties and imperial knights' estates into the enlarged middle states such as Bavaria, Württemberg, Baden and Hesse-Darmstadt. On 6 August 1806 Emperor Francis II dissolved the Holy Roman Empire itself. The Congress of Vienna (1814–15) largely confirmed these changes, leaving a German Confederation of thirty-nine states.

Consequences

Mediatised princely houses, the Standesherren, retained significant privileges — equality of birth with reigning houses, seats in upper chambers, and certain legal immunities — well into the nineteenth century and formally until 1918. Mediatisation permanently simplified Germany's political geography, strengthened the middle states, and is regarded as a decisive step on the path toward German unification.
