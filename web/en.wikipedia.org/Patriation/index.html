Patriation

Patriation is the political process that led to full Canadian sovereignty, culminating with the Constitution Act, 1982. The process was necessary because the British North America Act, 1867, Canada's foundational constitutional document, had been enacted by the British Parliament and could be amended only by it. Patriation brought the amending power home and added the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms and a domestic amending formula.

The proclamation of the Constitution Act, 1982, in Ottawa.

Background

Although Canada gained effective independence with the Statute of Westminster 1931, the constitution remained a British statute because federal and provincial governments could not agree on a formula for amending it in Canada. Decades of federal–provincial conferences failed, until Prime Minister Pierre Trudeau's government pressed ahead in 1980, proposing to patriate the constitution unilaterally with a charter of rights. The Supreme Court of Canada ruled in the Patriation Reference (1981) that unilateral action was legal but contrary to constitutional convention without a substantial degree of provincial consent.

The 1982 settlement

After further negotiation, Ottawa reached agreement with nine of the ten provinces in November 1981 — the so-called Kitchen Accord — while Quebec's government did not sign on, an grievance that shaped Canadian politics for a generation. The British Parliament passed the Canada Act 1982, and Queen Elizabeth II proclaimed the Constitution Act, 1982 in Ottawa on 17 April 1982. The Act patriated the constitution, entrenched the Charter, recognized Aboriginal rights, and ended all British legislative authority over Canada.
