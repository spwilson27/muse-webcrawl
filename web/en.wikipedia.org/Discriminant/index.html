Discriminant

In mathematics, the discriminant of a polynomial is a quantity that depends on the coefficients and allows deducing some properties of the roots without computing them. It is defined as a polynomial function of the coefficients, equal (up to a factor) to the product of the squares of the differences of the roots. The discriminant is zero if and only if the polynomial has a multiple root.

The quadratic case

The best-known discriminant is that of the quadratic polynomial ax² + bx + c, namely b² − 4ac, the expression under the square root in the quadratic formula. Its sign determines the nature of the roots: if positive, the quadratic has two distinct real roots; if zero, one repeated real root; if negative, two complex conjugate roots.

General definition

For a polynomial of degree n with roots r1, …, rn and leading coefficient an, the discriminant is an2n−2 Π (ri − rj)² over all pairs i < j. It can be computed from the coefficients alone as (up to sign) the resultant of the polynomial and its derivative, commonly evaluated as a Sylvester determinant.

Uses

Discriminants appear throughout algebra and number theory: the discriminant of a number field, defined from an integral basis, measures ramification of primes and is a fundamental invariant in algebraic number theory. Discriminants also classify conic sections and quadratic forms, and in statistics, "discriminant analysis" is an unrelated technique that takes its name from the same idea of distinguishing cases.
