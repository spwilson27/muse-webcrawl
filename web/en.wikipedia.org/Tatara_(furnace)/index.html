A tatara (鑪) is the traditional Japanese clay furnace used to smelt iron sand and charcoal into steel, most famously the tamahagane used for Japanese swords. The word also refers to the foot bellows that originally supplied the furnace's air blast.

Process

A tatara furnace is a temporary clay structure roughly 1.1 metres tall and several metres long, built anew for each smelt. Over about three days and nights, workers alternately charge it with iron sand (satetsu) and charcoal while bellows maintain temperatures around 1,200–1,500 °C. Unlike a blast furnace, the iron never fully melts: it is reduced in the solid state, producing a spongy bloom called the kera, from which the furnace is broken open. The best portions of the bloom — high-carbon tamahagane — are selected by swordsmiths' suppliers, while the lower-grade pig iron (zuku) is used for other purposes.

History

Iron smelting in Japan developed from continental techniques by the mid-first millennium AD, and the tatara method flourished especially in the Chūgoku region, where high-grade iron sand was abundant. By the late 19th century, Western blast furnaces displaced the tatara commercially, and the last commercial operation closed in 1925. To preserve the craft, the Society for Preservation of Japanese Art Swords (NBTHK) built the Nittoho tatara in Shimane Prefecture in 1977, which still operates several weeks each winter to produce tamahagane for licensed swordsmiths.
