Melodrama

A melodrama is a dramatic work characterized by exaggerated plot, sensational events and strongly stereotyped characters — virtuous heroes, suffering heroines and villainous antagonists — designed to appeal directly to the emotions of the audience. The word derives from Greek melos (music) and French drame: originally it denoted plays interspersed with music, a form developed in late 18th-century France and Germany.

History

Jean-Jacques Rousseau's Pygmalion (1770) pioneered the spoken-word-with-music form. In 19th-century Britain, France and America, stage melodrama became the dominant popular theatre, with stock figures, moral polarization, spectacular stage effects and musical underscoring heightening every emotional beat; plays such as The Octoroon and stage adaptations of Uncle Tom's Cabin drew mass audiences. Critics such as Peter Brooks, in The Melodramatic Imagination (1976), argued melodrama is a central mode of modern narrative rather than a debased genre.

Film and television

Melodrama flourished in cinema: silent films, the "weepies" and family melodramas of Douglas Sirk and Vincente Minnelli in 1950s Hollywood, and the works of directors such as Pedro Almodóvar and Wong Kar-wai. Its descendants include soap operas and telenovelas. In everyday usage "melodramatic" is often pejorative, meaning overwrought or exaggerated emotional behaviour.
