Fugue

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In classical music, a fugue is a contrapuntal compositional technique in two or more voices (or "parts"), built on a short melody called the subject, which is introduced at the beginning in imitation in different vocal ranges and recurs frequently throughout the piece. The word derives from the Latin fuga, related to both fugere ("to flee") and fugare ("to chase"), evoking the way each voice chases the subject.

Structure

A fugue typically opens with an exposition in which each voice enters in turn with the subject: the first statement is the subject proper, the second — transposed, usually to the dominant — is the answer, accompanied by a countersubject in the first voice. Free episodes of sequential material separate later subject entries in various keys, and devices such as stretto (overlapping entries), inversion, augmentation and diminution are used to intensify the argument towards the close.

History

Fugal technique grew out of Renaissance imitation and the ricercar, and reached its zenith in the Baroque era with Johann Sebastian Bach, whose The Well-Tempered Clavier (48 preludes and fugues in all keys) and The Art of Fugue remain the supreme examples of the form. Later composers from Mozart and Beethoven to Shostakovich and Hindemith continued to write fugues, often as demonstrations of learned craftsmanship within larger works.
