The Kawi script is a Brahmic script originating in Java, used across much of maritime Southeast Asia from the 8th to the 16th centuries. It is an abugida, in which each consonant letter carries an inherent vowel (usually /a/) modified by diacritics. The script takes its name from the Kawi language (Old Javanese), in which a rich court literature—epic poems called kakawin—was written. It developed from the Pallava script of South India, carried to the archipelago by trade and the spread of Hinduism and Buddhism.

History

Early Kawi appears in Central Javanese inscriptions of the Mataram kingdom period; the Laguna Copperplate Inscription of 900 AD, found in Luzon in the Philippines, is the earliest known written document in the Philippines and is written in a Kawi-derived script with Old Malay text. As Javanese culture flourished under Majapahit, Kawi calligraphy became increasingly ornate. Over time, regional descendants of Kawi diversified into the Javanese (Hanacaraka) and Balinese scripts, the scripts of the Philippines such as Baybayin, and the Batak, Lontara, Rejang and other scripts of Sumatra and Sulawesi, collectively the Nusantara scripts.

Characteristics

Like other Brahmic scripts, Kawi writes consonant conjuncts by stacking or subjoining letters, uses a virama-like killer stroke (pangkon in later Javanese) to suppress the inherent vowel, and includes independent vowel signs, nasalisation marks and punctuation. Subscript forms of consonants are highly developed in later "quadrate" styles of East Javanese inscriptions. Kawi has been encoded in Unicode since version 15.0 (2022), in its own block (U+11F00–U+11F5F).
