Propofol

Propofol, sold under the brand name Diprivan among others, is a short-acting intravenous general anaesthetic used for the induction and maintenance of general anaesthesia, procedural sedation, and sedation of ventilated adults in intensive care. It is a milky-white lipid emulsion, sometimes informally called "milk of amnesia" for its appearance and amnesic effect.

Medical uses

Propofol causes rapid loss of consciousness — typically within 15 to 30 seconds of an intravenous bolus — and recovery is usually swift and clear-headed, with a low incidence of nausea compared with many other agents. It is widely used for induction of anaesthesia, maintenance infusions, sedation for procedures such as endoscopy, and long-term sedation in intensive care units. It has no analgesic properties.

Mechanism of action

Propofol is thought to act principally as a positive allosteric modulator of the GABAA receptor, enhancing inhibitory neurotransmission, and it may also modulate sodium channels and endocannabinoid signalling.

Adverse effects

Common side effects include pain on injection, dose-dependent respiratory depression and apnoea, and a fall in blood pressure. Rare but serious complications include propofol infusion syndrome, a potentially fatal metabolic disturbance associated with prolonged high-dose infusions, especially in children. Because it contains soybean oil and egg lecithin, care is taken with certain allergies. Propofol has potential for misuse due to its euphoric effects, and it achieved wide public notice in 2009 as a factor in the death of singer Michael Jackson.

History

Propofol was developed by Imperial Chemical Industries (ICI) in the 1970s; the emulsion formulation was introduced clinically in 1986. It is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines and is available as a generic medication.
