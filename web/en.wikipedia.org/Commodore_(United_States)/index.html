Commodore (United States)

Commodore
Country: United States
Service branch: U.S. Navy, U.S. Coast Guard
Status: rank used 1794–1899; title thereafter; briefly rank again 1982–83

Commodore was historically a rank in the United States Navy and United States Coast Guard, senior to captain and junior to rear admiral. It later survived as an honorary title for senior captains commanding squadrons, and briefly returned as a one-star rank in the 1980s before being replaced by rear admiral (lower half).

History

The Continental Navy used commodore as a title for officers commanding squadrons. Congress formally recognised the grade in the early republic, and commodores such as Stephen Decatur and Matthew C. Perry became celebrated figures; Perry held the title while opening Japan in 1853–54.

Abolition and revival

In 1899 the rank was abolished, with commodores promoted to rear admiral. During the Second World War the grade was temporarily revived for wartime service. In 1982 the one-star grade was renamed commodore admiral, then simply commodore, before Congress settled on rear admiral (lower half) in 1985.

Modern use

Today commodore remains a courtesy title for captains in command of flotillas, squadrons and training commands, flown with a broad pennant.
