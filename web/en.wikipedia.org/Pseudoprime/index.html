Pseudoprime

[Placeholder image: Chart of pseudoprimes among composite numbers]

A pseudoprime is a probable prime that is not actually prime. Pseudoprimes are classified by the property of primes they satisfy. The most important class is the Fermat pseudoprimes: composite numbers n such that an−1 ≡ 1 (mod n) for some base a coprime to n, imitating the conclusion of Fermat's little theorem. The smallest Fermat pseudoprime to base 2 is 341 = 11 × 31.

Fermat pseudoprimes and Carmichael numbers

A composite number that is a Fermat pseudoprime to every base coprime to it is called a Carmichael number. The smallest is 561 = 3 × 11 × 17. Carmichael numbers are rare but infinite in number, as proved by Alford, Granville, and Pomerance in 1994. Because Carmichael numbers defeat the plain Fermat primality test from every base, stronger tests were developed.

Other types

Euler pseudoprimes satisfy a congruence derived from Euler's criterion; strong pseudoprimes satisfy the conditions of the Miller–Rabin test, which writes n − 1 as 2sd and checks a chain of square roots of 1 modulo n. No composite is a strong pseudoprime to all bases, and combining a few bases makes the Miller–Rabin test deterministic over practical ranges: for example, testing the first twelve prime bases is exact for all numbers below 3 × 1023. Other families include Perrin pseudoprimes, Catalan pseudoprimes, and elliptic pseudoprimes.

Applications

Pseudoprimes matter in cryptography, where fast probabilistic primality tests (Miller–Rabin, Baillie–PSW) are used to generate large prime numbers for RSA and related systems. The Baillie–PSW test, combining a strong probable-prime test with a Lucas test, has no known counterexample despite decades of searching and substantial prizes offered for one.
