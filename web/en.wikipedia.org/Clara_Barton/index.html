Clara Barton, founder and first president of the American Red Cross.

Clarissa Harlowe "Clara" Barton (December 25, 1821 – April 12, 1912) was an American nurse and humanitarian who founded the American Red Cross. She is best known for organizing relief for soldiers of the American Civil War — service that earned her the name "Angel of the Battlefield" — and for leading the organization's disaster relief work for more than two decades.

Early life and Civil War service

Born in Oxford, Massachusetts, Barton worked as a schoolteacher — founding a free school in Bordentown, New Jersey — and then as a clerk in the United States Patent Office in Washington, one of the first women to hold a significant federal clerkship. When the Civil War began in 1861, Barton collected and distributed medical supplies to Union soldiers and, by 1862, was bringing supplies directly to battlefields including Cedar Mountain, Second Bull Run, and Antietam, where she famously held a lantern for surgeons operating through the night. She later directed nursing at the Siege of Petersburg and ran the Office of Correspondence with the Friends of the Missing Men of the United States Army, answering tens of thousands of inquiries and helping to identify more than 20,000 missing soldiers; her work led to the marking of some 13,000 graves at Andersonville prison.

The American Red Cross

While convalescing in Europe in 1869–1873, Barton worked with the International Committee of the Red Cross during the Franco-Prussian War and resolved to bring the movement to the United States. After years of lobbying, she founded the American Association of the Red Cross in Dansville, New York, on May 21, 1881, and persuaded Congress to charter it and the United States to accede to the Geneva Convention in 1882. Barton expanded the society's mission beyond war relief to peacetime disaster response — an innovation known as the "American Amendment" — leading relief after the Johnstown Flood of 1889, the Galveston hurricane of 1900, and famine and war abroad.

Later life

Criticized for her autocratic management and lax record-keeping, Barton resigned the presidency in 1904 under pressure. She spent her last years in Glen Echo, Maryland, where her home is now a National Historic Site. She died in 1912, aged 90. Barton was inducted into the National Women's Hall of Fame in 1973, and schools, hospitals, and the humanitarian Clara Barton Medal commemorate her name.
