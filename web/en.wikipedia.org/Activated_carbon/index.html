Activated carbon, also called activated charcoal, is a form of carbon processed to have small, low-volume pores that increase the surface area available for adsorption or chemical reactions. One gram of activated carbon can have a surface area in excess of 3,000 m² as measured by gas adsorption, though typical commercial grades range from 500 to 1,500 m²/g.

Production

Activated carbon is produced from carbonaceous source materials such as coconut husks, wood, coal and peat. Production involves carbonisation (pyrolysis in the absence of oxygen) followed by activation. In physical activation the char is oxidised with steam or carbon dioxide at high temperature, etching out a network of micropores. In chemical activation the raw material is impregnated with phosphoric acid, potassium hydroxide or zinc chloride and heated at lower temperatures.

Properties and classification

The pore structure comprises micropores (<2 nm), mesopores (2–50 nm) and macropores (>50 nm). Adsorption capacity is commonly characterised by the iodine number; molasses and methylene blue numbers describe larger-pore performance. Products are sold as powdered (PAC), granular (GAC), extruded pellets, or impregnated carbons carrying reagents such as silver or sulphur for specific chemisorption tasks.

Applications

Major uses include municipal drinking-water treatment (removing taste, odour and micropollutants), wastewater treatment, air purification and solvent recovery, gas-mask and respirator filters, sugar and beverage decolourisation, gold recovery from cyanide solutions, and catalysis supports. In medicine, activated charcoal is administered orally as an emergency treatment for certain poisonings and drug overdoses, adsorbing toxins in the gastrointestinal tract; it is on the WHO List of Essential Medicines.

Reactivation

Spent granular carbon can be thermally reactivated in furnaces, burning off adsorbed contaminants and restoring most of its capacity, with a typical loss of 5–15% of the material per cycle.
