A metamaterial (from the Greek word meta, meaning "beyond" or "after", and the Latin materia, meaning "material") is a material engineered to have a property not usually found in naturally occurring materials. They are made from assemblies of multiple elements fashioned from composite materials such as metals and plastics, usually arranged in repeating patterns at scales smaller than the wavelengths of the phenomena they influence.

Negative index materials

The Russian physicist Victor Veselago predicted in 1967 that a material with simultaneously negative electric permittivity and magnetic permeability would exhibit a negative refractive index, reversing phenomena such as refraction and the Doppler effect. Around 2000, John Pendry proposed practical designs using split-ring resonators and thin wires, and David R. Smith's group demonstrated such a left-handed material at microwave frequencies.

Applications

Metamaterials underpin proposals for invisibility cloaks, superlenses that beat the diffraction limit, compact antennas, and novel acoustic and seismic shielding. Related research areas include photonic crystals, plasmonics, and transformation optics. Mechanical metamaterials, with designed properties such as negative Poisson's ratio (auxetics), are also an active field.
