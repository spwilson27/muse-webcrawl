Public opinion, or popular opinion, is the collective opinion on a specific topic or the aggregate views of a society — the combined views of a defined population, such as the citizens of a state. It plays a central role in democratic theory, in which governments are expected to be responsive to the preferences of the public, and it is studied by political scientists, sociologists and communication scholars.

History of the concept

The term arose in 18th-century France (opinion publique) with the growth of a literate bourgeois public sphere of salons, coffeehouses and newspapers. Walter Lippmann's Public Opinion (1922) argued that citizens respond to a mediated "pseudo-environment" of stereotypes rather than the world itself. Jürgen Habermas described the "structural transformation of the public sphere", and Elisabeth Noelle-Neumann proposed the "spiral of silence", in which fear of isolation suppresses minority views.

Formation

Opinions are shaped by family and socialisation, education, group identities, economic interest, events, and the media. Agenda-setting theory holds that media influence what the public thinks about; framing and priming influence the standards by which issues and leaders are judged. Political elites, parties and interest groups campaign to move opinion, while opinion also constrains elites.

Measurement

Scientific opinion polling, developed in the 1930s by George Gallup and others, uses representative samples and survey questions to estimate the distribution of views, with a stated margin of error. Modern challenges include declining response rates, telephone-to-online transitions, question-wording effects, and occasional systematic polling errors in elections. Other measures include referendums, by-elections, letters and social-media analysis.
