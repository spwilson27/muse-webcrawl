Ubik is a 1969 science fiction novel by American writer Philip K. Dick. Set in a future 1992 where psychic powers are commonplace and commercialised, and where the recently dead can be kept in "half-life," a state of suspended consciousness permitting limited communication with the living, the novel follows employees of a "prudence organisation" who, after an explosion apparently kills their boss, find their reality regressing to earlier decades and their colleagues dying one by one. Time magazine named Ubik one of the hundred best English-language novels published since 1923.

Plot

Glen Runciter runs Runciter Associates, which employs "inertials" — people whose talents neutralise those of telepaths and precognitives used for corporate espionage. Hired by the reclusive businessman Stanton Mick, Runciter assembles his eleven best inertials, including newcomer Pat Conley, whose talent is altering the past, for a mission to Luna. There Mick turns out to be an android bomb: the explosion apparently kills Runciter, and the team rushes his body to a moratorium in Zurich to place him in half-life.

Back on Earth, team member Joe Chip finds reality deteriorating around him: coins and telephones revert to obsolete models, food spoils instantly, and messages from Runciter appear on television and in graffiti. The inertials begin dying, withered like mummies, starting with those who stay behind alone. As the world regresses toward 1939, Joe learns that the group is itself in half-life — killed in the explosion — and is being sustained, and preyed upon, within the mind of a powerful half-lifer. The mysterious product Ubik, advertised at the head of every chapter in a different form, is the force that can hold their world together.

Themes

Ubik is a quintessential Dick work on the instability of reality: it is never resolved whether the characters are dead, dreaming, or manipulated, and the final chapter twists the ground once more, showing Runciter finding coins bearing Joe Chip's face. Critics read Ubik itself variously as a metaphor for God, for consumer capitalism's omnipresence (its name derives from the Latin ubique, "everywhere"), or for narrative faith itself. The novel also satirises a pay-for-everything future in which doors, refrigerators and showers charge coins per use.

Reception and adaptation

Though not a major commercial success on publication, Ubik has become one of Dick's most acclaimed novels, praised for its narrative construction and metaphysical vertigo. Dick wrote a screenplay adaptation in 1974, published posthumously, which was never filmed; director Michel Gondry was attached to a film version in the 2010s that did not proceed. The novel's regression-of-reality premise and the Ubik device have been widely influential in science fiction and popular culture.
