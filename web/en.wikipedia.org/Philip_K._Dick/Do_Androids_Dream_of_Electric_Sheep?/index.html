Do Androids Dream of Electric Sheep? is a 1968 dystopian science fiction novel by American writer Philip K. Dick. Set in a post-apocalyptic San Francisco in 2021 (1992 in earlier editions), after a global nuclear war has devastated the Earth and driven most of humanity to colonies on other planets, the novel follows bounty hunter Rick Deckard over a single day as he hunts six escaped Nexus-6 androids. It served as the primary basis for Ridley Scott's film Blade Runner (1982).

Plot

After World War Terminus, radioactive dust has killed most animals and damaged many humans. Owning a real animal is a status symbol; Deckard owns only an electric sheep. Offered a bounty large enough to buy a genuine animal, he accepts the assignment to retire six Nexus-6 androids — a new model nearly indistinguishable from humans, detectable only by the Voigt-Kampff empathy test, since androids lack empathy. His encounters with the android opera singer Luba Luft, whom he hesitates to kill, and with Rachael Rosen of the Rosen Association, blur the moral line of his work. In parallel, "special" (radiation-damaged) John R. Isidore shelters the remaining androids in his empty apartment building. Deckard retires all six androids in one day, finds a toad — supposedly extinct — in the desert, and returns home to learn it is artificial, accepting that its falsity does not entirely negate what it meant to him.

Themes

The novel explores what it means to be human, focusing on empathy as the defining human trait — institutionalised in the religion of Mercerism, whose followers fuse empathically with the suffering figure Wilbur Mercer through "empathy boxes." It examines the moral status of artificial beings, the value of authentic versus simulated life (real animals versus electric ones, genuine emotion versus mood-organ settings), decay and entropy, and the instability of identity and reality, recurrent concerns across Dick's fiction.

Publication and reception

Published by Doubleday in 1968, the novel was shortlisted for the Nebula Award. It received modest attention on release but grew in stature with Dick's reputation and, especially, with the film adaptation, after which editions were frequently reissued under the Blade Runner title. The novel differs markedly from the film: the setting is San Francisco rather than Los Angeles, Mercerism and mood organs are absent from the film, and Deckard is married and unambiguously human in the book's terms, though morally compromised by his work.

Adaptations and sequels

Beyond the 1982 film and its 2017 sequel, the novel has been adapted for radio (BBC Radio 4, 2014) and as a graphic novel. Dick's friend K. W. Jeter wrote three authorised sequel novels, Blade Runner 2: The Edge of Human (1995), Blade Runner 3: Replicant Night (1996) and Blade Runner 4: Eye and Talon (2000), bridging the novel and the film.
