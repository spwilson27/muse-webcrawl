Philip K. Dick, whose fiction explored the nature of reality.

Philip Kindred Dick (December 16, 1928 – March 2, 1982), often referred to by his initials PKD, was an American science fiction writer and novelist. He wrote 44 novels and about 121 short stories, most of which appeared in science fiction magazines during his lifetime. His fiction explored varied philosophical and social questions such as the nature of reality, perception, human nature, and identity, and commonly featured characters struggling in rudderless, surreal or altered environments.

Life and career

Born in Chicago and raised mainly in California, Dick sold his first story in 1951 and wrote full-time from 1952. His alternate-history novel The Man in the High Castle (1962) won the Hugo Award for Best Novel. Do Androids Dream of Electric Sheep? (1968) became the basis of the film Blade Runner (1982). Other major works include Martian Time-Slip (1964), Ubik (1969), Flow My Tears, the Policeman Said (1974), A Scanner Darkly (1977) and the VALIS trilogy. Despite critical esteem, Dick struggled financially for most of his life, troubled by drug use and mental illness.

The "2-3-74" experiences

In February and March 1974 Dick experienced a series of visions and revelations — anamnesis, an encounter with what he called VALIS (Vast Active Living Intelligence System) — which he spent the rest of his life interpreting in an 8,000-page journal, the Exegesis. These experiences permeate his late fiction.

Legacy

Dick died in 1982, shortly before the release of Blade Runner. His reputation has grown steadily since: films adapted from his work include Total Recall, Minority Report and A Scanner Darkly, and the television series The Man in the High Castle. The Philip K. Dick Award is given annually for distinguished science fiction published in paperback. In 2007 he became the first science fiction writer included in the Library of America series.
