Revisionism (Marxism)

Revisionism, in the context of Marxism, is a term used pejoratively by orthodox Marxists for views that revise, modify or abandon fundamental Marxist principles such as the necessity of revolution and the dictatorship of the proletariat. The term was first widely used in the debates of German social democracy in the 1890s.

Origins

Eduard Bernstein, a leading theorist of the Social Democratic Party of Germany, argued in Evolutionary Socialism (1899) that capitalism was not collapsing as Marx predicted, that the middle classes were not disappearing, and that socialists should pursue reforms through parliamentary democracy. Rosa Luxemburg answered in Reform or Revolution (1900), defending revolutionary goals, and Vladimir Lenin made anti-revisionism central to Bolshevik ideology.

Later usage

During the Cold War, "revisionist" was applied to Yugoslavia's market socialism, to Khrushchev's de-Stalinization by Maoist critics, and to Eurocommunism. In Chinese Communist discourse, the Soviet Union after Stalin was denounced as revisionist, one cause of the Sino-Soviet split. The term remains a common accusation within Marxist movements against opponents accused of diluting doctrine.
