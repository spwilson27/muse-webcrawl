Sir James Dewar FRS (20 September 1842 – 27 March 1923) was a Scottish chemist and physicist celebrated for his pioneering work at very low temperatures and for inventing the vacuum flask that bears his name. He held the posts of Jacksonian Professor of Natural Philosophy at Cambridge and Fullerian Professor of Chemistry at the Royal Institution in London simultaneously.

Low-temperature research

Dewar's cryogenic programme at the Royal Institution made him a leading rival in the international race to liquefy the so-called permanent gases. In 1898 he became the first to liquefy hydrogen (boiling point about −253 °C), using regenerative cooling and his vacuum-insulated vessels to store the liquid; the following year he solidified hydrogen, reaching roughly 14 kelvin, then the lowest temperature attained. He studied the properties of matter at these extremes — the disappearance of chemical reactivity, changes in electrical resistance, phosphorescence, and the absorptive power of charcoal at low temperatures, which he applied to producing high vacua and to gas analysis.

The Dewar flask

In 1892 Dewar devised a double-walled glass vessel with the space between the walls evacuated and the surfaces silvered to cut radiative transfer — the Dewar flask. He did not patent it for domestic use; German glassblowers commercialised it as the Thermos flask, and Dewar lost the ensuing court case, a famous missed fortune. In science, "dewars" remain the standard vessels for storing cryogens such as liquid nitrogen and helium.

Other work

With Frederick Abel, Dewar developed cordite, the smokeless propellant adopted by the British military — an invention that led to a priority dispute with Alfred Nobel over ballistite. He worked extensively in spectroscopy and on the chemistry of the atmosphere, and his spectacular Royal Institution lectures made low-temperature phenomena famous to the public. Despite his achievements he never received a Nobel Prize, an omission often attributed to his abrasive relations with rivals and committees.
