Sectarian violence

Sectarian violence or sectarian strife is a form of communal violence which is perpetrated against a group of people on the basis of their identity as members of a particular religious, ethnic, or political sect. Unlike civil war or terrorism conducted by organized states and groups, sectarian violence often involves rioting, lynching, and attacks on places of worship carried out by mobs or paramilitary organizations.

Causes

Scholars attribute sectarian violence to a combination of competition over resources, political manipulation of identity, historical grievances, and the collapse of state authority. Political entrepreneurs may inflame sectarian tensions to consolidate power or distract from economic grievances.

Examples

Notable instances include the violence between Protestants and Catholics in Northern Ireland during the Troubles, Sunni–Shia violence in Iraq following the 2003 invasion, anti-Sikh riots in India in 1984, communal violence between Hindus and Muslims in South Asia, and Buddhist–Muslim violence in Myanmar.

Consequences

Sectarian violence tends to harden group identities, displace populations, and create cycles of retaliation that can persist for generations, making reconciliation and power-sharing arrangements difficult.
