Marginal value theorem

The marginal value theorem (MVT) is an optimality model in behavioural ecology that predicts how an animal foraging in a patchy environment should decide when to leave a food patch it is depleting in order to maximise its long-term rate of food (energy) intake. The theorem was published by Eric Charnov in 1976 and is one of the foundational results of optimal foraging theory.

The model

The model imagines a forager moving between discrete patches of food separated by travel time. Within a patch, the forager's cumulative food gain rises with time spent there but at a diminishing rate, because the patch is progressively depleted. The forager's overall rate of intake depends both on the gains within patches and on the time lost travelling between them.

The theorem states that the optimal strategy is to leave a patch when the instantaneous (marginal) rate of gain within the patch falls to the average rate of gain obtainable from the environment as a whole (including travel time). Graphically, the optimal residence time is found by drawing a line from the point representing travel time, tangent to the cumulative gain curve; the slope of that line is the maximum achievable average intake rate, and the point of tangency gives the optimal time to leave.

Predictions

  • Foragers should stay longer in each patch when travel time between patches is long, and leave sooner when patches are close together.
  • Foragers should stay longer in high-quality patches than in low-quality ones.
  • All patches should be left at the same marginal intake rate — the "giving-up" rate equals the environmental average.

Tests and applications

The MVT has been tested in many species, including great tits gathering food for nestlings, starlings, bees, parasitoid wasps, and dung flies. Animals often qualitatively follow its predictions — staying longer when travel is costly and in richer patches — though they commonly stay somewhat longer than the strict optimum, leading researchers to refine the model with factors such as predation risk, nutrient constraints, incomplete information about patch quality, and future reproductive value. The same marginal logic has been applied outside ecology, including to information foraging by humans (when to leave a website or document), attention, and even cell biology, wherever a diminishing-return resource is exploited in patches.
