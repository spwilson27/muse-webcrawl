The Zollverein (pronounced [ˈtsɔlfɛɐ̯ˌʔaɪn]), or German Customs Union, was a coalition of German states formed to manage customs and economic policies within their territories. Officially established on 1 January 1834 under the Zollverein treaties of 1833, it removed internal tariff barriers between most German states while maintaining a common external tariff. Organised under Prussian leadership, it is widely regarded as a decisive step toward the economic — and eventually political — unification of Germany.

Background

After the Napoleonic Wars, the German Confederation contained dozens of sovereign states, each levying its own customs duties, which severely burdened trade. Prussia's tariff law of 1818 unified its own scattered territories and made its tariff relatively liberal. Through the 1820s Prussia concluded agreements with neighbouring enclave states, gradually building a northern customs system, while Bavaria and Württemberg formed a rival southern union in 1828. Negotiations merged these systems into the Zollverein in 1833–34.

Development and significance

By the 1840s the union encompassed most German states, though Austria remained outside — a point of major political significance, since it strengthened the Prussian-led "kleindeutsch" (lesser German) solution to the German question. Common weights, measures and eventually a common currency convention (the Zollzentner and later the Vereinsthaler) followed. Historians debate the union's direct economic effects, but it created an integrated market of some 26 million people and accustomed German states to cooperation under Prussian direction. After the Austro-Prussian War of 1866 the Zollverein was reorganised with a Customs Parliament (Zollparlament) in 1868, and its functions were absorbed into the German Empire in 1871.
