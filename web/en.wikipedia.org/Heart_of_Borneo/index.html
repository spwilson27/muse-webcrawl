The Heart of Borneo is a conservation agreement and initiative covering some 220,000 square kilometres of equatorial rainforest in the centre of the island of Borneo, spanning the territories of Brunei, Indonesia and Malaysia. Formally launched by a trilateral declaration at Bali on 12 February 2007, it commits the three governments to jointly manage one of the planet's most biodiverse regions, protecting watersheds, endangered species and indigenous livelihoods while allowing sustainable development.

Geography and biodiversity

The area takes in the upland forests of the central mountain ranges (including the Müller, Schwaner and Iran ranges), the headwaters of Borneo's great rivers — the Kapuas, Mahakam, Barito and Rajang — and adjoining lowland rainforest. The forests harbour an extraordinary richness of life: they are home to Bornean orangutans, clouded leopards, pygmy elephants, proboscis monkeys and the Sumatran rhinoceros's last Bornean populations (now considered extinct in the wild there). Surveys have repeatedly discovered species new to science; WWF reported more than 120 new species found in the area between 2007 and 2010 alone, and around 360 in the decade from the mid-1990s.

The initiative

The Heart of Borneo initiative, championed by WWF from 2003, proposed a single transboundary conservation and sustainable-development framework rather than fragmented national programmes. The 2007 declaration established national working groups and a shared vision centred on protected areas, sustainable forestry and plantation management, ecotourism, and the rights of local and indigenous communities such as the Dayak peoples.

Threats and challenges

Despite the declaration, the region faces continuing pressure from logging, conversion to oil palm plantations, mining, infrastructure and forest fires, as well as illegal wildlife trade. Implementation has been uneven: protected-area coverage and connectivity have improved, but deforestation in surrounding lowlands remains high, and the initiative's voluntary nature limits enforcement. Nonetheless it is frequently cited as a model of large-scale, multi-country landscape conservation.
