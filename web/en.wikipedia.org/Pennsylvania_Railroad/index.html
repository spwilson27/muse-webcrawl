Pennsylvania Railroad

The Pennsylvania Railroad (reporting mark PRR), legally the Pennsylvania Railroad Company, also known as the "Pennsy", was an American Class I railroad chartered in 1846. For much of its existence it was the largest transportation enterprise in the world, the biggest railroad by traffic and revenue, and at its peak operated about 16,774 km (10,423 miles) of track.

History

Built to link Philadelphia with Pittsburgh and the west, the PRR crossed the Allegheny Mountains at the celebrated Horseshoe Curve (1854). Under presidents such as J. Edgar Thomson and Alexander Cassatt it expanded into a system reaching New York, Washington, Chicago, and St. Louis, built New York's Pennsylvania Station (1910) with tunnels under the Hudson and East rivers, and electrified its busiest eastern main lines, operated by locomotives such as the iconic GG1. The railroad famously paid dividends for a century and styled itself the "Standard Railroad of the World".

Decline and merger

Postwar competition from highways and airlines eroded traffic. On 1 February 1968 the PRR merged with its arch-rival, the New York Central, to form Penn Central — a merger that ended in the largest corporate bankruptcy in U.S. history to that date in 1970. Its viable lines passed to Conrail in 1976 and Amtrak, and its New York–Washington corridor remains the busiest passenger railroad in the United States.
