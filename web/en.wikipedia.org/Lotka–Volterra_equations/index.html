Lotka–Volterra equations

The Lotka–Volterra equations, also known as the predator–prey equations, are a pair of first-order nonlinear differential equations frequently used to describe the dynamics of biological systems in which two species interact, one as a predator and the other as prey. They were proposed independently by Alfred J. Lotka in 1925 and Vito Volterra in 1926.

The equations

Let x be the population density of the prey and y that of the predator. The prey growth rate is reduced in proportion to encounters with predators, while predator growth increases with the food consumed and declines through natural mortality:

dx/dt = αx − βxy

dy/dt = δxy − γy

Here α is the intrinsic prey growth rate, β the predation rate coefficient, δ the rate at which predators increase by consuming prey, and γ the predator mortality rate.

Behaviour

Solutions of the system are periodic: prey numbers rise, allowing predator numbers to increase after a lag; heavy predation then reduces the prey population, starving the predators, and the cycle repeats. The equations were motivated in part by Volterra's analysis of fish catches in the Adriatic Sea during and after the First World War, when reduced fishing led to a greater proportion of predatory fish. The model omits environmental limits such as carrying capacity, which later generalisations incorporated.
