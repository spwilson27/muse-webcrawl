Art Nouveau (French for "New Art") is an international style of art, architecture and applied art, especially the decorative arts. It was most popular between 1890 and 1910 during the Belle Époque in Europe and the United States. A reaction against the academic art and historicism of the 19th century, it was inspired by natural forms and structures, particularly the curved lines of plants and flowers, and by Japanese art.

Characteristics

Art Nouveau is characterised by the "whiplash" curve — sinuous, asymmetrical lines based on plant stems, tendrils and insects — and by the ambition to create a total work of art (Gesamtkunstwerk) uniting architecture, furniture, glassware, jewellery and graphic design. New materials such as cast iron and glass were used to flood interiors with light.

Regional forms

In Brussels, Victor Horta's Hôtel Tassel (1893) is often cited as the first Art Nouveau building; in Paris, Hector Guimard designed the famous Métro entrances. In Barcelona, Catalan Modernisme produced the works of Antoni Gaudí and Lluís Domènech i Montaner. The Vienna Secession (Gustav Klimt, Josef Hoffmann, Otto Wagner), the German Jugendstil, and the Glasgow style of Charles Rennie Mackintosh were parallel movements. Alphonse Mucha's posters in Paris and Louis Comfort Tiffany's lamps and glass in New York defined the style in graphic and decorative arts.

By the outbreak of the First World War, Art Nouveau had fallen from fashion and was succeeded by Art Deco and modernism; interest revived strongly in the later 20th century.
