Cardiac tamponade

Cardiac tamponade is a life-threatening condition in which fluid accumulating in the pericardial sac compresses the heart and prevents it from filling properly, causing a fall in cardiac output and obstructive shock. It may develop rapidly after trauma or slowly from medical causes.

Causes

Causes include penetrating or blunt chest trauma, rupture of the heart or aorta, cancer involving the pericardium, pericarditis, complications of cardiac surgery or catheter procedures, kidney failure (uraemic pericarditis), and hypothyroidism.

Signs and diagnosis

The classic Beck's triad — hypotension, distended neck veins, and muffled heart sounds — is present in only a minority of patients. Other features include tachycardia, breathlessness, and pulsus paradoxus (an exaggerated fall in blood pressure on inspiration). Echocardiography is the key investigation, showing the effusion and collapse of the right-sided chambers in diastole.

Treatment

Tamponade is an emergency. Definitive treatment is drainage of the pericardial fluid, by needle pericardiocentesis (usually ultrasound-guided), a surgical pericardial window, or emergency thoracotomy in traumatic arrest. Intravenous fluids may temporarily support filling pressures while drainage is arranged.
