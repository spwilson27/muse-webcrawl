Twenty-fifth Amendment to the United States Constitution

The Twenty-fifth Amendment to the United States Constitution deals with presidential succession and disability. Proposed by Congress on July 6, 1965, it was ratified on February 10, 1967. It clarifies that the vice president becomes president if the president dies, resigns, or is removed from office, and establishes procedures for filling a vacancy in the office of the vice president. It also provides for the temporary transfer of the president's powers and duties to the vice president as acting president, either on the president's own initiative or when the vice president and a majority of the Cabinet determine the president is unable to serve.

Sections

Section 1 provides that in case of the president's removal, death, or resignation, the vice president shall become president — resolving the ambiguity left by the original text of Article II, under which John Tyler had asserted, controversially in 1841, that he became president rather than merely acting president.

Section 2 provides that whenever there is a vacancy in the vice presidency, the president shall nominate a vice president who takes office upon confirmation by a majority vote of both houses of Congress. This section was used twice in the 1970s: Gerald Ford was appointed vice president in 1973 after Spiro Agnew resigned, and Nelson Rockefeller was appointed in 1974 after Ford succeeded Richard Nixon as president.

Section 3 allows the president to voluntarily transfer power to the vice president by written declaration, and to resume power by a second declaration. It has been invoked for brief periods when presidents underwent medical procedures under anesthesia, including by Ronald Reagan in 1985, George W. Bush in 2002 and 2007, and Joe Biden in 2021.

Section 4 provides for involuntary transfer: the vice president and a majority of the Cabinet (or another body Congress designates) may declare the president unable to discharge the powers and duties of the office, whereupon the vice president becomes acting president. If the president disputes the declaration, Congress decides, requiring a two-thirds vote of both houses to keep the vice president in power as acting president. Section 4 has never been invoked.

Background

The amendment was spurred by recurring crises: the assassination of John F. Kennedy in 1963, which left Lyndon B. Johnson — who had himself suffered a heart attack — without a vice president for over a year; Dwight D. Eisenhower's illnesses in the 1950s; and the historical precedents of James Garfield's lingering incapacitation in 1881 and Woodrow Wilson's disabling stroke in 1919. Senator Birch Bayh of Indiana was the amendment's principal sponsor in Congress.
