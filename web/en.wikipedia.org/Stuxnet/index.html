Stuxnet

Stuxnet is a malicious computer worm first uncovered in 2010 and thought to have been in development since at least 2005. Stuxnet targets supervisory control and data acquisition (SCADA) systems and is believed to be responsible for causing substantial damage to the nuclear program of Iran. Although neither country has openly admitted responsibility, the worm is widely believed to be a cyberweapon built jointly by the United States and Israel in a collaborative effort known as Operation Olympic Games.

Mechanism

Stuxnet is unusually complex malware. It spreads via USB drives and Windows networks, exploiting multiple zero-day vulnerabilities, and uses stolen digital certificates to appear legitimate. While it infects ordinary PCs harmlessly, it activates only on systems running Siemens Step7 software connected to particular configurations of programmable logic controllers driving variable-frequency drives — the equipment used to control uranium enrichment centrifuges at Natanz.

Once active, the worm periodically spun centrifuges at damaging speeds while replaying recorded normal sensor data to operators, concealing the sabotage. Analysts estimate that roughly 1,000 centrifuges — about a tenth of those at Natanz — were destroyed.

Discovery and significance

The worm was identified in June 2010 by the Belarusian security firm VirusBlokAda after it began causing crashes on computers outside its target environment. Stuxnet is considered the first discovered cyberweapon designed to physically destroy industrial equipment, and its code — later analysed by firms including Symantec and Kaspersky Lab — has been described as a "game changer" that demonstrated the feasibility of state-sponsored cyber-physical sabotage. Related platforms, such as Duqu and Flame, were subsequently discovered.
