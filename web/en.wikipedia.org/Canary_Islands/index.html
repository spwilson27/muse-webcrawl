Canary Islands

The Canary Islands (Spanish: Canarias) are a Spanish archipelago in the Atlantic Ocean, off the coast of north-west Africa. The seven main islands are Tenerife, Fuerteventura, Gran Canaria, Lanzarote, La Palma, La Gomera, and El Hierro. The archipelago is an autonomous community of Spain and, as an outermost region of the European Union, is known for its mild subtropical climate and large tourism industry.

History

The islands were inhabited by the Guanches, an Indigenous people of probable Berber origin, before European conquest. Castile conquered the islands during the 15th century, completing the conquest with Tenerife in 1496. The islands became an important stopover on Spanish voyages to the Americas. Their name derives from the Latin Insula Canaria, "Island of the Dogs", not from the canary bird (which was named after the islands).

Geography and nature

All the islands are volcanic in origin. Tenerife's Mount Teide is Spain's highest peak and one of the world's largest volcanoes. The islands host distinctive ecosystems, including laurel forests and endemic species, protected in four national parks such as Teide National Park (a UNESCO World Heritage Site) and Garajonay National Park. The archipelago's observatories on Tenerife and La Palma are among the world's leading astronomical sites.
