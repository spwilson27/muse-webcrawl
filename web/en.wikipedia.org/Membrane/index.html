A membrane is a selective barrier; it allows some things to pass through but stops others. Such things may be molecules, ions, or other small particles. Membranes can be generally classified into synthetic membranes and biological membranes. Biological membranes include cell membranes (outer coverings of cells or organelles that allow passage of certain constituents); nuclear membranes, which cover a cell nucleus; and tissue membranes, such as mucosae and serosae. Synthetic membranes are made by humans for use in laboratories and industry (such as chemical plants).

The concept of a membrane has been important since the eighteenth century but only developed widely since the Second World War. Based on this usage, most membranes can be described by a combination of a molecule sieve and a pore flow or solution diffusion model.

Biological membranes

A biological membrane, biomembrane or cell membrane is a selectively permeable membrane that separates the interior of a cell from the outside environment or creates intracellular compartments. Biological membranes, in the form of eukaryotic cell membranes, consist of a phospholipid bilayer with embedded, integral and peripheral proteins used in communication and transportation of chemicals and ions. The bulk of lipids in a cell membrane provides a fluid matrix for proteins to rotate and laterally diffuse for physiological functioning. Proteins are adapted to high membrane fluidity environment of the lipid bilayer with the presence of an annular lipid shell, consisting of lipid molecules bound tightly to the surface of integral membrane proteins.

The fluid mosaic model, proposed by S. J. Singer and Garth L. Nicolson in 1972, describes the cell membrane as a two-dimensional liquid in which phospholipid molecules form a bilayer and proteins are embedded within it, free to move laterally like tiles in a mosaic.

Membrane transport

Movement across membranes occurs by several mechanisms:

  • Passive transport — diffusion and osmosis move substances down a concentration gradient without energy input.
  • Facilitated diffusion — channel and carrier proteins allow specific molecules to cross.
  • Active transport — pumps such as the sodium–potassium pump move substances against their gradients using ATP.
  • Vesicular transport — endocytosis and exocytosis move bulk material in membrane-bound vesicles.

Synthetic membranes

Synthetic membranes are used in filtration and separation processes including reverse osmosis for desalination, ultrafiltration in food processing, dialysis in medicine, and gas separation. Common materials include polymers such as polysulfone and polyamide, ceramics, and metals. Their performance is characterised by flux, selectivity, and resistance to fouling.
