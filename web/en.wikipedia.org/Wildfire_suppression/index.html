Wildfire suppression

Wildfire suppression is the range of firefighting tactics used to suppress wildfires. Firefighting efforts in wildland areas require different techniques, equipment, and personnel than structural firefighting in populated areas. Working in conjunction with specially designed aerial firefighting aircraft, trained wildland crews attempt to contain and extinguish fires while minimizing risk to life, property, and the environment.

History

In the United States, the devastating fires of 1910 — the "Big Blowup," which burned some three million acres in Idaho, Montana, and Washington and killed 87 people — galvanized a policy of total fire suppression. For much of the 20th century the U.S. Forest Service pursued a policy of extinguishing all fires as quickly as possible, famously aiming by the 1930s to have every fire out by 10 a.m. the day after detection.

Methods

Suppression ranges from direct attack at the fire's edge with hand tools, engines, and water, to indirect attack using bulldozed or hand-cut firelines, natural barriers, and backburning (burnout operations) to remove fuel ahead of the fire. Aircraft drop water and long-term retardant. Specialized personnel include smokejumpers, who parachute into remote fires, and hotshot crews. Modern fire management increasingly recognizes the ecological role of fire, and managers may monitor some lightning-caused fires under a "let burn" strategy, while prescribed burning is used to reduce hazardous fuels.
