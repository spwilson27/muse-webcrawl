Streptococcal pharyngitis

Streptococcal pharyngitis, also known as strep throat, is an infection of the back of the throat including the tonsils caused by Streptococcus pyogenes (group A streptococcus). Common symptoms include fever, sore throat, red tonsils, and enlarged lymph nodes in the front of the neck. Headache and nausea or vomiting may also occur, especially in children. A sandpaper-like rash, when present, is known as scarlet fever.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms typically begin one to three days after exposure and last seven to ten days. Strep throat is typically not accompanied by cough, runny nose, hoarseness, or conjunctivitis — features that point instead to a viral cause. Examination may show tonsillar exudates and tender anterior cervical lymphadenopathy. It most commonly affects children aged 5–15, though it can occur at any age.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is based on a rapid antigen detection test or throat culture from a throat swab. Clinical scoring systems such as the Centor criteria help estimate the probability of streptococcal infection and guide testing. Because most sore throats are viral and do not benefit from antibiotics, guidelines in many countries recommend testing before treatment, particularly in children and adolescents.

Treatment and complications

Strep throat is treated with antibiotics, typically penicillin or amoxicillin, which shorten symptoms modestly, reduce transmission, and prevent complications. Untreated infection can lead to peritonsillar abscess, otitis media, and post-streptococcal complications including acute rheumatic fever and post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis. Infected individuals are generally no longer contagious after 24 hours of effective antibiotic therapy.
