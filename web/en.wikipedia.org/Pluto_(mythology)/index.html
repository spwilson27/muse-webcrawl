In ancient Roman religion and mythology, Pluto (Latin: Plūtō; Greek: Plouton) was the ruler of the underworld and god of the dead, the Roman counterpart of the Greek Hades. The name derives from the Greek word for wealth, ploutos, reflecting his rule over the hidden riches of the earth — both its crops and its metals — as well as over the dead. In the division of the cosmos among the sons of Saturn, Jupiter received the sky, Neptune the sea, and Pluto the underworld.

Mythology

Pluto's most famous myth is the abduction of Proserpina (Greek Persephone), daughter of Ceres (Demeter), whom he carried off from Sicily to be his queen. Ceres' grief stopped the growth of crops until a compromise was reached: because Proserpina had eaten pomegranate seeds in the underworld, she would spend part of each year below with Pluto and part above with her mother — an aetiology for the seasons. Pluto was generally depicted as stern but just, rarely leaving his realm; his helmet of invisibility (the cap of Hades) and his three-headed dog Cerberus are among his attributes.

Cult

Pluto received sacrifice together with Proserpina, notably in the Ludi Taurii and at the Tarentum in the Campus Martius, where an underground altar was uncovered only for games held at long intervals. As Dis Pater ("Rich Father") he was paired with Proserpina in Roman cult and widely syncretised with Hades. Because naming death directly was considered unlucky, euphemistic names such as Pluto ("the Rich One") became standard.

Legacy

The dwarf planet Pluto, discovered in 1930, was named for the god, a name that also continued the convention of mythological names for planets and fitted the initials of Percival Lowell. The chemical element plutonium was in turn named after the dwarf planet.
