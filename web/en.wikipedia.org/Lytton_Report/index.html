The Lytton Report was the findings, adopted in 1933, of a commission of enquiry appointed by the League of Nations to investigate the Japanese invasion of Manchuria that followed the Mukden Incident of 18 September 1931. The commission, chaired by the British politician Victor Bulwer-Lytton, 2nd Earl of Lytton, travelled to the Far East in the spring of 1932, interviewed Chinese and Japanese leaders, and published its report in October 1932.

The report concluded that the Japanese military operations of 18–19 September 1931 could not be regarded as measures of legitimate self-defence, and that the new state of Manchukuo, proclaimed under Japanese sponsorship, was not the product of a genuine independence movement and could not be recognised. At the same time it acknowledged Japan's special rights and interests in Manchuria and recommended an autonomous Manchuria under Chinese sovereignty, with Japanese economic rights safeguarded and an international advisory role.

Background

On 18 September 1931, officers of the Japanese Kwantung Army staged an explosion on the South Manchuria Railway near Mukden and used it as a pretext to occupy southern Manchuria; within months Japanese forces controlled the whole region. China appealed to the League of Nations under the Covenant. The League Council, unwilling to confront Japan with sanctions, resolved on 10 December 1931 to send a commission of enquiry to study the situation on the spot.

The commission

The five commissioners were Lord Lytton (United Kingdom, chairman), General Henri Claudel (France), Count Luigi Aldrovandi Marescotti (Italy), Dr Heinrich Schnee (Germany), and Major General Frank Ross McCoy (United States, a non-member cooperating with the League). The commission arrived in Japan in February 1932, proceeded to China and Manchuria, met the former Chinese emperor Puyi, by then installed as Chief Executive of Manchukuo, and gathered evidence from officials, soldiers and civilians. Japanese pressure to recognise Manchukuo during the enquiry, and the state's formal proclamation on 1 March 1932, shadowed the commission's work.

Findings and recommendations

The report ran to ten chapters. Its key conclusions were:

  • China and Japan were both partly at fault for the deterioration of relations, but the military operations of the Japanese army on the night of 18 September 1931 "cannot be regarded as measures of legitimate self-defence," even though the commission did not assert that Japanese soldiers had necessarily acted on high-level orders in staging the incident.
  • Manchukuo owed its existence to the presence of Japanese troops and officials; it did not represent a genuine independence movement of the Manchurian population and should not be recognised.
  • Manchuria should receive an autonomous government under Chinese sovereignty, consistent with the Nine-Power Treaty, with Japanese rights protected and advisers, some Japanese, attached to the administration.
  • Both sides should withdraw armed forces from the railway zone and negotiate directly, assisted by a League-appointed conference.

Aftermath

The League Assembly adopted the report's conclusions on 24 February 1933 by 42 votes to 1 (Japan), with Siam abstaining. The Japanese delegate, Yōsuke Matsuoka, walked out of the Assembly, and Japan gave notice of withdrawal from the League in March 1933 (effective 1935), keeping Manchuria. The episode became the classic demonstration of the League's inability to restrain aggression by a determined great power, foreshadowing its failures over Ethiopia and the Rhineland. The United States, though not a member, had already issued the Stimson Doctrine of non-recognition of territorial changes made by force (January 1932), consistent with the report's conclusions.
