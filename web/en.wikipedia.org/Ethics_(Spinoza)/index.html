The Ethics, Demonstrated in Geometrical Order (Latin: Ethica, ordine geometrico demonstrata), usually known simply as the Ethics, is the magnum opus of the Dutch philosopher Baruch Spinoza. Written between about 1661 and 1675 and published posthumously in 1677, it presents a complete metaphysical, psychological, and ethical system in the deductive style of Euclid's geometry, proceeding from definitions and axioms through numbered propositions.

Contents

The work has five parts: (1) Of God, arguing that there is exactly one substance—God or Nature (Deus sive Natura)—of which everything else is a mode; (2) Of the Nature and Origin of the Mind, defending a parallelism of mind and body; (3) Of the Origin and Nature of the Affects, analysing the emotions as governed by the striving (conatus) of each thing to persevere in being; (4) Of Human Bondage, showing reason's power over passive affects; and (5) Of the Power of the Intellect, or On Human Freedom, culminating in the intellectual love of God.

Influence

Long condemned as atheistic, the Ethics was reclaimed by German Romantics and idealists—Goethe, Schelling, and Hegel among them—and remains a central text in debates over monism, determinism, and the philosophy of emotion.
