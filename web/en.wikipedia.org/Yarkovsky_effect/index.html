Yarkovsky effect

The Yarkovsky effect is a force acting on a rotating body in space caused by the anisotropic emission of thermal photons, which carry momentum. It is usually considered in relation to meteoroids or small asteroids (ten centimetres to about ten kilometres across), whose orbits it measurably alters over long timescales. The effect is named for the Russian-Polish civil engineer Ivan Osipovich Yarkovsky, who described the idea around 1900 in work largely lost until it was rediscovered and developed by Estonian astronomer Ernst Öpik and others in the 20th century.

Mechanism

A surface warmed by sunlight does not re-radiate that heat instantly: thermal inertia delays emission, so the warmest point of a rotating asteroid lies in its "afternoon," after local noon. The infrared photons radiated from this warm side carry away momentum, producing a tiny recoil force. For a prograde (counterclockwise as seen from the north) rotator, the afternoon thrust pushes the body slightly forward along its orbit, raising its semi-major axis over time; for a retrograde rotator, the drift is inward. A related "seasonal" component arises when the rotation axis is tilted, as one hemisphere warms through its summer. The strength of the effect depends on the body's size, density, spin rate and obliquity, surface thermal conductivity and albedo.

Observations and importance

The effect was first directly measured for the asteroid 6489 Golevka in 2003 using radar ranging data collected over more than a decade; measurements for the near-Earth asteroid Bennu, refined during NASA's OSIRIS-REx mission, agree closely with predictions. Though minute — shifting an asteroid's position by only kilometres per million years — the Yarkovsky effect dominates the orbital evolution of small asteroids, feeding bodies from the main belt into resonances that deliver them to near-Earth space, and it must be modelled precisely when assessing long-term impact hazards. The related YORP effect describes the analogous torque that speeds up or slows down an asteroid's rotation.
