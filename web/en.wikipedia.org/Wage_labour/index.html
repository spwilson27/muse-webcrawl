Wage labour


Definition: Sale of labour power in exchange for a wage or salary
Counterparty: Employer
Contrasts with: Slavery, self-employment, independent contracting

Wage labour (American spelling: wage labor) is the socioeconomic relationship between a worker and an employer in which the worker sells their labour power under a formal or informal contract, usually for an hourly wage, piece rate or annual salary. It is the dominant form of work in modern capitalist economies.

Characteristics

The wage worker lacks ownership of the means of production and of the product of their labour; in exchange for the wage, the employer directs the work and appropriates its output. Under employment law the relationship carries various rights and obligations — minimum wages, limits on working time, workplace safety, social insurance contributions — that distinguish employees from independent contractors, a boundary increasingly contested in the "gig economy".

History

Forms of hired labour existed in antiquity, but wage labour became the predominant form of work only with industrialisation, as enclosure and urbanisation separated workers from land and tools. Karl Marx analysed the system as resting on the sale of labour power as a commodity, arguing that workers produce more value than their wages return (surplus value), a critique developed in Capital (1867). Other critics, from nineteenth-century republicans who coined the phrase "wage slavery" to modern labour movements, have attacked dependence on wages as a form of unfreedom, while economists in the neoclassical tradition model wages as the price that clears a competitive labour market.

Labour markets

In practice wages are shaped by bargaining power, minimum wage legislation, trade unions, monopsony power of employers, skills and discrimination, and most workers in advanced economies are employees: International Labour Organization estimates suggest wage and salaried workers constitute roughly half of global employment, with the share rising steadily.
