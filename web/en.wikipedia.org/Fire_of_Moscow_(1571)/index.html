Fire of Moscow (1571)

The Fire of Moscow occurred in May 1571, when the army of the Crimean Khan Devlet I Giray, leading a major raid into the Tsardom of Russia during the Livonian War, reached Moscow and set the city ablaze. The wooden city burned almost completely; only the stone Kremlin survived. Contemporary accounts describe a catastrophic loss of life and tens of thousands of captives taken away to be sold into slavery.

Background

Seeking to punish Ivan the Terrible and to recover the khanates of Kazan and Astrakhan, which Russia had annexed in the 1550s, Devlet Giray assembled a large force of Crimean Tatars, commonly estimated at around 40,000 horsemen or more. The Russian army, depleted by the Livonian War and the oprichnina, failed to block the Tatars at the Oka River crossings; a key commander defected with information about an unguarded ford.

The fire

On 24 May 1571 the Tatars reached Moscow's outskirts and set fire to the suburbs. A strong wind spread the flames through the largely wooden city within hours. Panic at the gates caused deadly crushes, and gunpowder stores exploded, including magazines in the Kremlin towers, though the Kremlin's stone walls and palaces survived. Ivan had already fled the city. The fire is considered one of the worst urban disasters of 16th-century Europe; chroniclers' figures of over a hundred thousand dead are doubted by modern historians, but the devastation was plainly immense.

Aftermath

The Tatars withdrew with their captives and plunder. Devlet Giray returned with an even larger invasion the following year but was decisively defeated at the Battle of Molodi (1572), ending the threat of Moscow being taken. The burned city was rebuilt, and the disaster deepened the political crisis of Ivan's later reign.
