Combine harvester

The modern combine harvester, or simply combine, is a machine designed to harvest a variety of grain crops. Its name derives from combining three separate harvesting operations — reaping, threshing and winnowing — into a single process. Among the crops harvested with a combine are wheat, corn (maize), oats, rye, barley, sorghum, soybeans, flax and sunflowers.

Operation

A rotating reel pushes the crop toward a cutting bar at the front header, and augers convey the cut material to a threshing drum or rotor, which beats and rubs the grain from the straw. Sieves and fans then separate the grain from the chaff (winnowing), while the straw is discharged at the rear or chopped and spread. Clean grain is stored in an onboard tank and unloaded through an auger, often into a trailer while the combine keeps moving.

History

The first combine harvesters were patented in the 1830s in the United States, including Hiram Moore's 1834 Michigan machine, and early horse-drawn combines spread through California's wheat fields in the late 19th century. Self-propelled models appeared in the 20th century, and today's machines feature air-conditioned cabs, GPS guidance, yield monitoring and headers more than 12 metres wide.
