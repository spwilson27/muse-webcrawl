Open-wheel car

Open-wheel car
[Image: a Formula One open-wheel racing car]
Also called: Single-seater
Defining feature: Wheels outside the bodywork
Top series: Formula One, IndyCar
Layout: Mid-engine, rear-wheel drive

An open-wheel car is a car with the wheels outside the car's main body, and usually having only one seat. Open-wheel cars contrast with street cars, sports cars, stock cars, and touring cars, which have their wheels below the body or inside fenders. They are typically purpose-built for racing on road courses and oval tracks, with an emphasis on low weight, aerodynamic downforce, and precise handling.

Design

Modern open-wheel racing cars are mid-engined, with a carbon-fibre monocoque chassis, wings at the front and rear that generate substantial downforce, and wide slick tyres. The exposed wheels create significant aerodynamic drag but allow large brakes to be cooled efficiently. Cockpit protection has improved markedly in recent decades, most notably with the introduction of the halo device in Formula One and the aeroscreen in IndyCar.

Major series

  • Formula One — the premier global single-seater championship, sanctioned by the FIA, racing since 1950.
  • IndyCar Series — North America's top open-wheel series, featuring the Indianapolis 500.
  • Formula 2 and Formula 3 — feeder categories on the path to Formula One.
  • Formula E — the all-electric single-seater championship, racing mainly on street circuits.
  • Super Formula — Japan's top open-wheel series.

Safety

Wheel-to-wheel contact between open-wheel cars can launch a car airborne, a hazard that has driven continual safety work: deformable structures, the HANS device, higher cockpit sides, and closed-cockpit research. Fatal accidents, though rarer than in earlier eras, have prompted major regulation changes in every leading series.
