An ephemeris (plural: ephemerides; from Latin ephemeris and Greek ἐφημερίς, "diary" or "calendar") is a book, table, or computer file giving the calculated positions of a celestial object — such as the Sun, Moon, planets, asteroids, or comets — at regular intervals throughout a certain period, usually a year or more. Historically, positions were given as printed tables; modern ephemerides are most often computed and distributed electronically, as data files or software.

Ephemerides are fundamental to astronomy and celestial navigation. Given an ephemeris, an observer can predict where a planet will appear in the sky on a given date, compute the times of eclipses and transits, or determine a ship's position from sightings of the Sun and stars. Space agencies use high-precision ephemerides to navigate spacecraft throughout the Solar System.

History

The earliest known ephemerides were produced in Babylonian astronomy, where scribes compiled tables of planetary positions on clay tablets. In the second century AD, Ptolemy's Handy Tables, derived from the Almagest, allowed positions of the Sun, Moon, and planets to be calculated for any date. Medieval astronomers produced the Alphonsine tables (c. 1252), sponsored by Alfonso X of Castile, and later the Prutenic Tables of Erasmus Reinhold (1551), based on Copernican models. Johannes Kepler's Rudolphine Tables (1627) were dramatically more accurate and helped establish heliocentric astronomy.

From the eighteenth century, national almanac offices published ephemerides for navigators. Britain's Nautical Almanac, first issued for 1767 under Nevil Maskelyne, was essential to the determination of longitude at sea. France's Connaissance des temps dates to 1679, and the United States began publishing its own nautical almanac in 1852.

Modern ephemerides

Modern ephemerides are produced by numerical integration of the equations of motion of the Solar System, fitted to observations including lunar laser ranging and spacecraft tracking. The Jet Propulsion Laboratory's Development Ephemeris series (such as DE440) is the standard for spacecraft navigation and for the Astronomical Almanac published jointly by the US Naval Observatory and Her Majesty's Nautical Almanac Office. The French IMCCE produces the INPOP ephemerides, and the Russian Institute of Applied Astronomy produces the EPM series.

An ephemeris may tabulate, for each date, quantities such as right ascension and declination, heliocentric longitude and latitude, distance from Earth, apparent magnitude, and the times of rising, transit, and setting.
