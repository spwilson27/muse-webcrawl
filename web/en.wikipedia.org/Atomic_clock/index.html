Atomic clock

An atomic clock is a clock that measures time by monitoring the resonant frequency of atoms. It is based on atoms having different energy levels and the precisely defined frequency of radiation they absorb or emit in transitions between them.

A caesium fountain clock at a national metrology institute.

How it works

Electron transitions in atoms occur at extremely stable frequencies. In a caesium clock, atoms are exposed to microwaves whose frequency is tuned until it exactly matches the caesium-133 hyperfine transition of 9,192,631,770 cycles per second — the definition of the SI second since 1967. Quartz and other oscillators are locked to this atomic reference, yielding accuracies of one second in millions of years for laboratory clocks.

History and modern clocks

The first accurate caesium clock was built by Louis Essen and Jack Parry at the UK's National Physical Laboratory in 1955. Hydrogen masers and rubidium clocks offer compact alternatives. Optical lattice clocks using strontium or ytterbium now reach uncertainties near one second over the age of the universe and are candidates for a future redefinition of the second.

Applications

Atomic clocks underpin International Atomic Time (TAI) and Coordinated Universal Time (UTC), telecommunications synchronisation, and satellite navigation systems such as GPS, whose satellites each carry multiple atomic clocks.
