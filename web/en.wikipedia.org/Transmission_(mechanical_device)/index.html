Transmission (mechanical device)

A manual transmission gearbox, showing the gear pairs that provide different ratios.

A transmission (also called a gearbox) is a mechanical device that uses gears and gear trains to provide speed and torque conversion from a rotating power source to another device. By selecting between gear ratios, a transmission lets an engine operate within its efficient speed range while the driven wheels turn at widely varying speeds.

The term transmission properly refers to the whole drivetrain—the clutch or torque converter, gearbox, propeller shaft, differential, and axles—but in common usage, especially in North America, it denotes the gearbox alone.

Principle

A pair of meshing gears exchanges speed for torque: if the output gear is smaller than the input gear, output speed rises while torque falls, and vice versa. Early (low) gears in a vehicle multiply torque for starting and climbing; high gears (overdrive ratios) reduce engine speed for economical cruising.

Types

Manual transmission

The driver selects gears with a lever and operates a clutch pedal to interrupt power during shifts. Sliding-mesh designs gave way to constant-mesh gearboxes with synchromesh, which equalise gear speeds for smooth engagement.

Automatic transmission

Traditional automatics use a hydraulic torque converter and planetary gearsets shifted by clutches and brakes under hydraulic or electronic control. Modern units may have eight, nine, or ten forward ratios.

Continuously variable transmission

A CVT varies its ratio seamlessly, typically using a steel belt or chain running between two variable-diameter pulleys, keeping the engine near its optimal speed.

Dual-clutch transmission

A DCT uses two clutches for odd and even gears, pre-selecting the next gear to achieve very fast shifts; it is common in performance cars and increasingly in mainstream models.

Other applications

Transmissions are found in bicycles (derailleur and hub gears), wind turbines (step-up gearboxes), machine tools, marine propulsion, and helicopters. Most battery-electric vehicles use a single-speed reduction gear because electric motors deliver useful torque over a wide speed range.
