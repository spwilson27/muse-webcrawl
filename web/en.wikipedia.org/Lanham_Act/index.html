Lanham Act

Lanham (Trademark) Act of 1946
EnactedJuly 5, 1946
Codification15 U.S.C. §§ 1051 et seq.
Named forRep. Fritz G. Lanham of Texas
SubjectFederal trademark registration and protection; unfair competition

The Lanham (Trademark) Act, enacted in 1946 and named after Representative Fritz G. Lanham of Texas, is the primary federal statute governing trademarks, service marks, and unfair competition in the United States. Codified at 15 U.S.C. §§ 1051 et seq., it provides for federal registration of trademarks with the United States Patent and Trademark Office and creates civil causes of action for infringement of both registered and unregistered marks.

Key provisions

Section 2 (§ 1052) bars registration of marks that are scandalous, deceptive, or likely to cause confusion with prior marks, among other grounds. Section 32 (§ 1114) provides remedies for infringement of registered marks. Section 43(a) (§ 1125(a)) is the Act's broad unfair-competition provision, prohibiting false designations of origin and false advertising, and protecting unregistered marks. Section 43(c), added by later amendments, protects famous marks against dilution by blurring or tarnishment. The Act also supplies the standards for genericide — the loss of trademark rights when a mark becomes the generic name for a product, as happened to "aspirin" and "escalator" in the United States.

Significance

Although the U.S. Constitution and state common law also protect marks, the Lanham Act's federal registration system — with its nationwide constructive-use and notice benefits — dominates American trademark practice and is a frequent subject of Supreme Court decisions on issues such as genericness, the registrability of disparaging or immoral marks, and the territorial reach of U.S. trademark law.
