Social constructionism

Social constructionism is a theory in sociology and communication theory that examines how people develop shared understandings of the world. It proposes that much of what people perceive as reality — including categories such as gender, race, and mental illness — is shaped by social processes, language, and historical context rather than being purely natural or objective.

Origins

The theory is commonly traced to Peter L. Berger and Thomas Luckmann's 1966 book The Social Construction of Reality, which argued that society is created by human activity (externalization), comes to be experienced as an objective reality (objectivation), and is then absorbed by individuals (internalization). Kenneth Gergen became a leading proponent of a more explicitly constructionist social psychology from the 1970s onward.

Key concepts

Constructionists hold that knowledge is sustained by social processes: what counts as true is agreed upon within communities, and language does not merely describe reality but helps constitute it. A social construct is an idea or category that exists because people agree to treat it as real; money, marriage, and national borders are standard examples.

Relation to other theories

Social constructionism is related to but distinct from constructivism, which focuses on how individuals build knowledge, and from symbolic interactionism, which emphasizes meaning-making in face-to-face interaction. It also informs critical theory, feminist theory, and science and technology studies, where Ian Hacking's question "the social construction of what?" sharpened debates about which things are constructed.

Criticism

Critics argue that strong constructionism slides into relativism, denying material realities that constrain belief regardless of discourse. Others object that it underplays power or, conversely, that it functions as an unfalsifiable catch-all. Defenders reply that constructionism need not deny a material world, only that our categories for it are socially formed.
