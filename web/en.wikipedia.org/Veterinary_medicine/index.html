Veterinary medicine

Veterinary medicine is the branch of medicine concerned with the prevention, diagnosis, and treatment of disease, disorder, and injury in animals. Its practitioners, veterinarians, care for companion animals, livestock, zoo and wildlife species, and laboratory animals, and play a major role in food safety, public health, and the control of diseases transmissible between animals and humans (zoonoses).

Scope

Veterinary practice spans surgery, internal medicine, dentistry, dermatology, cardiology, oncology, and many other specialties, applied across species with very different anatomies and physiologies. Beyond clinical work, veterinarians inspect meat and other animal products, manage disease-control programmes for livestock, work in pharmaceutical and biomedical research, conserve endangered species, and staff government agencies concerned with animal and public health. The "One Health" framework emphasises the interconnection of veterinary and human medicine.

History

Animal healing is as old as animal domestication, but the profession formally began with the founding of the first veterinary school at Lyon, France, in 1761 under Claude Bourgelat, established in response to devastating cattle plagues. Schools followed across Europe and in the 19th century in North America. The 20th century saw the shift of practice in wealthy countries from horses and farm animals toward companion animals, alongside advances in anaesthesia, vaccination, antibiotics, and diagnostic imaging.

Education and regulation

Becoming a veterinarian typically requires a professional doctorate (such as the DVM) after five to six years of study, followed by licensure and, often, internship, residency, and board certification for specialists. Veterinary nurses and technicians form the supporting clinical team. Practice is regulated by national or state veterinary bodies, which set standards and discipline practitioners.
