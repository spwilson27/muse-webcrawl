The Nicene Creed (Greek: Σύμβολον τῆς Πίστεως; Latin: Symbolum Nicaenum), also called the Niceno-Constantinopolitan Creed or the Icon of the Faith, is the defining statement of belief of Nicene or mainstream Christianity and in those Christian denominations that adhere to it. The original Nicene Creed was first adopted at the First Council of Nicaea in 325. The council was summoned by the emperor Constantine I to resolve the Arian controversy over whether the Son of God was of the same substance as God the Father. The revised form, with expanded clauses on the Holy Spirit, is traditionally attributed to the First Council of Constantinople in 381.

Text (English translation, ICET)

We believe in one God, the Father, the Almighty, maker of heaven and earth, of all that is, seen and unseen. We believe in one Lord, Jesus Christ, the only Son of God, eternally begotten of the Father, God from God, Light from Light, true God from true God, begotten, not made, of one Being with the Father. Through him all things were made. For us and for our salvation he came down from heaven: by the power of the Holy Spirit he became incarnate from the Virgin Mary, and was made man. For our sake he was crucified under Pontius Pilate; he suffered death and was buried. On the third day he rose again in accordance with the Scriptures; he ascended into heaven and is seated at the right hand of the Father. He will come again in glory to judge the living and the dead, and his kingdom will have no end. We believe in the Holy Spirit, the Lord, the giver of life, who proceeds from the Father [and the Son], who with the Father and the Son is worshipped and glorified, who has spoken through the Prophets. We believe in one holy catholic and apostolic Church. We acknowledge one baptism for the forgiveness of sins. We look for the resurrection of the dead, and the life of the world to come. Amen.

History

The creed of 325 ended with an anathema against those who said of the Son "there was [a time] when he was not" — the heart of Arius's teaching. Its key word, homoousios ("of one essence/substance"), became the touchstone of orthodoxy defended by Athanasius of Alexandria. The 381 revision added the articles on the Holy Spirit, the Church, baptism, and the resurrection. The creed entered the eucharistic liturgy in the East in the 5th–6th centuries and in the West more slowly.

The Filioque controversy

In the Latin West, the phrase Filioque ("and the Son") was interpolated into the article on the procession of the Holy Spirit, first in Spain and later at Rome (1014). Eastern churches rejected both the addition and the theology they associated with it; together with disputes over papal authority, the Filioque became one of the emblematic causes of the East–West Schism of 1054, and remains an ecumenical issue today.
