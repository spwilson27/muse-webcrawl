Fractal compression

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Fractal compression is a lossy image compression method based on fractals and iterated function systems (IFS). It exploits self-similarity within natural images: instead of storing pixels, the encoder stores a set of mathematical transformations whose iterated application reconstructs an approximation of the image.

Principle

The method rests on the collage theorem, developed by Michael Barnsley in the 1980s. An image is partitioned into small "range" blocks; for each block, the encoder searches a pool of larger "domain" blocks for one that, after an affine transformation (scaling, rotation, reflection, and adjustment of contrast and brightness), closely matches it. The image is then represented by this set of transformations — a partitioned iterated function system. Decoding starts from any image and repeatedly applies the transformations, converging to a fixed-point attractor that resembles the original. Because the attractor is generated mathematically, decoding can in principle render the image at higher resolutions, synthesizing plausible detail.

History and use

Barnsley founded Iterated Systems Inc. to commercialize the technique, and it was used in products such as Microsoft's Encarta encyclopedia for storing images and in early fractal image format (FIF) software. Compression could achieve high ratios and decoding was fast, but encoding required an enormous search and was very slow, an asymmetry that limited adoption. Fractal compression also became entangled in patent disputes. With the rise of JPEG, wavelet methods and later standards, fractal compression remained mostly a research curiosity, though it retains interest in image retrieval and resolution enhancement.

Characteristics

  • Lossy, with compression ratios commonly reported from about 10:1 to 100:1 on suitable images.
  • Very slow encoding, fast decoding.
  • Resolution-independent decoding (with synthesized detail).
  • Works best on images with strong self-similarity.
