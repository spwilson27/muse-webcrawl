A stalactite (from the Greek stalaktós, "dripping", via stalassein, "to drip") is a mineral formation that hangs from the ceiling of caves, hot springs, or man-made structures such as bridges and mines. Any material that is soluble and can be deposited as a colloid, or is in suspension, or is capable of being melted, may form a stalactite.

Formation

Limestone stalactites form when rainwater absorbs carbon dioxide from the air and soil, becoming a weak carbonic acid solution that dissolves calcium carbonate as it percolates through cracks in the rock. When this mineral-laden water reaches air inside the cave, carbon dioxide escapes and calcite is deposited in tiny rings around each drip. Over thousands of years these deposits lengthen into tapering, icicle-like forms. Growth rates are typically fractions of a millimetre per year, though faster growth occurs where the water flow is rich in minerals.

Types

Limestone stalactites are the most common. Lava stalactites form in lava tubes when molten material drips from the ceiling. Ice stalactites (icicles) form from freezing water. Concrete structures can also grow stalactite-like deposits called calthemites, formed by leaching of calcium hydroxide from cement.

Stalactites and stalagmites

Where dripping water reaches the cave floor, a corresponding mound called a stalagmite grows upward. Given enough time, a stalactite and stalagmite may join to form a column. A popular mnemonic is that stalactites "hang tight" to the ceiling, while stalagmites "mite" reach up from the ground.
