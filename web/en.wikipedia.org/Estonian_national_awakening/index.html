Estonian national awakening

The Estonian national awakening (Estonian: Ärkamisaeg) was a period from the mid-19th century to the early 20th century during which Estonians began to recognize themselves as a nation deserving self-governance, develop an Estonian-language literature and press, and lay the cultural groundwork for Estonian independence in 1918.

Background

For centuries Estonia had been ruled by foreign powers — Danes, Germans, Swedes and Russians — while ethnic Estonians were largely peasants under Baltic German landlords. Emancipation from serfdom in 1816–1819 in the Governorates of Estonia and Livonia gave peasants personal freedom but no land, yet it allowed Estonian surnames, greater mobility and access to education.

Early cultural activity

Estophile Baltic German scholars such as those in the Learned Estonian Society (founded 1838) studied Estonian language and folklore. Friedrich Reinhold Kreutzwald compiled the national epic Kalevipoeg (1857–1861), modelled in part on Finland's Kalevala. Johann Voldemar Jannsen founded the newspaper Perno Postimees in 1857 and in 1869 organised the first Estonian Song Festival in Tartu.

Political awakening

Figures including Lydia Koidula, Carl Robert Jakobson and Jakob Hurt promoted Estonian-language schools, collections of folklore and political demands. Jakobson founded the newspaper Sakala in 1878 and campaigned against Baltic German privilege. Divisions emerged between moderate cultural nationalists and more radical political activists, and Tsarist Russification from the 1880s both hindered and inadvertently strengthened Estonian identity.

Legacy

By 1905 a dense network of societies, newspapers, cooperatives and schools existed. After the Russian Revolutions of 1917, Estonian leaders declared independence on 24 February 1918. See also Estonia, Kalevipoeg and Estonian Song Festival.
