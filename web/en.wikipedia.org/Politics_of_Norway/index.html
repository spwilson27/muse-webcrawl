Politics of Norway

The politics of Norway take place within a unitary parliamentary constitutional monarchy. The Constitution of 1814, the second-oldest written constitution still in force, establishes the King as head of state, executive power in the Council of State (cabinet), and legislative power in the Storting, the national parliament.

The Storting building in Oslo.

Institutions

The Storting has 169 members elected for four-year terms by proportional representation in multi-member constituencies; unlike many parliaments it cannot be dissolved early. The government, led by the prime minister, must command the confidence of the Storting, though Norway practices "negative parliamentarism" — a cabinet need not win an investiture vote, only avoid a majority against it. The monarch's role is largely ceremonial, though the King formally presides over the Council of State. The judiciary is headed by the Supreme Court (Høyesterett).

Parties

Norway has a multi-party system. The Labour Party historically dominated post-war politics; other major parties include the Conservatives (Høyre), the Progress Party, the Centre Party, the Socialist Left, the Liberals, the Christian Democrats, and the Red Party. Coalition and minority governments are the norm.

Features

Norway is a founding member of NATO and closely associated with the European Union through the EEA Agreement, though voters rejected EU membership in referendums in 1972 and 1994. Petroleum revenues are channeled into the Government Pension Fund Global, the world's largest sovereign wealth fund, under a fiscal rule limiting budget use of oil income. The country consistently ranks at or near the top of democracy and press-freedom indices.
