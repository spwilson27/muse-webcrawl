A herbarium (plural: herbaria) is a collection of preserved plant specimens and associated data used for scientific study. Specimens are usually pressed, dried, mounted on sheets of paper and stored in cabinets, accompanied by labels recording the collector, date, locality and habitat.

History

The practice of pressing and binding plants into "hortus siccus" (dry garden) books arose in sixteenth-century Italy; Luca Ghini, professor of botany at Pisa, is credited with inventing the herbarium around 1544, and his student Gherardo Cibo's collection of 1532 still survives. Botanical gardens at Pisa, Padua and Bologna founded early herbaria that remain in use. Carl Linnaeus's herbarium, preserved by the Linnean Society of London, anchors the names of thousands of species.

Specimens and curation

A typical specimen is collected in the field with flowers or fruit, pressed flat between absorbent papers, dried, and mounted on acid-free card with a label giving the scientific name, family, collection locality (often with GPS coordinates), date, collector and collection number. Sheets are filed by family and genus in pest-controlled cabinets; freezing or low-oxygen treatments protect against insect damage. Type specimens, on which species names are based, are specially marked and catalogued. Many herbaria also maintain spirit collections of flowers, carpological (fruit and seed) collections, and xylotheques of wood samples.

Uses

Herbaria underpin plant taxonomy, systematics and identification. Their historical records document changes in species distributions, flowering times in response to climate change, the spread of invasive species and the occurrence of plant diseases — famously, DNA from herbarium leaves helped trace the Irish potato famine pathogen Phytophthora infestans. Digitization programmes are imaging millions of sheets and transcribing label data into global databases such as GBIF and iDigBio, making collections accessible worldwide.
