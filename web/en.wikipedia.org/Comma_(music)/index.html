Comma (music)

In music theory, a comma is a very small interval, the difference between two pitches that are theoretically distinct within a tuning system but would coincide in another. Commas arise because simple frequency ratios cannot be reconciled with one another: stacking pure intervals eventually conflicts with the octave.

Examples

The syntonic comma (about 21.5 cents) is the difference between a just major third (5:4) and the Pythagorean major third produced by four perfect fifths. The Pythagorean comma (about 23.5 cents) is the gap between twelve perfect fifths and seven octaves — the discrepancy that makes a closed circle of pure fifths impossible.

Consequences

Commas are the fundamental problem that musical temperaments solve. Meantone temperaments flatten the fifths to absorb the syntonic comma and make thirds pure, while equal temperament distributes the Pythagorean comma evenly across all twelve fifths, slightly mistuning every interval except the octave. Singers and players of unfretted instruments often adjust intonation instinctively to reduce comma discrepancies in just intonation.
