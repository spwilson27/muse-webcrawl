Hermes (Greek: Ἑρμῆς) is an Olympian god in Greek religion and mythology, the herald of the gods and messenger between the divine and mortal worlds. He is the patron of travellers, merchants, thieves, orators, and athletes, the inventor of the lyre, and — as Hermes Psychopompus — the guide of souls to the underworld.

Mythology

The Homeric Hymn to Hermes tells how, on the day of his birth in a cave on Mount Cyllene, the infant god invented the lyre from a tortoise shell and stole the cattle of his brother Apollo; the two were reconciled when Hermes gave Apollo the lyre. Hermes aided heroes throughout Greek myth: he lent Perseus his winged sandals, guided Priam to Achilles in the Iliad, and slew Argus Panoptes to free Io, earning the epithet Argeiphontes. Boundaries and roads were marked by herms — squared pillars bearing his head — from which his name is traditionally derived.

Later influence

In Hellenistic Egypt, Hermes was fused with the Egyptian god Thoth as Hermes Trismegistus, the legendary author of the Hermetic Corpus, which profoundly influenced Renaissance magic, alchemy, and philosophy. His Roman equivalent is Mercury.
