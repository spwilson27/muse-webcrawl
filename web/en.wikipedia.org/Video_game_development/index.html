Video game development

Video game development is the process of creating a video game, from concept through design, programming, art and audio production, testing, and release. It combines software engineering with creative media production, and is carried out by developers ranging from solo creators to teams of hundreds, with development budgets for major "AAA" titles reaching hundreds of millions of dollars.

Process

Development typically begins with a concept and prototype, used to test core mechanics ("gameplay loops") and secure funding or approval. In pre-production the team creates the game design document, technical design, art style guides, and a vertical slice demonstrating the intended quality. Production, the longest phase, involves building levels, assets, code, and audio in iterative milestones, often managed with agile methods. Testing follows: alpha (feature complete), beta (content complete), and release candidate stages, with bug fixing and platform certification for consoles. After launch, live games receive patches, downloadable content, and seasonal updates.

Technology

Most modern games are built on a game engine that provides rendering, physics, animation, and scripting; the most widely used are Unity and Epic's Unreal Engine, alongside proprietary in-house engines. Games are written mainly in C++, C#, and scripting languages, and 2D/3D assets are made in tools such as Blender, Maya, and Photoshop. Middleware supplies physics (e.g. Havok), audio, and networking.

Industry characteristics

Development is project-based and hit-driven, with high costs, long schedules (three to six years for large titles), and persistent problems of "crunch" overtime and cancelled projects. Digital distribution, crowdfunding, early access, and accessible engines have enabled a large independent scene since the late 2000s, while mobile gaming created a huge market for smaller-scale development.
