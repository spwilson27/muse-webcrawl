Diabetic neuropathy

Peripheral nerves are damaged by chronic high blood sugar in diabetic neuropathy.

Diabetic neuropathy is nerve damage caused by diabetes mellitus. It is among the most common long-term complications of diabetes, affecting up to half of all people with the condition over their lifetimes. Chronically elevated blood glucose injures nerves both directly and by damaging the small blood vessels (vasa nervorum) that supply them.

Classification

The most common form is distal symmetric polyneuropathy, which begins in the feet and progresses upward in a "stocking-glove" distribution, causing numbness, tingling, burning pain, and loss of protective sensation. Other forms include autonomic neuropathy (affecting the gut, bladder, cardiovascular system, and erectile function — including gastroparesis and orthostatic hypotension), proximal neuropathy (diabetic amyotrophy, causing pain and weakness in the thigh and hip), and focal mononeuropathies, including cranial nerve palsies and entrapment syndromes such as carpal tunnel syndrome.

Mechanism

Sustained hyperglycaemia drives several damaging pathways: the polyol pathway (accumulation of sorbitol via aldose reductase), formation of advanced glycation end-products (AGEs), activation of protein kinase C, oxidative stress, and microvascular ischaemia of the nerves.

Consequences

Loss of sensation in the feet predisposes to unrecognised trauma, foot ulcers, infection, and ultimately amputation; diabetes is the leading cause of non-traumatic lower-limb amputation. Painful neuropathy can be severely disabling and disturb sleep.

Management

The cornerstone of prevention is tight glycaemic control, which substantially reduces risk in type 1 diabetes and modestly in type 2. Neuropathic pain is treated with drugs such as duloxetine, pregabalin, gabapentin, and tricyclic antidepressants. Regular foot examination and care are essential to prevent ulceration.
