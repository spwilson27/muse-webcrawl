Shanghai International Settlement

The Bund, the waterfront of the International Settlement, in the 1920s–30s.

The Shanghai International Settlement (Chinese: 上海公共租界) was a foreign concession in Shanghai, China, established in 1863 by the merger of the British and American enclaves created under the unequal treaties that followed the First Opium War. From 1854 it was administered by the Shanghai Municipal Council, dominated by British and later other Western interests, though Japanese influence grew markedly in the 20th century.

Under the Treaty of Nanking (1842) and subsequent agreements, Shanghai was opened to foreign trade and residence. The settlement developed its own municipal government, police force (the Shanghai Municipal Police), courts (with extraterritorial jurisdiction over foreign nationals), and volunteer defence corps. Together with the separate French Concession, it made Shanghai China's principal centre of foreign commerce, finance, and publishing, and a cosmopolitan city famed—and notorious—for its wealth, crime, and nightlife.

History

The settlement expanded repeatedly, reaching its final boundaries in 1899. Chinese residents always formed the great majority of its population but were excluded from its governing council until 1928. Crises included the May Thirtieth Movement of 1925, the Shanghai Incident of 1932, and the Battle of Shanghai in 1937, after which Japanese forces occupied the Chinese-administered parts of the city and, in December 1941, occupied the International Settlement itself. The settlement was formally retroceded to China in 1943, when Britain and the United States relinquished their treaty rights; its institutions were dissolved as the Republic of China government took control.

Legacy

The settlement's architecture—above all the Bund's bank buildings—remains a defining feature of Shanghai, and the era is remembered in Chinese historiography as a symbol both of national humiliation and of Shanghai's emergence as a global city.
