Iqaluit (Inuktitut: ᐃᖃᓗᐃᑦ, "place of many fish") is the capital of the Canadian territory of Nunavut, the territory's largest community, and its only city. It sits at the head of Frobisher Bay on the south coast of Baffin Island in the Canadian Arctic. With a population of about 7,700 (2021), it is one of the smallest provincial or territorial capitals in Canada by population, yet it is the political, administrative and transportation hub of the eastern Arctic.

History

The area was a traditional Inuit fishing and hunting site for thousands of years. The community grew around an American air base built in 1942 at the head of Frobisher Bay (named for Elizabethan explorer Martin Frobisher, who visited in 1576), and a Distant Early Warning (DEW) Line presence followed in the 1950s. The settlement, called Frobisher Bay, expanded further after the closure of nearby Apex and the concentration of federal services; Inuit voted in 1995 to make it the capital of the new territory of Nunavut, ahead of Rankin Inlet, and it officially became capital when Nunavut was created on 1 April 1999. The name Iqaluit replaced Frobisher Bay in 1987. City status followed in 2001.

Geography and climate

Iqaluit lies above the tree line in a tundra landscape of rocky hills; the Sylvia Grinnell River flows nearby. It has a polar climate moderated somewhat by the sea: long, severe winters (January averages around −30 °C) and short, cool summers (July averages about 8 °C). There are no roads connecting Iqaluit to other communities; access is by air year-round and by sea during the brief summer shipping season. A deep-water port, long planned to reduce freight costs, opened in 2023.

Government, culture and economy

The Legislative Assembly of Nunavut meets in Iqaluit, notable for its Inuit art and its consensus style of government. The city hosts the Nunatta Sunakkutaangit Museum, the annual Toonik Tyme spring festival and Alianait Arts Festival, and Nunavut Arctic College. Government is the largest employer; the community is majority Inuit, and Inuktitut is widely spoken alongside English. In 2021 a fuel contamination incident in the city's water supply drew national attention to Arctic infrastructure challenges.
