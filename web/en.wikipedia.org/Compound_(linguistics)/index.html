Compound (linguistics)

In linguistics, a compound is a lexeme (less precisely, a word or a sign) that consists of more than one stem. Compounding, composition or nominal composition is the process of word formation that creates compound lexemes. Compounding occurs in many languages; Germanic languages are especially prolific, and German is famous for very long compounds written as single words.

Types of compounds

Compounds are commonly classified by the relation between their parts. In an endocentric compound, one part (the head) determines the category and core meaning: a blackboard is a kind of board. In an exocentric compound, the meaning is not a hyponym of either part: a redhead is a person, not a kind of head. Copulative compounds join equals, as in singer-songwriter, and appositional compounds identify one element with another.

Form and spelling

English compounds may be written as single words (sunflower), hyphenated (mother-in-law), or as separate words (bus stop), and the spelling of a given compound often changes over time from open to hyphenated to closed. In speech, English noun compounds typically carry main stress on the first element, distinguishing a blackbird (a species) from a black bird.

Compounding across languages

Sanskrit grammar developed an elaborate classification of compounds (samāsa) that influenced later linguistics. Chinese forms most of its vocabulary from compounds of monosyllabic morphemes, and Japanese, Korean, Finnish and many other languages also compound productively, though languages differ in which parts of speech may combine and whether the head comes first or last.
