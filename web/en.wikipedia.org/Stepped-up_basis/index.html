Stepped-up basis

At death, the tax basis of inherited property is "stepped up" to its fair market value.

Stepped-up basis is a rule of United States tax law under which the cost basis of property acquired from a decedent is reset — stepped up (or down) — to the property's fair market value at the date of the decedent's death, as provided in section 1014 of the Internal Revenue Code. Capital gains that accrued during the decedent's lifetime are thereby never subject to income tax.

Example

If a decedent bought shares for $10,000 that were worth $100,000 at death, the beneficiary who inherits them takes a basis of $100,000. Selling immediately for $100,000 produces no taxable gain; the $90,000 of appreciation escapes income tax permanently.

Scope and exceptions

The rule generally applies to property passing through the estate, and community-property states may step up the entire property, including the surviving spouse's half. Some assets are excluded, notably items of income in respect of a decedent such as traditional retirement accounts. For deaths in 2010, executors could elect a modified carryover-basis regime with limited step-up allowances.

Policy debate

Supporters argue the rule simplifies administration — heirs often lack records of the decedent's original cost — and avoids taxing the same wealth twice alongside the estate tax. Critics call it a major loophole that disproportionately benefits wealthy families, encourages holding appreciated assets until death ("buy, borrow, die"), and costs the Treasury tens of billions of dollars per year; several legislative proposals would replace it with carryover basis or treat death as a realisation event.
