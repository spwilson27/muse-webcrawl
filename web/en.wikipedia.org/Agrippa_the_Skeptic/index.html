Agrippa the Skeptic

Agrippa is credited with the five modes that bear his name.

Agrippa (Greek: Ἀγρίππας) was a Pyrrhonist philosopher who probably lived in the late 1st or 2nd century AD. He is regarded as the author of "the five grounds of doubt" or "five modes" (pente tropoi), a powerful formulation of skeptical argument preserved by Sextus Empiricus in the Outlines of Pyrrhonism and summarized by Diogenes Laërtius. Almost nothing is known of his life, and it is uncertain whether Agrippa was his real name or a conventional attribution.

The five modes

The five modes of Agrippa are intended to show that any claim can be challenged, leading to suspension of judgment (epoché):

  • Dispute (diaphonia) — undecidable disagreement among philosophers and ordinary people about the matter under investigation.
  • Infinite regress (eis apeiron) — any proof offered requires a further proof, and so on to infinity.
  • Relativity (pros ti) — the object appears differently depending on the subject judging and the conditions, so nothing is apprehended in itself.
  • Hypothesis — defenders of a doctrine avoid regress only by assuming something as a self-evident starting point without proof.
  • Reciprocity or circularity (diallelus) — the proposed proof relies on the very thing under investigation, so the argument is circular.

Legacy

The combination of regress, circularity and unproven assumption is often called Agrippa's trilemma or, after Hans Albert, the Münchhausen trilemma: any attempt at ultimate justification ends in infinite regress, logical circularity, or dogmatic termination of the argument. The modes continue to be central to epistemological debates about foundationalism, coherentism and infinitism, and they were influential on early modern philosophers who encountered Sextus's writings after their recovery in the sixteenth century.
