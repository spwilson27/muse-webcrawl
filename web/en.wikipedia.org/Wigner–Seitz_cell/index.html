Wigner–Seitz cell

The Wigner–Seitz cell, named after Eugene Wigner and Frederick Seitz, is a particular kind of primitive cell in a crystal. It consists of all points that are closer to a given lattice point than to any other lattice point, and is a fundamental concept of solid-state physics and crystallography. Its construction is mathematically the Voronoi cell of the lattice.

Construction

A Wigner–Seitz cell can be built by choosing a lattice point, drawing line segments to all nearby lattice points, and bisecting each segment with a perpendicular plane (a line in two dimensions). The smallest enclosed region around the lattice point is the cell. Because it is a primitive cell, it contains exactly one lattice point and has the full point-group symmetry of the lattice site.

Examples

In two dimensions the Wigner–Seitz cell of a square lattice is a square, and that of a hexagonal lattice is a regular hexagon. In three dimensions, the cell for a body-centred cubic lattice is a truncated octahedron, and for a face-centred cubic lattice it is a rhombic dodecahedron.

Brillouin zone

The Wigner–Seitz cell of the reciprocal lattice is called the first Brillouin zone, a central object in the theory of wave propagation in periodic media and in the electronic band structure of solids. Wigner and Seitz introduced the cell in their 1933 paper on the structure of metallic sodium.
