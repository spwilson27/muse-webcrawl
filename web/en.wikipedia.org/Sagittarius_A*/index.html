Sagittarius A* (pronounced "Sagittarius A-star"; abbreviated Sgr A*) is a supermassive black hole at the Galactic Center of the Milky Way. It is associated with a bright, compact radio source within the larger Sagittarius A complex and is orbited closely by a cluster of stars whose motions revealed its enormous mass concentrated in a tiny volume.

Discovery and evidence

Radio astronomers discovered the Sagittarius A source in 1974; the compact component A* was identified shortly afterward. Beginning in the 1990s, teams led by Reinhard Genzel and Andrea Ghez tracked individual stars (the "S-stars"), especially S2, which completes an orbit around A* in about 16 years, proving the central object has about four million times the Sun's mass within a radius smaller than the orbit — evidence only a black hole can plausibly explain. Genzel and Ghez shared the 2020 Nobel Prize in Physics for this work (with Roger Penrose).

Observations

Sgr A* accretes only weakly and is far dimmer than typical quasars, though it produces daily X-ray and infrared flares from hot gas near the event horizon. In May 2022 the Event Horizon Telescope collaboration released the first image of Sgr A*: a bright ring of emission around the black hole's shadow, confirming predictions of general relativity at a scale of millions of solar masses.

Surroundings

The black hole sits amid the dense central star cluster of the Galaxy, young massive stars whose presence near the black hole (the "paradox of youth") puzzles astronomers, and clouds of gas such as the G2 object that survived a close passage in 2014.
