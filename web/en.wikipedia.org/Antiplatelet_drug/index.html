Antiplatelet drug

Antiplatelet drugs inhibit platelet aggregation and thrombus formation

An antiplatelet drug is a class of pharmaceuticals that decrease platelet aggregation and inhibit thrombus (blood clot) formation. They are widely used in the primary and secondary prevention of thrombotic cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease, including myocardial infarction and ischaemic stroke.

Mechanism and classes

Antiplatelet agents act at different points in platelet activation. Aspirin irreversibly inhibits the enzyme cyclooxygenase-1 (COX-1), blocking production of thromboxane A2. P2Y12 receptor inhibitors—such as clopidogrel, prasugrel, and ticagrelor—block ADP-mediated platelet activation. Glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitors (abciximab, eptifibatide, tirofiban) prevent the final common pathway of platelet aggregation and are given intravenously in acute settings. Other agents include dipyridamole and cilostazol, which act through phosphodiesterase inhibition.

Clinical use

Dual antiplatelet therapy, usually aspirin combined with a P2Y12 inhibitor, is standard after coronary stent placement and in acute coronary syndromes. Antiplatelet drugs differ from anticoagulants, which target the clotting cascade rather than platelet function, though the two may be combined in selected patients.

Adverse effects

The chief risk of antiplatelet therapy is bleeding, including gastrointestinal haemorrhage and, rarely, intracranial bleeding. Aspirin can also cause gastric irritation and hypersensitivity reactions.
