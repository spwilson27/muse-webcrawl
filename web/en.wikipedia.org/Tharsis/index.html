Tharsis is a vast volcanic plateau centred near the equator in the western hemisphere of Mars. One of the largest volcanic provinces in the Solar System, it hosts some of the largest known volcanoes and has profoundly influenced the planet's geology, rotation and climate.

Geography

The Tharsis rise covers an area of roughly 25–30 million square kilometres and stands several kilometres above the surrounding terrain. Aligned along its crest are the three great shield volcanoes of the Tharsis Montes — Arsia Mons, Pavonis Mons and Ascraeus Mons. To the northwest lies Olympus Mons, the tallest volcano in the Solar System at about 21.9 km high, and to the north the enormous, shallow Alba Mons. East of the rise, the crust has fractured into the immense canyon system of Valles Marineris.

Formation and effects

Tharsis is thought to have formed from a mantle plume over the first billion years of Martian history, with volcanism continuing sporadically into geologically recent times. Its immense mass is believed to have driven true polar wander, shifting Mars's rotation axis, and the stresses it imposed on the crust created the planet-spanning systems of grabens radiating from the rise. Outgassing from Tharsis volcanoes may have contributed significantly to an early, thicker Martian atmosphere and to catastrophic flood events.

Observation

Telescopic observers mapped Tharsis as a bright albedo feature; its nature as a volcanic highland was revealed by the Mariner 9 orbiter in 1971–72, and it has since been mapped in detail by Mars Global Surveyor, Mars Odyssey and Mars Express.
