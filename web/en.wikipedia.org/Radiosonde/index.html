Radiosonde

A radiosonde is a battery-powered telemetry instrument carried into the atmosphere, usually by a weather balloon, that measures various atmospheric parameters and transmits them by radio to a ground receiver. Modern radiosondes measure or calculate pressure, altitude, temperature, relative humidity, wind speed and direction, and geographic position. The data they provide are essential inputs to numerical weather prediction models.

Launch of a radiosonde attached to a hydrogen-filled weather balloon.

Operation

The instrument package, typically weighing about 100–300 grams, is launched attached to a balloon filled with hydrogen or helium. The balloon ascends at roughly 300 metres per minute and bursts at altitudes of 25–35 km, after which the radiosonde descends by parachute. A flight lasts about two hours. Wind speed and direction are derived from the radiosonde's GPS position as it drifts.

The term "radiosonde" was coined by the German meteorologist Hugo Hergesell from "radio" and the French sonde (probe). The first successful instruments were developed in the late 1920s by Pavel Molchanov in the Soviet Union, Vilho Väisälä in Finland, and Robert Bureau in France.

Global network

Radiosondes are launched worldwide, usually twice daily at 0000 and 1200 UTC, from about 1,300 upper-air stations coordinated through the World Meteorological Organization. Data are encoded in TEMP messages and shared internationally. Leading manufacturers include Vaisala (Finland) and Lockheed Martin Sippican (United States).

Dropsondes and related instruments

A variant, the dropsonde, is released from research and hurricane-reconnaissance aircraft and measures the atmosphere as it falls. Radiosonde observations remain the reference standard against which satellite soundings are calibrated, despite their cost and the environmental concern of expendable instruments.
