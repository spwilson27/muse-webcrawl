Paris Peace Conference (1919–1920)

Paris Peace Conference
Dates18 January 1919 – 21 January 1920
LocationParis and Versailles, France
ParticipantsAllied and Associated Powers (over 30 delegations)
Key figuresWoodrow Wilson, Georges Clemenceau, David Lloyd George, Vittorio Orlando
OutcomeTreaties of Versailles, Saint-Germain, Neuilly, Trianon, Sèvres; League of Nations

The Paris Peace Conference was an international meeting convened in January 1919 at the end of the First World War to establish the terms of peace between the victorious Allied and Associated Powers and the defeated Central Powers. It produced five treaties, redrew the map of Europe and the Middle East, and created the League of Nations.

Organisation

The conference opened on 18 January 1919 and formally closed on 21 January 1920, when the League of Nations held its first council meeting. Delegates from more than thirty nations attended, but real power lay first with the "Council of Ten" and then, from March 1919, with the "Big Four": US President Woodrow Wilson, French Premier Georges Clemenceau, British Prime Minister David Lloyd George and Italian Prime Minister Vittorio Emanuele Orlando. The defeated states were excluded from negotiation and presented with terms; Russia, in civil war, was not represented. Wilson's Fourteen Points, especially national self-determination and the creation of a League of Nations, shaped the agenda, though they were repeatedly compromised by Allied territorial and reparations demands.

Treaties

  • Treaty of Versailles (28 June 1919) with Germany: loss of territory and colonies, disarmament, the "war guilt" clause (Article 231) and reparations.
  • Treaty of Saint-Germain (10 September 1919) with Austria, dissolving the Habsburg lands.
  • Treaty of Neuilly (27 November 1919) with Bulgaria.
  • Treaty of Trianon (4 June 1920) with Hungary, which lost roughly two-thirds of its territory.
  • Treaty of Sèvres (10 August 1920) with the Ottoman Empire, later superseded by the Treaty of Lausanne (1923).

Consequences

The settlement created new states (Poland, Czechoslovakia, Yugoslavia), distributed former German and Ottoman colonies as League mandates, and imposed a settlement whose enforcement weakened as the United States Senate refused to ratify the Treaty of Versailles and the US never joined the League. The harshness and perceived illegitimacy of the German treaty, and the unfulfilled promises of self-determination in the colonial world, left grievances that historians connect to the instability of the interwar order and the coming of the Second World War.
