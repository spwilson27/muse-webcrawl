Conversion disorder

Conversion disorder, also known in current classification as functional neurological symptom disorder, is a condition in which a person experiences neurological symptoms — such as weakness or paralysis, abnormal movements, non-epileptic seizures, sensory loss or speech disturbance — that are not explained by a recognized neurological disease and are incompatible with known neuroanatomy. In the DSM-5 it is classified among the somatic symptom and related disorders; the ICD-11 places it under dissociative neurological symptom disorder.

Presentation and diagnosis

Symptoms are genuine and involuntary, not intentionally produced. Diagnosis rests on positive clinical signs of internal inconsistency or incongruity — for example, Hoover's sign in functional leg weakness, midline splitting of sensory loss, or the features distinguishing psychogenic non-epileptic seizures from epilepsy — rather than being merely a diagnosis of exclusion. Symptoms often begin after stress or trauma, though such a link is not always identifiable and is no longer required for diagnosis. The condition is relatively common in neurology clinics, and misdiagnosis rates in modern series are low.

Treatment

Management begins with a clear, respectful explanation of the diagnosis. Physical therapy is a mainstay for motor symptoms, psychological therapies (including cognitive behavioral therapy) address maintaining factors and comorbid anxiety or depression, and multidisciplinary rehabilitation is used for persistent cases. Prognosis varies; symptoms may remit spontaneously, but a substantial proportion persist without treatment.

History

The condition was historically encompassed by the term "hysteria," studied by Charcot and Freud; the name "conversion" reflects the psychoanalytic idea that psychological conflict is converted into bodily symptoms. Modern approaches are biopsychosocial, emphasizing brain network dysfunction, attention and expectation.
