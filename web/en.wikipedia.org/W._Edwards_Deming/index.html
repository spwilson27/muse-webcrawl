W. Edwards Deming

William Edwards Deming (14 October 1900 – 20 December 1993) was an American business theorist, economist, engineer, and statistician best known for his work on quality management and statistical process control. His lectures in postwar Japan are widely credited with shaping the Japanese quality revolution and, through it, modern manufacturing management worldwide.

Deming's teaching transformed postwar Japanese industry.

Career

Trained in electrical engineering and mathematical physics, Deming worked at the U.S. Department of Agriculture and the Census Bureau, where he applied sampling methods to the 1940 census. During the Second World War he taught statistical quality control to American war industries. In 1950 he lectured in Japan at the invitation of the Union of Japanese Scientists and Engineers (JUSE), teaching engineers and executives that improving quality reduces costs and increases productivity and market share — the "chain reaction" he drew on the blackboard at his first meeting.

Japanese industry embraced his methods; the Deming Prize, established in 1951, became Japan's premier quality award. Deming remained comparatively little known in the United States until a 1980 television documentary, If Japan Can... Why Can't We?, sparked American interest. His books Out of the Crisis (1982) and The New Economics (1993) set out his 14 Points for Management, his condemnation of management-by-quota and inspection-based quality, and his System of Profound Knowledge. The plan-do-check-act cycle associated with his name — he called it the Shewhart cycle — became a foundation of total quality management and later of lean and continuous-improvement practice.
