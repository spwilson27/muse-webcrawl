Surface exposure dating

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Surface exposure dating is a collection of geochronological techniques for estimating the length of time that a rock surface has been exposed at or near the Earth's surface. Surface exposure dating is used to date glacial advances and retreats, erosion history, lava flows, meteorite impacts, rock slides, fault scarps, cave development, and other geological events. It is most useful for rocks which have been exposed for between about 1,000 and 5,000,000 years.

Cosmogenic radionuclide dating

The most common technique is cosmogenic radionuclide dating. The Earth is constantly bombarded by primary cosmic rays — high-energy protons and alpha particles originating outside the Solar System — which collide with nuclei in the atmosphere and rock to produce secondary particles and rare cosmogenic nuclides within minerals at and near the surface. After a rock is exposed — for example, when a glacier retreats and leaves a previously buried surface in place — these nuclides accumulate at a rate that depends on latitude, altitude, shielding by topography and snow, and the geometry of the surface. Measuring the concentration of a nuclide in a rock sample, usually by accelerator mass spectrometry, and comparing it against a calibrated production rate gives the exposure age.

Commonly used nuclides include beryllium-10 (half-life about 1.39 million years) and aluminium-26 in quartz, chlorine-36 in carbonate and volcanic rocks, helium-3 in olivine and pyroxene, and neon-21 in quartz. Using two nuclides with different half-lives in the same sample, such as 26Al and 10Be, can reveal complex histories involving periods of burial beneath ice or sediment.

Assumptions and corrections

Ages must be corrected for any erosion of the surface (which removes nuclides and makes the measured age an underestimate), for shielding by snow, soil, or vegetation, for the shielding of the sampling site by surrounding mountains, and for changes in cosmic-ray flux associated with variations in the Earth's magnetic field. Where erosion can be assumed negligible — for instance, on polished bedrock in recently deglaciated terrain or in hyper-arid deserts — ages are most reliable.

Applications

Surface exposure dating has been used to date moraines and reconstruct the timing of the Last Glacial Maximum and subsequent deglaciation in mountain ranges worldwide; to establish eruption ages of young lava flows; to measure catchment-wide erosion rates from nuclide concentrations in stream sediments; to date offset landforms across active faults and thereby derive slip rates; and to date impact structures and ancient shorelines. In Antarctica and in arid regions such as the Atacama Desert, some surfaces have yielded exposure ages of several million years.

Other techniques

Related exposure indicators include the degree of rock weathering, development of desert varnish and weathering rinds, and lichenometry, which uses the growth of lichens on exposed surfaces to estimate exposure over shorter, historical timescales.
