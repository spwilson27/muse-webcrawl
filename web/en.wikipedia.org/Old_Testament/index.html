The Old Testament (OT) is the first division of the Christian Bible, comprising a collection of religious texts that are largely the same as the Hebrew Bible (Tanakh). It contains books written mainly in Hebrew, with some passages in Aramaic, composed over many centuries and completed by roughly the 2nd century BCE. In Christian theology the Old Testament is followed by the New Testament, which Christians regard as its fulfilment.

The number and order of the books differ among Christian traditions. The Protestant canon contains 39 books; Roman Catholic and Eastern Orthodox canons include additional deuterocanonical books (such as Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch, and 1–2 Maccabees) drawn from the Greek Septuagint tradition.

Contents

The Old Testament is conventionally divided into four sections:

  • The Pentateuch (Torah): Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, and Deuteronomy — the creation, the patriarchs, the Exodus from Egypt, and the giving of the Law to Moses.
  • The Historical Books: Joshua, Judges, Ruth, Samuel, Kings, Chronicles, Ezra, Nehemiah, and Esther — the history of Israel from the conquest of Canaan to the return from Babylonian exile.
  • The Wisdom and Poetic Books: Job, Psalms, Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, and the Song of Songs.
  • The Prophetic Books: the major prophets Isaiah, Jeremiah, Lamentations, Ezekiel, and Daniel, and the twelve minor prophets.

Development and canon

The texts were composed, edited, and transmitted over many centuries. The Torah was largely complete by the Persian period; the Prophets were canonized by around the 2nd century BCE, and the Writings (Ketuvim) were finalized later. The Greek translation known as the Septuagint, made in Alexandria from the 3rd century BCE onward, became the scriptures of the early Church and included books not later accepted in the Jewish canon. The Council of Trent (1546) formally affirmed the Catholic canon, while Protestant reformers restricted the Old Testament to the books of the Hebrew canon, classing the others as Apocrypha.

Themes

Central themes include creation, covenant, law, sin and atonement, kingship, prophecy, and the expectation of a Messiah. Christian interpretation traditionally reads the Old Testament christologically, seeing its sacrifices, festivals, and prophecies as types and shadows fulfilled in the New Testament.
