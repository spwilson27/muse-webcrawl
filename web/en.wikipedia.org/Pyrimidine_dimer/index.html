Pyrimidine dimer

Pyrimidine dimers are molecular lesions formed in DNA when adjacent pyrimidine bases — thymine or cytosine — become covalently linked following exposure to ultraviolet (UV) light. The two principal types are cyclobutane pyrimidine dimers (CPDs) and 6-4 photoproducts. These lesions distort the DNA helix, block replication and transcription, and are the principal cause of the mutagenic and carcinogenic effects of sunlight, including the skin cancers associated with sun exposure.

Formation

Absorption of a UV photon (principally in the UVB range, 280–315 nm) excites the double bonds of neighbouring pyrimidines, which react to form a four-membered cyclobutane ring between them — a thymine dimer being the classic example — or a bond between the 6 and 4 positions of adjacent bases, yielding a 6-4 photoproduct. CPDs are the more abundant lesion and form at TT, TC, CT, and CC sites.

Repair

Cells counteract dimers by several routes. Many organisms possess photolyases, light-dependent enzymes that directly reverse CPDs using visible light energy (photoreactivation); placental mammals, including humans, lack functional photolyase and rely instead on nucleotide excision repair, which cuts out a short oligonucleotide containing the lesion and resynthesizes the strand. Translesion synthesis polymerases can bypass dimers during replication, sometimes at the cost of mutations — notably the C→T transitions that constitute the "UV signature" of skin tumours.

Clinical significance

Inherited defects in nucleotide excision repair cause xeroderma pigmentosum, in which patients are exquisitely sensitive to sunlight and develop skin cancers in childhood. Accumulation of unrepaired dimers in the skin of the general population underlies sunburn and contributes to basal cell carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, and melanoma, making dimer prevention — via shade, clothing, and sunscreen — a central public-health measure against skin cancer.
