Grounded theory

Grounded theory is a systematic methodology in the social sciences involving the construction of theories through the methodical gathering and analysis of data. Rather than beginning with a hypothesis to test, the researcher starts with data collection and lets theory emerge from — be "grounded" in — the data itself. The approach was developed by sociologists Barney Glaser and Anselm Strauss in their 1967 book The Discovery of Grounded Theory, written during their studies of dying patients in hospitals.

Method

Key features of grounded theory research include:

  • Constant comparison — each new piece of data is continuously compared with previously coded data and emerging categories.
  • Theoretical sampling — further data are collected based on the developing analysis, to elaborate and refine categories, rather than according to a fixed plan set in advance.
  • Coding — data are broken into incidents and labelled; in Strauss and Juliet Corbin's later codification this proceeds through open, axial, and selective coding, while Glaser favoured open and theoretical coding.
  • Memo-writing — the analyst records ideas about codes and their relationships throughout the study.
  • Saturation — sampling continues until new data no longer add properties to the categories.

Schools and debates

After their initial collaboration, Glaser and Strauss diverged. Glaser insisted on pure emergence and criticized Strauss and Corbin's more procedural version as "forcing" the data; Strauss's school, in turn, emphasized systematic techniques and verification. Later scholars, notably Kathy Charmaz, developed constructivist grounded theory, which treats data and analysis as co-constructed by researcher and participants rather than discovered. Grounded theory is now widely used in sociology, nursing, education, business studies, and information systems research.
