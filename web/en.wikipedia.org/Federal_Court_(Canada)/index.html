Federal Court (Canada)

The Federal Court (French: Cour fédérale) is a Canadian superior court of law, equity, and admiralty, which hears cases arising under federal law. It sits across Canada and is headquartered in Ottawa. Appeals from the Federal Court go to the Federal Court of Appeal and ultimately to the Supreme Court of Canada.

History

The court traces its origins to the Exchequer Court of Canada, created in 1875. In 1971 the Federal Court of Canada was established, with trial and appeal divisions. On 2 July 2003, the two divisions became separate courts: the Federal Court and the Federal Court of Appeal.

Jurisdiction

As a statutory court, the Federal Court may only hear matters assigned to it by federal legislation. Its principal areas of jurisdiction include:

  • Judicial review of decisions of federal boards, commissions, and tribunals
  • Immigration and refugee matters
  • Intellectual property (patents, trademarks, copyright)
  • Admiralty and maritime law
  • Claims by and against the federal Crown
  • National security matters, including security certificates
  • Intergovernmental disputes between the federal and provincial governments

Composition

The court consists of a chief justice and a number of puisne judges appointed by the Governor in Council, together with supernumerary and prothonotaries (judicial officers). Judges of the Federal Court are also members of the Court Martial Appeal Court of Canada. The court's administration is provided by the Courts Administration Service.
