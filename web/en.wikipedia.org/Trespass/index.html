Trespass

Trespass is an area of tort law broadly divided into three groups: trespass to the person, trespass to chattels, and trespass to land. Trespass to land is the most common and best-known type: it consists of the wrongful interference with a person's possession of land, typically by entering or remaining on the land, or placing objects on it, without lawful justification.

Trespass to land

At common law, trespass to land is actionable per se, without proof of damage. The entry may be by the defendant personally, by an object they cause to enter, or by remaining after permission has expired. Even a minimal physical intrusion — stepping onto a corner of a plot — is technically a trespass, though claims are constrained by doctrines such as implied licence (for example, walking up a garden path to knock on a door) and by rights of way and public access legislation. Remedies include damages, injunctions, and in some cases self-help through reasonable force. In many jurisdictions, aggravated forms of trespass are also criminal offences.

Trespass to the person

The three trespasses to the person are assault (causing apprehension of immediate unlawful force), battery (the actual infliction of unlawful force), and false imprisonment (unlawful total restraint of movement). These are among the oldest torts, descending from the medieval writ of trespass vi et armis.

Trespass to chattels

Trespass to chattels is the intentional interference with another's possession of personal property, short of the full dispossession required for the related tort of conversion. In modern case law the doctrine has been applied, sometimes controversially, to electronic intrusions such as bulk unsolicited email and web crawling.

Defences

Consent, necessity, lawful authority, and statutory rights of access are common defences. In England and Wales, the Countryside and Rights of Way Act 2000 created a limited "right to roam" over mapped access land, qualifying the landowner's right to exclude.
