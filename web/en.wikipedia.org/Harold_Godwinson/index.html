Harold Godwinson (c. 1022 – 14 October 1066), also styled Harold II, was the last crowned Anglo-Saxon king of England. The powerful Earl of Wessex and brother-in-law of Edward the Confessor, he was chosen king on Edward's death in January 1066. His nine-month reign was consumed by two invasions: he destroyed a Norwegian army at the Battle of Stamford Bridge but was killed three weeks later at the Battle of Hastings, which began the Norman Conquest of England.

Rise to power

Harold was the second son of Godwin, Earl of Wessex, the most powerful magnate in England, and his Danish wife Gytha. He became Earl of East Anglia in 1045 and succeeded his father as Earl of Wessex in 1053, thereafter effectively ruling England as Edward aged. He campaigned successfully against Gruffydd ap Llywelyn of Wales in 1062–63. Norman sources claim that after a shipwreck in 1064 Harold swore an oath in Normandy to support William's claim to the English throne — the alleged broken oath that William used to justify his invasion and that the Bayeux Tapestry depicts.

Reign and the crisis of 1066

Crowned the day after Edward's death and funeral, Harold faced immediate threats. His exiled brother Tostig raided the coast, then allied with King Harald Hardrada of Norway, who invaded Yorkshire in September 1066. Harold marched north at remarkable speed and annihilated the invaders at Stamford Bridge on 25 September, where both Hardrada and Tostig were killed. Three days later William of Normandy landed at Pevensey. Harold force-marched his army south and met William near Hastings on 14 October. After a day-long battle in which the English shield wall held against repeated attacks, Harold was killed — tradition says by an arrow in the eye — and his army collapsed.

Legacy

Harold's death ended Anglo-Saxon kingship and transformed England's language, aristocracy and political order. Later medieval writers treated him with surprising sympathy, and legends grew that he had survived Hastings and lived as a hermit. His body was identified, according to Norman accounts, by his mistress Edith Swan-neck and buried at Waltham Abbey, the church he had refounded.
