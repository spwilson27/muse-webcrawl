Foot (prosody)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In verse, a foot (also called a metron or metrical foot) is a basic unit of poetic meter consisting of a combination of stressed and unstressed syllables. The term is the English translation of the Greek pous and Latin pes. A line of verse is described by its meter in terms of the kind of foot it uses and the number of feet per line: a line of five iambs, for instance, is iambic pentameter.

Classical and English usage

In classical Greek and Latin poetry, meter was quantitative, based on syllable length (long and short) rather than stress, and the foot comprised long and short syllables: the dactyl (long–short–short) and its equivalents. English verse is accentual-syllabic, so feet are counted by stress patterns; the concept was imported into English prosody during the Renaissance.

Line lengths

Lines are named by the number of feet they contain: monometer (one), dimeter (two), trimeter (three), tetrameter (four), pentameter (five), hexameter (six), and so on. Iambic pentameter is the dominant meter of English narrative and dramatic verse, used by Chaucer, Shakespeare, and Milton.

Criticism

Some prosodists argue that the foot is an abstraction that does not correspond to how verse is heard, and alternative approaches — such as generative metrics and beat prosody — analyse rhythm in terms of stress maxima and phrasing. Nevertheless, the foot remains the standard descriptive vocabulary for classical and much English meter.
