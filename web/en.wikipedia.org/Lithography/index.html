Lithography

Lithography (from Greek lithos, "stone," and graphein, "to write") is a planographic method of printing originally based on the immiscibility of oil and water. The printing is done from a flat stone (lithographic limestone) or a metal plate with a smooth surface. It was invented in 1796 by the German author and actor Alois Senefelder as a cheap method of publishing theatrical works.

Principle

The image is drawn on the stone or plate with a greasy medium (a lithographic crayon or tusche). The surface is then treated with a mild acid and gum arabic so that the non-image areas become receptive to water and repel ink, while the drawn areas accept oil-based ink. During printing the surface is kept damp; ink adheres only to the greasy image, which is transferred to paper directly or via a rubber blanket in offset printing.

History

Senefelder developed the process in Munich and patented it in England in 1799. Through the nineteenth century lithography became the dominant medium for commercial illustration, maps, sheet music, and posters. Color lithography (chromolithography), using a separate stone for each color, produced the richly colored trade cards, labels, and posters of artists such as Jules Chéret and Toulouse-Lautrec. Major art movements adopted it for fine-art prints: Daumier, Delacroix, Manet, Munch, and Picasso all made celebrated lithographs.

Offset lithography

In offset printing, developed around 1904 for paper (after earlier use on tin), the inked image is first transferred ("offset") to a rubber blanket cylinder and then to the sheet. Offset lithography became the standard process for newspapers, books, packaging, and commercial printing through the twentieth century.

Photolithography

The term is also used for photolithography, the microfabrication process that patterns thin films on semiconductor wafers using light — the foundational technology of integrated-circuit manufacturing. Successive generations, from contact printing to deep-ultraviolet and extreme-ultraviolet (EUV) lithography, have driven the shrinking of transistor dimensions.
