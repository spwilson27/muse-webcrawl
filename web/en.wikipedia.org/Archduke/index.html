Archduke

Archduke (German: Erzherzog; feminine form: Archduchess) was the title borne from 1358 by the Habsburg rulers of the Archduchy of Austria, and later by all senior members of that dynasty. It denoted a rank within the Holy Roman Empire above a duke but below a king and emperor.

History

The title was first invented in the Privilegium Maius, a document forged in 1358–1359 by Duke Rudolf IV of Austria, who sought to elevate Austria's status after the Golden Bull of 1356 excluded its ruler from the college of prince-electors. Emperor Frederick III formally recognized the archducal title in 1453, making it hereditary in the House of Habsburg.

Usage

From the 16th century onward, all members of the Habsburg dynasty, including the Austrian imperial family after 1804, bore the title Archduke or Archduchess from birth. The most famous bearer is Archduke Franz Ferdinand of Austria, whose assassination in Sarajevo in June 1914 precipitated the First World War. The use of noble titles was abolished in Austria in 1919.
