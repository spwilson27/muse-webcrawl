Ancestor veneration

Ancestor veneration, or ancestor worship, is a religious practice based on the belief that the spirits of deceased family members continue to exist, retain an interest in the affairs of their descendants, and can influence their fortunes. Practitioners make offerings, maintain altars or tablets for the dead, and observe festivals honouring them, in return seeking their blessing and protection.

In East and Southeast Asia

Ancestor reverence is central to Chinese folk religion and to Confucian filial piety: families keep ancestral tablets, burn incense and offerings, and visit graves during festivals such as Qingming and the Ghost Festival. Related customs are prominent in Vietnam, Korea (where the memorial rite jesa is performed), and Japan, where Buddhist memorial rites and household altars (butsudan) honour the dead.

Elsewhere

Veneration of ancestors also appears in many African traditional religions, in Malagasy practice (such as the famadihana reburial ceremony), in Roman religion (the household lares and di Manes), and in various Indigenous traditions worldwide. Elements survive within world religions in the commemoration of saints and prayers for the dead.

Interpretation

Scholars debate whether such practices constitute "worship" of the dead as gods or reverent commemoration; within Confucian thought they are usually described as expressions of gratitude and filial duty rather than deity worship, and the distinction was at the heart of the Chinese Rites Controversy between Jesuit missionaries and Rome in the 17th–18th centuries.
