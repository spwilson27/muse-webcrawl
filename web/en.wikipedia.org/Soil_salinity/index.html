Soil salinity

White salt efflorescence on salinised soil

Soil salinity is the salt content in the soil; the process of increasing the salt content is known as salinisation. Salts occur naturally within soils and water, but human activities — especially irrigation with saline water and land clearing that raises water tables — can concentrate salts to levels that damage crops and degrade land. Salinisation is a major form of land degradation worldwide, affecting an estimated one-fifth of irrigated land.

Causes

Primary salinisation results from natural processes: weathering of salt-containing minerals, deposition of sea salts by wind and rain, and seawater intrusion in coastal areas. Secondary salinisation is human-induced. Irrigation water always contains some dissolved salts; when water evaporates or is taken up by plants, the salts remain and accumulate. Clearing deep-rooted perennial vegetation can also raise the water table, bringing stored salts toward the surface — a severe problem in parts of Australia.

Effects on plants

High salinity makes it harder for plant roots to extract water (osmotic stress) and can cause specific ion toxicities from sodium and chloride. Excess sodium also disperses clay particles, destroying soil structure and reducing infiltration. Salt-tolerant plants are called halophytes.

Measurement and management

Salinity is usually measured as the electrical conductivity of a saturated soil extract (ECe), expressed in decisiemens per metre. Soils with ECe above about 4 dS/m are classed as saline. Management includes leaching salts below the root zone with good-quality water, installing drainage, applying gypsum to sodic soils, and growing salt-tolerant crops.
