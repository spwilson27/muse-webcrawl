String theory

In physics, string theory is a theoretical framework in which the point-like particles of particle physics are replaced by one-dimensional objects called strings. String theory describes how these strings propagate through space and interact with each other. On distance scales larger than the string scale, a string looks just like an ordinary particle, with its mass, charge and other properties determined by the vibrational state of the string. One of the vibrational states corresponds to the graviton, the hypothetical particle carrying the gravitational force, making string theory a candidate theory of quantum gravity.

History

String theory originated in the late 1960s as a theory of the strong nuclear force, with the Veneziano amplitude and the work of Yoichiro Nambu, Holger Bech Nielsen and Leonard Susskind. Abandoned for hadrons after the rise of quantum chromodynamics, it was revived in 1974 when John Schwarz and Joël Scherk proposed that a massless spin-2 mode of the string could be the graviton. The "first superstring revolution" followed in 1984, when Michael Green and Schwarz showed that certain string theories are free of anomalies. Five consistent superstring theories were identified, and in 1995 Edward Witten proposed that they are limits of a single eleven-dimensional framework, M-theory — the "second superstring revolution."

Core ideas

  • Extra dimensions: consistency requires more dimensions than the observed four — ten for superstring theories, eleven for M-theory. The extra dimensions are typically compactified on tiny Calabi–Yau manifolds.
  • Branes: extended objects (D-branes) on which open strings can end; our universe may itself be a brane.
  • Dualities: equivalences between apparently different theories (T-duality, S-duality) that reveal a single underlying structure.
  • AdS/CFT correspondence: Juan Maldacena's 1997 duality between string theory in anti-de Sitter space and a conformal field theory on its boundary — the most concrete realisation of the holographic principle.

Criticism and status

String theory has generated deep mathematics and influential ideas, but it has not yet produced experimentally testable predictions; the enormous number of possible compactifications (the "landscape," estimated at 10500 vacua) makes selecting our universe's configuration difficult. Critics such as Lee Smolin and Peter Woit argue the field is insufficiently constrained by evidence, while supporters regard it as the most promising path toward a unified theory of quantum mechanics and general relativity.
