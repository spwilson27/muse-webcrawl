Glacial lake

A glacial lake is a body of water whose basin and water supply originate from a glacier, either past or present. Glacial lakes form through processes such as glacial erosion, which carves depressions in bedrock, and deposition, in which moraine material dams meltwater. Many of the world's largest lake districts — including the Great Lakes of North America, the lakes of Finland, and the alpine lakes of Switzerland — occupy basins scoured by Pleistocene ice sheets.

Formation

Glacial lakes arise in several ways. Ice can gouge out hollows in the underlying rock, a process called glacial plucking and abrasion, leaving basins that fill with meltwater when the ice retreats. Ridges of unsorted debris called moraines may block valleys and impound water. Depressions left by melting blocks of buried ice, known as kettle holes, form small lakes and ponds. Water may also be held directly against the ice margin as a proglacial lake, dammed by the glacier itself.

Characteristics

Glacial lakes are often distinctive for their milky turquoise colour, caused by finely ground rock flour suspended in the water. This sediment scatters light and limits biological productivity, so many glacial lakes are oligotrophic. Famous examples include Lake Louise and Moraine Lake in Canada, Lake Tekapo in New Zealand, and the ribbon lakes of the English Lake District.

Hazards

Lakes dammed by moraines or ice can drain suddenly in glacial lake outburst floods, posing serious risks to settlements downstream, particularly in the Himalaya and the Andes.
