Durbin Amendment

The amendment regulates interchange fees on debit card transactions

The Durbin Amendment is a provision of the United States Dodd–Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act of 2010, named for Senator Dick Durbin of Illinois, who introduced it. It caps the interchange fees that large banks may charge merchants for processing debit card transactions.

Provisions

The amendment directed the Federal Reserve to ensure that debit interchange fees charged by issuers with assets over $10 billion are "reasonable and proportional" to the cost of processing. The Federal Reserve's implementing rule, Regulation II, set a cap of 21 cents per transaction plus 5 basis points of the transaction value, with a possible extra cent for fraud-prevention costs. The amendment also required debit cards to be usable on at least two unaffiliated payment networks and allowed merchants to set minimums for credit card purchases and offer discounts for cash.

Effects and debate

Before the amendment, average debit interchange was roughly 44 cents per transaction. Supporters, mainly retailers, argued the cap lowered costs that had been set by the Visa–Mastercard duopoly. Banks argued the lost revenue led to higher account fees and the disappearance of free current accounts and debit rewards programmes. Studies differ on how much of the merchants' savings was passed on to consumers in lower prices.
