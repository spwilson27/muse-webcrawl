Constitution of South Korea

The National Assembly of South Korea in Yeouido, Seoul.

The Constitution of the Republic of Korea is the supreme law of South Korea. It was first adopted on 17 July 1948, shortly before the establishment of the Republic of Korea on 15 August 1948, and has been amended nine times since, most recently in 1987.

History

The original 1948 constitution was drafted under the supervision of the United States Army Military Government in Korea and established a presidential system, although early drafts had contemplated a parliamentary cabinet system. Over the following decades the constitution was repeatedly revised, often to suit the political needs of incumbent rulers during the authoritarian periods of the First through Fifth Republics.

The current constitution, that of the Sixth Republic, was adopted by referendum on 28 October 1987 during the democratic transition that followed the June Democratic Uprising. It provided for the direct election of the president to a single five-year term, strengthened the National Assembly, restored judicial review, and expanded guarantees of civil liberties.

Structure and principles

The constitution consists of a preamble, 130 articles, and supplementary provisions. Its opening article declares that the Republic of Korea is a democratic republic and that its sovereignty resides in the people. It affirms the aspiration for peaceful reunification with North Korea and guarantees a broad catalogue of fundamental rights, including equality, personal liberty, freedom of speech, press, assembly and religion, and the right to education and work.

Government is divided among the executive, headed by the President; the unicameral National Assembly; and the judiciary, which includes the Supreme Court and a separate Constitutional Court established in 1988 to review the constitutionality of laws, adjudicate impeachments, and protect constitutional rights.

Amendment

Amendments require a proposal by the President or a majority of the National Assembly, approval by two-thirds of the Assembly, and ratification by a national referendum. Periodic debates over constitutional reform, including proposals to introduce a four-year, renewable presidential term, have continued into the twenty-first century.
