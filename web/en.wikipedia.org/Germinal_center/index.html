Germinal center

Germinal center
LocationSecondary lymphoid organs (lymph nodes, spleen, Peyer's patches)
Cell typesB cells (centroblasts, centrocytes), follicular dendritic cells, T follicular helper cells
FunctionSite of B-cell proliferation, somatic hypermutation, class-switch recombination and affinity selection
DurationForms within ~1 week of immunization; peaks around days 10–14

A germinal center within a lymph node follicle.

A germinal center (GC) is a specialized microstructure that forms in secondary lymphoid organs — principally the lymph nodes, spleen and Peyer's patches of the gut — during an immune response to a T cell-dependent antigen. Germinal centers are the sites where B cells proliferate, mutate their immunoglobulin genes, and are selected for high-affinity binding to antigen, thereby generating long-lived plasma cells and memory B cells that underpin durable humoral immunity.

Structure

A mature germinal center is polarized into two zones. The dark zone, oriented toward the T-cell area of the follicle, is densely packed with rapidly dividing B cells called centroblasts. The light zone, facing the mantle zone, contains smaller, non-dividing B cells called centrocytes intermingled with a network of follicular dendritic cells (FDCs) and T follicular helper (Tfh) cells. The chemokine CXCL12 retains centroblasts in the dark zone, while CXCL13, produced by FDCs, organizes the light zone.

Process

Somatic hypermutation

In the dark zone, centroblasts proliferate rapidly and the enzyme activation-induced cytidine deaminase (AID) introduces point mutations into the variable regions of their immunoglobulin genes at a rate roughly a million times higher than background — a process called somatic hypermutation. This generates a diverse pool of B-cell receptors with altered affinities for the antigen.

Selection

Centroblasts then migrate to the light zone as centrocytes, where they compete to capture antigen displayed as immune complexes on the processes of follicular dendritic cells. Cells whose mutated receptors bind antigen with high affinity internalize and present it to Tfh cells, receiving survival signals in return; low-affinity or autoreactive cells fail to obtain help and die by apoptosis, their remains cleared by tingible body macrophages. Successful cells either exit as plasma cells or memory B cells, or recycle back to the dark zone for further rounds of division and mutation.

Class-switch recombination

Within the germinal center, AID also mediates class-switch recombination, exchanging the constant region of the antibody heavy chain (for example from IgM to IgG, IgA or IgE) while preserving antigen specificity, tailoring the effector function of the antibody to the type of pathogen.

Clinical significance

Germinal center reactions are central to the effectiveness of most vaccines. Abnormalities of the germinal center underlie several diseases: many B-cell lymphomas, including follicular lymphoma, Burkitt lymphoma and diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, arise from germinal center B cells and carry the molecular signatures of AID activity. Dysregulated germinal center responses also contribute to autoimmunity, as in systemic lupus erythematosus, where autoreactive clones escape selection.
