World War I reparations

World War I reparations were payments imposed on Germany and its allies after the First World War, principally under Article 231 — the so-called 'war guilt' clause — and related articles of the Treaty of Versailles (1919).

Amounts and plans

In 1921 the London Schedule set Germany's liability at 132 billion gold marks. The Dawes Plan (1924) and Young Plan (1929) restructured payments and linked them to American loans. Payments faltered during the Great Depression; the Lausanne Conference of 1932 effectively ended them, and Germany had paid roughly 20 billion marks in cash and kind.

Effects

Reparations became a central grievance in Weimar politics and were blamed — though historians debate the extent — for hyperinflation in 1923 and for fuelling extremism. John Maynard Keynes criticised them in The Economic Consequences of the Peace (1919). Final payments on related bonds were made by reunified Germany in 2010.
