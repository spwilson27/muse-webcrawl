Civil war

A civil war is a war between organized groups within the same state, fought over control of the government, secession of a region, or major changes in policy. Civil wars are among the most destructive forms of conflict, frequently causing large numbers of deaths, mass displacement, and long-lasting political and economic damage.

Definitions

Scholars commonly define civil war as internal armed conflict involving the state as a principal party and reaching a threshold of battle deaths—often 1,000 in total, with at least 100 on each side, in datasets such as the Correlates of War project. International humanitarian law uses the broader term "non-international armed conflict", governed by Common Article 3 of the Geneva Conventions and the 1977 Additional Protocol II.

Causes

Research by scholars such as James Fearon, David Laitin, and Paul Collier associates civil war onset with poverty, weak state institutions, rough terrain, large populations, and dependence on natural resource exports, with ethnic and religious grievances providing mobilizing frames. Wars often persist because of war economies, external support, and the commitment problems that make negotiated settlements hard.

Examples

Notable civil wars include the English Civil War (1642–51), the American Civil War (1861–65), the Russian Civil War (1917–22), the Chinese Civil War (1927–49), the Spanish Civil War (1936–39), and more recent conflicts in Syria, Yemen, Ethiopia, and Myanmar. Since 1945 civil wars have greatly outnumbered interstate wars.
