The Burma Railway, also known as the Death Railway, the Siam–Burma Railway, and the Thailand–Burma Railway, is a 415-kilometre (258 mi) railway between Ban Pong, Thailand, and Thanbyuzayat, Burma (now Myanmar), built from 1940 to 1943 by the Empire of Japan during World War II to support its forces in the Burma campaign. The line crossed the River Kwai at Kanchanaburi over the bridge made famous by the 1952 novel and 1957 film The Bridge on the River Kwai.

Construction and death toll

Construction was carried out by forced labour: about 60,000 Allied prisoners of war — British, Australian, Dutch, and American — and more than 200,000 Asian labourers (romusha) recruited or coerced across Southeast Asia. Working in remote jungle under brutal conditions with minimal food and medical care, labourers suffered from cholera, dysentery, beriberi, and tropical ulcers. An estimated 12,000–13,000 Allied POWs and 90,000 or more Asian labourers died. Completed in October 1943 under intense schedule pressure ("speedo" work periods), the railway carried supplies until Allied bombing and the war's end put it out of service.

Aftermath and legacy

After the war, the remains of POW dead were concentrated in cemeteries at Kanchanaburi, Chungkai, and Thanbyuzayat maintained by the Commonwealth War Graves Commission. Sections of the Thai end still operate as a scenic railway, and memorials and museums, including the Thailand–Burma Railway Centre, commemorate the dead. The construction was prosecuted as a war crime in postwar trials of Japanese officers.
