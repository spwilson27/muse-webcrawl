Conservation of energy

In physics and chemistry, the law of conservation of energy states that the total energy of an isolated system remains constant; it is said to be conserved over time. Energy can neither be created nor destroyed; rather, it can only be transformed or transferred from one form to another. For instance, chemical energy is converted to kinetic energy in the explosion of a stick of dynamite.

History

Ancient philosophers such as Thales of Miletus had an idea of the conservation of some underlying substance of which everything is made. The mechanical equivalence of work and heat was established in the 1840s by James Prescott Joule, Julius Robert von Mayer, and Hermann von Helmholtz, leading to the first law of thermodynamics. Émilie du Châtelet and others had earlier clarified the conservation of vis viva (kinetic energy) in collisions.

First law of thermodynamics

The law of conservation of energy is the first law of thermodynamics, often expressed as ΔU = Q − W: the change in internal energy of a closed system equals the heat supplied to the system minus the work done by it.

Noether's theorem

Emmy Noether proved in 1918 that energy conservation follows from the time-translation symmetry of physical laws: the fact that the laws of physics do not change over time implies that energy is conserved. In general relativity and in cosmology, defining global energy conservation requires care because of the expansion of the universe and the absence of a global time symmetry.

Mass–energy equivalence

Einstein's relation E = mc² shows that mass and energy are interchangeable aspects of a single quantity, mass–energy, which is what is conserved in relativistic physics and nuclear reactions.
