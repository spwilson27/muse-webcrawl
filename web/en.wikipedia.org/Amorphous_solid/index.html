Amorphous solid

In condensed matter physics and materials science, an amorphous solid (or non-crystalline solid) is a solid that lacks the long-range order characteristic of a crystal. In some older books the term has been used synonymously with glass; today "glass" more precisely refers to an amorphous solid that undergoes a glass transition on heating towards the liquid state. Examples of amorphous solids include window glass, many polymers such as polystyrene, and foods such as cotton candy.

Structure

Amorphous materials have an internal structure made of interconnected structural blocks, but no periodic arrangement of atoms over long distances. X-ray and neutron diffraction of amorphous solids produce broad diffuse halos rather than the sharp Bragg peaks of crystals. Although local ordering may extend over a few atomic spacings, it does not produce diffraction peaks.

Glass transition

Most amorphous solids produced by cooling a liquid pass through a glass transition, a temperature range (Tg) over which the material changes from a hard, brittle "glassy" state to a viscous or rubbery state. Below Tg, molecular motion is severely constrained; above it, segments of molecules can rearrange.

Applications

Amorphous semiconductors such as amorphous silicon are used in thin-film transistors and solar cells. Metallic glasses (bulk metallic glasses) combine high strength with elasticity and are used in sporting goods and medical devices. Amorphous pharmaceuticals are often more soluble than their crystalline counterparts, improving drug bioavailability.
