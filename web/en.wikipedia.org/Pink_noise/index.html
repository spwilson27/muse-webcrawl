Pink noise

Pink noise, also called 1/f noise or flicker noise, is a signal or process with a frequency spectrum such that the power spectral density is inversely proportional to the frequency. In pink noise, each octave interval carries an equal amount of noise energy. It is named for the pink appearance of visible light with this power spectrum.

Pink noise power decreases by about 3 dB per octave as frequency rises.

Properties

Unlike white noise, which has equal power per unit frequency, pink noise has equal power per octave, so it sounds balanced to human hearing, which perceives frequencies logarithmically. It resembles many natural sounds, such as rainfall, wind, and waterfalls, and is often described as softer and less harsh than white noise.

Occurrence

1/f noise appears in a remarkable range of systems: electronic devices (where it is called flicker noise), the loudness fluctuations of music, heartbeats, neural activity, traffic flow, and the brightness variations of astronomical objects. The ubiquity of pink noise, and the difficulty of explaining it with a single mechanism, has made it a longstanding scientific puzzle.

Generation and uses

Pink noise can be generated by filtering white noise with a 1/f filter, or by algorithms such as the Voss–McCartney algorithm. It is used in audio testing and loudspeaker measurement, in sound masking, and in studies of sleep, where some research suggests steady pink noise may aid sleep stability.
