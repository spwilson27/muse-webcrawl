Sepoy

Sepoy (from Persian sipāhī, "soldier") was the designation given to an Indian infantryman employed by the European colonial powers, principally the British East India Company and later the British Indian Army. The term later became the standard rank name for a private soldier in the armies of India and Pakistan.

History

The French East India Company under Joseph François Dupleix was the first European power to raise sepoy units in significant numbers in the 1740s; the British soon followed. By the early 19th century the East India Company's three presidency armies — Bengal, Madras, and Bombay — employed well over 200,000 sepoys, far outnumbering European troops in India.

Indian Rebellion of 1857

Dissatisfaction among the Bengal Army's sepoys, inflamed by the introduction of cartridges for the Pattern 1853 Enfield rifle rumoured to be greased with animal fat offensive to both Hindu and Muslim soldiers, erupted into the Indian Rebellion of 1857, long known in Britain as the Sepoy Mutiny. After its suppression, the Company's armies were transferred to the British Crown.

Later service

Sepoys served with distinction in campaigns across Asia and Africa and in both World Wars. The term survives today as the lowest enlisted rank in the Indian and Pakistani armies.
