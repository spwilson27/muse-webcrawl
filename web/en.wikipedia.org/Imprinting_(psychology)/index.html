Imprinting (psychology)

Imprinting is a form of rapid, largely irreversible learning that occurs during a brief critical or sensitive period early in an animal's life, in which the young animal forms an attachment to, or preference for, the first moving object it encounters — normally its parent.

Lorenz's studies

The phenomenon was brought to scientific prominence by Austrian ethologist Konrad Lorenz in the 1930s, who showed that greylag goslings would follow him and treat him as a parent figure if he was the first moving object they saw after hatching. Oskar Heinroth had earlier noted the effect, and Douglas Spalding had described similar following behaviour in chicks in the nineteenth century.

Types

Filial imprinting produces the following response and social attachment of the young bird. Sexual imprinting, occurring somewhat later, shapes later mate preferences; birds reared by foster species may court members of the foster species as adults. Imprinting-like processes have been discussed in mammals, though the attachment systems of mammals are generally more flexible.

Mechanisms and significance

Neurobiological studies, especially in domestic chicks, implicate the intermediate and medial mesopallium in the rapid learning. Imprinting is a classic example in the nature–nurture debate of an innate predisposition channelled by early experience, and it influenced theories of attachment in developmental psychology.
