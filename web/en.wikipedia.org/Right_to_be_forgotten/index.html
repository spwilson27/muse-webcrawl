Right to be forgotten

Right to be forgotten
Also calledRight to erasure
Key rulingGoogle Spain v AEPD and González (2014)
StatuteGDPR Article 17 (EU, 2016)
ScopePersonal data no longer necessary, consent withdrawn, etc.

The right to be forgotten, or right to erasure, is the right of individuals under data-protection law to have personal data about them removed — from data controllers' systems and, in its search-engine form, delisted from results for searches on their name — when the data is inadequate, irrelevant, no longer relevant, or excessive.

Google Spain ruling

In May 2014 the Court of Justice of the European Union ruled in Google Spain SL v Agencia Española de Protección de Datos and Mario Costeja González that search engines are data controllers under EU law and must, on request, remove links to pages containing personal information that is inadequate, irrelevant or excessive in relation to the purposes of processing — even if the underlying publication was lawful. Costeja González had objected to search results linking his name to a 1998 newspaper notice of a debt-related property auction.

GDPR codification

The General Data Protection Regulation (Regulation (EU) 2016/679), applicable from May 2018, codified a broader right to erasure in Article 17, covering cases where data is no longer necessary, consent is withdrawn, processing is unlawful, or the individual objects — balanced against freedom of expression and legal obligations to retain records.

Debate and reach

Critics argue the right threatens freedom of expression and the historical record, and raises the risk of a "Streisand effect". A 2019 CJEU ruling (Google v CNIL) held that delisting need only apply to EU versions of a search engine, not globally, while member states may apply their own balancing tests. Outside Europe the concept has limited recognition: the United States generally protects search results under the First Amendment, though sectoral erasure rights exist (for example for minors' social-media posts in California).
