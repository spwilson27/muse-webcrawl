Basil

Basil (Ocimum basilicum)
FamilyLamiaceae (mint family)
GenusOcimum
OriginTropical regions from Central Africa to Southeast Asia
UsesCulinary herb; essential oil

Basil (Ocimum basilicum), also called great basil, is a culinary herb of the mint family Lamiaceae. Native to tropical regions from Central Africa to Southeast Asia, it is grown worldwide for its aromatic leaves, which are a defining ingredient of Italian cooking and are also widely used in Southeast Asian cuisines.

Description

Sweet basil is a tender annual plant with green (sometimes purple) stems, ovate glossy leaves, and small white flowers borne in whorls. The leaves contain aromatic essential oils dominated by compounds such as eugenol, linalool, and methyl chavicol, whose proportions give the many cultivars their distinctive scents — from the clove-like note of Thai basil to the citrus of lemon basil.

Culinary use

In Italian cuisine, fresh basil leaves are the main ingredient of Genoese pesto and are paired with tomato and mozzarella in the Caprese salad and on pizza Margherita. Thai, Vietnamese, and other Southeast Asian cuisines use varieties such as Thai basil, holy basil (tulsi), and lemon basil in curries, stir-fries, and soups. The leaves are usually added at the end of cooking, as heat quickly dissipates their flavor.

Cultural and religious significance

Basil's name derives from the Greek basileus, "king," reflecting its esteemed status. Holy basil (Ocimum tenuiflorum) is sacred in Hinduism and used in Ayurvedic medicine. Basil also features in European folklore, religious ritual, and traditional medicine.
