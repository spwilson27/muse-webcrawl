The Greatest Generation is a term for the demographic cohort born roughly between 1901 and 1927 — the generation that came of age during the Great Depression and fought in, or supported, the Allied war effort in the Second World War. The phrase was popularised in the United States by journalist Tom Brokaw's 1998 book The Greatest Generation.

Characteristics

Members of this generation typically grew up amid economic hardship: the optimism of the 1920s gave way to the mass unemployment of the 1930s, which shaped enduring habits of thrift and an embrace of Franklin D. Roosevelt's New Deal. Around 16 million Americans served in the armed forces during the Second World War, while millions more — including the women symbolised by "Rosie the Riveter" — worked in war industries on the home front.

After the war

Returning veterans used the benefits of the G.I. Bill (Servicemen's Readjustment Act of 1944) to attend college, buy homes and start families, fuelling the post-war economic boom and the baby boom, suburbanisation and the growth of the American middle class. The generation staffed the institutions of the post-war order and dominated American politics for decades: every US president from John F. Kennedy (born 1917) through George H. W. Bush (born 1924) was a member of the cohort.

Usage and criticism

The label, used mainly in the United States, has been criticised as overly laudatory and Americentric — comparable sacrifices were made by the same age cohorts in Britain, the Soviet Union and elsewhere — and as glossing over the era's racial segregation and the experiences of those excluded from post-war prosperity. Historians also note the arbitrariness of generational boundaries.

By the 2020s the cohort had largely passed away; remaining Second World War veterans are in their late nineties or older, and commemorations increasingly focus on preserving their recorded testimony.
