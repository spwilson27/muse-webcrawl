Hardy–Weinberg principle

The Hardy–Weinberg principle, also known as the Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium, model, theorem, or law, states that allele and genotype frequencies in a population will remain constant from generation to generation in the absence of other evolutionary influences. These influences include genetic drift, mate choice, assortative mating, natural selection, sexual selection, mutation, gene flow, meiotic drive, genetic hitchhiking, population bottleneck and founder effect. In the simplest case of a single locus with two alleles denoted A and a with frequencies p and q, the expected genotype frequencies under random mating are:

p2 (AA)  :  2pq (Aa)  :  q2 (aa),   with  p + q = 1

The principle is named after G. H. Hardy and Wilhelm Weinberg, who demonstrated it independently in 1908 (Weinberg's paper was published in German in January of that year; Hardy's brief letter to Science followed in July).

Assumptions

The equilibrium holds for a population that is infinitely large (or large enough that drift is negligible), mates randomly with respect to the locus, and experiences no mutation, migration or selection at that locus. Real populations violate these idealisations to varying degrees, so the principle serves as a null model: significant deviation of observed genotype frequencies from Hardy–Weinberg expectations signals that some evolutionary process, genotyping error or population structure is at work.

Applications

Hardy–Weinberg calculations are used to estimate carrier frequencies for recessive genetic disorders from disease incidence (for example, deducing the frequency of carriers of cystic fibrosis from the frequency of affected births), to test for population stratification in genetic association studies, and as a quality-control check in genotyping. The principle also answered an early objection to Mendelism by showing that dominant alleles do not automatically increase at the expense of recessive ones.

Generalisations

The framework extends to multiple alleles, multiple loci (where linkage disequilibrium decays over generations toward linkage equilibrium), sex-linked genes (which approach equilibrium gradually), and polyploid organisms. William Castle had earlier noted the stability of genotype frequencies under random mating, and the equilibrium condition is sometimes associated with his name as well.
