Cairo Geniza

The Cairo Geniza is a collection of some 300,000–400,000 Jewish manuscript fragments found in the genizah (storeroom) of the Ben Ezra Synagogue in Fustat (Old Cairo), Egypt. A genizah is a repository where worn-out writings bearing the name of God — and, in practice, almost any Hebrew-script document — were stored rather than destroyed. Because the Cairo community deposited papers for nearly a millennium, the Geniza preserves an unmatched archive of medieval Mediterranean life, spanning roughly the 9th to the 19th centuries.

Contents

The fragments include biblical and Talmudic texts, liturgy, poetry (including works of Yehuda Halevi), philosophy, and unique works such as the Damascus Document and the Hebrew original of Ben Sira — alongside an enormous body of documentary material: letters, marriage contracts, court records, merchants' accounts, shopping lists and children's school exercises. Most secular documents are written in Judeo-Arabic, the Arabic vernacular of Egyptian Jews written in Hebrew characters.

Discovery and scholarship

Fragments reached European collectors through the 19th century, but the collection's scholarly importance was established in 1896–97 when Solomon Schechter, alerted by Agnes Lewis and Margaret Gibson's finds, removed the bulk of the Geniza to Cambridge University, forming the Taylor-Schechter Collection. Other large holdings are at the Jewish Theological Seminary in New York and libraries in St. Petersburg and elsewhere. The historian S. D. Goitein devoted his life's work to the documentary Geniza, summarized in his five-volume A Mediterranean Society (1967–1993). Digitization projects have since reunited the worldwide fragments virtually.
