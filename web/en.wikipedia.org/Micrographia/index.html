Micrographia

Author: Robert Hooke
Published: January 1665, London
Publisher: Royal Society

Micrographia: or Some Physiological Descriptions of Minute Bodies Made by Magnifying Glasses is a historically significant book by Robert Hooke, published in January 1665 by the Royal Society. The first major work on microscopy, it was a bestseller that revealed to the public a previously invisible world of magnified fleas, lice, plant tissues, moulds, and crystals through large, detailed copperplate engravings.

Contents

Examining thin slices of cork, Hooke saw a honeycomb of empty compartments, which he called "cells" — the first use of the term in biology. Other famous plates depict a flea and a louse rendered as armoured monsters, the eye of a fly, needle points and razor edges (rough under magnification), snowflakes, and the Moon's surface. The preface advocates the new experimental philosophy and careful instruments.

Impact

Samuel Pepys called it "the most ingenious book that I ever read in my life." Micrographia spurred microscopy across Europe, influenced Antonie van Leeuwenhoek, and demonstrated the publishing power of images in science. It also contains Hooke's speculations on light, combustion, and fossils, including early suggestions that fossils are petrified remains of once-living organisms.
