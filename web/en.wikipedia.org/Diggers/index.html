Diggers

The Diggers were a group of radical religious dissenters in England, sometimes seen as forerunners of modern anarchism, and also associated with the agrarian socialism movement. The Diggers' beliefs were based on the ideas of Gerrard Winstanley, who was a lead theorist of the group. The group was originally known as the True Levellers; their name "Diggers" came from their act of digging and cultivating common land. The Diggers attempted to reform the existing social order with an agrarian lifestyle based on their ideas for the creation of small, egalitarian rural communities. They were one of several dissenting groups that emerged around this time during the English Civil War.

Background

The Diggers emerged during the political upheaval that followed the execution of Charles I in January 1649 and the establishment of the Commonwealth of England. High food prices, unemployment and the enclosure of common land created widespread discontent. Winstanley, a bankrupt cloth merchant turned pamphleteer, had already published theological works arguing that the earth was a "common treasury" given by God to all people, and that private property in land was the root of tyranny and social misery.

The movement must be distinguished from the Levellers, led by John Lilburne, who sought primarily political and legal reform. Winstanley criticised the Levellers for neglecting the economic basis of freedom: without access to land, he argued, political liberty was hollow.

The St George's Hill colony

In April 1649, Winstanley and a small group of followers began digging and planting vegetables on waste land at St George's Hill in Walton-on-Thames, Surrey. They declared that the land belonged to all and invited others to join them. The colony attracted both support and hostility: local landowners and clergy organised harassment and legal actions, and soldiers were sent by General Fairfax to investigate, though Fairfax initially treated them leniently. Further Digger communities sprang up at Cobham Heath in Surrey, Iver in Buckinghamshire, Wellingborough in Northamptonshire, and elsewhere in Kent, Gloucestershire, Nottinghamshire and Middlesex.

The Surrey colonies were eventually destroyed by a combination of lawsuits, violence by local landowners' men, and the burning of the Diggers' houses in 1650. By early 1651 the movement had effectively collapsed.

Beliefs and writings

Winstanley set out the Diggers' programme in pamphlets including The True Levellers Standard Advanced (1649), The New Law of Righteousness (1649) and Fire in the Bush (1650). His most systematic work, The Law of Freedom in a Platform (1652), was addressed to Oliver Cromwell and proposed a commonwealth in which land and resources were held in common, buying and selling of land was abolished, and goods were distributed from common storehouses. Winstanley's theology was rationalist and pantheist in tendency: he identified God with Reason and anticipated a universal restoration of humanity to harmony with creation.

Legacy

The Diggers were rediscovered by historians and political movements in the twentieth century. They have been celebrated by socialists, anarchists and land-reform campaigners as pioneers of communal land ownership. The folk song "The World Turned Upside Down", popularised by Leon Rosselson and Billy Bragg, recounts their story. San Francisco's 1960s anarchist street-theatre collective also took the name Diggers in conscious homage to the English original.
