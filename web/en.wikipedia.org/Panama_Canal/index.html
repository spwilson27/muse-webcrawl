The Panama Canal (Spanish: Canal de Panamá) is an artificial 82-kilometre (51-mile) waterway in Panama that connects the Atlantic Ocean with the Pacific Ocean, cutting across the Isthmus of Panama. It is a key conduit for international maritime trade: ships travelling between the east and west coasts of the Americas save roughly 13,000 km compared with the route around Cape Horn.

Construction

France began work on a sea-level canal in 1881 under Ferdinand de Lesseps, builder of the Suez Canal, but the project collapsed in 1889 amid disease, engineering failures and bankruptcy, with the loss of more than 20,000 workers' lives, mainly to malaria and yellow fever. The United States took over in 1904, adopted a lock-based design, and — after a campaign of mosquito control led by William Gorgas — completed the canal in 1914. The American construction effort still cost over 5,600 lives. Ships are raised 26 metres to Gatun Lake by three sets of locks and lowered again on the far side.

Control and expansion

The canal was operated by the United States and surrounded by the US-controlled Canal Zone until the Torrijos–Carter Treaties of 1977 set a timetable for transfer; Panama assumed full control on 31 December 1999. A major expansion, including a third, larger set of locks able to take "Neopanamax" vessels, opened on 26 June 2016. Water shortages in Gatun Lake, worsened by drought in 2023–24, periodically force reductions in daily transits and vessel drafts.
