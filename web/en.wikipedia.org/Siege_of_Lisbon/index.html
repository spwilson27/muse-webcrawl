Siege of Lisbon

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The siege of Lisbon, from 1 July to 25 October 1147, was the military action that brought the port city of Lisbon under the definitive control of the Kingdom of Portugal after 450 years of Muslim rule. It was one of the few successes of the Second Crusade and a formative event in Portuguese history.

Background

In the spring of 1147, a fleet of Crusaders from England, Flanders, Frisia and the Rhineland — some 164 ships — set out for the Holy Land and put into Porto. King Afonso I of Portugal persuaded them to help him take Lisbon, offering plunder and land. A previous attempt on the city in 1142, also with Crusader help, had failed.

The siege

The combined Portuguese and Crusader forces besieged Lisbon, then a prosperous Muslim trading city on the Tagus, for nearly four months. The attackers built siege towers, catapults and a battering ram, mined the walls, and fought off sorties; Crusader preachers, including the anonymous author of the eyewitness account De expugnatione Lyxbonensi, exhorted the troops. Starvation and the undermining of the walls eventually forced the garrison to negotiate.

Aftermath

The city surrendered on 24–25 October 1147 on terms allowing the inhabitants to leave with their lives, though the terms were partly violated amid plundering. Afonso entered Lisbon, which became the growing kingdom's most important city and eventually its capital. The English Crusader Gilbert of Hastings became its first bishop. Many Crusaders settled in Portugal, while the rest sailed on to the Holy Land the following spring.
