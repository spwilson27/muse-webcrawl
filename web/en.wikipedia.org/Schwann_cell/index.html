Schwann cell

Schwann cells, named after the German physiologist Theodor Schwann, are the principal glial cells of the peripheral nervous system. They support neurons by wrapping around their axons, forming the myelin sheath in myelinated fibres or enclosing groups of small unmyelinated axons in structures known as Remak bundles.

Function

Myelinating Schwann cells each wrap a single segment of one axon in many concentric layers of membrane, creating an insulating sheath interrupted at intervals by short gaps called nodes of Ranvier. This arrangement allows saltatory conduction, in which the action potential jumps from node to node, greatly increasing the speed of nerve impulses compared with unmyelinated fibres of the same diameter.

Non-myelinating Schwann cells, by contrast, cradle multiple thin axons within grooves of their cytoplasm. Schwann cells also guide regeneration: after peripheral nerve injury they dedifferentiate, proliferate, clear debris together with macrophages, and form bands of Büngner that direct regrowing axons back toward their targets — a repair capacity largely absent from the central nervous system, where the analogous myelinating cells are oligodendrocytes.

Development

Schwann cells develop from the neural crest. Their precursors travel along growing peripheral nerves, and their maturation is governed in part by the axonal signal neuregulin-1, which determines whether a cell becomes myelinating and how thick the sheath it produces will be. Transcription factors such as Oct-6 and Krox-20 (Egr2) drive the myelination programme.

Clinical significance

Damage to Schwann cells or their myelin underlies several peripheral neuropathies, including Guillain–Barré syndrome, Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease and chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy. Tumours of Schwann cells include the benign schwannoma (such as the vestibular schwannoma, or acoustic neuroma) and, rarely, malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumours, which are associated with neurofibromatosis type 1. The Schwann cell is also the target of Mycobacterium leprae, the bacterium that causes leprosy.
