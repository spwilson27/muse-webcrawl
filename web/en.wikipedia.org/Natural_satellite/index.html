A natural satellite is, in astronomy, an astronomical body that orbits a planet, dwarf planet, or small Solar System body (or sometimes another natural satellite). Such objects are informally called moons, after Earth's Moon. There are hundreds of known natural satellites in the Solar System, with new ones still being discovered, particularly around the giant planets.

Formation and origins

Several mechanisms are proposed. Regular satellites are thought to have formed from the same collapsing cloud of gas and dust (a circumplanetary disk) as their planet. Earth's Moon is widely believed to have formed from debris after a giant impact between the early Earth and a Mars-sized body called Theia. Irregular satellites, on eccentric and often retrograde orbits, are likely captured asteroids or fragments; Neptune's moon Triton is the classic example of a captured Kuiper belt object.

Characteristics

Many moons are tidally locked, always showing the same face to their planet. Some are geologically remarkable: Jupiter's Io is the most volcanically active body known, Europa and Saturn's Enceladus hide subsurface oceans beneath icy crusts, and Saturn's Titan has a thick nitrogen atmosphere and lakes of liquid methane. Ganymede, the largest moon, exceeds the planet Mercury in diameter.

Distribution

Mercury and Venus have no moons. Earth has one large moon; Mars has two small moons, Phobos and Deimos. The gas and ice giants possess large satellite systems — Saturn's rings and moons, Jupiter's four Galilean moons (Io, Europa, Ganymede, Callisto, observed by Galileo in 1610), and dozens to hundreds of smaller bodies. The dwarf planet Pluto has five known moons, the largest being Charon.
