In biochemistry and pharmacology, the Hill equation is an empirical formula used to describe the fraction of a macromolecule saturated by a ligand as a function of the ligand concentration. It was first derived by Archibald Vivian Hill in 1910 to describe the sigmoidal oxygen-binding curve of haemoglobin.

θ = [L]n / (Kd + [L]n)

where θ is the fraction of binding sites occupied, [L] is the (free) ligand concentration, Kd is an apparent dissociation constant, and n is the Hill coefficient. When n = 1 the equation reduces to a simple hyperbolic binding isotherm equivalent in form to Michaelis–Menten kinetics; n > 1 indicates positive cooperativity, and n < 1 indicates negative cooperativity or heterogeneity of binding sites.

Hill coefficient

The Hill coefficient provides a measure of the steepness of the binding curve and an indication of cooperativity, though it is generally only a lower bound on the number of interacting binding sites, not an exact count. For haemoglobin, which has four subunits, the Hill coefficient is about 2.8–3.0, reflecting strong but incomplete cooperativity. A linearised form, the Hill plot, graphs log(θ/(1−θ)) against log[L]; its slope at half-saturation gives the Hill coefficient.

Applications

The equation is widely used in pharmacology to model dose–response relationships, where the response (such as enzyme activity, ion-channel opening, or a physiological effect) replaces θ and the ligand concentration at half-maximal response is denoted EC50 or IC50. It is also used for analysing allosteric enzymes, transcription-factor binding, and oxygen transport by haemoglobin and myoglobin. Because the equation is empirical, mechanistic models such as the Monod–Wyman–Changeux and Koshland–Némethy–Filmer models are preferred when detailed mechanistic interpretation is required.

History

Hill introduced the equation in 1910, initially suggesting that haemoglobin molecules aggregate; the physical picture was soon corrected, but the equation survived as a convenient description of cooperative binding. The Adair equation, which treats sequential binding to multiple sites with individual constants, provides a more detailed alternative for tetrameric haemoglobin.
