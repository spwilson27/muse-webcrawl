Kaleidoscope

Kaleidoscope
Inventor: David Brewster
Patented: 1817

A kaleidoscope is an optical instrument containing two or more reflecting surfaces (mirrors) tilted toward each other inside a tube, with loose coloured objects — such as beads, glass fragments, or liquid-filled chambers — at one end. When the viewer looks through an eyepiece at the far end, multiple reflections produce symmetrical, mandala-like patterns that shift as the object chamber is rotated.

History

The kaleidoscope was invented by the Scottish scientist David Brewster in 1816 during his experiments on light polarization, and patented in 1817. Although Brewster intended it as a scientific instrument, it became an enormously popular parlour toy; pirated copies vastly outsold authorized versions, and Brewster made little money from it. The name derives from Greek words meaning "observer of beautiful forms".

Design

The angle between the mirrors determines the symmetry of the pattern: mirrors at 45 degrees yield eightfold symmetry, at 60 degrees sixfold, and at 30 degrees twelvefold. Later makers, notably the American glassworker Charles Bush in the 1870s, refined the design with liquid-filled object cells. Modern kaleidoscopes range from cardboard toys to large sculptural works, and kaleidoscopic imagery has influenced art, textiles, and psychedelic design.
