Skin effect

[Image: Cross-section of a conductor showing current density concentrated near the surface]
Skin effect
PhenomenonElectromagnetism
EffectAC current density greatest at conductor surface
CauseSelf-induced eddy currents from changing magnetic field

ExampleCopper at 60 Hz: δ ≈ 8.5 mm; at 1 MHz: δ ≈ 66 µm

The skin effect is the tendency of an alternating electric current (AC) to become distributed within a conductor such that the current density is largest near the surface of the conductor and decreases exponentially with greater depth. The effect causes the effective resistance of the conductor to increase at higher frequencies, since the current uses only a thin surface layer — the "skin" — of the cross-section.

Cause

An alternating current generates an alternating magnetic field within and around the conductor. In the interior, this changing flux induces eddy currents that oppose the flow of current in the centre and reinforce it at the surface. The current density decays with depth d from the surface approximately as e−d/δ, where δ is the skin depth.

δ = √( 2ρ / (ωμ) )

where ρ is the resistivity of the conductor, ω = 2πf is the angular frequency, and μ is its magnetic permeability. The skin depth therefore decreases with the square root of frequency.

Consequences and mitigation

At power frequencies the effect is modest but matters in large busbars and transmission lines, where hollow conductors or bundled conductors are used because little current flows in the core of a thick wire. At radio frequencies the effect dominates: radio-frequency conductors are often hollow tubes, and Litz wire — made of many individually insulated strands woven so each samples all positions in the bundle — is used in inductors to reduce losses at frequencies up to a few megahertz. The effect is also exploited in induction heating and induction furnaces, where surface heating is desirable.

History

The phenomenon was described mathematically by Horace Lamb and Oliver Heaviside in the 1880s, and Lord Kelvin published experimental analyses in 1887–89. It was an early practical concern in the design of high-frequency telegraph cables.
