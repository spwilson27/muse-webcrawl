Limit order

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A limit order specifies the maximum price a buyer will pay, or the minimum price a seller will accept.

A limit order is a type of order used in financial markets to buy or sell a security at a specified price or better. A buy limit order can only be executed at the limit price or lower, and a sell limit order can only be executed at the limit price or higher. This stipulation allows traders to control the prices at which they trade, but it also means the order may never be executed if the market does not reach the limit price.[1]

Limit orders contrast with market orders, in which a security is bought or sold as soon as possible at whatever price is currently available. Because a limit order can sit unfilled in the order book for an extended period, it is one of the principal ways in which traders provide liquidity to a market.

Buy limit orders

A buy limit order is an order to purchase a security at or below a specified price, known as the limit price. For example, an investor who wants to buy shares of a company currently trading at $50 per share might place a limit order at $48. The order will be executed only if the price falls to $48 or lower. If the price never falls that far, the order remains unfilled and eventually expires or is cancelled.

Traders use buy limit orders when they believe the current market price is too high but expect a more favourable price to become available, or when they wish to avoid paying more than a predetermined amount informed by their own valuation of the security.

Sell limit orders

A sell limit order is an instruction to sell a security at or above a specified price. For example, a holder of shares purchased at $40 might place a sell limit order at $55, ensuring the position is sold only if the price reaches that level or higher. This is commonly used to lock in profits at a target price without having to monitor the market continuously.

Execution and priority

When a limit order is placed, it is typically recorded in the exchange's order book and assigned a place in the queue. Priority among orders at the same price is generally determined by the time at which they were received — orders submitted earlier are filled first, a convention known as price–time priority. An order may also be filled partially if only part of the requested quantity is available at the limit price or better.

A limit order guarantees the price (or a better one) but not execution. When a limit order is submitted for immediate execution but the market price prevents it — for example, a buy order placed at or above the current ask — the order may instead be treated as marketable and filled right away at the prevailing price or better. Orders that cannot execute immediately are said to be "posted" to the book, and in many modern electronic markets traders who post orders receive small rebates, while those who take liquidity by crossing the spread pay fees; this arrangement is known as the maker–taker model.

Most limit orders carry a time-in-force instruction. A day order expires at the end of the trading session if unfilled, while a good-til-cancelled (GTC) order remains active until it is executed or the trader cancels it.

Variations

Several order types are built on the limit order concept:

  • Stop orders (stop-loss and stop-buy orders), which are dormant until the market trades at a specified stop price, after which they typically become market orders.
  • Stop-limit orders, which combine a stop trigger with a limit price, so that once triggered the order becomes a limit order rather than a market order.
  • Fill-or-kill and immediate-or-cancel orders, which constrain how much of the order must be filled and how quickly, after which any unexecuted remainder is cancelled.
  • Iceberg orders, in which only a small visible portion of a large limit order is displayed in the order book.
