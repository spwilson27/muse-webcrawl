Cygnus is a northern constellation on the plane of the Milky Way, deriving its name from the Latinised Greek word for swan. It is one of the most recognisable constellations of the northern summer and autumn, and it features a prominent asterism known as the Northern Cross. Cygnus was among the 48 constellations listed by the 2nd-century astronomer Ptolemy, and it remains one of the 88 modern constellations.

Stars

Deneb, its brightest star, is a blue-white supergiant about 2,600 light-years away and forms one corner of the Summer Triangle with Vega and Altair; it marks the swan's tail, while Albireo at the head is among the sky's most admired double stars, showing a striking gold-and-blue colour contrast. Sadr lies at the centre of the cross, surrounded by the diffuse emission of the Gamma Cygni nebula region. The constellation also contains 61 Cygni, the first star whose distance was measured by parallax (by Friedrich Bessel in 1838), and the Kepler mission's field of view, centred largely in Cygnus and Lyra, which yielded thousands of exoplanet candidates.

Deep-sky objects and other features

Lying in the Milky Way, Cygnus is rich in nebulae: the North America Nebula (NGC 7000) near Deneb, the Veil Nebula — a supernova remnant — and the Crescent Nebula. The Great Rift, a band of dark interstellar dust, begins in Cygnus and runs southward. Cygnus X-1, a powerful X-ray source in the constellation, was one of the first objects widely accepted to be a black hole.

Mythology

Greek myth associated Cygnus with several swans, most often Zeus, who took swan form to seduce Leda, and Orpheus transformed after death, placed beside his lyre in the constellation Lyra. The swan also figures in the story of Phaethon's friend Cycnus, who grieved by a river and was changed into a swan.
