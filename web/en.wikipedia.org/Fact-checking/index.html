Fact-checking

FieldJournalism
PurposeVerifying factual assertions in text, speech, or media
Key organizationsInternational Fact-Checking Network (IFCN)
Notable outletsPolitiFact, FactCheck.org, Snopes, Full Fact

Fact-checking is the process of verifying the factual accuracy of questioned reporting and statements. In journalism it takes two principal forms: ante hoc fact-checking, performed internally before publication — historically by dedicated fact-checkers at magazines such as Time and The New Yorker, who verify names, dates, quotations, and claims against primary sources — and post hoc fact-checking, in which independent organizations assess claims already made by politicians, media outlets, and public figures.

Post hoc fact-checking movement

Dedicated fact-checking organizations grew rapidly in the 2000s and 2010s. FactCheck.org (founded 2003, University of Pennsylvania) and PolitiFact (founded 2007, winner of the 2009 Pulitzer Prize for its coverage of the 2008 U.S. election) rate claims on scales such as PolitiFact's Truth-O-Meter ("True" to "Pants on Fire"). The Duke Reporters' Lab counted more than 400 active fact-checking outlets worldwide at the movement's early-2020s peak. The International Fact-Checking Network, founded at the Poynter Institute in 2015, maintains a code of principles covering nonpartisanship, transparency of sources, funding, and methodology.

Methods and effects

Fact-checkers consult primary documents, data, expert sources, and the claimants themselves, then publish evidence-linked verdicts. Research indicates corrections generally reduce misperceptions, though effects can be weakened by repetition of the false claim (the illusory truth effect) and by partisan motivated reasoning; fears of a widespread "backfire effect" have largely not been borne out in later studies. Social media platforms partnered with third-party fact-checkers after 2016 to label disputed content, an arrangement several platforms later scaled back in favour of community-notes systems.

Criticism

Critics question the selection of claims, argue that some verdicts involve subjective judgement rather than verifiable fact, and dispute the neutrality of raters. Fact-checkers respond that transparent sourcing and published corrections policies are the appropriate safeguards.
