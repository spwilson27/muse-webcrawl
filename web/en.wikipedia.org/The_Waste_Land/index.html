The Waste Land

The Waste Land

Author: T. S. Eliot

Published: 1922

Length: 434 lines, five sections

Editor: Ezra Pound

The Waste Land is a poem by T. S. Eliot, published in 1922, regarded as one of the most important poems of the twentieth century and a central work of modernist poetry. Written after the First World War, it portrays a spiritually barren Europe through shifting voices, fragments of myth, and quotations in several languages.

Structure

The poem has five sections — "The Burial of the Dead", "A Game of Chess", "The Fire Sermon", "Death by Water", and "What the Thunder Said" — moving between London scenes, the Fisher King and Grail legends, the hyacinth girl, the clairvoyante Madame Sosostris, and the Thames. Its famous opening line calls April "the cruellest month". Ezra Pound's editing cut the manuscript drastically, and Eliot appended his own notes to the book edition.

Themes and reception

Drawing on fertility myths from Jessie Weston's From Ritual to Romance and Frazer's The Golden Bough, the poem juxtaposes sterility and the hope of renewal. Initially puzzling to many readers, it came to define literary modernism; its closing words are the Sanskrit invocation "Shantih shantih shantih".
