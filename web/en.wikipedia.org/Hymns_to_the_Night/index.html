Hymns to the Night (German: Hymnen an die Nacht) is a cycle of six poems by the German Romantic author Novalis (Friedrich von Hardenberg), first published in 1800 in the journal Athenaeum. Mixing verse and rhythmic prose, the hymns are regarded as a peak of early German Romantic lyric poetry and one of Novalis's most important works.

Background

The cycle was written in 1799–1800 against the background of the death of Novalis's young fiancée, Sophie von Kühn, in 1797 and his mystical experience at her grave. Where the classical tradition associated light with reason and life, Novalis inverts the valuation: night, darkness, and death become the gateway to a higher, spiritual existence, associated with the figure of Christ and with reunion with the beloved.

Structure and themes

The first hymns trace a movement from the realm of light, through grief and a visionary dream at the beloved's grave, toward longing for death as a homecoming. Later hymns widen the meditation into a religious and historical vision, praising Christ and anticipating an eternal kingdom in which night reigns forever. The cycle blends personal lament, Christian devotion, and the Romantic programme of dissolving the boundaries between life and death, finite and infinite.

Influence

The hymns influenced later German literature and music; the third hymn's imagery resonates in Richard Wagner's Tristan und Isolde, and the work has been translated into English several times, including by George MacDonald.
