The eastern gorilla (Gorilla beringei) is a critically endangered species of gorilla and the largest living primate, endemic to the mountainous forests of central Africa (eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo, southwestern Uganda, and Rwanda). It comprises two subspecies: the mountain gorilla (G. b. beringei) of the Virunga volcanoes and Bwindi, and the eastern lowland or Grauer's gorilla (G. b. graueri) of lowland forests in the eastern DRC. The species is distinguished from the western gorilla by its larger size, narrower hands, and darker coloration.

Taxonomy

The eastern gorilla was named by German zoologist Paul Matschie in 1903 as Gorilla beringei, after Captain Robert von Beringe, who shot specimens in the Virunga Mountains in 1902. It was long treated as a subspecies of the western gorilla but was elevated to full species status on genetic and morphological grounds in the 1990s–2000s.

Description

Adult males ("silverbacks," named for the saddle of grey hair on their backs) can stand 1.4–1.85 m (4.6–6.1 ft) tall and weigh 160–210 kg (350–460 lb) in the wild, making the eastern gorilla the largest living primate. Females are about half the male's weight. Mountain gorillas have longer, thicker fur adapted to cold mountain nights; Grauer's gorillas are shorter and stockier with shorter muzzles and larger hands.

Behaviour

Eastern gorillas live in stable family groups led by a dominant silverback, with several females and their offspring. They are primarily herbivorous, feeding on leaves, stems, pith, bark, and seasonally fruit; diet varies by altitude and season. They build nests of vegetation each night. Communication includes vocalisations, chest-beating, and gestures. Mountain gorillas are notably calm and have been habituated to human observers since the research of Dian Fossey beginning in 1967.

Threats and conservation

Both subspecies are listed as Critically Endangered. Mountain gorilla numbers plummeted to about 620 in 1989 but recovered to over 1,000 by 2018 through intensive conservation, earning a downlisting to Endangered for the subspecies; Grauer's gorilla, by contrast, crashed from an estimated 16,900 in the mid-1990s to roughly 3,800 by 2015 due to civil war, mining (including coltan), and bushmeat hunting in the DRC. Gorilla tourism in Rwanda and Uganda funds much of the protection, though disease (including human respiratory infections and Ebola) remains a grave risk.
