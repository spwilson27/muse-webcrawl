Dissolution of the monasteries

Period1536–1541
LocationEngland and Wales
Ordered byHenry VIII
Administered byThomas Cromwell
Houses dissolvedMore than 800

The dissolution of the monasteries, occasionally referred to as the suppression of the monasteries, was the set of administrative and legal processes between 1536 and 1541 by which King Henry VIII disbanded Catholic monasteries, priories, convents and friaries in England and Wales, expropriated their income, disposed of their assets and provided for their former members. The policy came as part of the English Reformation following Henry's break with Rome.

Background

By the 1530s some 900 religious houses in England housed roughly 12,000 people, and monasteries controlled an estimated one-sixth of the cultivated land. Henry's chief minister Thomas Cromwell commissioned a survey of church wealth, the Valor Ecclesiasticus (1535), and visitations that reported scandal and laxity in many houses.

Process

The First Suppression Act of 1536 closed houses with incomes under £200 per year. The surrender of the greater houses followed, accelerated after the Pilgrimage of Grace uprising; abbots of major houses such as Glastonbury and Reading were executed. A Second Suppression Act in 1539 confirmed the surrenders. The Court of Augmentations managed the seized estates, much of which was sold to fund Henry's wars and enrich the gentry.

Effects

The dissolution destroyed much of England's monastic heritage: libraries were dispersed, buildings stripped of lead and stone, and shrines such as that of Thomas Becket at Canterbury were destroyed. Monks and nuns received pensions. The redistribution of land strengthened the gentry and is considered a major factor in the rise of a land market and later political change.
