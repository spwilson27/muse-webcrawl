Jackalope

The jackalope is a mythical animal of North American folklore, described as a jackrabbit with antelope horns. The name is a portmanteau of "jackrabbit" and "antelope". Jackalopes are a staple of tall tales in the American West and are popularly sold to tourists as mounted specimens — in reality, taxidermy fabrications made by attaching deer antlers to a rabbit carcass.

Origins

The jackalope is usually credited to Douglas Herrick, a taxidermist from Douglas, Wyoming, who with his brother produced the first mounted "jackalope" in the 1930s after seeing a rabbit carcass lying beside a set of deer antlers, and sold it to a local hotel. The specimens were so popular that thousands were made; Douglas calls itself the "Jackalope Capital of the World" and issues tongue-in-cheek jackalope hunting licences. The town of Douglas erected a large jackalope statue, and Wyoming even considered making the jackalope its official mythical creature.

Possible natural basis

The legend may have a genuine biological inspiration: rabbits infected with the Shope papilloma virus can grow hard, horn-like tumours (keratinous carcinomas) on their heads and bodies, giving them an antlered appearance. Images of horned rabbits and hares also appear in much older European and Asian art and bestiaries, suggesting the idea of a horned hare long predates the American jackalope.

In culture

Jackalopes appear throughout popular culture — in cartoons, advertisements, video games and as mascots — and are a classic example of a "fearsome critter" of lumberjack folklore, alongside creatures such as the hidebehind and the hoop snake. Similar horned rabbits feature in German folklore as the Wolpertinger.
