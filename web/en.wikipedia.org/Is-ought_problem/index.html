Is–ought problem

The is–ought problem, also known as Hume's law or the is–ought gap, is the claim that one cannot validly derive statements about what ought to be (prescriptive or evaluative statements) solely from statements about what is (descriptive statements). The problem was articulated by the Scottish philosopher David Hume in his A Treatise of Human Nature (1739–40).

Hume's passage

In Book III of the Treatise, Hume observed that in every system of morality he had read, the author proceeds for a while in ordinary reasoning about what is or is not, and then suddenly the conclusions are connected not with "is" and "is not" but with "ought" and "ought not" — a change that, Hume argued, is "of the last consequence" and must be explained rather than assumed. The remark became central to later debates about moral realism, the naturalistic fallacy, and metaethics generally.

Interpretations

Scholars dispute the passage's exact scope: some read it as a purely logical thesis that a normative conclusion requires at least one normative premise; others read it as part of Hume's broader sentimentalism, in which moral distinctions arise from feelings rather than reason. The thesis parallels G. E. Moore's naturalistic fallacy and underlies twentieth-century distinctions between fact and value, including the fact–value distinction in social science promoted by Max Weber.

Responses

Various philosophers have attempted to bridge the gap: John Searle argued that institutional facts (such as promising) entail obligations; Philippa Foot and other neo-Aristotelians ground norms in facts about human nature and flourishing; others simply accept the gap and treat moral premises as supplied by sentiment, convention or practical reason.
