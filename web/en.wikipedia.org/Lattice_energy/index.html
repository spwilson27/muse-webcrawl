Lattice energy

The ionic lattice of sodium chloride

In chemistry, the lattice energy is the energy change upon the formation of one mole of a crystalline ionic compound from its gaseous ions, assumed initially to be infinitely far apart. It is a measure of the cohesive forces binding the ions in the crystal.

Definition and sign conventions

The formation of a lattice from gaseous ions is exothermic, so lattice energy is often reported as the positive energy that would be required to break the crystal apart into gaseous ions. Values are expressed in kilojoules per mole. Lattice energy cannot be measured directly; it is calculated using the Born–Haber cycle or estimated with models such as the Born–Landé equation.

Factors affecting magnitude

Lattice energy increases with the charge on the ions and decreases as ionic radii increase. For example, magnesium oxide, with doubly charged small ions, has a much larger lattice energy than sodium chloride, which accounts for its very high melting point.

Consequences

Large lattice energies explain why ionic compounds tend to have high melting points, hard and brittle crystals, and low solubility when the lattice energy exceeds the hydration energy of the ions.
