Yu the Great (c. 2123 – 2025 BCE, by traditional dating) was a legendary Chinese ruler famed in Chinese mythology for taming the Great Flood and for founding the Xia dynasty, traditionally considered the first dynasty of China. He is one of the most venerated sage-kings of Chinese antiquity, held up by Confucians as a model of selfless diligence and moral virtue.

Taming the flood

According to the classic accounts in texts such as the Book of Documents and Sima Qian's Records of the Grand Historian, China in the time of Emperor Yao was ravaged by a great flood. Yu's father, Gun, was appointed to control it but failed after nine years of building dams and dikes, and was executed by Emperor Shun. Yu took up the task and, instead of damming the waters, spent thirteen years dredging channels and guiding the rivers to the sea. Tradition holds that he was so devoted that he passed his own home three times without entering — a story that became the emblem of dedication to public duty. His success in ordering the waters also allowed him to survey and divide the land into nine provinces.

Founder of Xia

Impressed by Yu's achievement, Shun yielded the throne to him rather than to his own son. Upon Yu's death, his son Qi succeeded him, establishing the principle of hereditary succession and, in traditional historiography, the Xia dynasty. Yu is credited with casting the Nine Tripod Cauldrons, symbols of legitimate rule over the nine provinces.

Historicity and legacy

Scholars debate how much, if any, of Yu's story reflects historical events; Western scholarship long doubted the Xia's historicity, though archaeological work at sites such as Erlitou has revived discussion. In Chinese culture Yu (Da Yu, "Yu the Great") remains an enduring symbol of perseverance and flood control, and his mausoleum near Shaoxing in Zhejiang is a major historic site.
