Gerolamo Cardano (Italian: [dʒeˈrɔːlamo karˈdaːno]; also Girolamo or Geronimo; Latin: Hieronymus Cardanus; 24 September 1501 – 21 September 1576) was an Italian polymath whose interests ranged over mathematics, medicine, biology, physics, chemistry, astrology, astronomy, philosophy, and gambling. He was one of the most influential scientists of the Renaissance and a central figure in the history of algebra and probability.

Life

Cardano was born in Pavia, the illegitimate son of Fazio Cardano, a jurist and friend of Leonardo da Vinci. Despite a sickly childhood and his mother's attempt to abort him, he studied medicine at the universities of Pavia and Padua, graduating in 1525. The Milan College of Physicians long refused him admission because of his illegitimate birth and combative temperament, but he eventually built a celebrated medical practice, treating cardinals and nobles and becoming rector of the University of Pavia. He accepted chairs at Pavia and Bologna, travelled to Scotland in 1552 to treat Archbishop John Hamilton's asthma, and published prolifically — his complete works fill ten folio volumes.

His later life was darkened by tragedy. His elder son Giambattista was executed in 1560 for poisoning his wife; his younger son Aldo became a gambler and robbed him; and in 1570 Cardano was arrested by the Inquisition, apparently for having cast a horoscope of Jesus Christ, and spent several months in prison in Bologna. Released through the intervention of powerful patrons, he moved to Rome, received a papal pension, and was admitted to the College of Physicians there. Legend holds that he starved himself to die on the date he had astrologically predicted for his own death.

Mathematics

Cardano's Ars Magna ("The Great Art," 1545) was the first Latin treatise devoted to algebra and one of the landmark books of Renaissance science. It published the solutions of the cubic and quartic equations: Cardano had obtained the cubic solution from Niccolò Tartaglia in 1539 under an oath of secrecy, but published it after learning that Scipione del Ferro had found it earlier — provoking a bitter public feud with Tartaglia. The quartic solution was the work of Cardano's brilliant servant and student Lodovico Ferrari. In manipulating the cubic formula, Cardano encountered square roots of negative numbers, the first recorded appearance of what became complex numbers, which he found "as subtle as [they are] useless."

In his Liber de Ludo Aleae ("Book on Games of Chance," written c. 1564, published 1663), Cardano gave the first systematic treatment of probability, defining it as a ratio of favourable to total outcomes and analysing dice and card games from his own experience as a compulsive gambler.

Other work

Cardano published two great encyclopedias of natural science, De subtilitate (1550) and De rerum varietate (1557), wrote an influential autobiography, De propria vita (1576), and gave his name to the Cardan shaft (universal joint), the Cardan grille used in cryptography, and Cardano's rings. His medical writings include the first clinical description of typhoid fever.
