The Fergana Valley is a valley in Central Asia, spread across eastern Uzbekistan, southern Kyrgyzstan and northern Tajikistan. It is one of the most fertile and densely populated regions in Central Asia, watered by the Syr Darya and its tributaries and enclosed by the Tian Shan and Pamir-Alay mountain ranges.

Geography

The valley is roughly triangular, narrowing at its western end near Khujand where the Syr Darya exits toward the Kyzyl Kum desert. Its irrigated plains support intensive cultivation of cotton, wheat, fruits, and vegetables. The climate is continental, with hot summers and cold winters, and irrigation from mountain rivers has sustained oasis agriculture for millennia.

History

The valley was part of ancient Sogdiana and Bactria's sphere and lay on the northern branch of the Silk Road, with cities such as Kokand, Margilan, and Khujand serving as trading entrepôts. Alexander the Great founded Alexandria Eschate (modern Khujand) at its western edge. The region later passed through Arab, Persian, Mongol, and Timurid rule; the Khanate of Kokand dominated the valley in the 18th and 19th centuries before Russian annexation in 1876 created Fergana Oblast.

In the Soviet period, the valley was divided among the Uzbek, Kyrgyz, and Tajik republics with famously interlocking borders and enclaves, a legacy that has complicated relations since independence in 1991. The valley is the historical heartland of the Timurid prince Babur, born in Andijan, founder of the Mughal Empire.

Economy and society

Agriculture, especially cotton, remains central, alongside silk production in Margilan, oil and gas extraction, and cross-border trade. The valley is known for religious conservatism relative to other parts of the region and has experienced periodic ethnic and border tensions, notably the Osh riots of 1990 and 2010 in its Kyrgyz portion.
