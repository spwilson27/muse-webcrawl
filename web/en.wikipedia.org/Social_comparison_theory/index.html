Social comparison theory
FieldSocial psychology
Proposed byLeon Festinger
Year proposed1954
Key conceptsUpward comparison, downward comparison, similarity hypothesis

Social comparison theory, initially proposed by social psychologist Leon Festinger in 1954, centres on the belief that individuals determine their own social and personal worth based on how they stack up against others. People are driven to gain accurate self-evaluations, and when objective, non-social means are unavailable, they evaluate their opinions and abilities by comparison with others.

Festinger's theory

Festinger proposed several hypotheses: people prefer to compare themselves with similar others; discrepancies tend to produce pressure toward uniformity in groups; and in Western cultures there is a unidirectional drive upward for abilities—people strive to do slightly better than those with whom they compare themselves. He also argued that people stop comparing with those who are too divergent, whether much better or much worse.

Upward and downward comparison

Later research distinguished upward comparison, comparison with someone perceived as better off, which can inspire self-improvement but also provoke envy or lowered self-esteem, and downward comparison, comparison with someone worse off, which often serves to protect or enhance self-esteem. Shelley Taylor's work on downward evaluation showed that people under threat, such as cancer patients, frequently compare with less fortunate others to maintain a sense of well-being.

Developments and applications

The theory has been extended through social comparison orientation measures (Gibbons and Buunk, 1999), which capture individual differences in the tendency to compare. It underlies research on relative deprivation, body image, organisational behaviour, and the effects of social media, where curated displays of others' lives provide constant upward-comparison targets and have been linked to anxiety and dissatisfaction. Related constructs include the self-evaluation maintenance model of Abraham Tesser and Thomas Wills's downward comparison theory.
