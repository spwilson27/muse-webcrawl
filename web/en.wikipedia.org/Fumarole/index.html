Fumarole

Opening in the Earth's crust emitting steam and volcanic gases

A fumarole (from Latin fumus, smoke) is an opening in the Earth's crust, usually within a volcanic area, that emits steam and gases such as carbon dioxide, sulfur dioxide, hydrogen chloride and hydrogen sulfide, without any liquid or solid material. Fumaroles occur where groundwater is heated by magma or hot igneous rock and flashes to steam, or where volcanic gases escape directly along fractures.

Characteristics

Fumarole temperatures range from about 100 °C to over 500 °C. Around their vents, condensing gases deposit minerals — most visibly yellow sulfur, giving strongly sulfurous fumaroles the name solfataras, after the Solfatara crater near Naples. Persistent fumarole fields are found at Yellowstone National Park, in Iceland, around Mount Usu and other Japanese volcanoes, in Kamchatka's Valley of Geysers, and at Vulcano in the Aeolian Islands, whose name gave us the word "volcano".

Significance

Changes in fumarole temperature and gas composition are monitored as indicators of volcanic unrest, since rising magmatic gas flux can precede eruptions. Fumarolic gases are hazardous — hydrogen sulfide and carbon dioxide can accumulate in depressions — but the mineral deposits have historically been mined for sulfur, and fumarole systems host heat-loving microorganisms studied as analogues for early life and for life on other planets. Similar vents are believed to exist on Mars and on icy moons.
