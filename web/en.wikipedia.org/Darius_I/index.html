Darius I (Old Persian: Dārayavaʰuš; c. 550 – 486 BC), commonly known as Darius the Great, was the third King of Kings of the Achaemenid Empire, reigning from 522 BC until his death in 486 BC. He ruled at the empire's territorial peak, when it included much of Western Asia, parts of the Balkans and the Caucasus, most of the Black Sea's coastal regions, Central Asia, the Indus Valley in the far east, and portions of North Africa including Egypt, eastern Libya, and coastal Sudan.

Accession

Darius ascended the throne after overthrowing the alleged usurper Bardiya (whom Darius identified as the magus Gaumata). His account of these events is carved, in three languages, on the great Behistun Inscription on a cliff in western Iran — a key to the decipherment of cuneiform. Revolts across the empire greeted his accession; Darius spent his first years suppressing them and securing his rule.

Administration and building

Darius reorganized the empire into satrapies with fixed tributes, codified laws, introduced a uniform coinage (the gold daric), and standardized weights and measures. He built a royal road system with relay stations that Herodotus admired, completed a canal from the Nile to the Red Sea, and founded a new ceremonial capital at Persepolis, while also building at Susa. Aramaic became the empire's administrative lingua franca under his rule.

Foreign campaigns

Darius expanded the empire in the Indus Valley and in Thrace, but his expedition against the Scythians across the Danube achieved little. Most famously, his punishment of Athens and Eretria for supporting the Ionian Revolt ended with the Persian defeat at the Battle of Marathon in 490 BC. He died in 486 BC while preparing a new expedition, and was succeeded by his son Xerxes I. His rock tomb at Naqsh-e Rostam depicts him before a fire altar, upheld by representatives of the subject nations.
