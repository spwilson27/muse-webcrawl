Protein (nutrient)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Proteins are essential nutrients for the human body. They are one of the building blocks of body tissue and can also serve as a fuel source. As a fuel, proteins provide as much energy density as carbohydrates: 4 kcal (17 kJ) per gram. The body requires amino acids obtained from dietary protein to synthesize its own proteins, including enzymes, structural proteins such as collagen and keratin, transport proteins such as hemoglobin, and many hormones and signaling molecules.

Protein is found in a wide range of foods. Animal sources — meat, fish, eggs, and dairy — typically provide all nine essential amino acids in adequate proportions and are termed "complete" proteins. Most plant sources, including legumes, grains, nuts, and seeds, are lower in one or more essential amino acids, though a varied plant-based diet can supply all of them.

Dietary requirements

The Recommended Dietary Allowance (RDA) for protein for sedentary adults is 0.8 grams per kilogram of body weight per day. Requirements are higher during growth, pregnancy, lactation, recovery from illness or injury, and for athletes engaged in intensive training, for whom intakes of 1.2–2.0 g/kg/day are commonly recommended. Protein needs also tend to rise in older adults to counteract age-related loss of muscle mass (sarcopenia).

Digestion and metabolism

Dietary protein is denatured by stomach acid and broken down by proteases such as pepsin in the stomach and trypsin and chymotrypsin in the small intestine into amino acids and small peptides, which are absorbed into the bloodstream. Amino acids in excess of the body's synthetic needs cannot be stored; they are deaminated in the liver, their nitrogen converted to urea and excreted, and their carbon skeletons used for energy or converted to glucose or fat.

Deficiency and excess

Protein deficiency, usually occurring together with energy deficiency as protein–energy malnutrition, causes kwashiorkor and marasmus in severe cases and remains a major cause of childhood mortality in low-income regions. Symptoms include edema, fatty liver, skin and hair changes, and impaired immunity. In affluent countries, deficiency is uncommon outside specific risk groups. Very high protein intakes have been debated in relation to kidney strain in people with pre-existing kidney disease and to bone health, though evidence of harm in healthy individuals is limited.
