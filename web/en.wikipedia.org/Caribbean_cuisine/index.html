Caribbean cuisine is the food culture of the Caribbean region, a fusion shaped over five centuries by the Indigenous peoples of the islands and the African, European, Indian, Chinese, and Middle Eastern communities who settled there. While each island has its own specialities, the region shares core ingredients and techniques: rice, beans and peas, plantains, root vegetables such as cassava and yam, coconut, seafood, and generous seasoning with peppers, allspice, thyme, and scallion.

History

The original inhabitants, including the Taíno and Kalinago peoples, cultivated cassava (yuca), sweet potato, maize, beans, squash, and peppers, and cooked on the barbacoa — a wooden grate over fire that gave the world the word "barbecue". From 1492 onwards, European colonisers (Spanish, British, French, Dutch, and Danish) introduced livestock, citrus, sugarcane, and breadfruit, and the transatlantic slave trade brought West African ingredients and techniques — okra, black-eyed peas, yams, and one-pot stews — that became foundational. After emancipation, indentured labourers from India brought curry, roti, and dhal, especially to Trinidad and Tobago, Jamaica, and Guyana, while Chinese and Lebanese and Syrian immigrants added stir-frying, soy-based sauces, and Middle Eastern breads and sweets.

Common ingredients

  • Starches: rice, plantain (green and ripe), cassava, yam, dasheen (taro), breadfruit, cornmeal.
  • Proteins: goat, chicken, salt cod (saltfish), pig, oxtail, conch, crab, and a wide variety of reef fish.
  • Seasonings: Scotch bonnet and other hot peppers, allspice (pimento), thyme, scallion (spring onion), garlic, ginger, coconut milk, and prepared blends such as jerk seasoning, curry powder, and green seasoning or sofrito.
  • Fruits: mango, guava, soursop, passion fruit, tamarind, and coconut.

Regional specialities

Jamaica

Jamaica is famous for jerk — meat, especially chicken and pork, rubbed with a fiery paste of Scotch bonnet peppers, allspice, and thyme and slow-smoked over pimento wood. The national dish is ackee and saltfish; other staples include curry goat, oxtail stew, patties, and rice and peas (rice cooked with kidney beans or gungo peas in coconut milk).

Trinidad and Tobago

Trinidadian cooking shows the strongest Indian influence: roti wraps, doubles (curried chickpeas in fried flatbread), and curries of all kinds. Callaloo, pelau (a one-pot rice dish), bake and shark, and the islands' celebrated street food and Carnival fare are widely known.

Spanish-speaking Caribbean

Cuba, Puerto Rico, and the Dominican Republic centre meals on rice and beans, roast pork (lechón), mofongo and tostones from mashed or fried plantain, and seasonings built on sofrito, adobo, and sazón. Cuba's ropa vieja and black beans are signature dishes.

French and Dutch Caribbean

Martinique, Guadeloupe, and Haiti blend French technique with Creole seasoning; Haitian griot (fried pork), diri ak pwa (rice and beans), and pikliz (spicy pickled vegetables) are staples. The Dutch islands add dishes such as keshi yena (stuffed cheese) from Curaçao.

Drinks

The Caribbean is the home of rum, distilled from sugarcane since the seventeenth century, and of classic rum cocktails such as the daiquiri, mojito, and piña colada. Non-alcoholic drinks include sorrel (hibiscus) punch, ginger beer, mauby, fresh coconut water, and tropical fruit juices.
