Globalization and Its Discontents

First edition cover of Stiglitz's critique of market fundamentalism.

Globalization and Its Discontents is a 2002 book by the economist and Nobel laureate Joseph E. Stiglitz. Drawing on his experience as chief economist of the World Bank (1997–2000), Stiglitz criticizes the International Monetary Fund (IMF) and the policies of economic globalization as managed during the 1990s, particularly the handling of the East Asian financial crisis of 1997–98 and the transition of former communist economies to markets.

Content

Stiglitz argues that the IMF pursued "market fundamentalism" — a one-size-fits-all package of fiscal austerity, rapid privatization, and capital-market liberalization known as the Washington Consensus — regardless of local conditions. In his account, these policies deepened recessions in East Asia, produced chaotic and corrupt privatization in Russia, and often harmed the poorest in developing countries. He contrasts the IMF's approach with the more gradual, state-guided strategies of China and the East Asian developmental states, and calls for reform of global economic governance toward greater transparency, representation, and attention to equity and employment.

Reception

The book became an international bestseller, selling millions of copies and being translated into dozens of languages, and it was widely embraced by the alter-globalization movement. It also drew sharp criticism: Kenneth Rogoff, then the IMF's chief economist, published a public open letter rebutting Stiglitz's account, and other reviewers questioned some of his claims about crisis policy. The debate is credited with shifting mainstream discussion of globalization toward questions of distribution, sequencing, and institutional reform.
