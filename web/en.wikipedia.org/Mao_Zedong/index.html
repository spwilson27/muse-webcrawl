Mao Zedong

Mao Zedong (26 December 1893 – 9 September 1976) was a Chinese communist revolutionary who founded the People's Republic of China (PRC) and led it from 1949 until his death. Chairman of the Chinese Communist Party (CCP), he is the central figure of Maoism and one of the most influential — and controversial — leaders of the 20th century.

Rise to power

Born to a prosperous peasant family in Hunan, Mao became a founding member of the CCP in 1921, developed a strategy of peasant-based guerrilla war, and emerged as party leader during the Long March (1934–35). After war against Japan and renewed civil war, his People's Liberation Army defeated the Nationalists, and Mao proclaimed the PRC from Tiananmen on 1 October 1949.

Rule

Mao's China underwent land reform, industrialisation drives and social transformation. The Great Leap Forward (1958–62), intended to collectivise agriculture and boost industry, contributed to a catastrophic famine in which tens of millions died. In 1966 Mao launched the Cultural Revolution, mobilising Red Guards against alleged capitalist elements; it caused widespread persecution, cultural destruction and chaos until his death. China under Mao also fought in the Korean War, split with the Soviet Union, gained the UN China seat in 1971, and hosted Nixon's 1972 visit.

Legacy

Mao's embalmed body lies in a mausoleum on Tiananmen Square and his portrait overlooks the square; his image appears on renminbi banknotes. The CCP's official verdict holds he was “70% right, 30% wrong”. Supporters credit him with unifying China, raising literacy and life expectancy and asserting national independence; critics hold him responsible for mass deaths and repression. His sayings were compiled in the Little Red Book.
