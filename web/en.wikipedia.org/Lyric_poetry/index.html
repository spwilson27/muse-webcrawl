Lyric poetry

Lyric poetry is a formal type of poetry which expresses personal emotions or feelings, typically spoken in the first person. The term derives from the Greek word λυρικός (lyrikos), meaning "singing to the lyre": in ancient Greece, lyric verse was sung or recited to the accompaniment of that instrument. Alongside epic and drama, lyric formed one of the three broad categories of poetry recognised from antiquity onwards.

In the modern sense, lyric poetry is usually short, concentrates on a single speaker and a single mood or moment, and privileges musicality and imagery over narrative. Aristotle discussed poetry chiefly in terms of epic and tragedy, but the Hellenistic scholars of Alexandria canonised the Nine Lyric Poets of Greece — among them Sappho, Alcaeus, Anacreon, Pindar and Bacchylides.

History

Ancient Greece and Rome

Greek lyric flourished in the 7th–5th centuries BC. Monodic lyric (sung by a single voice), such as the love poetry of Sappho of Lesbos and the drinking songs of Anacreon, contrasted with choral lyric, performed by a chorus at public festivals; Pindar's victory odes for athletes at the Olympic and Pythian games represent choral lyric at its most complex. Roman poets adapted Greek lyric forms: Catullus wrote intimate, metrically varied lyrics, and Horace's Odes and Epodes made the genre central to Latin literature.

Medieval and Renaissance lyric

Lyric traditions thrived worldwide: the ghazal in Arabic and Persian poetry, the tanka and haiku in Japan, and the regulated verse of Tang-dynasty China (Li Bai, Du Fu). In medieval Europe, troubadours of Provence and the German Minnesänger sang of courtly love. Petrarch's Canzoniere (366 poems, mostly sonnets, to "Laura") defined the Renaissance lyric sequence and spread the sonnet across Europe; Shakespeare's 154 sonnets and the lyrics of the English metaphysical poets followed.

Romanticism to the present

The Romantic movement elevated the lyric to the dominant poetic form: Wordsworth defined poetry as "the spontaneous overflow of powerful feelings," and the short subjective poem of Wordsworth, Coleridge, Shelley, Keats, and later Emily Dickinson and Walt Whitman, became the default understanding of "a poem." Modernist lyric (Yeats, Eliot, Rilke) complicated the speaker and the form, and the confessional poets of the mid-20th century (Plath, Lowell, Sexton) pushed first-person intensity further. Critics such as Northrop Frye and Helen Vendler have treated the lyric as a distinctive mode defined by overheard, inward utterance rather than public address.

Forms and subgenres

  • Ode — a formal, often ceremonious address to a person, object or abstraction (Keats's "Ode on a Grecian Urn").
  • Elegy — a lament, classically for the dead (Gray's "Elegy Written in a Country Churchyard"; Milton's "Lycidas").
  • Sonnet — fourteen lines, in Petrarchan or Shakespearean schemes.
  • Song and ballad — lyric set, or settable, to music; the ballad adds narrative.
  • Dramatic monologue — a lyric spoken by a persona distinct from the poet (Browning's "My Last Duchess").
  • Pastoral, hymn, and epigram — older Greek-derived subgenres that survive in adapted forms.

The lyric "I"

Scholarship distinguishes the poem's speaker from the historical poet: the lyric "I" is a constructed voice, even in apparently autobiographical work. Twentieth-century criticism (the New Criticism) insisted on this distinction, while later theorists have examined how the lyric constructs subjectivity, address (apostrophe to absent or inanimate addressees, as Jonathan Culler has argued), and a distinctive present tense of enunciation.
