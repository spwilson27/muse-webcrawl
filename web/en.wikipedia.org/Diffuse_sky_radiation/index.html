Diffuse sky radiation

Diffuse sky radiation is solar radiation reaching the Earth's surface after being scattered by molecules and particles in the atmosphere, so that it arrives from all directions rather than directly from the Sun. On a clear day it is the reason the sky appears blue and why shaded areas are still lit. Together with direct beam radiation it makes up global horizontal irradiance.

Rayleigh scattering by air molecules produces the blue diffuse sky.

Why the sky is blue

Air molecules much smaller than the wavelength of light scatter short (blue) wavelengths far more strongly than long ones — Rayleigh scattering, proportional to the inverse fourth power of wavelength. The scattered light reaching an observer from the whole sky is therefore predominantly blue. At sunrise and sunset, sunlight traverses a longer atmospheric path; most blue light is scattered away along the way, so both the direct light and parts of the sky take on orange and red hues.

Importance

Under complete cloud cover essentially all surface radiation is diffuse. Diffuse light penetrates plant canopies more effectively than direct beams, affecting photosynthesis and crop yields, and it determines the performance of photovoltaic panels and daylighting in buildings. Its fraction of total sunlight, and its colour, also depend on aerosols and pollution, which is why skies over cities can appear whitish.
