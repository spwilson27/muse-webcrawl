The Economic Recovery Tax Act of 1981 (ERTA), also known as the Kemp–Roth Tax Cut, was a federal law enacted in the United States in 1981. It was designed to encourage economic growth through reductions in individual income tax rates, expensing of depreciable property, incentives for small businesses and incentives for saving, and a major piece of President Ronald Reagan's program of supply-side economics — often called "Reaganomics". The act was sponsored by Representative Jack Kemp of New York and Senator William Roth of Delaware.

Reagan signing the Act, August 1981

Provisions

The act cut individual income tax rates across the board by about 23% over three years (implemented as reductions of 5%, 10%, and 10%), and lowered the top marginal tax rate from 70% to 50%. It also:

  • Indexed tax brackets to inflation beginning in 1985, ending "bracket creep".
  • Reduced capital gains taxes, bringing the maximum effective rate to 20%.
  • Introduced the Accelerated Cost Recovery System (ACRS), which greatly accelerated depreciation deductions for business investment.
  • Expanded individual retirement accounts (IRAs), allowing all workers to contribute.
  • Lowered estate taxes by raising the exemption and cutting the top rate.

Impact

Supporters credit ERTA with helping end the stagflation of the 1970s and spurring the long economic expansion of the 1980s. Critics argue that the cuts substantially increased federal budget deficits and disproportionately benefited high-income taxpayers. Concerned about growing deficits, Congress passed the Tax Equity and Fiscal Responsibility Act of 1982, which rolled back some of ERTA's business provisions, and several of its provisions were later modified by the Tax Reform Act of 1986.
