Surrogate endpoint

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In clinical trials, a surrogate endpoint is a measure of effect of a specific treatment that may correlate with a real clinical endpoint but does not necessarily have a guaranteed relationship. The National Institutes of Health defines a surrogate endpoint as "a biomarker intended to substitute for a clinical endpoint". Surrogate endpoints are used when the clinical endpoint is undesired (e.g., death), or when the number of events is very small, making it impractical to conduct a clinical trial to gather a statistically significant number of endpoints.

Examples

Well-known surrogate endpoints include blood pressure and LDL cholesterol for cardiovascular events; HIV viral load and CD4 cell count for progression to AIDS and death; blood glucose (and HbA1c) for the complications of diabetes; tumour shrinkage and progression-free survival for overall survival in oncology; and bone mineral density for fracture risk in osteoporosis. The United States Food and Drug Administration and other regulatory agencies often accept evidence from clinical trials that show a direct clinical benefit to surrogate markers, particularly through the accelerated approval pathway for serious diseases.

Validation

A valid surrogate must not merely correlate with the clinical outcome: the effect of the intervention on the surrogate should reliably predict its effect on the outcome. Statistical frameworks for validation include the criteria proposed by Prentice, individual- and trial-level meta-analytic approaches, and the proportion-of-treatment-effect explained. Correlation at the patient level alone is insufficient, because a disease may affect both marker and outcome without the marker lying on the causal path of the treatment's effect.

Criticism and failures

Over-reliance on surrogate endpoints has led to celebrated failures. Antiarrhythmic drugs that suppressed premature ventricular contractions after myocardial infarction were found in the Cardiac Arrhythmia Suppression Trial (CAST) to increase mortality. Torcetrapib raised HDL cholesterol substantially but increased deaths and cardiovascular events. Intensive glucose lowering improved HbA1c but in the ACCORD trial increased mortality. Such cases have prompted calls for higher validation standards, confirmatory trials with clinical outcomes after accelerated approval, and caution in equating a change in a biomarker with patient benefit.
