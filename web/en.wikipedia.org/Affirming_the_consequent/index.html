Affirming the consequent

Affirming the consequent, sometimes called the converse error, fallacy of the converse, or necessary and sufficient condition confusion, is a formal fallacy of taking a true conditional statement (e.g., "if the lamp were broken, then the room would be dark") under certain assumptions, and invalidly inferring its converse ("the room is dark, so the lamp must be broken") even though the converse may not be true under the same assumptions.

Form

The fallacious argument has the form:

If P, then Q.
Q is true.
Therefore, P is true.

This is invalid because Q may be true for reasons other than P. In propositional logic, from P → Q and Q one cannot deduce P. The fallacy is the mirror image of the valid inference modus ponens, and is related to denying the antecedent, which commits the analogous error with the inverse.

Examples

"If someone owns Fort Knox, then they are rich. Bill is rich, therefore Bill owns Fort Knox." The argument fails because wealth can arise from many sources. In everyday reasoning the error appears in medical diagnosis ("if you have flu you have a fever; you have a fever, so you have flu") and in conspiracy reasoning, where a predicted consequence is observed and the hypothesized cause is treated as confirmed.

Bayesian perspective

In probabilistic terms, observing Q raises the probability of P only to the extent that Q is more likely given P than given ¬P. Treating confirmation of a prediction as proof of a hypothesis, without weighing alternative explanations, is the qualitative error underlying the fallacy.
