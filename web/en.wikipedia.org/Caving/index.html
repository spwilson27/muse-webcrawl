Caving

Also calledSpelunking (US), potholing (UK)
TypeOutdoor recreation / exploration
Essential gearHelmet, lights (multiple), ropes for vertical caving
ScienceSpeleology

Caving — known as spelunking in the United States and Canada and potholing in the United Kingdom — is the recreational exploration of wild cave systems, as distinct from visiting show caves open to tourists. Cavers explore, survey, and photograph caves, which range from short horizontal passages to deep vertical systems requiring single-rope technique. The scientific study of caves is called speleology.


A caver abseiling a vertical pitch

Equipment and technique

Standard equipment includes a helmet, at least three independent light sources, sturdy clothing, and gloves. Vertical caving uses ropes, harnesses, descenders, and ascenders; the single rope technique (SRT) is standard in most of the world. Cave surveying with compass, clinometer, and tape (now supplemented by laser devices) produces maps used for science and rescue.

Safety and conservation

Hazards include falls, flooding, loose rock, hypothermia, and disorientation; organised caving emphasises training, telling others of plans, and cave-rescue preparedness — high-profile rescues such as that of the Thai junior football team in 2018 drew worldwide attention. Because cave ecosystems are fragile, conservation codes urge cavers to "take nothing but pictures, leave nothing but footprints, kill nothing but time", and access to some caves is restricted to protect bat colonies from white-nose syndrome.

History

Systematic cave exploration began in the late nineteenth century with figures such as Édouard-Alfred Martel in France, often called the father of speleology. The world's deepest known caves, in the Arabika massif of Georgia, exceed 2,000 metres of vertical extent, and their exploration remains an active frontier.
