Solvable group

In mathematics, more specifically in the field of group theory, a solvable group or soluble group is a group that can be constructed from abelian groups using extensions. Equivalently, a solvable group is a group whose derived series terminates in the trivial subgroup.

Formally, a group G is called solvable if it has a subnormal series

1 = G₀ ◃ G₁ ◃ ... ◃ Gₖ = G

such that each quotient group Gi+1/Gi is abelian. Closely related is the derived series, formed by repeatedly taking commutator subgroups; G is solvable if and only if the derived series reaches the trivial group in finitely many steps.

Motivation and history

The term arises from Évariste Galois's work on polynomial equations: the Galois group of a polynomial is solvable if and only if the polynomial's roots can be expressed in terms of radicals (addition, multiplication, and extraction of roots). This Galois-theoretic criterion explains why general quintic equations are not solvable by radicals — the symmetric group S₅ is not solvable, as it contains the non-abelian simple group A₅.

Examples and theorems

All abelian groups are solvable, as are all nilpotent groups and all finite p-groups (Burnside's pᵃqᵇ theorem states that groups of order pᵃqᵇ are solvable). The Feit–Thompson theorem proves that every finite group of odd order is solvable, implying that every finite simple non-abelian group has even order. Subgroups, quotients, and direct products of solvable groups are solvable.
