Dragon dance

A dragon dance performed during Chinese New Year celebrations

Dragon dance (simplified Chinese: 舞龙; traditional Chinese: 舞龍; pinyin: wǔ lóng) is a form of traditional dance and performance in Chinese culture most often seen during festive celebrations such as Chinese New Year. The dance is performed by a team of experienced dancers who manipulate a long flexible giant puppet of a dragon using poles positioned at regular intervals along the length of the dragon. The dragon's movements — undulating, spiralling and chasing a "pearl of wisdom" — are choreographed to the beat of drums, gongs and cymbals, and are believed to bring good luck and prosperity.

History

Dances imitating dragons date to the Han dynasty, when performers in dragon form took part in rain-rituals and court entertainments described in texts such as the "Treatise on Ritual". By the Tang and Song dynasties, dragon processions were established features of festivals. The modern parade dragon — a lightweight frame of bamboo, wood and cloth sections joined by poles — developed with Chinese diaspora celebrations around the world.

Performance

A dragon can range from a few metres to over 100 metres; the longest dragons require dozens or hundreds of performers. The head is large and ornate, with movable jaw and sometimes built-in effects such as water sprays or pyrotechnics. Acrobatic formations include "Cloud Cave", "Whirlpool", spirals, and figures of the dragon chasing the pearl carried by a lead performer. Teams train for the strength and coordination needed; unlike the lion dance, which is performed by one or two people inside a lion costume, the dragon is held aloft on poles.

Around the world

Dragon dances are performed by Chinese communities worldwide and by cultural troupes in Vietnam, Malaysia, Singapore and elsewhere. The Bendigo Easter Festival in Australia features Dai Gum Loong, among the world's longest imperial parade dragons. In 2011, dragon dance was inscribed on China's national list of intangible cultural heritage.
