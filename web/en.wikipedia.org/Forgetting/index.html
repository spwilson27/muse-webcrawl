Forgetting

The forgetting curve described by Hermann Ebbinghaus.

Forgetting or disremembering is the apparent loss or modification of information already encoded and stored in an individual's short or long-term memory. It is a spontaneous or gradual process in which old memories are unable to be recalled from memory storage. Problems with remembering, learning and retaining new information are a few of the most common complaints of older adults.

The forgetting curve

In the 1880s, Hermann Ebbinghaus memorized lists of nonsense syllables and measured his own ability to relearn them over time. He found that forgetting follows an approximately exponential curve: loss is steepest in the first hours after learning and then levels off. Ebbinghaus also showed that distributed practice (the spacing effect) and overlearning slow the rate of forgetting.

Theories of forgetting

  • Decay theory proposes that memory traces fade with time if they are not used.
  • Interference theory holds that forgetting results from competition between memories: proactive interference from older material and retroactive interference from newer material.
  • Retrieval failure explanations emphasize the absence of appropriate cues; information may remain in storage but be inaccessible, as in the tip-of-the-tongue phenomenon.
  • Motivated forgetting, including Freudian repression and laboratory suppression paradigms, attributes some forgetting to active processes that keep material out of awareness.

Amnesia and pathology

Abnormal forgetting occurs in amnesia, which may be retrograde (loss of memories formed before onset) or anterograde (inability to form new memories), and in dementias such as Alzheimer's disease. The famous patient H.M., who underwent bilateral medial temporal lobe resection, demonstrated the central role of the hippocampus in consolidating new long-term memories.

Adaptive functions

Forgetting is not merely a defect: it prevents interference from outdated information, supports generalization by discarding irrelevant detail, and may protect wellbeing by weakening painful memories. Contemporary neuroscience treats some forgetting as an active process involving neurogenesis and dedicated molecular pathways.
