Theophrastus (Greek: Θεόφραστος, Theóphrastos; c. 371 – c. 287 BC) was a Greek philosopher and the successor to Aristotle in the Peripatetic school. He was a native of Eresos in Lesbos. His given name was Tyrtamus (or Tirtamus), but he later acquired the nickname "Theophrastus" — "divine speaker" — traditionally said to have been given to him by Aristotle for the grace of his conversation. He came to Athens as a young man, studied under Plato and then Aristotle, and directed the Lyceum for about 35 years after Aristotle's death, presiding over a school of some 2,000 students.

Botany

Theophrastus is called the "father of botany." His two surviving botanical treatises, Enquiry into Plants (Historia Plantarum) and On the Causes of Plants, classify some 500 plants, distinguish trees, shrubs, undershrubs, and herbs, describe sexual reproduction in plants (using the date palm as an example), and discuss cultivation, timber, and plant geography with information drawn in part from Alexander the Great's campaigns. The works remained the foundation of botanical science into the Renaissance.

Other works

Diogenes Laërtius credits Theophrastus with 227 treatises spanning logic, physics, natural history, metaphysics, ethics, rhetoric, and poetics; most are lost. His surviving Characters sketches thirty moral types — the Flatterer, the Boor, the Superstitious Man — with wit and economy, founding a literary genre later imitated by La Bruyère. Fragments of his On Stones anticipate mineralogy, and his doxographical summaries preserved much of what is known of the Presocratic philosophers. Aristotle bequeathed him his library and named him guardian of his children; Theophrastus in turn left the school's garden and buildings to his colleagues in his will, one of the earliest preserved Greek wills.
