Microbiology

Microbiology is the scientific study of microorganisms — living things too small to be seen with the naked eye, including bacteria, archaea, fungi, algae, protozoa, and viruses. Microbes inhabit virtually every environment on Earth and drive nutrient cycles, cause and prevent disease, and are harnessed in food production, biotechnology, and medicine.

History

Antonie van Leeuwenhoek first observed "animalcules" with his single-lens microscopes in the 1670s. The field took off in the 19th century: Louis Pasteur refuted spontaneous generation, developed pasteurization and vaccines; Robert Koch formulated his postulates linking specific microbes to diseases such as tuberculosis and cholera; Joseph Lister pioneered antisepsis. Martinus Beijerinck and Sergei Winogradsky founded environmental microbiology, and Alexander Fleming's 1928 discovery of penicillin opened the antibiotic era.

Branches

Sub-disciplines include bacteriology, virology, mycology, parasitology, and phycology; applied fields cover medical, food, industrial, agricultural, and environmental microbiology. Modern methods — pure culture, microscopy, genomics, and metagenomics — have revealed vast microbial diversity, including the human microbiome's role in health, and CRISPR systems borrowed from bacterial immunity.
