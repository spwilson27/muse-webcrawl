Depth perception

Depth perception is the visual ability to perceive the world in three dimensions and to judge the distance of objects. The retinal image is two-dimensional, so the visual system must infer the third dimension from a set of cues, some available to one eye alone (monocular cues) and others arising from the combination of the two eyes' images (binocular cues).

Binocular cues

Because the eyes are separated by about 6.5 cm, each receives a slightly different view of the world. The brain measures this binocular disparity — objects nearer than the fixation point fall on different relative positions in the two retinas — and converts it into a sense of depth known as stereopsis. Convergence, the inward turning of the eyes to fixate near objects, provides an additional cue through the muscular feedback and proprioception of the eye muscles.

Monocular cues

Monocular cues include relative size, overlap (interposition), linear perspective, texture gradient, aerial perspective, shading, and motion parallax — the apparent movement of near objects against far ones as the observer moves. These pictorial cues are exploited by painters to create depth on a flat canvas, and they continue to support depth judgement in people with vision in only one eye, though fine stereoscopic precision is lost.

Development and clinical aspects

Infants show sensitivity to pictorial depth cues within months of birth, and the famous "visual cliff" experiments of Gibson and Walk (1960) suggested that crawling infants avoid an apparent drop-off. Depth perception can be impaired by strabismus, amblyopia or damage to visual areas of the brain. Tests such as the Randot stereotest measure stereoacuity, typically expressed in seconds of arc; normal stereoacuity can be as fine as 20–40 arcseconds.

Applications

Stereoscopic techniques underlie 3D cinema, virtual reality headsets, stereo microscopes and machine-vision systems that estimate distance for robotics and autonomous vehicles.
