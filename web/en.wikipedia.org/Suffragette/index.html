Suffragette

A suffragette was a member of an activist women's organisation in the early twentieth century which, under the banner "Votes for Women", fought for the right of women to vote in public elections in the United Kingdom. The term particularly refers to members of the Women's Social and Political Union (WSPU), a women-only movement founded in 1903 by Emmeline Pankhurst, which engaged in direct action and civil disobedience.

Origins of the term

The word "suffragette" was first used in a 1906 article in the Daily Mail by journalist Charles E. Hands, who coined it as a diminutive — and intended as derisive — variant of "suffragist". The WSPU embraced the term, naming its newspaper The Suffragette from 1912.

Tactics

Frustrated by the slow progress of constitutional campaigning, the WSPU adopted the motto "Deeds, not words". Its members chained themselves to railings, disrupted political meetings, went on hunger strike in prison (and were forcibly fed), and in later years carried out window-smashing and arson campaigns against empty property.

Outcome

The Representation of the People Act 1918 enfranchised women over 30 who met a property qualification; equal voting rights with men were achieved by the Equal Franchise Act 1928.
