SubjectBloody Sunday, 30 January 1972, Derry, Northern Ireland

The Bloody Sunday Inquiry, also known as the Saville Inquiry, was a public inquiry established by the British government in 1998 to investigate the events of Bloody Sunday, 30 January 1972, when British soldiers shot 26 unarmed civilians during a protest march in Derry, Northern Ireland, killing 14.

The inquiry was chaired by Lord Saville of Newdigate, sitting with international jurists William Hoyt of Canada and John Toohey of Australia. It was announced by Prime Minister Tony Blair in January 1998, following years of campaigning by the families of the victims and widespread rejection of the earlier Widgery Tribunal report of 1972, which had largely exonerated the soldiers.

Proceedings

The inquiry heard evidence over several years, principally in Derry and in London, from hundreds of civilian witnesses, soldiers, politicians, and officials. It was the longest and most expensive public inquiry in British legal history, costing roughly £195 million.

Findings

The Saville Report was published on 15 June 2010. It concluded that none of those killed or wounded was armed, that the soldiers fired first without justification, and that none of the casualties was posing a threat of causing death or serious injury. Prime Minister David Cameron formally apologised in the House of Commons, describing the killings as "unjustified and unjustifiable."
