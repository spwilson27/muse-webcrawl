Oregon Country

The Oregon Country was a large region of the Pacific Northwest of North America that was subject to a long dispute between the United Kingdom and the United States in the early 19th century. The region, known to the British as the Columbia District, comprised the land north of 42°N latitude, south of 54°40′N, and west of the Rocky Mountains down to the Pacific Ocean. Under the Anglo-American Convention of 1818 the two nations agreed to a "joint occupation" of the territory for ten years, an arrangement extended indefinitely in 1827.

History

European and American claims grew out of exploration and the maritime fur trade. The United States based its claim on Robert Gray's entry into the Columbia River in 1792, the Lewis and Clark Expedition (1804–1806), and rights acquired from Spain by the Adams–Onís Treaty of 1819. Britain pointed to the explorations of James Cook and George Vancouver and to the activities of the North West Company and later the Hudson's Bay Company, which established Fort Vancouver in 1825 as its regional headquarters under Chief Factor John McLoughlin.

Settlement and the boundary dispute

American settlers began arriving in significant numbers over the Oregon Trail in the 1840s, settling mainly in the Willamette Valley. They formed a provisional government in 1843. The boundary became a political issue in the United States; the slogan "Fifty-four Forty or Fight!" was associated with demands for the entire territory up to Russian Alaska. War was avoided, and the Oregon Treaty of 1846 set the boundary at the 49th parallel, extending it through the Strait of Georgia and the Strait of Juan de Fuca, leaving Vancouver Island to Britain. The American portion was organised as Oregon Territory in 1848; the British mainland portion later became the Colony of British Columbia.
