Electrophilic aromatic substitution

Electrophilic aromatic substitution
TypeOrganic reaction
SubstratesAromatic compounds
ExamplesNitration, halogenation, Friedel–Crafts reactions, sulfonation

Electrophilic aromatic substitution (SEAr) is an organic reaction in which an atom attached to an aromatic ring — usually hydrogen — is replaced by an electrophile, leaving the aromatic system intact.

Mechanism

The aromatic pi system attacks the electrophile to form a resonance-stabilised carbocation known as an arenium ion (or sigma complex, Wheland intermediate). A base then removes the proton from the carbon bearing the new substituent, restoring aromaticity. Because aromaticity is preserved in the product, substitution is favoured over addition for aromatic compounds.

Important reactions

Classic examples include nitration with mixed nitric and sulfuric acids, bromination and chlorination with Lewis-acid catalysts, sulfonation with fuming sulfuric acid, and the Friedel–Crafts alkylation and acylation reactions.

Directing effects

Existing substituents influence the rate and regiochemistry of further substitution: activating groups such as –OH and –NH2 direct ortho/para, while deactivating groups such as –NO2 direct meta.
