Customs union

A customs union is a type of trade bloc in which member countries eliminate tariffs on trade among themselves and adopt a common external tariff on imports from non-members. It is a deeper form of integration than a free trade area, whose members keep independent external tariffs and must use rules of origin to prevent trade deflection.

Examples

The most prominent example is the European Union Customs Union, which, combined with the single market, allows goods to circulate freely within the EU. Others include the Eurasian Customs Union, Mercosur (a partial customs union), the Southern African Customs Union — the world's oldest, dating to 1910 — and the Gulf Cooperation Council customs union. The German Zollverein of 1834 was a historically influential precursor that promoted German unification.

Economics

Jacob Viner's 1950 analysis distinguished trade creation (shifting consumption to more efficient member producers, welfare-enhancing) from trade diversion (shifting imports from a more efficient outsider to a less efficient member because of the tariff preference, welfare-reducing). A customs union also requires members to surrender independent trade policy, since external tariffs and trade deals must be negotiated collectively.
