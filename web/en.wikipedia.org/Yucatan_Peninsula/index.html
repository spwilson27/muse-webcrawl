The Yucatán Peninsula is a large peninsula in southeastern Mexico and adjacent portions of Belize and Guatemala (the department of Petén). It separates the Caribbean Sea from the Gulf of Mexico, with the Yucatán Channel between its northeastern tip and Cuba. The Mexican portion comprises the states of Yucatán, Campeche, and Quintana Roo.

Geology

The peninsula is a vast, low-lying limestone platform — the exposed part of the Yucatán Platform — fringed by the Mesoamerican Barrier Reef System in the Caribbean. Because the rock is porous karst, surface rivers are almost absent; instead, water flows underground through caves and emerges in sinkholes known as cenotes. Beneath the northwestern coast lies the Chicxulub crater, about 180 km in diameter, created by the asteroid impact 66 million years ago associated with the extinction of the non-avian dinosaurs.

History

The peninsula was a core region of the Maya civilisation: the great lowland cities of the Classic period gave way to northern centres such as Chichén Itzá and Uxmal in the Terminal Classic and Postclassic periods. Spanish conquest in the 16th century founded cities such as Mérida and Campeche; in the colonial and early national periods the peninsula was a remote periphery of New Spain and Mexico. The Caste War (1847–1901) saw Maya rebels maintain a de facto independent state in the east. In the 20th century, the henequen industry, then chicle extraction, and from the 1970s mass tourism along the "Riviera Maya" transformed the region.

Environment and economy

Much of the interior is covered by tropical forest, home to jaguars, monkeys, and rich birdlife; the Calakmul Biosphere Reserve in Campeche is one of the largest protected areas in Mexico. Tourism (Cancún, Playa del Carmen, Tulum, Mérida) and agriculture (citrus, henequen historically, honey) dominate the economy, alongside oil production offshore in the Bay of Campeche.
