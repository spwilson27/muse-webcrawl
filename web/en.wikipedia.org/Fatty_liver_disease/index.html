Fatty liver disease (FLD), also known as hepatic steatosis, is a condition in which excess fat builds up in the liver. It is classified into non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), driven by metabolic risk factors such as obesity and diabetes, and alcoholic liver disease, caused by excessive alcohol consumption. Fatty liver is very common, affecting roughly a quarter of adults worldwide, and often causes no symptoms.

Signs and symptoms

Most people with simple steatosis feel well, and the condition is frequently discovered incidentally on imaging done for other reasons. A minority develop inflammation of the liver — non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) or alcoholic hepatitis — which can progress over years to fibrosis, cirrhosis, and liver failure. Progressive disease may cause fatigue, weight loss, jaundice, and complications of portal hypertension.

Causes and diagnosis

NAFLD is strongly associated with the metabolic syndrome: central obesity, insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidemia. Diagnosis typically begins with ultrasound, CT, or MRI showing a bright, fat-laden liver, together with liver enzyme tests; transient elastography and biopsy can stage fibrosis. Other causes of liver disease, such as viral hepatitis, must be excluded.

Treatment and prognosis

The mainstay of treatment is weight loss through diet and exercise, control of diabetes and lipids, and abstinence from alcohol. Simple steatosis has a good prognosis, whereas steatohepatitis with fibrosis carries increased risk of cirrhosis and cardiovascular disease. In 2023, professional societies proposed new nomenclature — metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) — to replace the NAFLD terminology.
