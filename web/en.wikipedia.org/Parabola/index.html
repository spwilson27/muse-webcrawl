Parabola

Parabola Type: Conic section
Eccentricity: 1
Standard form: y = ax² (vertex at origin)
General equation: y = ax² + bx + c
Focus: (h, k + 1/4a) for vertex form

A parabola is a plane curve which is mirror-symmetrical and approximately U-shaped. It fits several superficially different mathematical descriptions, which can all be proved to define exactly the same curves. One description of a parabola involves a point (the focus) and a line (the directrix): the parabola is the locus of points in that plane that are equidistant from both the directrix and the focus.

The parabola is a member of the family of conic sections, formed by the intersection of a plane with a cone parallel to a generating line of the cone; it is the conic section with eccentricity exactly equal to 1. The word "parabola" is due to Apollonius of Perga, whose treatise Conics systematised its study in the 3rd century BC.

Equations

In Cartesian coordinates, a parabola with axis parallel to the y-axis and vertex at (h, k) is described by y = a(x − h)² + k. When the vertex is at the origin and the axis is the y-axis, this reduces to y = ax², with focus at (0, 1/4a) and directrix y = −1/4a. The graph of a quadratic function in one variable is always a parabola.

Reflective property and applications

A parabola has the property that any ray parallel to its axis of symmetry is reflected through its focus, and conversely a ray emitted from the focus reflects into a beam parallel to the axis. This property is exploited in parabolic reflectors: satellite dishes and radio telescopes concentrate incoming parallel signals at the focus, while searchlights, headlights, and solar furnaces use the reverse principle. Trajectories of projectiles under uniform gravity (neglecting air resistance) are parabolas, and suspension bridge main cables under uniformly distributed load hang in approximately parabolic curves.

History

The parabola was first studied by Menaechmus in the 4th century BC in connection with the problem of doubling the cube. Apollonius gave the curve its name, from the Greek parabolē, meaning "application" or "comparison." Galileo showed that projectiles follow parabolic paths, and Newton used parabolic mirrors in his reflecting telescope design.
