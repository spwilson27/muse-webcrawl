Lidar
A lidar point cloud generated by an airborne scanner

Lidar (from "light detection and ranging," or "laser imaging, detection, and ranging") is a method of remote sensing that measures distances by illuminating a target with pulsed laser light and timing the reflections. The resulting dense set of measurements, called a point cloud, is used to build precise three-dimensional models of surfaces. Lidar is analogous to radar but uses light rather than radio waves, giving much finer resolution.

Principle

A lidar instrument emits rapid pulses of laser light — often hundreds of thousands per second — and records the time each pulse takes to return. Distance follows from the speed of light: d = ct/2. Scanning the beam across a scene, or using an array of beams, yields millions of georeferenced points. Many systems record multiple returns per pulse, allowing vegetation structure to be separated from the ground beneath.

Types

  • Airborne lidar (aerial laser scanning): mounted on aircraft or drones for topographic mapping, forestry and archaeology.
  • Terrestrial lidar: tripod- or vehicle-mounted scanners used in surveying, construction and heritage recording.
  • Bathymetric lidar: green-wavelength lasers that penetrate water to map coasts and riverbeds.

Applications

Lidar is used in autonomous vehicles for obstacle detection and navigation, in meteorology for measuring aerosols, clouds and wind (Doppler lidar), in geology for fault and floodplain mapping, and in archaeology, where it has revealed hidden structures beneath jungle canopy at sites such as Caracol and Angkor. Spaceborne instruments include NASA's ICESat-2 and GEDI, which measure ice sheets and forest canopies.
