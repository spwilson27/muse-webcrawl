Diencephalon

The diencephalon sits between the cerebral hemispheres and the midbrain.

The diencephalon is a division of the forebrain situated between the telencephalon (cerebral hemispheres) and the midbrain, surrounding the third ventricle. It is largely hidden within the adult human brain, visible mainly on its inferior surface. Its structures are central to sensory processing, bodily homeostasis, and endocrine control.

Structures

The diencephalon comprises four main parts. The thalamus, the largest component, is a paired mass of grey matter that relays nearly all sensory information — visual, auditory, somatosensory, gustatory — to the cerebral cortex, and participates in consciousness, sleep, and motor control. The hypothalamus, below it, regulates hunger, thirst, temperature, circadian rhythms, and the autonomic nervous system, and controls the pituitary gland, linking the nervous and endocrine systems. The epithalamus includes the pineal gland, which secretes melatonin, and the subthalamus participates in motor circuits of the basal ganglia.

Development

In the embryo, the diencephalon develops from the middle portion of the prosencephalon, one of the three primary brain vesicles. The optic vesicles that form the retina and optic nerve are outgrowths of the diencephalon, making the visual pathway technically part of the forebrain.

Functions

Beyond its relay and homeostatic roles, the diencephalon is involved in the regulation of sleep and wakefulness (via the thalamus and the pineal gland), the processing of emotion through connections with the limbic system, and the coordination of endocrine responses to stress. Damage to the diencephalon can cause disorders of consciousness, memory (as in Korsakoff syndrome, affecting the mammillary bodies), and hormonal regulation.
