Wallace fountains are public drinking fountains named after the Englishman Sir Richard Wallace (1818–1890), who financed their construction in Paris after the Franco-Prussian War. They are cast-iron sculptures scattered throughout the city of Paris, France, mainly in the most frequented areas, and are recognised worldwide as one of the symbols of Paris.

History

Following the Siege of Paris (1870–71) and the Commune, much of the city's water infrastructure was damaged and the price of water rose, making access to clean drinking water difficult for the poor. Wallace, an art collector and philanthropist who had lived in Paris through the siege, funded the fountains so that Parisians could have free access to potable water. The first fountains were installed in 1872.

Design

Designed by the sculptor Charles-Auguste Lebourg of Nantes, the large model consists of an octagonal base supporting four caryatids — representing Kindness, Simplicity, Charity and Sobriety — who hold up a small dome decorated with dolphins, from which water trickles into a basin. The fountains are painted a distinctive dark green, like other Parisian street furniture. Wallace stipulated that they be beautiful and useful, placed so as to be easily found, and cheap enough that many could be built. Several models exist: the large caryatid model, a smaller wall-mounted model, and simple push-button models.

About one hundred large-model fountains remain in Paris, nearly all still dispensing chilled drinking water, and examples donated by Wallace and later admirers can be found in cities around the world, including London, and in Richard Wallace's former estate town of Lisburn in Northern Ireland.
