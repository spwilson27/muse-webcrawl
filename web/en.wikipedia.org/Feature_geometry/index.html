Feature geometry

Feature geometry is a theory of phonology in which distinctive features are arranged hierarchically in a tree-like structure beneath segmental "root" nodes, rather than being listed as an unordered bundle. Developed in the mid-1980s by linguists including G. N. Clements and Elizabeth Hume, the framework is used to explain patterns of assimilation, dissimilation and feature spreading in the sound systems of languages.

Structure

In a typical feature geometry, a segment is represented by a root node that branches into tiered class nodes — such as the laryngeal node, the supralaryngeal node, place and manner nodes — each dominating the individual features beneath it. Assimilation is modelled as the spreading of a node from one segment to a neighbouring segment along association lines.

Applications

Feature geometry accounts naturally for the Obligatory Contour Principle effects, place assimilation (such as a nasal taking the place of articulation of a following consonant), vowel harmony, and the behaviour of contour segments such as affricates and prenasalised stops. The theory forms part of the broader autosegmental tradition in phonological analysis.
