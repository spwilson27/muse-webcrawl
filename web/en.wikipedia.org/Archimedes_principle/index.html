Archimedes' principle

Archimedes' principle states that the upward buoyant force exerted on a body immersed in a fluid, whether fully or partially submerged, is equal to the weight of the fluid that the body displaces. It is a fundamental law of fluid mechanics formulated by Archimedes of Syracuse.

Explanation

The principle explains why objects float or sink: if the weight of displaced fluid equals the object's weight, it floats in equilibrium; if the object is denser than the fluid, it sinks. The buoyant force acts through the centre of buoyancy, the centroid of the displaced volume.

The crown anecdote

According to Vitruvius, Archimedes realized how to measure the volume of an irregular object — King Hiero II's crown — while stepping into a bath, prompting his famous cry of "Eureka!" He could then test whether the crown was pure gold by comparing its density with that of gold.

Applications

The principle underlies ship design, submarine ballast, hydrometers, and hot-air ballooning.
