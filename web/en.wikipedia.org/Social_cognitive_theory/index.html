Social cognitive theory

Social cognitive theory (SCT) is a learning theory developed by psychologist Albert Bandura, holding that people acquire behaviors, attitudes, and emotional reactions largely by observing others, within a framework of continuous interaction among personal factors, behavior, and the environment. It grew out of Bandura's earlier social learning theory and was given its mature form in his 1986 book Social Foundations of Thought and Action.

Reciprocal determinism

The theory's organizing principle is triadic reciprocal determinism (or reciprocal causation): behavior, cognitive and other personal factors, and environmental events all operate as interacting determinants of one another. Unlike strict behaviorism, SCT treats people as agents who interpret and shape their environments rather than merely responding to stimuli.

Observational learning

Much human learning is observational: people watch models — parents, peers, teachers, media figures — and reproduce their actions, especially when the model's behavior is rewarded (vicarious reinforcement). Bandura's famous Bobo doll experiments (1961–63) showed that children imitated aggressive behavior they had seen an adult perform toward an inflatable doll, particularly when the adult was rewarded. Effective observational learning requires attention, retention, motor reproduction, and motivation.

Self-efficacy

The theory's most influential construct is self-efficacy: a person's belief in their capability to organize and execute the actions required to manage prospective situations. Self-efficacy, built from mastery experiences, vicarious experience, verbal persuasion, and physiological states, predicts which goals people choose, how much effort they expend, and how long they persist. It has been applied extensively in health behavior, education, and clinical psychology.

Other constructs

SCT also emphasizes outcome expectations, self-regulation (self-observation, judgment, and self-reaction), goals and feedback, and moral agency, including mechanisms of moral disengagement. The theory underpins interventions in smoking cessation, exercise promotion, and chronic disease management, and Bandura later extended it into an agentic framework with collective efficacy at the societal level.
