Hyperbola

Hyperbola
Type: Conic section
Standard equation: x²/a² − y²/b² = 1
Eccentricity: e > 1
Features: Two branches, two foci, two asymptotes

In mathematics, a hyperbola is a type of smooth curve lying in a plane, defined by its geometric properties or by equations for which it is the solution set. A hyperbola consists of two disconnected curves called branches, which are mirror images of each other and resemble two infinite bows.

Definitions

A hyperbola is the locus of points for which the absolute difference of the distances to two fixed points, the foci, is constant. It can also be defined as a conic section formed by intersecting a double cone with a plane cutting both halves, or as the locus of points whose distance from a focus is a constant multiple — the eccentricity, greater than 1 — of the distance from a corresponding directrix.

Equations and properties

In Cartesian coordinates a hyperbola centred at the origin with transverse axis horizontal satisfies x²/a² − y²/b² = 1; its asymptotes are the lines y = ±(b/a)x. A rectangular hyperbola has perpendicular asymptotes and equation xy = c. Hyperbolas have reflective properties: a ray directed toward one focus reflects off the curve toward the other focus, a property used in some telescope and antenna designs.

Applications

Hyperbolic trajectories describe objects escaping a gravitational body, hyperbolic functions model catenary-like curves and special-relativistic spacetime diagrams, and multilateration navigation systems (such as LORAN) locate positions using hyperbolas derived from time differences. Cooling towers use hyperboloid structures for strength and efficient airflow.

History

The hyperbola was studied by Menaechmus and named by Apollonius of Perga in his Conics (c. 200 BC), the name referring to the “excess” in its construction compared with the parabola.
