Maiden Castle, Dorset

Maiden Castle is a large Iron Age hillfort about 2.5 kilometres southwest of Dorchester in Dorset, England. Among the largest and most complex hillforts in Europe, it covers about 19 hectares (47 acres) enclosed by enormous multiple ramparts. The site was occupied from the Neolithic period through the Roman era and is famous both for its massive defences and for Mortimer Wheeler's 1930s excavations, which popularised the theory of a violent Roman assault.

Development

The earliest activity was a Neolithic causewayed enclosure and a long bank barrow, around 4000–3000 BC. The first Iron Age fort (c. 600 BC) enclosed the eastern knoll; around 450 BC it was expanded westward to its full size, with up to four lines of rampart and elaborate, maze-like entrances designed to expose attackers. The interior held roundhouses, storage pits, and streets; caches of sling stones testify to its military role. It was a stronghold of the Durotriges tribe.

Roman period and the "war cemetery"

After the Roman conquest of AD 43, occupation declined as the population moved to the new town of Durnovaria (Dorchester); a small Romano-British temple was built on the hill in the 4th century. Wheeler excavated a group of burials near the east gate — including a man with an iron ballista bolt in his spine — and interpreted them as victims of a storming of the fort by Vespasian's Second Legion. Later re-examination suggests the cemetery is a more ordinary late Iron Age burial ground, with deaths from various causes over time, though a Roman assault remains possible.

Today

Maiden Castle is in the care of English Heritage and open to the public. Its name is first recorded in 1607 and probably derives from a British word meaning "great hill" (cf. mai dun), rather than from any legend about an unconquered "maiden" fortress.
