Robert Ferdinand Wagner I (June 8, 1877 – May 4, 1953) was an American politician and diplomat. A member of the Democratic Party, he served as a U.S. Senator from New York from 1927 to 1949, and was a leading liberal voice and a chief architect of the New Deal.

Early life

Born in Nastätten in the German Empire, Wagner emigrated with his family to the United States in 1885, settling in New York City, where his father worked as a janitor. He attended public schools, graduated from City College and New York Law School, and entered Tammany Hall politics in the Yorkville district, winning election to the New York State Assembly and then Senate. As chairman of the state factory investigating commission after the Triangle Shirtwaist fire of 1911, working with Al Smith and Frances Perkins, he helped pass landmark workplace-safety and labour laws.

Senate career

Elected to the U.S. Senate in 1926, Wagner became Franklin D. Roosevelt's floor leader for much of the New Deal programme. He drafted Section 7(a) of the National Industrial Recovery Act and then authored the National Labor Relations Act of 1935 — universally known as the Wagner Act — which guaranteed workers the right to organise and bargain collectively and created the National Labor Relations Board. He also sponsored the Wagner–Steagall Housing Act of 1937, founding the federal public-housing programme, and played a key role in the Social Security Act. He chaired the Senate Banking Committee and co-sponsored the Wagner–Rogers Bill (1939) to admit Jewish refugee children, which failed in Congress.

Legacy

Declining health forced Wagner's resignation in 1949. He died in 1953. His son, Robert F. Wagner Jr., served three terms as mayor of New York City. The Robert F. Wagner Graduate School of Public Service at NYU bears his name.
