Empresario

An empresario (Spanish: [empɾeˈsaɾjo]) was a person who, in the early 19th century, received a land grant from the government of Mexico or Spain and had the responsibility of hiring settlers to inhabit the grant area. Stephen F. Austin, known as the "Father of Texas", is the most famous empresario, though the institution reshaped the population of Mexican Texas generally.

Origins and system

Spain and, after independence, Mexico sought to populate and defend their sparsely settled northern frontier by inviting foreign immigration through empresarios. Under colonization laws of the 1820s, an empresario contracted to settle a specified number of families within a period (often six years) and received premium lands for each hundred families introduced; settlers received generous grants of farming and grazing land at low cost, on condition they profess Roman Catholicism and become Mexican citizens.

Notable empresarios

Moses Austin received the original Spanish permission in 1821, and his son Stephen F. Austin carried out the "Old Three Hundred" colony at San Felipe de Austin. Other empresarios included Green DeWitt, Haden Edwards (whose grievances produced the Fredonian Rebellion), Sterling Robertson, and the Irish colonies of John McMullen and James McGloin, and James Power and James Hewetson.

End of the system

Growing Mexican concern about Anglo-American domination of Texas led to the Law of April 6, 1830, which banned further immigration from the United States and cancelled unfilled contracts. Empresario colonization ceased with the Texas Revolution of 1835–36, but its demographic legacy was decisive: Anglo settlers and their enslaved workers had become the majority population of Texas.
