Siege of Edessa (1144)

Date28 November – 24 December 1144
LocationEdessa (modern Şanlıurfa, Turkey)
AttackerImad al-Din Zengi, atabeg of Mosul and Aleppo
DefenderCounty of Edessa (Count Joscelin II absent)
ResultZengid victory; fall of the County of Edessa

The siege of Edessa from 28 November to 24 December 1144 saw the forces of Imad al-Din Zengi capture the city of Edessa, the capital of the Crusader County of Edessa — the first of the Crusader states to be founded, in 1098, and the first to fall. Its loss shocked Latin Christendom and was the direct trigger of the Second Crusade.

Background

The County of Edessa, lying east of the Euphrates, was the most exposed of the Crusader states. Count Joscelin II quarreled with his Latin neighbours, allied at times with local powers against Antioch and Tripoli, and was frequently absent at Turbessel. Zengi, who had consolidated Mosul and Aleppo and was building a reputation as a champion of jihad against the Franks, seized his opportunity while Joscelin was away with the county's field army.

The siege

Zengi's army invested Edessa on 28 November 1144. Denied relief, the defence fell to Archbishop Hugh, the Armenian and Syriac clergy and the citizenry. Zengid sappers undermined the north wall, which collapsed on 24 December; the city was stormed, much of the Latin population was massacred or enslaved, though Zengi spared the native Christians and restored their churches. The citadel surrendered two days later.

Aftermath

News of Edessa's fall reached Europe in 1145. Pope Eugene III issued the bull Quantum praedecessores, and Bernard of Clairvaux preached what became the Second Crusade (1147–1149), which ended in failure before Damascus. A brief Frankish reoccupation of Edessa in 1146 ended in a second massacre; the rump of the county was sold to the Byzantines and absorbed by Nur al-Din.
