A fighting knife is a knife designed primarily as a weapon for close combat, as distinct from a utility knife designed mainly as a tool — though in practice most military knives serve both purposes and are often called combat knives. Fighting knives typically have a blade optimised for thrusting and cutting, a guard to protect the hand, and a handle shaped for a secure grip in varied holds. They have been standard equipment for soldiers, commandos and special forces since the mass trench warfare of the First World War.

History

Daggers have served as fighting weapons since antiquity. The modern fighting knife emerged in the trenches of the First World War, where improvised and issued "trench knives" — sometimes fitted with knuckle-duster guards — were used in raids. The Second World War produced the most famous designs: the British Fairbairn–Sykes fighting knife, a slender double-edged stiletto developed for commandos by William E. Fairbairn and Eric A. Sykes, and the American Ka-Bar, issued to US Marines from 1942 as a combined fighting and utility knife. Later designs include the SOG knife of Vietnam-era reconnaissance units and modern survival/combat hybrids.

Design

Fighting knife blades commonly range from about 15 to 20 cm, in clip-point, spear-point, tanto or stiletto profiles, single- or double-edged. Designers balance thrusting efficiency, cutting ability, strength and utility: pure stilettos excel at penetration but are fragile and poor tools, so most issued knives are compromises. Full-tang construction, non-reflective finishes and lanyard holes are common features.

Use and law

Knife combat is taught in many military combatives systems, though in modern warfare fighting knives are used far more often as tools than as weapons. Civilian ownership and carrying of fighting knives — particularly double-edged daggers and automatic knives — is restricted in many jurisdictions.
