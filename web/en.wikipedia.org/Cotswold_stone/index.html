Cotswold stone

TypeOolitic limestone
AgeJurassic
RegionCotswolds, England
ColourHoney, cream, golden buff

Cotswold stone is a yellow oolitic limestone quarried in the Cotswolds region of south-central England. Formed in shallow tropical seas during the Jurassic period, the stone is famous for the honey-coloured houses, churches, and dry-stone walls that give Cotswold villages their distinctive appearance.

Geology

The stone belongs chiefly to the Inferior Oolite and Great Oolite groups, laid down roughly 165–175 million years ago. It consists of small spherical grains of calcium carbonate (oolites) cemented together. Colours range from the deep gold of the northern Cotswolds around Broadway to paler creams in the south near Bath.

Use in building

Cotswold stone has been used since Roman times and is the traditional material for the region's vernacular architecture: rubble-stone cottages, manor houses, and churches roofed with stone slate tiles split from fissile beds of the limestone. Planning rules in the Cotswolds Area of Outstanding Natural Beauty often require new buildings to use natural or reconstituted Cotswold stone to preserve the landscape character.

Quarries

Historic quarries include those around Burford, Northleach, and Temple Guiting. Because the stone weathers and splits along its bedding planes, it is also used for dry-stone walling, which criss-crosses the region's fields.
