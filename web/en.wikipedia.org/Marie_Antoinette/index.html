Marie Antoinette (born Maria Antonia Josepha Johanna; 2 November 1755 – 16 October 1793) was the last queen consort of France before the French Revolution. An archduchess of Austria and the penultimate child of Empress Maria Theresa, she married the future Louis XVI in 1770 at the age of fourteen to seal the Franco-Austrian alliance, and became queen when he ascended the throne in 1774.

Queen of France

Initially charmed by her youth and beauty, French opinion turned against her. Her perceived extravagance at Versailles — her private retreat at the Petit Trianon, her fashions, and her circle of favourites — earned her the nickname "Madame Déficit", and the Affair of the Diamond Necklace (1785), a fraud in which she was falsely implicated, badly damaged her reputation. The famous phrase "let them eat cake" (qu'ils mangent de la brioche) is often attributed to her but appears in Rousseau's Confessions, written before she arrived in France, and there is no evidence she said it.

Revolution

During the Revolution she became a focus of popular hatred as l'Autrichienne ("the Austrian woman") and was blamed for obstructing reform. After the royal family's failed Flight to Varennes in June 1791 and the storming of the Tuileries in August 1792, the monarchy was abolished. Louis XVI was executed in January 1793; Marie Antoinette was tried by the Revolutionary Tribunal on charges of treason and executed by guillotine on the Place de la Révolution on 16 October 1793, nine months after her husband.

Legacy

She has remained an enduring cultural figure — a symbol for some of frivolity and the injustices of the ancien régime, and for others of a maligned woman destroyed by propaganda. Her life has been the subject of many biographies (notably Stefan Zweig's) and films, including Sofia Coppola's Marie Antoinette (2006). In 1815 her remains were reburied in the Basilica of Saint-Denis.
