Peerage

A peerage is a legal system historically comprising various titled ranks of hereditary and lifetime nobility, particularly in the United Kingdom. Peers are members of the peerage; the five ranks, in descending order, are duke, marquess, earl, viscount, and baron.

The British peerages

There are five peerages in British history: the Peerage of England (before 1707), the Peerage of Scotland, the Peerage of Ireland, the Peerage of Great Britain (1707–1800), and the Peerage of the United Kingdom (from 1801). Peerages are created by the sovereign by writ or letters patent. Until the Life Peerages Act 1958, nearly all peerages were hereditary; hereditary peers historically sat in the House of Lords, but the House of Lords Act 1999 removed most, leaving 92 hereditary peers elected to remain.

Peers are addressed by styles such as "Lord" and "Lady", and their titles derive from the feudal baronies of the medieval period. The collective body of peers is sometimes called the peerage as a class; a person raised to it is said to be elevated or created a peer. Works of reference such as Burke's Peerage and Debrett's record the genealogies of titled families.

Other countries

Analogous systems existed elsewhere in Europe, including the peerage of France (pairie), whose peers were the great vassals of the crown, and Japan's Meiji-era kazoku. In most modern states such titles carry no legal privilege.
