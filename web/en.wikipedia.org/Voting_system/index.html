Voting system

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A voting system (or electoral system) is a set of rules that determine how elections are conducted and how their results are decided: who may vote, what choices appear on the ballot, how votes are counted, and how they are converted into outcomes such as the winners of seats in a legislature. The choice of system has major effects on party systems, representation, and political stability.

Families of systems

Plurality and majoritarian systems include first-past-the-post, used for the UK House of Commons and the US House of Representatives, two-round systems as in France, and the alternative vote. Proportional representation systems — party-list PR and the single transferable vote — allocate seats in proportion to votes. Mixed systems, such as mixed-member proportional representation in Germany and New Zealand, combine local plurality contests with compensatory proportional seats.

Criteria and theory

Social choice theory evaluates voting rules against criteria such as majority rule, the Condorcet criterion, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and resistance to strategic voting. Arrow's impossibility theorem shows that no ranked system can satisfy all plausible fairness criteria simultaneously, and the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem shows every non-dictatorial system with more than two options is manipulable in principle. Duverger's law observes that plurality systems tend toward two-party competition.

Practice

Electoral systems are frequently contested and reformed: New Zealand adopted MMP after referendums in the 1990s, and various jurisdictions have adopted ranked-choice voting. Practical design also covers ballot access, districting (and its abuse, gerrymandering), counting technology, and electoral administration.
