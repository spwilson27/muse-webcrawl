Binary star

A binary star or binary star system is a system of two stars that are gravitationally bound to and in orbit around each other. Binary stars are very common: a large fraction, possibly a majority, of stars are members of binary or multiple-star systems. The term must be distinguished from a double star, which may appear close together in the sky but is not physically bound.

Classification

Binary systems are classified by how they are observed: visual binaries can be resolved by telescope; spectroscopic binaries are detected by periodic Doppler shifts in their spectral lines; eclipsing binaries vary in brightness as the stars pass in front of each other; and astrometric binaries are inferred from the wobble of one visible star. By configuration, systems may be detached, semidetached (one star fills its Roche lobe), or contact binaries, in which the stars share an envelope.

Importance

Binary stars allow astronomers to measure stellar masses directly by applying Kepler's laws to their orbits; the mass–luminosity relation for main-sequence stars was derived largely from binaries. Interacting binaries produce phenomena such as novae, Type Ia supernovae (from accreting white dwarfs), and X-ray binaries, and mass transfer can radically alter the evolution of both stars.
