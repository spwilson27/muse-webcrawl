Qualitative research

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Qualitative research is a type of research that aims to gather and analyse non-numerical data in order to gain an understanding of individuals' social reality, including understanding their attitudes, beliefs, and motivation. It is widely used in the social sciences — sociology, anthropology, psychology, education, and health research — and relies on methods that explore meanings, experiences, and processes in natural settings.

Rather than testing hypotheses with measurements and statistics, qualitative research typically asks "how" and "why" questions, works with small, purposively selected samples, and treats the researcher as an instrument of data collection whose interpretations are part of the analytic process. Findings are usually reported as rich, thick description rather than numerical estimates.

Methods

  • Interviews — in-depth, semi-structured, or unstructured conversations with participants.
  • Focus groups — guided discussions among small groups.
  • Participant observation and ethnography — sustained fieldwork in a community or organization.
  • Case study — intensive study of a bounded system or instance.
  • Document and textual analysis, including discourse and content analysis.
  • Grounded theory — building theory inductively from systematically coded data (Glaser and Strauss, 1967).
  • Phenomenology and narrative research — studying lived experience and personal stories.

Analysis

Analysis commonly proceeds through coding of transcripts and field notes, constant comparison, and theme development, aided by software such as NVivo or ATLAS.ti. Quality criteria emphasized in the qualitative tradition include credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability (Lincoln and Guba), reflexivity, and member checking, rather than the statistical validity and reliability criteria used in quantitative work.

Comparison with quantitative research

Quantitative research gathers numerical data and applies statistical analysis to test hypotheses and estimate population parameters. The two approaches are often combined in mixed-methods designs, in which qualitative work may generate hypotheses, explain quantitative findings, or explore context. Debates over the relative standing of the traditions — sometimes called the "paradigm wars" — have largely given way to pragmatic pluralism in many fields.
