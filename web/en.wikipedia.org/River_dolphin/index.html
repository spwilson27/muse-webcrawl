River dolphin

River dolphins
GroupsPlatanistidae; Iniidae; (extinct Lipotidae); Pontoporiidae (partly estuarine)
DistributionGanges–Brahmaputra, Indus, Amazon–Orinoco basins
SensesReduced eyes; highly developed echolocation
StatusAll living species endangered or critically endangered

River dolphins are a polyphyletic group of fully aquatic mammals that live exclusively in freshwater rivers and estuaries. Unlike oceanic dolphins, they are not a single lineage but the surviving members of several toothed-whale families that independently colonised the great river systems of Asia and South America.

[Image placeholder: A pink Amazon river dolphin surfacing in dark river water]

Species

Living river dolphins include the Ganges river dolphin and the Indus river dolphin (Platanista), functionally blind and feeling their way with echolocation in silt-laden water; and in South America the Amazon river dolphin or boto (Inia geoffrensis), the largest river dolphin and famously pink as an adult, along with the smaller tucuxi and the franciscana of the Río de la Plata estuary. China's Yangtze river dolphin, the baiji (Lipotes vexillifer), was declared functionally extinct in 2006 — the first cetacean driven to extinction by human activity.

Adaptations

River dolphins typically have long, forceps-like beaks with many teeth for snatching fish in murky water, small eyes with poor vision, unfused neck vertebrae allowing the head to turn, broad flippers, and a low or absent dorsal fin so they can manoeuvre in flooded forests and shallow channels.

Threats

All river dolphins are threatened by dam construction fragmenting their habitat, entanglement in fishing nets, depletion of fish, pollution, and boat traffic. The Ganges dolphin is the national aquatic animal of India, yet numbers only in the low thousands.
