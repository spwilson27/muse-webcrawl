Transport in Switzerland is characterised by a dense, highly integrated public transport network and some of the highest levels of rail use per capita in the world. Despite the country's mountainous Alpine terrain, Switzerland has built an extensive system of railways, roads, tunnels, cable cars and lake shipping that connects its cantons and links northern and southern Europe across the Alps.

Railways

Switzerland has one of the densest railway networks in the world, with around 5,300 kilometres of line, almost all electrified. The largest operator is the federally owned Swiss Federal Railways (SBB CFF FFS), alongside numerous private and cantonal railways such as the Rhaetian Railway in Graubünden, famous for the Landwasser Viaduct and the Bernina line, a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Swiss trains run on a coordinated clock-face schedule (Taktfahrplan) introduced in 1982, allowing regular-interval connections between trains, buses, boats and cable cars across the country. The Swiss Travel Pass and the half-fare card (Halbtax) integrate ticketing across operators.

The New Railway Link through the Alps (NRLA) project built base tunnels to speed transalpine freight and passenger traffic: the Lötschberg Base Tunnel opened in 2007, the Gotthard Base Tunnel — at 57 kilometres the world's longest railway tunnel — in 2016, and the Ceneri Base Tunnel in 2020. Swiss voters have repeatedly approved rail investment in referendums, funded partly by a heavy-vehicle fee and fuel taxes, reflecting a policy of shifting freight from road to rail.

Roads

The Swiss road network covers about 85,000 kilometres, including roughly 1,500 kilometres of motorways (Autobahnen). Motorway use requires an annual vignette. Alpine road passes such as the Gotthard, Great St Bernard, Furka and Julier passes are historic transalpine routes, several supplemented by road tunnels; the Gotthard Road Tunnel, opened in 1980, is one of the longest road tunnels in the world. Speed limits are generally 120 km/h on motorways, 80 km/h outside built-up areas and 50 km/h in towns.

Air and water

The main international airport is Zurich Airport, hub of Swiss International Air Lines, with other international airports at Geneva, Basel–Mulhouse, Bern and Lugano. Scheduled boat services operate on the major lakes — Geneva, Lucerne, Zurich, Constance and Lugano — and the Rhine port at Basel connects Switzerland to Rotterdam and the North Sea, giving the landlocked country access to maritime trade; Basel is home to Switzerland's small merchant fleet registered under the Swiss flag.

Urban and mountain transport

Cities operate tram and bus networks, notably in Zurich, Basel, Bern, Geneva and Lausanne, the latter also having Switzerland's only full metro. Mountain transport is extensive: funiculars, gondolas and aerial tramways serve ski resorts and summits, and rack railways such as the Gornergrat and Jungfrau railways are major tourist attractions. The country also maintains a dense network of signed hiking trails and cycling routes.
