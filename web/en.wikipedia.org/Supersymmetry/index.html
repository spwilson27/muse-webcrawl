Supersymmetry

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Supersymmetry (SUSY) is a theoretical framework in particle physics that proposes a symmetry between fermions and bosons. Each particle of the Standard Model would have a "superpartner" differing in spin by one-half unit: quarks pair with squarks, leptons with sleptons, and gauge bosons with gauginos, while the Higgs sector is extended.

Motivations

Supersymmetry addresses several problems of the Standard Model. It stabilises the Higgs boson mass against large quantum corrections (the hierarchy problem), improves the unification of the gauge coupling constants at high energies, and — in models with conserved R-parity — provides a candidate dark matter particle, the lightest supersymmetric particle, often a neutralino. It also plays an essential role in string theory.

Models and experiments

The Minimal Supersymmetric Standard Model (MSSM) is the most studied realisation, adding the smallest possible set of new particles and a second Higgs doublet. If supersymmetry exists, it must be broken at accessible energies, since superpartners of equal mass would already have been observed. Searches at the Large Hadron Collider have found no superpartners so far, excluding many natural parameter ranges and pushing superpartner masses for coloured particles above roughly a teraelectronvolt — results that have strained, though not eliminated, supersymmetric theories.

History

The symmetry was developed in the early 1970s by researchers including Yuri Golfand and Evgeny Likhtman, Jean-Loup Gervais and Bunji Sakita, and Julius Wess and Bruno Zumino, and became a central research programme in theoretical physics from the 1980s onward.
