Classical Arabic

Classical Arabic or Quranic Arabic is the standardised literary form of Arabic used from the 7th century until roughly the 19th century. It is the language of the Quran, of classical Islamic scholarship, and of the great corpus of medieval Arabic literature in poetry, philosophy, science and law. Modern Standard Arabic descends directly from it, differing mainly in vocabulary and some stylistic conventions.

History

Classical Arabic was codified by grammarians of Basra and Kufa — notably Sibawayh, whose Kitāb laid the foundations of Arabic grammar — on the basis of the Quran, pre-Islamic poetry, and the speech of Bedouin informants considered to preserve the purest usage. With the Islamic conquests it spread across the Middle East and North Africa as the language of administration, religion and learning.

Features

Classical Arabic retains a full system of case endings (ʾiʿrāb), a dual number, and a rich verb morphology with ten common derived stems, most of which are reduced or lost in the modern spoken dialects. Its script, vocalised with diacritics in careful editions, records short vowels and nunation that are often omitted in everyday writing.

Legacy

Classical Arabic remains the liturgical language of Islam and the object of study in traditional religious education; its literature forms one of the largest bodies of pre-modern writing in any language.
