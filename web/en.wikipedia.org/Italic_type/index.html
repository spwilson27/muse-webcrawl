Italic type

Italic type is a cursive font based on a stylised form of calligraphic handwriting. Its letters slope slightly to the right and, unlike a merely slanted ("oblique") roman, many letterforms are drawn differently — most noticeably the single-storey a and the descender of the f. Italics are used principally to emphasise words in running text, and for such purposes as the titles of works, foreign words and phrases, and the scientific names of organisms.

History

Italic type was created in Venice around 1500 for the printer Aldus Manutius, whose punchcutter Francesco Griffo cut the first italic fount, based on the humanist cursive hand associated with scholars such as Niccolò de' Niccoli and the papal chancery. It was first used in 1500 and famously in Aldus's portable octavo edition of Virgil in 1501. The name "italic" records its Italian origin. Early italics were complete typefaces in their own right, used to set whole books compactly and cheaply, with upright (roman) capitals. Only over the following centuries did italic settle into its modern role as a companion face to roman, used within text set in roman type.

Use

In modern typography italics mark emphasis; titles of books, films, artworks and periodicals; names of ships, trains and aircraft; foreign words not naturalised in the language; and words mentioned as words. In scientific writing, genus and species names in binomial nomenclature are italicised (for example Homo sapiens), as are gene names in many conventions, while legal writing italicises case names. Style guides differ on details, and in handwriting underlining serves the same purpose — a survival from the typewriter era, when italic type was unavailable.

True italics and obliques

Type designers distinguish a true italic, with its own cursive letterforms, from an oblique, in which roman letters are mechanically slanted. Many sans-serif families supply obliques labelled as italics. In digital typography, where a requested italic is missing, software may synthesise an oblique automatically.
