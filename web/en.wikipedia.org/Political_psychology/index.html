Political psychology

Political psychology is an interdisciplinary academic field, blending psychology and political science, that applies psychological theory and methods to politics. It studies the psychological origins of political behavior — voting, ideology, leadership, group conflict, and political decision-making — and the political consequences of psychological processes.

Political psychology examines how the mind shapes political life.

History

The field's roots include William McDougall's early work, Harold Lasswell's Psychopathology and Politics (1930), which applied psychoanalysis to leaders, and Frankfurt School research on the authoritarian personality (Adorno et al., 1950). It was institutionalized in the 1970s with the founding of the International Society of Political Psychology (1978) and the journal Political Psychology (1979).

Main topics

Research areas include political socialization; personality and leadership assessment at a distance; ideology and system justification; motivated reasoning and misinformation; intergroup relations, prejudice, and ethnic conflict; terrorism and political violence; foreign policy decision-making and crisis behavior; and the psychology of voting and political persuasion. Major concepts include authoritarianism, social dominance orientation, cognitive biases in judgment, and groupthink in advisory groups.

Methods

Political psychologists use surveys, laboratory and field experiments, psychobiography, content analysis, and increasingly behavioral and neuroscientific measures, while debating the ethics of profiling public figures, codified in debates over the American Psychiatric Association's Goldwater rule.
