Highland dress

Highland dress is the traditional regional dress of the Highlands and Isles of Scotland. It is often characterised by tartan (plaid) patterns in specific colours and designs. A modern set for men, usually worn for formal occasions and at Highland games, comprises a kilt (or trews), a contemporary jacket, a sporran, and other accessories. Highland dress is also worn by women, whose outfits may include tartan sashes and skirts.

History

The earliest form of Highland dress was the féileadh mór or belted plaid: a large length of tartan cloth gathered into pleats and belted at the waist, with the excess draped over the shoulder. The tailored small kilt (féileadh beag) emerged in the early eighteenth century. After the Jacobite rising of 1745, the Dress Act of 1746 banned the wearing of Highland dress by civilians in an effort to suppress Highland culture; the act was repealed in 1782. During the nineteenth century, spurred by romantic interest in Scotland, the pageantry surrounding George IV's visit to Edinburgh in 1822, and Queen Victoria's enthusiasm for Balmoral, Highland dress took its modern, codified form, with tartans assigned to particular clans.

Components

The centerpiece is the kilt, a knee-length pleated garment of tartan wool. It is worn with a sporran (a pouch hung at the front), kilt hose with flashes, sturdy brogues or ghillie brogues, and a sgian-dubh (a small knife tucked into the hose). Jackets range from the formal Prince Charlie coatee with waistcoat, worn with a bow tie, to the tweed Argyll jacket for day wear. Women's Highland dress commonly features a tartan sash pinned with a brooch, worn over a dress or with a skirt.

Modern use

Highland dress is worn at weddings, ceilidhs, formal dinners, military ceremonies, and Highland games in Scotland and throughout the Scottish diaspora. Scottish regiments of the British Army retain Highland dress for ceremonial duties, and pipe bands wear it worldwide.
