Civil rights

The March on Washington for Jobs and Freedom, 28 August 1963, a landmark of the American civil rights movement.

Civil rights are guarantees of equal social opportunities and equal protection under the law, regardless of race, religion, sex, disability, national origin or other protected characteristics. The term is often contrasted with civil liberties: civil liberties are freedoms that governments may not infringe (such as speech and religion), whereas civil rights concern the right to equal treatment in areas like employment, housing, education and public accommodation.

Legal protection

In the United States, civil rights are protected by the Reconstruction Amendments — the Thirteenth, Fourteenth and Fifteenth — and by statutes such as the Civil Rights Act of 1964, the Voting Rights Act of 1965 and the Fair Housing Act of 1968. Similar protections exist in other constitutional systems, including the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms and the European Convention on Human Rights.

Movements

The term is closely associated with the American civil rights movement of the 1950s and 1960s, which used boycotts, sit-ins, freedom rides and marches to overturn Jim Crow segregation in the South, culminating in landmark legislation and Supreme Court decisions such as Brown v. Board of Education (1954). Comparable struggles for equal rights have occurred in many countries, including the anti-apartheid movement in South Africa and the Northern Ireland civil rights movement.
