Soil horizon

A soil profile with its master horizons

A soil horizon is a layer parallel to the soil surface whose physical, chemical, and biological characteristics differ from the layers above and beneath it. Horizons are defined in many cases by obvious physical features, mainly colour and texture. A vertical sequence of horizons, from the surface down to unweathered parent material, constitutes a soil profile.

Master horizons

Soil scientists designate the main horizons with capital letters: O, A, E, B, C, and R.

  • O horizon — dominated by organic material, such as leaf litter and partially decomposed plant matter.
  • A horizon — topsoil; mineral soil mixed with humus, usually dark in colour and biologically active.
  • E horizon — an eluvial (leached) layer, pale in colour, from which clay, iron, and aluminium oxides have been washed out.
  • B horizon — subsoil; a zone of accumulation (illuviation) of materials leached from above, often richer in iron and clay.
  • C horizon — weathered parent material, little affected by soil-forming processes.
  • R horizon — hard bedrock.

Formation

Horizons develop through the soil-forming processes of additions, losses, translocations, and transformations, driven by climate, organisms, relief, parent material, and time — the five soil-forming factors identified by Hans Jenny. Not all soils possess every horizon; young or heavily eroded soils may show only an A horizon over C material.

Use in classification

The presence, thickness, and properties of diagnostic horizons are central to soil classification systems such as USDA soil taxonomy and the World Reference Base for Soil Resources.
