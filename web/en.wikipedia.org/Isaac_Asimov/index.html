Isaac Asimov

Isaac Asimov (/ˈæzɪmɒv/; January 2, 1920 – April 6, 1992) was an American writer and professor of biochemistry at Boston University, known for his works of science fiction and popular science. He is considered one of the "Big Three" science fiction writers, along with Robert A. Heinlein and Arthur C. Clarke.

Works

Asimov wrote or edited more than 500 books. His most famous fiction is the Foundation series, which won the Hugo Award for "Best All-Time Series", and the Robot series, in which he formulated the Three Laws of Robotics. Standalone novels such as The Gods Themselves (1972) and the story "Nightfall" (1941), voted the best science fiction short story of all time, are landmarks of the genre. His Guide to the Bible, Asimov's Chronology of Science and Discovery, and countless essays exemplify his popular science writing.

Life

Born in Petrovichi, Russia, Asimov emigrated to Brooklyn, New York, as a child. He earned a PhD in biochemistry from Columbia University in 1948. He coined the term "robotics" and was a famously prolific explainer — "the Great Explainer" — as well as vice-president of Mensa International and president of the American Humanist Association.
