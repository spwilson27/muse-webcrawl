Battle of Midway

The Battle of Midway was a major naval battle in the Pacific Theater of World War II that took place on 4–7 June 1942, six months after Japan's attack on Pearl Harbor. The United States Navy, under Admirals Chester W. Nimitz, Frank J. Fletcher and Raymond A. Spruance, defeated an Imperial Japanese Navy fleet under Admirals Isoroku Yamamoto and Chūichi Nagumo. The battle is widely considered the turning point of the Pacific War.

Background

Seeking to eliminate the United States as a Pacific power and draw its carriers into a decisive battle, Yamamoto planned to invade Midway Atoll and ambush the American fleet that came to its relief. Unbeknownst to the Japanese, US cryptanalysts at Station HYPO under Joseph Rochefort had partially broken the JN-25 naval code and confirmed Midway (designated "AF") as the target. Nimitz positioned his three carriers—Enterprise, Hornet and the hurriedly repaired Yorktown—northeast of Midway to ambush the ambushers.

The battle

On the morning of 4 June, Japanese carrier aircraft struck Midway while Nagumo held a second wave armed for anti-ship attack. Conflicting reports about an American carrier force led Nagumo to order his reserve aircraft re-armed with land bombs and then back to torpedoes, leaving his decks crowded with fuelled and armed planes. American torpedo squadrons from the three US carriers attacked first and were slaughtered—most famously Torpedo Squadron 8, which lost all fifteen Devastators—but their sacrifice drew Japanese combat air patrols down to low altitude and scattered the Japanese formation. At about 10:22 a.m., SBD Dauntless dive-bombers under Wade McClusky and Maxwell Leslie arrived overhead almost unopposed and, in the space of a few minutes, fatally damaged the carriers Akagi, Kaga and Sōryū. The fourth Japanese carrier, Hiryū, launched counter-strikes that disabled Yorktown, but was herself sunk that evening; Yorktown was finished off by a Japanese submarine while under tow.

Aftermath

Japan lost four fleet carriers and the heavy cruiser Mikuma, over 300 aircraft and, most critically, a large cadre of irreplaceable veteran naval aviators. American losses were one carrier and one destroyer. The defeat destroyed Japan's strategic initiative; from Midway onward, Japan fought a defensive war against America's growing industrial might. Historians rank Midway among the most consequential naval battles in history.
