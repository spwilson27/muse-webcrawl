A tendon is a tough band of fibrous connective tissue that connects muscle to bone and transmits the mechanical force of muscle contraction to the skeleton, enabling movement of the joints. Tendons are composed mainly of parallel bundles of type I collagen fibres produced by fibroblasts called tenocytes, giving them high tensile strength. They are similar to ligaments, which connect bone to bone, and to fasciae, which connect muscle to muscle.

Structure

Collagen fibres are organised hierarchically into fibrils, fibres, fascicles and the tendon itself, surrounded by sheaths (the epitenon and, in some locations, a lubricated synovial sheath). Tendons are relatively poorly supplied with blood, which contributes to their slow healing. Some tendons, such as the Achilles tendon, also act as springs, storing and releasing elastic energy during walking and running.

Clinical significance

Tendons are subject to overuse injuries (tendinopathy, historically called tendinitis), inflammation of their sheaths (tenosynovitis), and acute ruptures—most famously of the Achilles tendon and the rotator cuff tendons of the shoulder. Healing proceeds through inflammation, proliferation and remodelling phases over weeks to months.
