Civilian control of the military

FieldCivil–military relations
Key theoristsSamuel P. Huntington, Morris Janowitz
OppositeMilitary dictatorship, praetorianism

Civilian control of the military is a doctrine in military and political science holding that ultimate responsibility for a country's strategic and military decisions rests with its civilian political leadership rather than with professional military officers. It is a defining norm of democratic governance.

Principles

Civilian control is typically secured through constitutional provisions making an elected civilian the commander-in-chief, legislative control of military budgets and declarations of war, and civilian defence ministries supervising the armed forces. The armed forces themselves are expected to be politically neutral and to refuse unlawful orders.

Theory

Samuel P. Huntington's The Soldier and the State (1957) distinguished "objective control" — maximising military professionalism and autonomy in a narrow sphere — from "subjective control", in which civilian groups bind the military to themselves. Morris Janowitz's The Professional Soldier (1960) emphasised integration of the military with society. Later scholarship examines the "civil–military problematique": a military strong enough to protect the state is also strong enough to threaten it.

Failures and challenges

Where civilian control breaks down, outcomes range from undue military influence over policy to outright coups d'état and military juntas, historically common in parts of Latin America, Africa, and Southeast Asia. Modern challenges include the influence of intelligence agencies, private military companies, and military lobbying within democratic systems.
