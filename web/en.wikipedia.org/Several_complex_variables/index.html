Several complex variables is the branch of mathematics that studies holomorphic functions of several complex variables, that is, functions f : ℂn → ℂ with n ≥ 2. Although it grows out of the classical theory of functions of one complex variable, the subject in several variables is strikingly different: phenomena that are routine in one variable, such as isolated zeros and arbitrary domains of holomorphy, behave in entirely new ways when n ≥ 2. The theory is closely tied to complex geometry, algebraic geometry, and the theory of partial differential equations.

History

The subject began in the late nineteenth century with work of Weierstrass and Poincaré, but took recognisably modern form with Friedrich Hartogs's discoveries around 1906, showing that separate holomorphicity implies joint holomorphicity and that holomorphic functions in several variables cannot have isolated singularities. In the 1930s and 1940s Henri Cartan and Kiyoshi Oka built the foundations of the modern theory: Oka solved the Cousin problems and the Levi problem, and with Cartan developed the theory of coherent analytic sheaves. After the Second World War, sheaf cohomology, in the hands of Cartan, Serre, and Grauert, unified the subject, and in the 1960s Lars Hörmander recast many of its central existence theorems using L² estimates for the Cauchy–Riemann equations.

Holomorphic functions

A function f defined on an open set in ℂn is holomorphic if it is complex differentiable in each variable separately, or equivalently if it satisfies the Cauchy–Riemann equations in each variable. By Osgood's lemma and Hartogs' theorem, separate holomorphicity implies that f can be expanded locally in a convergent power series in all variables jointly. Holomorphic functions are therefore automatically infinitely differentiable and real-analytic, and obey a Cauchy integral formula on polydiscs.

Hartogs' phenomenon

Hartogs' phenomenon (or Hartogs' extension theorem) states that if n ≥ 2 and K is a compact subset of a domain Ω ⊂ ℂn such that Ω \ K is connected, then every holomorphic function on Ω \ K extends holomorphically to all of Ω. Consequently, holomorphic functions of several variables never have isolated zeros or isolated singularities, and their natural domains are severely restricted. A classical special case is the Hartogs figure — a "hollow" domain in ℂ², missing an interior polydisc in one direction — every holomorphic function on which extends to the full bidisc.

Domains of holomorphy and pseudoconvexity

A domain Ω is a domain of holomorphy if there is no larger domain to which every holomorphic function on Ω extends. Eugenio Elia Levi proposed that domains of holomorphy are characterised locally by a geometric convexity condition with respect to holomorphic functions, now called pseudoconvexity. The resulting Levi problem — whether every pseudoconvex domain is a domain of holomorphy — was solved affirmatively by Oka in the 1940s and later by Bremermann and Norguet independently. Pseudoconvexity can be expressed through the existence of a plurisubharmonic exhaustion function, linking the theory to potential theory.

The ∂̄ problem and Stein manifolds

Solving the inhomogeneous Cauchy–Riemann equation ∂̄u = f is the key analytic step in most existence theorems. Hörmander's L² method gives solutions with weighted estimates on pseudoconvex domains. Abstractly, the theory is developed on Stein manifolds — complex manifolds that are holomorphically convex and holomorphically separable — which play the role in complex geometry that affine varieties play in algebraic geometry. Cartan's theorems A and B state that coherent sheaves on Stein manifolds are generated by global sections and have vanishing higher cohomology, making global constructions possible.
