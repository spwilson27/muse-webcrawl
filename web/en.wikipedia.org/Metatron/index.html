Metatron

Metatron (Hebrew: מֶטָטְרוֹן, Meṭāṭrōn) is an archangel in Jewish mysticism and folklore, regarded in many traditions as the highest of the angels, the celestial scribe, and the "Lesser YHWH". He does not appear by name in the Hebrew Bible or the New Testament, but he is prominent in the Talmud, in Merkabah and Hekhalot literature, and in the apocryphal Book of Enoch tradition.

Origins and Enoch

In the best-known tradition, Metatron is the transformed patriarch Enoch, who "walked with God" and was taken up into heaven without dying (Genesis 5:24). In the Third Book of Enoch (3 Enoch), Enoch recounts how he was raised to heaven, his flesh turned to flame, and enthroned beside God as Metatron, prince of the Presence and keeper of the heavenly archives. The Babylonian Talmud mentions Metatron a few times, including a passage in which the heretic Elisha ben Abuyah mistakes the seated Metatron for a second divine power.

Roles

Metatron is described as the scribe who records the deeds of humanity, the guide of the souls of the righteous, the angel who led Israel through the wilderness, and the chief of the ministering angels, sometimes said to have seventy names. In Kabbalah he is associated with the sefirah Keter or, in other schemes, serves as the active presence of divinity in the world (the Shekhinah's counterpart). As a being close to the throne, he mediates between God and other angels.

Name and etymology

The origin of the name is debated. Proposed derivations include Greek meta ("after/beyond") combined with thronos ("throne") — "the one who serves behind the throne" — Latin metator ("measurer, guide"), or a purely mystical coinage. The name appears in numerous variant spellings in manuscripts.

In later culture

Metatron has entered popular imagination: he is a character in Philip Pullman's His Dark Materials, the television series Supernatural and Good Omens (where Alan Rickman's film portrayal in Dogma made him "the voice of God"), and the geometric figure known as Metatron's Cube in sacred geometry derives from his name. His status in Islam is debated by scholars, some linking him to the figure Mīṭaṭrūn in early Islamic texts.
