Philosopher king

Philosopher king
OriginPlato, Republic (c. 375 BC)
ConceptRule by those who know the Form of the Good
Key textRepublic, Book V, 473c–e

The philosopher king is a hypothetical ruler in whom political power and philosophical wisdom are united, introduced by Plato in the Republic. In one of the dialogue's most famous passages, Socrates declares that "there will be no end to the troubles of states, or of humanity itself, till philosophers become kings in this world, or till those we now call kings and rulers really and truly become philosophers."

In Plato's Republic

Philosopher kings form the guardian class of the ideal city (Kallipolis). Raised and educated communally, they study mathematics, dialectic, and the theory of Forms for decades; only at about fifty are those who grasp the Form of the Good permitted—indeed compelled—to rule. Crucially, Plato's philosophers rule reluctantly, preferring contemplation, which is presented as a safeguard against tyranny. They hold property and family in common to eliminate private interests that might corrupt their judgment.

Criticism and reception

Critics from antiquity onward have doubted that wisdom guarantees just rule or that a population would accept such rulers. In the twentieth century, Karl Popper's The Open Society and Its Enemies attacked Plato's proposal as a blueprint for totalitarianism. Others note that Plato himself grew more cautious in the Statesman and Laws. The Roman emperor Marcus Aurelius, author of the Meditations, is often cited as the closest historical approximation of a philosopher king.
