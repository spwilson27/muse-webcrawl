Boethius

Anicius Manlius Severinus Boethius (c. 477 – 524 AD), commonly known simply as Boethius, was a Roman senator, consul, magister officiorum, philosopher and theologian of the early 6th century. He is best known for The Consolation of Philosophy, written in prison while awaiting execution, one of the most influential books of the Middle Ages.

Life

Boethius was born in Rome into the wealthy and distinguished Anicii family. Orphaned young, he was raised by Quintus Aurelius Memmius Symmachus, whose daughter Rusticiana he later married. He became consul in 510 and rose to become magister officiorum (head of the civil service) under Theodoric the Great, the Ostrogothic king of Italy. His two sons were made joint consuls in 522.

His fall was swift. Accused of treason — of conspiring with the Byzantine Empire against Theodoric — he was arrested, imprisoned at Pavia, and executed in 524, traditionally by a cord tightened around his forehead followed by clubbing, or by beheading.

Works

Boethius undertook to translate all of Plato and Aristotle into Latin and comment on them; he completed translations of Aristotle's logical works (the Organon), which for centuries formed the basis of logic in the Latin West. His textbooks on arithmetic and music (De institutione arithmetica and De institutione musica) were standard university texts for a thousand years and helped define the medieval quadrivium.

The Consolation of Philosophy

Written in prison in 523–524, the Consolation is a dialogue between Boethius and Lady Philosophy, who visits his cell and teaches him that worldly fortune is fickle and that true happiness lies in God, the highest good. It alternates prose and verse, drawing on Plato, Aristotle and Neoplatonism while — surprisingly — making no explicit appeal to Christian revelation. The work was translated by King Alfred the Great, Chaucer, and Queen Elizabeth I, among many others, and its image of Fortune's wheel became a central symbol of medieval culture.

Boethius was venerated as a martyr-saint in the diocese of Pavia, and Pope Benedict XVI discussed his life and thought in a general audience in 2008.
