Native American religions are the spiritual practices of the Indigenous peoples of North America. There is no single "Native American religion": ceremonial ways vary greatly among hundreds of distinct nations and are shaped by each people's language, history, and homeland. Nevertheless, scholars identify recurrent features across many traditions: predominantly oral transmission; a world animated by spiritual powers (often glossed as animism); the centrality of land and place; cyclical ceremonies keyed to seasons, hunts, and harvests; and ritual specialists such as medicine people and shamans.

Regional traditions

Plains peoples developed the Sun Dance and the vision quest; the Pueblos of the Southwest maintain katsina ceremonies within kivas; Iroquoian peoples hold thanksgiving cycles addressed to the Creator; Northwest Coast peoples hold potlatches and winter ceremonials; Arctic peoples traditionally honoured sea-mammal spirits through shamans (angakkuit). Many traditions centre on a supreme being or mystery — for example Wakan Tanka (Lakota), the Great Spirit.

Colonial impact and revitalisation

European colonisation, forced removal, missionisation, and government suppression — including Canada's and the United States' bans on ceremonies such as the potlatch and Sun Dance, and the residential/boarding school systems — severely disrupted Indigenous religions. Responses included prophetic movements such as the Longhouse Religion founded by Handsome Lake (1799), the Ghost Dance of Wovoka (1889–90), which ended in the Wounded Knee massacre, and the peyote religion organised as the Native American Church (incorporated 1918).

Legal recognition

The American Indian Religious Freedom Act (1978) affirmed the right to traditional practice, though courts limited its reach (e.g., Lyng v. Northwest Indian Cemetery Protective Association, 1988; Employment Division v. Smith, 1990, after which Congress protected sacramental peyote use in 1994). The Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act (1990) required return of human remains and sacred objects. Practitioners today continue to advocate for access to sacred sites and the protection of ceremonies.
