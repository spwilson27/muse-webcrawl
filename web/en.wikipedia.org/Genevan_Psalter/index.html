Genevan Psalter

The Genevan Psalter, also known as the Psautier de Genève, is a metrical psalter in French that was created under the supervision of John Calvin for use by the Reformed church in Geneva. Over the years that followed, it became the defining musical repertoire of the Reformed churches of France, the Netherlands, and beyond, and several of its tunes remain in wide use today, most notably the tune known in English as "Old 100th" for Psalm 134 and Psalm 100.

History

In Strasbourg, where he ministered to a French refugee congregation from 1538 to 1541, Calvin published the first collection of metrical psalms in French, Aulcuns Pseaulmes et cantiques mys en chant (1539), containing versifications by Clément Marot and by Calvin himself. Returning to Geneva, he encouraged the project of a complete psalter: Marot, and after his death Théodore de Bèze (Theodore Beza), versified all 150 psalms, while composers including Loys Bourgeois and Pierre Davantès supplied the melodies. The complete psalter appeared in 1562, and a four-part harmonisation by Claude Goudimel followed soon after.

Characteristics

The melodies of the Genevan Psalter are modal, syllabic, and confined to a range a congregation could sing; most settings were intended for unison, unaccompanied singing in worship, with harmonised versions reserved for home use. Because each psalm has its own metre and tune, the collection contains over a hundred distinct melodies — an unusually rich repertoire for a single psalter.

Influence

Translated into Dutch, German, Hungarian, and other languages, the Genevan tunes shaped Reformed psalmody for centuries. Several melodies passed into Lutheran and Anglican use; the Psalm 124 tune became associated with the Dutch national anthem tradition, and the Psalm 68 tune was used as a battle hymn by Huguenots and Dutch rebels alike. Modern hymnals still print a number of Genevan tunes.
