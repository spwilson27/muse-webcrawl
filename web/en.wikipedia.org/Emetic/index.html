Emetic

Emetic
EffectInduces vomiting
ExamplesSyrup of ipecac, apomorphine, copper sulfate
OppositeAntiemetic

An emetic is a substance that induces vomiting. Emetics have historically been used in cases of poisoning to expel toxins from the stomach, although their routine use has declined sharply in modern toxicology because of limited effectiveness and the risk of aspiration and other complications. The most famous emetic is syrup of ipecac, prepared from the roots of the ipecacuanha plant.

Mechanism

Emetics act either by irritating the lining of the stomach or by stimulating the chemoreceptor trigger zone in the medulla oblongata. Apomorphine, a dopamine agonist, acts centrally and has been used in veterinary medicine and in some clinical settings. Copper sulfate and zinc sulfate act as gastric irritants but carry risks of their own toxicity.

Medical use

Poison control authorities in many countries no longer recommend keeping syrup of ipecac in the home; the American Academy of Pediatrics advised against its routine household use in 2003. Activated charcoal and hospital-based treatment have largely replaced emesis as first-line management of ingested poisons.

Misuse

Emetics have been misused by people with eating disorders such as bulimia nervosa to purge after eating. Chronic ipecac abuse can cause serious cardiac and muscular toxicity from its alkaloid emetine.
