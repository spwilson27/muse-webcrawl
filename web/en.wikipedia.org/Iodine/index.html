Iodine

Iodine is a chemical element; it has symbol I and atomic number 53. The heaviest of the stable halogens, it exists at standard conditions as a semi-lustrous, non-metallic solid that melts to a deep violet liquid and boils to a violet gas. The element was discovered by the French chemist Bernard Courtois in 1811 and named by Joseph Louis Gay-Lussac after the Ancient Greek for "violet".

Properties and occurrence

Iodine is the least abundant of the stable halogens, being the sixty-first most abundant element. It occurs in seawater and in some soils and minerals, and is extracted commercially from Chilean caliche deposits and Japanese and American brines. Elemental iodine is slightly soluble in water and readily sublimes.

Biological role

Iodine is an essential trace element: the thyroid gland uses it to synthesize the hormones thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3). Iodine deficiency, the leading preventable cause of intellectual disability worldwide, leads to goitre and hypothyroidism; iodized salt programmes have greatly reduced its prevalence. The recommended daily intake for adults is about 150 micrograms.

Applications

Uses include tincture of iodine and povidone-iodine antiseptics, contrast agents for medical imaging, catalysts in producing acetic acid and polymers, LCD polarizing films, and the radioactive isotope iodine-131 in treating thyroid disorders. Potassium iodide tablets can block thyroid uptake of radioactive iodine after nuclear accidents.
