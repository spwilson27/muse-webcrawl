Plebeian Council

The Plebeian Council (Latin: Concilium Plebis) was the principal popular assembly of the Roman Republic through which the plebeians of Rome — the common citizens — could pass laws, elect magistrates, and conduct legal trials. Only plebeians could vote in it; patricians were excluded.

Organisation

The council was organised by tribes, eventually numbering thirty-five, and was presided over by a tribune of the plebs or a plebeian aedile. By tradition it first met in 471 BC, when the Lex Publilia Voleronis reorganised its elections by tribe rather than by curia, reducing aristocratic influence.

Powers and legislation

Resolutions of the council, called plebiscites (plebiscita), initially bound only the plebeians themselves. A series of laws extended their force, and the Lex Hortensia of 287 BC made plebiscites binding on all citizens, patricians included — a landmark in the Conflict of the Orders. Thereafter the Plebeian Council became the main legislative body of the Republic, enacting most new statutes and electing the ten tribunes and the plebeian aediles each year.
