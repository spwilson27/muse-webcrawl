Ross Gyre

The Ross Gyre in the Pacific sector of the Southern Ocean

The Ross Gyre is a large cyclonic (clockwise, in the Southern Hemisphere) ocean gyre in the Ross Sea sector of the Southern Ocean, offshore of Antarctica. Together with the Weddell Gyre, it is one of the two great wind- and buoyancy-driven gyres that dominate the circulation south of the Antarctic Circumpolar Current.

Circulation

The gyre is bounded to the north by the Antarctic Circumpolar Current and to the south by the Antarctic continental shelf and the Ross Ice Shelf front. Its western limb is fed by the westward-flowing Antarctic Slope Current, while along its eastern side relatively warm Circumpolar Deep Water flows southward toward the continental shelf. This onshore flow of warm water is important because it can reach the underside of ice shelves—particularly in the Amundsen and Ross Sea sectors—and influence basal melting.

The surface circulation carries sea ice and cold, fresh shelf waters northward and eastward, and the gyre plays a central role in the formation and export of Antarctic Bottom Water precursors: dense, salty shelf water produced by brine rejection during sea-ice formation in Ross Sea coastal polynyas spills down the continental slope and ventilates the deep global ocean.

Variability and research

The strength and position of the Ross Gyre vary with the winds, particularly with phases of the Southern Annular Mode and tropical Pacific variability (ENSO), which modulate the Amundsen Sea Low. Strengthening westerly and easterly wind patterns can shift how much warm deep water intrudes onto the shelf, with consequences for ice-shelf stability and sea-level-relevant ice loss in West Antarctica. Because the region is remote and ice-covered for much of the year, the gyre is monitored largely through satellite altimetry and sea-ice drift, Argo and seal-borne sensors, moorings and occasional icebreaker surveys.
