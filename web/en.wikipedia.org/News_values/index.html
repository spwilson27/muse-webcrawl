News values

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A newsroom at work

News values are "the criteria that influence the selection and presentation of events as published news". These values help explain what makes something "newsworthy". The concept has been studied systematically since the 1960s, notably in the classic study by Johan Galtung and Mari Holmboe Ruge, "The Structure of Foreign News" (1965), which analysed how foreign events became news in Norwegian newspapers.

Galtung and Ruge's factors

Galtung and Ruge proposed a chain of factors including: frequency (events that fit the news cycle), threshold or scale, unambiguity, meaningfulness or cultural proximity, consonance with expectations, unexpectedness, continuity, composition of the news mix, reference to elite nations, reference to elite persons, personalisation, and negativity. The more of these factors an event satisfied, the more likely it was to be registered and reported.

Later developments

Subsequent researchers — including Herbert Gans, Tony Harcup and Deirdre O'Neill — have updated and contested the lists, adding entertainment, magnitude, relevance, "good news", follow-ups, the news organisation's own agenda, and, in the social-media era, shareability. Critics note that news values describe selection practices shaped by commercial and organisational pressures, not simply audience interest, and that they help explain systematic biases in coverage.

The idea is taught to trainee journalists as a practical guide to recognising a story: novelty, conflict, proximity, prominence and human interest.
