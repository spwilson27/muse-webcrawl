United States Department of Agriculture

The United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) is the federal executive department responsible for developing and executing government policy on farming, agriculture, forestry, rural economic development, and food. It aims to meet the needs of farmers and ranchers, promote agricultural trade and production, work to assure food safety, protect natural resources, foster rural communities, and end hunger in the United States and beyond. Its motto, unofficial motto aside, reflects Abraham Lincoln's description of it as "the people's department."

History

President Abraham Lincoln established the USDA in 1862, the same year he signed the Homestead Act and the Morrill Land-Grant Colleges Act. In 1889 it was elevated to Cabinet rank. The department grew through the agricultural crises of the early twentieth century, administering New Deal farm programs, and after the Second World War its food assistance and conservation programs expanded greatly. Nutrition programs — above all the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) — now account for the majority of departmental spending.

Agencies and programs

  • Food Safety and Inspection Service — inspects meat, poultry, and processed egg products.
  • Forest Service — manages 193 million acres of national forests and grasslands.
  • Natural Resources Conservation Service — soil and water conservation on private land.
  • Food and Nutrition Service — administers SNAP, school meals, and WIC.
  • Agricultural Research Service and APHIS — research, and animal and plant health protection.
  • Farm Service Agency and Risk Management Agency — farm loans, disaster aid, and crop insurance.

Influence

Through the periodic farm bill, the USDA shapes commodity supports, conservation incentives, rural broadband, and nutrition policy affecting millions of Americans. Its crop reports and grading standards are benchmarks for world agricultural markets.
