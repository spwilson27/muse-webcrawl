Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi (2 October 1869 – 30 January 1948), popularly known as Mahatma Gandhi, was an Indian lawyer, anti-colonial nationalist and political ethicist who employed nonviolent resistance to lead the successful campaign for India's independence from British rule. He inspired movements for civil rights and freedom across the world. Born and raised in a Hindu family in coastal Gujarat, Gandhi trained in law at the Inner Temple in London and first employed nonviolent civil disobedience as an expatriate lawyer in South Africa in the resident Indian community's struggle for civil rights.

Gandhi in London, 1931.

Indian independence movement

Returning to India in 1915, Gandhi organised peasants, farmers and urban labourers to protest against discrimination and excessive land tax. Assuming leadership of the Indian National Congress in 1921, he led nationwide campaigns for swaraj (self-rule), easing poverty, expanding women's rights and ending untouchability. His most famous protest, the 1930 Salt March to Dandi, saw him walk 240 miles to make salt in defiance of the British monopoly. The Quit India movement of 1942 demanded an end to British rule.

Assassination

On 30 January 1948, Gandhi was shot dead in New Delhi by Nathuram Godse, a Hindu nationalist who opposed his efforts to conciliate Muslims. India had achieved independence the previous August, accompanied by the Partition Gandhi had opposed and the mass violence he tried to quell through fasts.

Philosophy and legacy

Gandhi's philosophy of satyagraha (devotion to truth) and ahimsa (non-violence) drew on Hindu, Jain and Christian thought and influenced Martin Luther King Jr., Nelson Mandela and many others. He is honoured in India as the "Father of the Nation", and his birthday, 2 October, is observed worldwide as the International Day of Non-Violence.
