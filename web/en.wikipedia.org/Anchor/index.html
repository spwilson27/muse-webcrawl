Anchor

An anchor is a device, normally made of metal, that connects a vessel to the bed of a body of water to prevent the craft from drifting due to wind or current. Anchors can be temporary (carried aboard and deployed as needed) or permanent (used for moorings). Most work either by weight and friction, or by hooking into the seabed with flukes so that pulling on the rode sets them more firmly.

History

Anchors have been used since antiquity: early anchors were simply heavy stones, and the Greeks and Romans developed iron anchors with arms and flukes closely resembling later designs. The traditional Admiralty-pattern anchor has a fixed crossbar (stock) at the top; the stockless anchor, patented in the 19th century, can be drawn up into a hawse pipe and became standard on large ships.

Types

  • Admiralty (fisherman) anchor — traditional design with a stock and sharp flukes, effective in rock and weed.
  • Stockless anchor — pivoting flukes, standard on commercial ships.
  • Plough/CQR, Danforth, and Bruce anchors — modern small-craft designs for sand and mud.
  • Grapnel anchor — multiple tines, used for retrieving objects and on small boats.
  • Sea anchor and drogue — drag devices not attached to the bottom, used to slow drift or stabilize a vessel in heavy weather.

Use

The anchor is lowered on a chain or rope (the rode) with a scope — the ratio of rode length to depth — commonly of 5:1 to 7:1, so that the pull on the anchor is nearly horizontal, keeping it set. Permanent moorings may use mushroom anchors or massive concrete blocks. The anchor is also a widespread maritime symbol, appearing in heraldry, naval insignia, and tattoos.
