South Asian cuisine comprises the cuisines of the countries of South Asia — India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Nepal, Bhutan, Afghanistan and the Maldives — as well as the food traditions carried worldwide by the South Asian diaspora. It is one of the world's most distinctive culinary families, characterised by the extensive use of spices, herbs, vegetables and legumes, by rice and flatbreads as staple starches, and by complex layering of flavours achieved through techniques such as tempering (tadka) and slow simmering.

History

The roots of South Asian cooking reach back to the Indus Valley Civilisation, where wheat, barley, lentils and sesame were already cultivated. Vedic texts describe meals of grains, dairy and legumes, and the Ayurvedic tradition shaped lasting ideas about balancing tastes and the medicinal properties of spices. The medieval period brought Persian and Central Asian influences through trade and the Mughal Empire, contributing kebabs, pilafs and rich nut-and-cream sauces. From the sixteenth century, Portuguese traders introduced New World ingredients — chillies, tomatoes and potatoes — that became so central that they are now inseparable from the region's food.

Regional variations

North India and Pakistan

Wheat flatbreads such as naan, roti and paratha dominate, alongside tandoor-cooked meats, creamy tomato-based gravies, and dishes like butter chicken and biryani. Dairy in the form of ghee, paneer and yoghurt is prominent.

South India

Rice is the staple, served with fermented rice-and-lentil batters made into dosa and idli, coconut-based curries, tamarind-flavoured dishes such as sambar and rasam, and fiery Chettinad cooking.

Bengal and the east

The cuisines of West Bengal and Bangladesh revolve around rice and freshwater fish, mustard oil and mustard seed, panch phoron spice blends, and celebrated sweets such as rasgulla and sandesh.

Sri Lanka and Nepal

Sri Lankan food features coconut milk curries, hoppers and string hoppers, and pol sambol. Nepalese food centres on dal bhat (lentil soup with rice), steamed momos, and Newari specialities.

Staple ingredients and spices

Common ingredients include rice, wheat, lentils (dal), chickpeas, onions, garlic, ginger, and fresh herbs such as coriander leaf and curry leaf. Spice mixtures such as garam masala combine black cardamom, cloves, cinnamon, cumin and black pepper. Turmeric gives many dishes their golden colour, while asafoetida, fenugreek and mustard seeds are essential in regional repertoires.

Religious and cultural influences

Hindu traditions have made vegetarianism widespread, and much South Asian cooking is meatless by design rather than by substitution. Muslim communities observe halal rules and avoid pork; Ramadan fasting shapes festive iftar meals. Jain cooking excludes root vegetables, onions and garlic. Sikh community kitchens (langar) serve free vegetarian meals to all comers, one of the largest food-service traditions in the world.

Global spread

South Asian restaurants spread through Britain, North America, the Gulf states and Southeast Asia with migration. Dishes such as chicken tikka masala have been described as national dishes abroad, and South Asian grocery ingredients — from basmati rice to spice blends — are now stocked in supermarkets worldwide.
