British nobility

The British nobility consists of members of the peerage — the titled aristocracy of the United Kingdom — together with their families. The peerage is divided into five ranks, in descending order of precedence: duke, marquess, earl, viscount and baron. (In Scotland, the fifth rank equivalent to baron is "Lord of Parliament".) Titles may be hereditary or, since the Life Peerages Act 1958, granted for life; life peerages carry the rank of baron.

History

The peerage emerged in the feudal period, when great landholders were summoned to the king's council — the origin of the House of Lords. Separate peerages existed for England, Scotland and Ireland; after the Acts of Union, peerages of Great Britain (1707) and of the United Kingdom (1801) were created. Peerages were historically granted for military, political and court service and, in later centuries, more controversially in recognition of party donations.

Until 1999, most hereditary peers had the right to sit and vote in the House of Lords. The House of Lords Act 1999 removed that right from all but 92 hereditary peers, who remain elected by their fellows. New hereditary peerages have not been created for non-royals since 1964 (under the premiership of Harold Wilson), though the sovereign continues to grant titles within the royal family.

Forms of address

Dukes are styled "Your Grace" (the Duke/Duchess of X); marquesses, earls, viscounts and barons are "Lord/Lady X" (for barons) or "the Marquess/Earl/Viscount of X", and are addressed as "my lord". Younger children of senior peers use courtesy titles, and the prefix "The Honourable" or "Lord/Lady" before their Christian names according to rank. The gentry — baronets, knights, esquires and untitled landed families — are not part of the peerage, though often grouped with the nobility in the wider sense. Debrett's and Burke's Peerage are the standard reference works of the titled classes.
