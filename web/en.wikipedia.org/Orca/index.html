The orca (Orcinus orca), or killer whale, is a toothed whale and the largest member of the oceanic dolphin family, Delphinidae. Instantly recognisable by its black-and-white patterning, tall dorsal fin and robust build, the orca is a cosmopolitan apex predator found in every ocean, from Arctic and Antarctic waters to tropical seas. Orcas are highly social and intelligent; distinct populations specialise in different prey — fish, seals, penguins, sharks or other whales — and pass hunting techniques and vocal dialects down through generations, a form of animal culture.

Biology and behaviour

Orcas live in stable, matrilineal family groups (pods) led by older females, some of which live for decades beyond menopause — a trait shared with few other species. They hunt cooperatively: pods herd fish into bait balls, create waves to wash seals off ice floes, and deliberately strand themselves briefly to snatch sea lion pups from beaches. Communication uses clicks, whistles and pulsed calls, with pods sharing distinct dialects. Echolocation allows them to locate prey in darkness; some populations target the livers of great white sharks with surgical precision.

Ecotypes and populations

Researchers recognise numerous ecotypes differing in appearance, diet and genetics — for example the fish-eating "resident" and mammal-eating "transient" (Bigg's) orcas of the North Pacific, and the pack-ice and Ross Sea types of Antarctica — and some have proposed splitting them into separate species. Certain populations, such as the salmon-dependent Southern Residents of the Salish Sea, are endangered, threatened by prey scarcity, pollution and vessel disturbance.

Humans and orcas

Long feared as dangerous killers, orcas have never been recorded killing a human in the wild, though captive orcas have caused several deaths, fuelling debate about keeping them in oceanaria. Since the 1970s, when live captures for display were curtailed and wild research began (notably by Michael Bigg), the orca has become an icon of marine conservation.
