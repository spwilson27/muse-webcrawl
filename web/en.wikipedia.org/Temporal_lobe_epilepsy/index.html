Temporal lobe epilepsy

Temporal lobe epilepsy
Specialty: Neurology
Symptoms: Focal seizures, auras, automatisms, memory disturbance
Common cause: Hippocampal sclerosis
Treatment: Anti-seizure medication, surgery

Temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE) is a chronic disorder of the nervous system characterised by recurrent, unprovoked focal seizures that originate in the temporal lobe of the brain. It is the most common form of focal epilepsy in adults. The seizures may involve impaired awareness and are frequently preceded by an aura, sometimes described as a sense of déjà vu, a rising sensation in the abdomen, fear, or an unusual smell or taste.

Signs and symptoms

TLE seizures are focal seizures that may remain focal or spread to become bilateral tonic-clonic seizures. During a focal impaired-awareness seizure, the person may stare, become unresponsive, and perform repetitive automatic movements (automatisms) such as lip-smacking, chewing, swallowing, fumbling with the hands, or picking at clothing. Auras — now classified as focal aware seizures — include experiential phenomena such as déjà vu, jamais vu, intense emotion, and epigastric rising sensations, as well as olfactory or gustatory hallucinations arising from the uncus. After a seizure there is often a period of confusion and amnesia for the event.

Causes

The most common underlying pathology is hippocampal (mesial temporal) sclerosis, a pattern of neuronal loss and gliosis in the hippocampus, often associated with a history of prolonged febrile seizures in childhood. Other causes include tumours, cortical malformations, vascular lesions, trauma, infection, and autoimmune inflammation. International classification distinguishes mesial temporal lobe epilepsy, arising from the hippocampus, amygdala and parahippocampal gyrus, from neocortical (lateral) temporal lobe epilepsy.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis rests on the clinical history, electroencephalography (EEG) showing temporal epileptiform discharges, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), which may reveal hippocampal atrophy and signal change. Prolonged video-EEG monitoring is used to localise the seizure focus when surgery is considered. Neuropsychological testing often shows material-specific memory deficits.

Treatment

First-line treatment is anti-seizure medication, such as carbamazepine, lamotrigine, levetiracetam, or lacosamide. TLE associated with hippocampal sclerosis is frequently drug-resistant; for such patients, resective surgery — most commonly anterior temporal lobectomy or selective amygdalohippocampectomy — offers the best chance of seizure freedom, supported by randomised trial evidence. Neuromodulation options include vagus nerve stimulation and responsive neurostimulation.
