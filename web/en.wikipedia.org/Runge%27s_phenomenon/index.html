Runge's phenomenon

Runge's phenomenon is a problem of oscillation at the edges of an interval that occurs when using polynomial interpolation with polynomials of high degree over a set of equispaced interpolation points. It was discovered by the German mathematician Carl David Tolmé Runge in 1901 when investigating the behaviour of errors when using polynomial interpolation to approximate certain functions.

The Runge function

Runge considered the function f(x) = 1 / (1 + 25x²) on the interval [−1, 1]. Interpolating this smooth, analytic function at equally spaced points with a polynomial of increasing degree does not converge uniformly: near the ends of the interval the interpolant oscillates with an amplitude that grows as the degree increases, even though the maximum error near the centre shrinks.

Explanation

The phenomenon is related to the growth of the Lebesgue constant for equispaced nodes, which increases exponentially with the number of points, amplifying errors. It is sometimes confused with the Gibbs phenomenon, which concerns Fourier series, but the two effects are distinct.

Remedies

Oscillation can be avoided by choosing interpolation nodes that cluster near the ends of the interval, such as Chebyshev nodes, for which the Lebesgue constant grows only logarithmically; by using piecewise low-degree interpolation such as splines; or by using least-squares approximation rather than exact interpolation. For Chebyshev nodes, interpolation of analytic functions converges geometrically.
