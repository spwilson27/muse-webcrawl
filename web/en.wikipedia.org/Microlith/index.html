Microlith

A microlith is a small stone tool, typically of flint or chert, about a centimetre long and half a centimetre wide, produced by retouching a small blade or flake. Microliths were made to be hafted — mounted, often in series, into wooden or bone handles to form composite tools such as arrows, harpoons, spears and sickles.

Production

Microliths were usually shaped from bladelets struck from prepared cores, using techniques such as the microburin technique to snap blades into segments that were then retouched into standard forms: crescents (lunates), triangles, trapezes, trapezoids and backed blades.

Distribution and chronology

Microlithic industries appear in the Late Upper Paleolithic and became characteristic of Mesolithic hunter-gatherer cultures across Europe, North Africa and Asia from roughly 35,000 to 3,000 years ago. In Africa, microliths in the Howiesons Poort industry of South Africa date to about 60,000–65,000 years ago and are among the early signs of complex composite technology. Microliths persisted into the Neolithic as arrowheads and sickle inserts that harvested early crops, identifiable by the characteristic "sickle gloss" from cutting silica-rich plant stems.

Significance

Because they were standardized, expendable and replaceable, microliths represent a shift toward maintainable, modular tool design. Their changing shapes are sensitive chronological markers used by archaeologists to sequence Mesolithic cultures.
