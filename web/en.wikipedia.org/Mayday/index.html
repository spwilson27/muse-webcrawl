Mayday

Mayday is the word used as a distress signal in voice-procedure radio communications. It is used to signal a life-threatening emergency primarily by aviators and mariners, but in some countries local organisations such as firefighters, police forces, and transportation organisations also use the term. Convention requires the word to be repeated three times in a row during the initial emergency declaration ("mayday, mayday, mayday") to prevent it being mistaken for some similar-sounding phrase under noisy conditions, and to distinguish an actual mayday call from a message about a mayday call.

Origin

The term derives from the French m'aider — a shortened form of venez m'aider ("come and help me"). It was devised in 1923 by Frederick Stanley Mockford, a senior radio officer at Croydon Airport, London, who was asked to think of a word that would be easily understood by all pilots and ground staff in an emergency; since much of the traffic at Croydon was to and from Le Bourget Airport in Paris, he proposed the adaptation of m'aider. It became the official voice distress call in 1927.

Usage

A mayday call has absolute priority over all other transmissions. It is made on designated emergency frequencies (121.5 MHz in aviation; VHF channel 16 at sea). Lesser urgencies are signalled by the call "pan-pan". Making a false distress call is a criminal offence in many jurisdictions. The equivalent in Morse code telegraphy is SOS.
