The University of Glasgow (abbreviated as Glasg. in post-nominals; Scottish Gaelic: Oilthigh Ghlaschu) is a public research university in Glasgow, Scotland. Founded by papal bull in 1451, it is the fourth-oldest university in the English-speaking world and one of Scotland's four ancient universities, along with Edinburgh, St Andrews, and Aberdeen. It is a member of the Russell Group of leading British research universities and of the international Universitas 21 network.

The university was founded at the initiative of King James II of Scotland by a bull of Pope Nicholas V, at the suggestion of Bishop William Turnbull. Teaching began in the chapter house of Glasgow Cathedral, and the institution later moved to the High Street in the east end of the city, where it remained for over four centuries. In 1870 the university relocated to its present site at Gilmorehill in Glasgow's West End, centred on the Gilbert Scott Building, one of the largest Gothic revival buildings in Britain.

History

During the Scottish Enlightenment, Glasgow became a centre of intellectual life: Francis Hutcheson held the chair of moral philosophy from 1729, and Adam Smith, author of The Wealth of Nations, studied and later taught at the university. The engineer James Watt worked as an instrument maker at the university, where conversations with Joseph Black — discoverer of latent heat — informed his improvements to the steam engine. In the nineteenth century Lord Kelvin taught natural philosophy at Glasgow for over half a century, and Joseph Lister developed antiseptic surgery while professor of surgery there.

The university admitted women from 1892, following the foundation of Queen Margaret College. It has continued to expand, absorbing institutions such as the Royal College of Science and Technology's predecessor bodies, St Andrew's College of Education in 1999, and developing a campus at Dumfries in partnership with the University of the West of Scotland.

Structure

The university is organised into four colleges: the College of Arts, the College of Medical, Veterinary and Life Sciences, the College of Science and Engineering, and the College of Social Sciences. Its principal officers include the Chancellor, the Rector — elected by the students, a tradition shared with other ancient Scottish universities — and the Principal and Vice-Chancellor. The General Council, comprising all graduates, elects assessors to the University Court.

Notable alumni and staff

Associated figures include theologian John Knox's contemporaries of the Reformation era, economist Adam Smith, physicists Lord Kelvin and Joseph Black, surgeon Joseph Lister, explorer David Livingstone (who studied medicine at Anderson's College, an affiliate), former First Minister Nicola Sturgeon, and Nobel laureates such as chemist Sir Derek Barton and physicist's collaborator Frederick Soddy. Seven Nobel Prize winners have been associated with the university.
