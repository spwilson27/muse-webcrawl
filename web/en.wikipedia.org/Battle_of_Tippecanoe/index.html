The Battle of Tippecanoe was fought on November 7, 1811, in Indiana Territory between American forces led by Governor William Henry Harrison and Native American forces associated with the Shawnee leaders Tecumseh and his brother Tenskwatawa, known as the Prophet. The battle took its name from the nearby Tippecanoe River.

Background

Tecumseh and Tenskwatawa had built a pan-Indian confederacy and a settlement at Prophetstown to resist American expansion and land cessions. While Tecumseh was away recruiting allies in the South, Harrison marched about 1,000 troops toward Prophetstown intending to disperse the confederacy.

The battle

Harrison's army camped near Prophetstown on November 6 after inconclusive talks. Before dawn on November 7, warriors attacked the camp. The Americans held their perimeter after hard fighting, and the attackers withdrew as daylight came and their ammunition ran low. The following day Harrison's men found Prophetstown abandoned and burned it along with its stored food supplies.

Aftermath

The battle destroyed Prophetstown and badly damaged the Prophet's reputation and the confederacy, though Tecumseh rebuilt his movement and allied with Britain in the War of 1812. Harrison's reputation as "Tippecanoe" later propelled him to the presidency, echoed in the famous slogan "Tippecanoe and Tyler Too."
