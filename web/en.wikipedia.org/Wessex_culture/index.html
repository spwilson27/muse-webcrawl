Wessex culture

The Wessex culture is the predominant prehistoric culture of central and southern England in the early Bronze Age, originally defined by the archaeologist Stuart Piggott in 1938. Flourishing roughly between 2000 and 1400 BC, it is known almost entirely from its rich burial mounds — round barrows on the chalk downlands of Wessex, including the cemeteries around Stonehenge.

Burials and goods

The culture is divided into Wessex I and Wessex II. Its richest graves, such as the Bush Barrow burial near Stonehenge excavated in 1808, contain gold ornaments, bronze daggers, amber beads, and shale and faience objects attesting to trade links with Brittany, Ireland and even the Mycenaean world; the gold lozenge from Bush Barrow is a masterpiece of early Bronze Age metalwork. Inhumations were later largely replaced by cremations, often in distinctive urns.

Interpretation

Piggott envisioned an invading warrior aristocracy; modern archaeologists instead see an indigenous society grown wealthy from trade and the prestige of monuments such as Stonehenge and Avebury. The culture's rich barrows near those monuments suggest its elite were buried close to sacred centres.
