VHF omnidirectional range

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A VOR ground station antenna — placeholder]

VHF omnidirectional range (VOR) is a type of short-range radio navigation system for aircraft, enabling aircraft with a receiving unit to determine their position and stay on course by receiving radio signals transmitted by a network of fixed ground radio beacons. It uses frequencies in the very high frequency band from 108.00 to 117.95 MHz.

Principle of operation

A VOR beacon transmits two 30 Hz signals: a reference signal, the same in all directions, and a variable signal whose phase varies with azimuth. The aircraft's receiver compares the phases of the two signals; the phase difference equals the magnetic bearing of the aircraft from the station, known as the radial. By tuning two VORs or by combining a bearing with a distance from DME (distance measuring equipment), a pilot can fix position precisely.

History and use

Developed in the United States in the late 1940s, VOR became the standard air navigation system worldwide from the 1960s, supporting the network of Victor airways in the US and similar route structures elsewhere. Since the 2000s, satellite-based GPS navigation has progressively displaced VOR, and aviation authorities have decommissioned many stations while retaining a minimum operational network (MON) as a backup in case of GPS outage.

Types

Stations include terminal VORs serving airports, low- and high-altitude en-route VORs, and combined VORTAC facilities that pair VOR with the military TACAN system for distance and bearing.
