Halite, the mineral form of sodium chloride

Sodium chloride (chemical formula NaCl), commonly known as salt, is an ionic compound consisting of sodium and chloride ions in a 1:1 ratio. It is the salt most responsible for the salinity of seawater and is essential to animal life, serving as a principal electrolyte in extracellular fluid. In cooking it is used worldwide as a seasoning and food preservative.

Production

Salt is produced by mining underground deposits of the mineral halite, by solution mining, and by evaporating seawater or brine in salt pans. World production exceeds 280 million tonnes per year. Most industrial salt is used in the chemical industry — as the feedstock for chlorine and sodium hydroxide via the chloralkali process — and for de-icing roads; a smaller share is refined for food use, often with iodine added to prevent iodine deficiency.

Structure and properties

Sodium chloride crystallises in a face-centred cubic lattice in which each sodium ion is surrounded by six chloride ions in an octahedral arrangement, and vice versa. It is highly soluble in water and its solutions conduct electricity. In the human body, sodium chloride regulates fluid balance and blood pressure; excessive dietary intake is associated with hypertension, and health authorities recommend limiting consumption to about 5 grams of salt per day.

History

Salt has been produced and traded since prehistory; its value as a preservative made it a strategic commodity, taxed by states (such as the French gabelle) and famously protested in Gandhi's Salt March of 1930. The English word "salary" is popularly linked to Roman payments for salt.
