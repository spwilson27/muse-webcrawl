Max Wertheimer

Max Wertheimer (15 April 1880 – 12 October 1943) was an Austro-Hungarian-born psychologist who was one of the three founders of Gestalt psychology, along with Kurt Koffka and Wolfgang Köhler. His 1912 paper on the phi phenomenon — the perception of apparent motion from rapidly presented static images — is conventionally taken as the founding document of the school. His posthumous book Productive Thinking (1945) examined insight and problem solving.

Life and work

Wertheimer studied at Prague, Berlin, and Würzburg, where he earned his doctorate under Oswald Külpe. At Frankfurt he conducted the phi-phenomenon experiments with Koffka and Köhler as his observers. He formulated principles of perceptual grouping — proximity, similarity, closure, and Prägnanz — arguing that the whole is other than the sum of its parts. Fleeing Nazi Germany in 1933, he joined the New School for Social Research in New York.

Influence

Gestalt ideas shaped perception research, design, and later cognitive psychology, and Wertheimer's seminars influenced figures such as Abraham Maslow and Solomon Asch.
