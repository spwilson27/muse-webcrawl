Earthquake prediction

Earthquake prediction is the attempt to specify, in advance and within narrow limits of time, place and magnitude, when a future earthquake will occur. It is distinct from earthquake forecasting, which gives probabilistic estimates over years or decades, and from earthquake early warning, which detects an earthquake already in progress and alerts distant locations seconds before shaking arrives. No method of short-term prediction has been demonstrated to work reliably, and the mainstream seismological view is that deterministic prediction of individual earthquakes is not currently — and may never be — feasible.

Proposed precursors

Many phenomena have been proposed as precursors: foreshocks, changes in groundwater levels and chemistry (such as radon emissions), electromagnetic signals (including the VAN method in Greece), ground deformation, seismic quiescence or activation patterns (such as the Mogi doughnut), and unusual animal behaviour. Claims of successful prediction include the evacuation before the 1975 Haicheng earthquake in China, credited largely to foreshocks; but the catastrophic 1976 Tangshan earthquake the following year was not predicted. The 2004 Parkfield earthquake, awaited by an intensively instrumented experiment in California, occurred later than forecast and showed no detected precursors.

Scientific assessment

Evaluations by bodies such as the International Commission on Earthquake Forecasting have found that most claimed precursors do not pass prospective, statistically rigorous testing. Research effort has therefore largely shifted toward probabilistic forecasting, operational aftershock forecasting, and early warning systems such as Japan's and the US ShakeAlert system.
