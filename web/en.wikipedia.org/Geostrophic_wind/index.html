Geostrophic wind

Balance of the pressure gradient force and the Coriolis force produces geostrophic flow parallel to the isobars.

In atmospheric science, the geostrophic wind is the theoretical wind that would result from an exact balance between the Coriolis force and the pressure gradient force — a situation known as geostrophic balance. The geostrophic wind flows parallel to isobars (lines of constant pressure) and provides a good approximation to the actual wind in the free atmosphere above the friction layer, especially in mid-latitudes.

Theory

When air begins to move from high toward low pressure under the pressure gradient force, the Coriolis effect deflects it — to the right in the Northern Hemisphere and to the left in the Southern Hemisphere — until the two forces balance and the flow is along, rather than across, the isobars. In the Northern Hemisphere this means low pressure lies to the left of the direction of travel, a rule of thumb known as Buys Ballot's law.

Equations

Neglecting friction and acceleration, the geostrophic wind components can be written in terms of the geopotential height gradient on a constant-pressure surface. The wind speed is proportional to the slope of the pressure surface and inversely proportional to the Coriolis parameter, so geostrophic winds are strong where isobars are closely spaced and weak near the equator, where the Coriolis parameter vanishes and the approximation fails.

Applications

Meteorologists use geostrophic balance to estimate winds from pressure charts, to diagnose vertical motion, and as the starting point for more complete approximations such as the gradient wind, which adds curvature effects.
