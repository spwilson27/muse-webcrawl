Federal Open Market Committee

The Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) is the branch of the United States Federal Reserve System that determines the direction of monetary policy by specifying the target for the federal funds rate — the interest rate at which banks lend reserves to one another overnight. The committee consists of twelve voting members: the seven members of the Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System, the president of the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, and four of the other eleven Reserve Bank presidents, who serve one-year terms on a rotating basis.

Meetings and decisions

The FOMC holds eight regularly scheduled meetings per year in Washington, D.C. At each meeting the committee reviews economic and financial conditions and votes on the stance of policy; its decisions are announced at 2:00 p.m. Eastern Time on the second day of the meeting, followed by minutes three weeks later and full transcripts after five years. Four times a year, members submit economic projections — including the closely watched "dot plot" of expected interest rates — published in the Summary of Economic Projections.

Operations

The committee's directives are implemented by the Open Market Trading Desk at the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, which buys and sells U.S. Treasury and agency securities to steer the federal funds rate into the target range. Since 2008 the committee has also used large-scale asset purchases ("quantitative easing") and forward guidance as supplementary tools, and it pays interest on reserve balances as its primary rate-control mechanism.

History

Open market operations were centralised under a committee by the Banking Acts of 1933 and 1935, which gave the Board of Governors a majority on the FOMC and made it the dominant policy-making body of the Federal Reserve. Its modern era of transparency began in 1994, when the committee started announcing policy changes immediately after meetings.
