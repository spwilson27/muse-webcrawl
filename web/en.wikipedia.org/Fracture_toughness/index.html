Fracture toughness

Fracture toughness
SymbolKIc
SI unitMPa·m1/2
FieldFracture mechanics

Fracture toughness is a property of a material that describes its ability to resist fracture when a crack is present. It is quantified by the critical stress intensity factor, KIc, at which a crack begins to propagate unstably under plane-strain conditions.

Definition

In linear elastic fracture mechanics, the stress field near a crack tip is characterised by the stress intensity factor K. When K reaches the material's fracture toughness, rapid crack growth ensues. Plane-strain fracture toughness testing, standardised for example by ASTM E399, uses pre-cracked compact tension or bend specimens to obtain a valid KIc value.

Typical values

Tough structural steels may exceed 100 MPa·m1/2, aluminium alloys typically range from 20 to 45 MPa·m1/2, while ceramics and glasses are often below 5 MPa·m1/2. Fracture toughness generally decreases at low temperatures in ferritic steels, a phenomenon central to the study of brittle failures such as the Liberty ship fractures of the Second World War.

Related measures

For materials that deform plastically before fracture, toughness may instead be characterised by the J-integral or crack tip opening displacement. Impact tests such as the Charpy test give a qualitative, though not equivalent, measure of notch toughness.
