Relativism

Protagoras: "Man is the measure of all things."

Relativism is a family of philosophical views holding that some central class of things — truth, moral values, knowledge, beauty or concepts — is relative to some framework, such as a culture, a language, a historical period or a conceptual scheme, and is not absolute. Relativism thus denies that there are standpoint-independent facts of the relevant kind.

Varieties

Cultural relativism, in anthropology, is the methodological principle that a culture's practices should be understood in their own terms (associated with Franz Boas and his students). Moral relativism holds that moral judgments are true or false only relative to a culture or moral framework. Epistemic relativism claims that standards of justification vary across frameworks; linguistic relativism (the Sapir–Whorf hypothesis) concerns the influence of language on thought. Alethic relativism concerns truth itself.

Arguments and criticism

Supporters appeal to the diversity of beliefs and practices across cultures and history, and to the apparent absence of neutral criteria for adjudicating deep disagreements. Critics raise the charge of self-refutation — if relativism is asserted as absolutely true, it contradicts itself — and argue that thoroughgoing moral relativism cannot condemn practices such as slavery or genocide when endorsed by a culture, and that it makes cross-cultural criticism and reform unintelligible. Defenders reply that relativism is itself relative, or that it functions as tolerance-promoting restraint rather than as a metaphysics.

History

Relativist themes appear in the Greek sophist Protagoras, in Sextus Empiricus's Pyrrhonism, and in Montaigne; modern forms were shaped by anthropology, historicism, Kuhn's account of scientific paradigms, and post-structuralist thought.
