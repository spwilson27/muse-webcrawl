Sargon of Akkad

Sargon of Akkad (Akkadian: Šarru-kīn, "the true king"; reigned c. 2334–2279 BC, middle chronology) was the founder of the Akkadian Empire, the first empire of Mesopotamia. Rising from humble origins to become cupbearer to the king of Kish, he conquered the Sumerian city-states of southern Mesopotamia and united them under his rule, creating a model of multi-ethnic empire that endured in Near Eastern memory for millennia.

Rise and conquests

Sargon defeated Lugal-zage-si of Uruk, who had briefly united Sumer, and went on to conquer all of southern Mesopotamia, extending his power into Elam, Syria (including Mari and Ebla), and possibly Anatolia. Later tradition credits him with 34 military campaigns. He founded a new capital, Akkad (Agade), whose location remains unidentified, and installed his daughter Enheduanna as high priestess of the moon god at Ur — she is regarded as the earliest named author in history.

Administration and legacy

Sargon appointed loyal officials and family members across his empire, standardized weights and measures, and promoted the Akkadian language in administration. His dynasty ruled for about 140 years, reaching its height under his grandson Naram-Sin. In later Mesopotamian literature Sargon became a legendary figure: the "Sargon Birth Legend," in which the infant Sargon is set adrift in a basket on the river, is often compared to the story of Moses.
