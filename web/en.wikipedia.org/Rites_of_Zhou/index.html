Rites of Zhou

The Rites of Zhou (Chinese: 周禮; pinyin: Zhōu lǐ), originally known as the Offices of Zhou, is a Chinese classic describing the bureaucracy and organisational system of the Zhou dynasty. Traditionally attributed to the Duke of Zhou, it was probably compiled in the late Warring States period (roughly the 3rd century BCE) and is counted among the "Three Rites" (San li) of the Confucian canon, alongside the Book of Rites and the Etiquette and Ceremonial.

Contents

The text lays out an idealised government of six ministries — the Offices of Heaven (education and palace administration), Earth (finance and census), Spring (rites and religion), Summer (war), Autumn (justice), and Winter (public works) — subdivided into some 360 offices. The original Winter chapter was lost and replaced in Han times by the Kaogong ji ("Record of the Examination of Crafts"), an important early source on technology and craft standards, covering wheelwrights, bronzeworkers, city planning and acoustics.

History and influence

The work surfaced in the Western Han dynasty and was championed by the scholar Liu Xin; the usurper Wang Mang used its blueprint in his reforms, and later reformers and statecraft writers, down to the Song dynasty, repeatedly invoked it as a model for government. Modern scholarship generally treats the text as an idealised, partly utopian construction rather than a record of actual Zhou practice, though it preserves genuine early material on ritual, ranks and crafts.
