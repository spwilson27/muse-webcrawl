Astrolabe

An astrolabe is an ancient astronomical instrument that was a handheld model of the universe. It could be used to measure the altitude above the horizon of a celestial body, determine the time of day or night, survey, and locate latitude. Its many uses made it described by scholars as a kind of early analog computer.

History

The principles of the astrolabe were known in ancient Greece; the instrument was developed further in the Islamic world during the Middle Ages, where it was refined for astronomy and for determining the direction of Mecca and prayer times. It was introduced to Europe via Islamic Spain by around the 11th century and remained widely used by astronomers and navigators until the 17th and 18th centuries, when it was superseded by the sextant and accurate clocks.

Construction

A planispheric astrolabe consists of a mater (body), interchangeable plates (tympans) engraved with altitude lines for a specific latitude, a rotating openwork rete marking bright stars and the ecliptic, a rule, and an alidade on the back for sighting celestial objects.

Uses

With an astrolabe, an observer could tell the time from the position of the Sun or a star, predict sunrise and sunset, find the heights of buildings and mountains, and cast horoscopes. The mariner's astrolabe was a simpler ring used at sea to measure the altitude of the Sun or Pole Star.
