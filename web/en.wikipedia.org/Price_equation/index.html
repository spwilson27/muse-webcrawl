Price equation

The Price equation (also known as Price's equation or Price's theorem) describes how a trait or gene frequency changes over time in evolutionary biology. Derived by George R. Price in 1970 while working in London, the equation partitions the total change in the average value of a trait into two components: a selection term, given by the covariance between the trait and fitness, and a transmission term, capturing changes that occur during inheritance, such as mutation or biased transmission.

Statement

In its common form, the equation reads: the change in mean trait value equals the covariance of fitness and trait divided by mean fitness, plus the expectation over parents of fitness-weighted trait change in their offspring, also divided by mean fitness. Because it is a mathematical identity derived from definitions rather than an empirical law, the Price equation holds exactly for any system of entities with identifiable "parents" and "offspring".

Applications

The equation underlies modern derivations of kin selection and Hamilton's rule, multilevel (group) selection theory, and the breeder's equation of quantitative genetics. It has also been applied outside biology, including in economics and statistical mechanics. Its generality is also its main limitation: by itself the equation is a bookkeeping statement, and dynamical predictions require additional assumptions about inheritance and fitness.
