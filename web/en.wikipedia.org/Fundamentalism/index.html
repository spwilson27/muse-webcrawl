Fundamentalism

Fundamentalism is a tendency among certain religious groups characterised by a strict literal interpretation of scripture and a belief that the doctrines established are beyond dissent. The term originated in early-20th-century American Protestantism, where it named a movement defending the "fundamentals" of the Christian faith against theological liberalism and modernism. It has since been applied, sometimes controversially, to movements within many religions, and more loosely to rigid adherence to any set of basic principles.

Origins

The word derives from The Fundamentals (1910–1915), a twelve-volume set of essays financed by the oil magnate Lyman Stewart and distributed free to pastors and missionaries. The Niagara Bible Conference (1878–1897) had earlier drawn up points of belief that fed into the movement. Five "fundamentals" are commonly listed: the inerrancy of Scripture, the virgin birth of Christ, substitutionary atonement, the bodily resurrection of Christ, and the authenticity of miracles (in some lists, the premillennial second coming of Christ replaces miracles). The 1925 Scopes "Monkey Trial" in Tennessee, over the teaching of evolution, became a cultural flashpoint for the movement.

Characteristics and broader use

Scholars such as those of the Fundamentalism Project (led by Martin Marty and R. Scott Appleby) identify common features across traditions: a reactive stance against perceived threats to religious identity, selective retrieval of doctrines and practices from tradition, moral dualism, absolutism and inerrancy toward sacred texts, and millennial or messianic expectations. The label has been applied to movements in Islam, Judaism, Hinduism, Sikhism, Buddhism, and Confucianism, though critics argue the term's Protestant origins make cross-religious use imprecise or pejorative. "Islamic fundamentalism", for example, is often replaced by scholars with terms such as Islamism or revivalism, which carry different connotations.

Usage and criticism

Because the term is frequently used by outsiders as a term of disapproval, many labelled groups reject it. Historians distinguish fundamentalism from evangelicalism generally: fundamentalism typically adds separatism (refusal of fellowship with theological liberals) and militant opposition to modernist theology and secular culture. Outside religion, "fundamentalism" is used metaphorically — for instance "market fundamentalism" — to denote doctrinaire adherence to a principle regardless of consequences.
