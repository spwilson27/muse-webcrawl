Phoenix Park Murders

Phoenix Park, Dublin, scene of the assassinations of 6 May 1882.

The Phoenix Park murders were the fatal stabbings on 6 May 1882 of Lord Frederick Cavendish, the newly appointed Chief Secretary for Ireland, and Thomas Henry Burke, the Permanent Under-Secretary, in Phoenix Park, Dublin. The killings were carried out by members of the Irish National Invincibles, a radical splinter group of the Fenian movement, using surgical knives.

The attack

Cavendish had arrived in Dublin only that morning and was walking through the park with Burke toward the Viceregal Lodge when the assassins, who had been waiting for Burke, struck. Burke was the intended target — as head of the Irish civil service administration he was hated by nationalists; Cavendish, unrecognized, was killed because he tried to defend Burke. Both men died of their wounds.

Investigation and trials

The murders shocked Britain and Ireland and embarrassed Charles Stewart Parnell's nationalist movement, which was pursuing constitutional Home Rule; Parnell condemned the killings. Superintendent John Mallon's investigation, aided by the informer James Carey, broke the Invincibles. Five men — Joe Brady, Michael Fagan, Thomas Caffrey, Tim Kelly, and Dan Curley — were hanged in 1883. Carey, who had turned approver, was himself shot dead aboard ship by Patrick O'Donnell.

Consequences

The murders hardened British policy, producing coercion legislation, and a cross in the park's roadway still marks the site. The killings remain a landmark in the history of Irish republican violence and of the Land War era.
