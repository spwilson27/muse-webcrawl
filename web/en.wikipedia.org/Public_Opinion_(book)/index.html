Public Opinion (book)

Public Opinion

Author: Walter Lippmann

Published: 1922

Publisher: Harcourt, Brace & Co.

Subject: Political science, media

Public Opinion is a 1922 book by the American journalist and political commentator Walter Lippmann. It is one of the foundational works of modern media studies and democratic theory, examining how citizens form beliefs about a world too large and complex to experience directly.

Argument

Lippmann argued that people act not on the world itself but on a "pseudo-environment" — the "pictures in our heads" — assembled from news, cultural stereotypes, and personal experience. Because the press is an imperfect and commercially driven instrument for representing reality, public opinion in a mass democracy is systematically distorted, and the ideal of the informed, omnicompetent citizen is unattainable. The book popularized the word "stereotype" in its modern social sense and influenced later research on agenda-setting, framing, and propaganda.

Reception and debate

The philosopher John Dewey reviewed Public Opinion as perhaps the most effective indictment of democracy ever penned, and answered it in The Public and Its Problems (1927) with a more participatory vision of democratic life. The exchange remains a touchstone debate in democratic theory. Lippmann's proposals for expert intelligence bureaus to inform policy anticipated later technocratic institutions, while his skepticism about mass opinion continues to provoke criticism from defenders of participatory democracy.
