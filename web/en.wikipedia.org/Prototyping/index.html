Prototyping

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Prototyping is the process of creating an early sample, model, or release of a product built to test a concept or process. It is used in many contexts — engineering, industrial design, electronics, and software development — to validate form, fit, and function, to gather user feedback, and to reduce the risk and cost of proceeding in the wrong direction. A prototype may range from a rough sketch or paper model (low fidelity) to a fully functional pre-production unit (high fidelity).

Types and approaches

Prototypes are commonly classified along several dimensions. Evolutionary prototypes are refined iteratively into the final product, while throwaway (rapid) prototypes are discarded after serving their learning purpose. Horizontal prototypes show breadth — many features at shallow depth — whereas vertical prototypes implement a narrow slice in depth. Physical prototypes test tangible properties such as ergonomics and structural strength; virtual prototypes use simulation and computer-aided design to test performance before anything is built.

In software development, prototyping is a recognized development model in which a working version with core features is built early, demonstrated to users, and revised in cycles. It contrasts with specification-heavy approaches when requirements are uncertain or when user interaction is the main risk. Paper prototyping and interactive wireframes are widely used for testing user interfaces.

Fabrication methods

Modern prototyping is closely associated with rapid prototyping technologies, particularly additive manufacturing (3D printing) methods such as fused deposition modeling, stereolithography, and selective laser sintering, which build parts layer by layer directly from digital models. Subtractive methods — CNC machining, laser cutting — and traditional model making are also used. Electronics prototyping typically proceeds on breadboards and custom printed circuit boards in small runs.

Purpose and benefits

Prototyping lets designers and stakeholders explore ideas concretely, uncover usability problems early when changes are cheap, communicate design intent, and support investment or regulatory decisions. In product development frameworks such as design thinking and lean startup methodology, the prototype — and its distilled form, the minimum viable product — is the central instrument for testing hypotheses with real users.
