Contempt of Congress

Congress may cite witnesses who obstruct its investigations for contempt.

Contempt of Congress is the offence of obstructing the work of the United States Congress or one of its committees, most commonly by refusing to testify or to produce documents in response to a subpoena. The power derives from Congress's implied constitutional authority to conduct investigations in aid of its legislative function, affirmed by the Supreme Court in cases such as McGrain v. Daugherty (1927).

Procedures

Three enforcement routes exist. Under the statutory procedure in force since 1857, a committee reports a contempt citation to its full chamber, which may certify the matter to the United States Attorney for the District of Columbia for prosecution; conviction carries a fine and up to twelve months' imprisonment. Under the older inherent contempt power, Congress may itself arrest, try, and detain a contemnor, a power last used in the 1930s. Finally, Congress may sue in federal court to enforce a subpoena, a route used in disputes over executive privilege.

Notable cases

Contempt citations featured in the investigations of the Teapot Dome scandal, the Hollywood blacklist era (the "Hollywood Ten" were convicted of contempt in 1947), and the Watergate period. In 2012 the House of Representatives held Attorney General Eric Holder in contempt over documents concerning the "Fast and Furious" gun-running operation, the first sitting cabinet officer so cited. In 2021 and 2022, several figures were cited for defying subpoenas from the committee investigating the January 6 attack on the Capitol; Steve Bannon and Peter Navarro were convicted at trial.

Disputes over contempt frequently raise questions of executive privilege and separation of powers, and their resolution often depends on negotiation between the branches.
