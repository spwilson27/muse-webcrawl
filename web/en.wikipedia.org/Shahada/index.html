The Shahada (Arabic: ٱلشَّهَادَة, aš-šahādah, "the testimony"), also transliterated as Shahadah, is an Islamic oath and creed, and the first of the Five Pillars of Islam and part of the adhan. It reads: Lā ʾilāha ʾillā Allāh, Muḥammadun rasūlu Allāh — "There is no god but God; Muhammad is the Messenger of God." Reciting this statement sincerely before witnesses is the sole formal requirement for converting to Islam.

Meaning and variants

The first clause affirms absolute monotheism (tawhid): the word Allāh is simply the Arabic word for God, used also by Arab Christians and Jews. The second clause accepts Muhammad as God's final prophet. Shia Islam adds an optional third phrase, "'Ali is the wali (guardian/friend) of God," reflecting Ali's special status, and the Shahada is therefore sometimes given a tripartite form in Shia contexts. A longer version appears in the Quran's verses individually (e.g., 37:35 and 48:29), though the combined formula as a creed is attested in hadith and in early inscriptions such as those of the Dome of the Rock in Jerusalem (691–692 AD).

Usage

The Shahada is whispered into the ears of newborns, included in the call to prayer and the prayer itself, repeated in daily devotion, and traditionally hoped to be a dying person's last words. Calligraphic renderings decorate mosques, coins, and documents; the text appears on the flags of Saudi Arabia, Afghanistan, and the former Islamic State of Iraq and the Levant, among others. It is distinct from shirk, the sin of associating partners with God, which the Shahada's first clause explicitly negates.
