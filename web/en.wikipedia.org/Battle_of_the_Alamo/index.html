The Battle of the Alamo (23 February – 6 March 1836) was a pivotal event in the Texas Revolution. Following a 13-day siege, Mexican troops under President General Antonio López de Santa Anna reclaimed the Alamo Mission in San Antonio de Béxar (modern-day San Antonio, Texas), killing all of the Texian defenders. The stand of the garrison became an enduring symbol of resistance, and "Remember the Alamo!" became a rallying cry of the revolution.

Background

After Texian forces drove Mexican troops out of Texas in late 1835, a small garrison fortified the former mission known as the Alamo. Santa Anna marched north with several thousand men to crush the rebellion. The Alamo's roughly 180–260 defenders were commanded jointly by William Barret Travis, who led the regulars and volunteers, and James Bowie, who fell ill during the siege; the garrison included the famed frontiersman and former US congressman Davy Crockett with a company of Tennessee volunteers. Travis's letter "To the People of Texas & All Americans in the World", appealing for reinforcements "in terms of Victory or Death", became one of the most famous documents of the revolution; only 32 men from Gonzales arrived in answer.

The assault

Before dawn on 6 March 1836, Mexican columns assaulted the walls after a bombardment. The defenders repelled two attacks but were overwhelmed by the third; fighting moved room to room inside the compound, and nearly all the Texian combatants were killed. Mexican casualties were heavy — estimates range from several hundred to around 600 killed and wounded. Non-combatants, including Susanna Dickinson and the enslaved man Joe, were spared and sent to spread word of the defeat.

Legacy

Six weeks later, Sam Houston's army routed Santa Anna at San Jacinto, shouting "Remember the Alamo!" The Alamo chapel survives as a shrine and museum in downtown San Antonio and is among the most visited historic sites in Texas; its defenders remain central figures in Texan and American popular memory, though modern scholarship also emphasises the revolution's context, including Mexico's abolition of slavery and disputes over federalism.
