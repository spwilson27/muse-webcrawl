A herding dog, also known as a stock dog or working dog, is a type of dog that is trained to herd livestock such as sheep and cattle, or that belongs to a breed developed for herding work. Herding dogs work in partnership with a handler, responding to voice, whistle, and hand commands to gather, drive, and hold animals. Some breeds instead work as livestock guardians, living with the flock to protect it from predators; those are a distinct type from the herding breeds proper.

Behaviour and instinct

Herding behaviour is a modified predatory sequence: selection has emphasised the "eye–stalk–chase" elements of hunting while suppressing the kill-bite. Strong-eyed breeds such as the Border Collie work wide and low, fixing sheep with an intense stare ("eye"), while "loose-eyed" upright breeds (many cattle dogs) work closer and may nip heels to move stubborn stock ("heelers"). Handlers direct dogs with commands for flanking movements — traditionally "come-bye" (clockwise) and "away" (counter-clockwise) in Britain — and for pace ("steady"), stopping ("lie down"), and gathering ("that'll do").

Breeds

Well-known herding breeds include the Border Collie, Rough and Smooth Collies, Shetland Sheepdog, Australian Cattle Dog, Australian Shepherd, German Shepherd Dog, Belgian Shepherds, Old English Sheepdog, Bearded Collie, Briard, Puli, and the Cardigan and Pembroke Welsh Corgis — the corgis' low stature suited them to driving cattle while avoiding kicks. Many kennel clubs place these breeds in a Herding or Pastoral group.

Training and trials

Puppies from working lines show herding instinct from a few months old and are trained progressively on calm livestock. Sheepdog trials, formalised in Britain from 1873, judge dogs on outruns, lifts, fetches, drives, sheds, and pens over a set course; the International Sheep Dog Society organises national and international championships. Herding ability is also assessed in instinct tests for pet and show dogs of herding breeds.

Working life

On farms and ranches worldwide, herding dogs remain economically essential, doing the work of several people in gathering stock from rough or mountainous country. Their intelligence, energy, and need for a job also make many herding breeds popular — and demanding — companion animals.
