In Greek mythology, Cronus (also spelled Kronos or Cronos) was the leader and youngest of the first generation of Titans, the children of Uranus (Sky) and Gaia (Earth). He overthrew his father and ruled during the mythological Golden Age, until he was in turn overthrown by his own son Zeus and imprisoned in Tartarus. In Roman mythology he was identified with Saturn.

Mythology

Castration of Uranus

According to Hesiod's Theogony, Uranus imprisoned his children within Gaia, who fashioned a great sickle of adamant and urged her sons to punish him. Only Cronus answered; from ambush he castrated Uranus and cast the severed genitals into the sea, from whose foam Aphrodite was born. Cronus thereby became king of the gods and, with his sister-wife Rhea, fathered the elder Olympians.

The devoured children and the Titanomachy

Warned by a prophecy that he would be overthrown by his own child, Cronus swallowed each of his children at birth: Hestia, Demeter, Hera, Hades and Poseidon. When Zeus was born, Rhea hid him in Crete and gave Cronus a stone wrapped in swaddling clothes, which he swallowed instead. Grown to manhood, Zeus forced Cronus to disgorge his siblings (in some accounts with the help of Metis's potion) and, after a ten-year war called the Titanomachy, defeated the Titans and cast Cronus and his allies into Tartarus, guarded by the Hecatonchires.

Cult and interpretation

Cronus was associated with the harvest and with a bygone age of plenty; the Athenian festival of Kronia and the Roman Saturnalia celebrated a temporary return to that golden, egalitarian age. Because Greek Kronos was conflated in folk etymology with Chronos (Time), Cronus was later depicted as "Father Time", an old man with a scythe or sickle — an image captured in art such as Goya's Saturn Devouring His Son.
