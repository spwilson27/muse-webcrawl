Chinua Achebe


Chinua Achebe in 2008

Chinua Achebe (16 November 1930 – 21 March 2013) was a Nigerian novelist, poet, and critic, regarded as a central figure of modern African literature. His first novel and magnum opus, Things Fall Apart (1958), occupies a pivotal place in African literature and remains the most widely studied, translated, and read African novel. Achebe sought to counter colonial-era portrayals of Africa by writing from an Igbo perspective, in English infused with Igbo language and oral tradition.

Life

Achebe was born in Ogidi, in present-day Anambra State, Nigeria, to Christian convert parents. He studied at University College, Ibadan, initially reading medicine before switching to English, history and theology. After graduating he worked for the Nigerian Broadcasting Service, rising to become Director of External Broadcasting. When civil war broke out over the secession of Biafra (1967–1970), Achebe served as a roving ambassador for the Biafran cause. His experiences informed his poetry and the short-story collection Girls at War. He later taught at universities in Nigeria and the United States; a car accident in 1990 left him paralysed from the waist down, after which he spent most of his remaining career at Bard College and Brown University in the United States.

Works and influence

Achebe's novels include No Longer at Ease (1960), Arrow of God (1964), A Man of the People (1966) and Anthills of the Savannah (1987). His 1975 essay "An Image of Africa: Racism in Conrad's Heart of Darkness" was an influential critique of Western literary representations of Africa. As founding editor of Heinemann's African Writers Series, he helped launch the careers of many African writers. His memoir There Was a Country (2012) recounts the Biafran war. He won the Man Booker International Prize in 2007, and Things Fall Apart has been translated into more than 50 languages and sold millions of copies worldwide.
