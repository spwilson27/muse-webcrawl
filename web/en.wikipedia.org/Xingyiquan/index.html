Xingyiquan is one of the three classic internal Chinese martial arts.

Xingyiquan (Chinese: 形意拳, "Form-Intention Fist") is a Chinese martial art characterized by aggressive, explosive, predominantly linear movements and simultaneous attack and defence. Along with tai chi and baguazhang, it is one of the three major "internal" (Neijia) styles of Chinese martial arts.

History

Legend attributes the art to the Song dynasty general Yue Fei, but its documented history begins with Ji Jike (also called Ji Longfeng) of Shanxi in the 17th century, who is said to have adapted spear techniques into empty-hand methods. The art spread through Shanxi, Henan and Hebei branches, and was popularized in the late Qing dynasty by masters such as Li Luoneng, who codified much of the modern system, his student Guo Yunshen — famous for the "crushing fist" (beng quan) — and Sun Lutang, who wrote some of the earliest published manuals on the internal arts.

Principles and training

The foundation of Xingyiquan is the standing practice santishi ("three-body posture"). Its basic striking methods are the Five Fists, corresponding to the five elements of Chinese philosophy: pi (splitting, metal), zuan (drilling, water), beng (crushing, wood), pao (pounding, fire) and heng (crossing, earth). Beyond these, practitioners study forms imitating twelve animals, including the dragon, tiger, monkey, horse, rooster, hawk and snake. The art stresses whole-body coordinated power (zheng ti jin), stepping in directly on the opponent, and the unity of intention (yi) and form (xing).
