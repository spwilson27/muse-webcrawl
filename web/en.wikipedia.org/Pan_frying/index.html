Pan frying

Pan frying
TypeDry-heat cooking method with shallow fat
EquipmentFrying pan (skillet)
Fat usedSmall amount of oil or fat
Related methodsSautéing, shallow frying, deep frying, searing

Pan frying is a form of frying characterised by the use of a minimal amount of cooking oil or fat in a shallow frying pan, typically over medium to high heat. Unlike deep frying, in which food is fully submerged, pan-fried food sits in a thin layer of fat and must be turned at least once to cook and brown both sides.

Technique

The pan and fat are heated before the food is added, so that its surface quickly browns through the Maillard reaction and caramelisation. Foods are often coated in flour, breadcrumbs or batter to form a crisp crust, and the food is usually portion-sized or cut flat so that the interior cooks through in roughly the time the exterior needs to brown — cutlets, fish fillets, fritters, pancakes and eggs are classic examples. Because only a small quantity of fat is used, pan frying sits between sautéing (still less fat, food kept moving) and shallow frying (fat perhaps a third of the way up the food).

Equipment and fats

Almost any heavy pan serves, including cast-iron skillets, stainless steel and non-stick pans. The choice of fat depends on the desired flavour and its smoke point: butter gives flavour but burns easily, while neutral oils such as grapeseed or refined olive oil tolerate the heat better. The browned residues left in the pan — the fond — are commonly deglazed with wine or stock to make a pan sauce.
