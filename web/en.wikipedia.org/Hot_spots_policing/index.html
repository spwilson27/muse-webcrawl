Hot spots policing

Hot spots policing is a crime-prevention strategy in which law enforcement resources are focused on small geographical areas or places, usually street segments or addresses, where crime is concentrated. The strategy rests on criminological research showing that crime is not evenly spread across a city but clusters heavily: studies in several cities have found that roughly half of crime occurs at about 5 percent of street segments, a pattern sometimes called the "law of crime concentration".

Evidence

Hot spots policing is one of the most rigorously evaluated strategies in policing. Randomised experiments, notably the Minneapolis Hot Spots Patrol Experiment of the 1990s, and later systematic reviews by the Campbell Collaboration, have found that focusing patrol and problem-solving efforts on hot spots produces significant reductions in crime and calls for service. Research generally finds little evidence that crime simply displaces to nearby areas; instead, benefits often "diffuse" to surrounding streets.

Criticism

Critics raise concerns that intensive enforcement in hot spots can strain police–community relations, lead to over-policing of disadvantaged neighbourhoods, and, if implemented aggressively, harm police legitimacy. Proponents respond that carefully implemented, place-based approaches with problem-solving rather than indiscriminate arrests can reduce both crime and the need for enforcement contacts.
