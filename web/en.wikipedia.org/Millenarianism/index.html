Millenarianism

Millenarianism (from Latin mille, "thousand", and annus, "year") is the belief, held by a religious, social or political movement, that a fundamental transformation of society is imminent, after which "all things will be changed" — usually for the better. Millenarian movements expect the coming order to be collective, earthly, total and miraculous, sweeping away the existing corrupt world and replacing it with a just one.

The concept derives from the Christian doctrine of the Millennium, the thousand-year reign of Christ described in the Book of Revelation, but scholars apply the term comparatively to movements in many cultures. The closely related term millennialism is often used more narrowly for Christian end-time expectation, while millenarianism is the broader anthropological category.

Characteristics

Anthropologist Peter Worsley, studying Melanesian "cargo cults", and historian Norman Cohn, in The Pursuit of the Millennium (1957), identified recurring features of millenarian movements: they arise among the dispossessed or colonised; they gather around a charismatic prophet; they promise the imminent reversal of fortune, sometimes expecting the return of ancestors or the arrival of miraculous goods; and they often prescribe ritual preparation for the new age. Cohn traced such movements through medieval Europe, including the flagellants, the Free Spirit heresy and the revolutionary Anabaptists of Münster.

Examples

Well-known millenarian movements include the Taiping movement in nineteenth-century China, the Ghost Dance among Native Americans in 1890, the Xhosa cattle-killing movement of 1856–57 in South Africa, the Mahdist movement in the Sudan, and various Melanesian cargo cults of the twentieth century. Millenarian expectations have also been detected within secular ideologies, in revolutionary movements promising a radically renewed society.

Study and criticism

Scholars caution that expectations of dramatic collective salvation are widespread and take many forms, and that labelling a movement "millenarian" can flatten local meanings. Nevertheless, the concept remains a standard tool in the comparative study of religion, anthropology and the history of social movements, particularly for explaining how religious hope mobilises collective action under conditions of crisis or oppression.
