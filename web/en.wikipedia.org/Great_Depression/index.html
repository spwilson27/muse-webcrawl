Great Depression

The Great Depression was a severe global economic downturn that began in the United States in 1929 and lasted through the 1930s. It was the longest and deepest depression of the 20th century, devastating both rich and poor countries: world trade fell by more than half, personal income, tax revenue, profits and prices dropped, and unemployment in the United States reached about 25% at its 1933 trough — higher still in some countries. Cities built around heavy industry were hit hardest; construction virtually halted, and farming regions suffered as crop prices fell by 60%.

Causes

The Depression followed the Wall Street Crash of October 1929, but economists stress deeper causes: a structurally weak banking system prone to panics (thousands of U.S. banks failed, 1930–33), a collapsing money supply and debt deflation, tight monetary policy under the gold standard, collapsing demand, and protectionism such as the Smoot–Hawley Tariff of 1930. Milton Friedman and Anna Schwartz famously blamed the Federal Reserve for allowing the money supply to contract by a third; Keynesian economists emphasized the collapse of spending; Barry Eichengreen and others identify gold-standard constraints as the main international transmission mechanism.

Spread and depth

From the U.S., the crisis spread to Europe — the 1931 failure of Austria's Creditanstalt triggered banking crises in Germany — and worldwide, compounded by war-debt and reparations disputes. Countries that left the gold standard early (Britain and Japan in 1931; the U.S. in 1933) recovered sooner; the gold bloc, led by France, stayed depressed longest.

Social and political effects

The Depression drove mass migration (the Dust Bowl "Okies"), shantytowns dubbed "Hoovervilles," and political upheaval. In the United States it brought Franklin D. Roosevelt's landslide in 1932 and the New Deal — relief, banking reform (Glass–Steagall, FDIC), Social Security, and labor rights. In Germany, unemployment near 30% helped propel the Nazi Party to power in 1933, with world-historical consequences.

Recovery

Recovery came gradually with monetary expansion, devaluation and, ultimately, rearmament and World War II mobilization, which finally restored full employment in the early 1940s. Unemployment nonetheless remained elevated through the decade, and 1937–38 saw a sharp "recession within the Depression" after premature fiscal and monetary tightening.

Explanations

Major schools — monetarist, Keynesian, Austrian, and gold-standard accounts — continue to dispute the relative weight of money, spending, expectations, and policy error. The episode profoundly reshaped economic policy, central banking, and the welfare state.
