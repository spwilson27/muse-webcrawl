Blasphemy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Blasphemy is an act of insulting or showing contempt or lack of reverence to a deity, to religious or holy persons or sacred things, or toward something considered sacred or inviolable. The word derives from the Greek blasphēmia (βλασφημία), meaning "slander" or "defamation."

In religions

Judaism and Christianity

In the Hebrew Bible, blaspheming the name of God is punishable by death by stoning (Leviticus 24). In the Gospels, Jesus is accused of blasphemy by the religious authorities. Theologians distinguished blasphemy from heresy and sacrilege; in medieval Europe it was treated as a grave sin and, in some jurisdictions, a capital crime.

Islam

In Islam, blasphemy against God (kufr) and insults to the prophet Muhammad (sabb al-rasul) are considered serious offences; penalties vary widely among schools of law and modern states, from fines and imprisonment to, in a few countries, death.

Other traditions

Hindu, Buddhist, and Sikh traditions contain their own norms of reverence toward deities, scriptures, and sacred figures, though the category of blasphemy as a legal offence is principally associated with the Abrahamic faiths.

Blasphemy laws

As of the early 21st century, roughly a quarter of the world's countries retain laws against blasphemy, though enforcement varies enormously. Many Western countries have repealed such laws: England and Wales abolished the common-law offences in 2008, Denmark in 2017, Ireland by referendum in 2018, and Greece in 2019. Pakistan's blasphemy laws, which prescribe death for insulting the Prophet, are among the most severe and frequently criticised by human-rights organisations. International bodies such as the UN Human Rights Committee have held that blasphemy laws are generally incompatible with freedom of expression except where they prohibit incitement to hatred.
