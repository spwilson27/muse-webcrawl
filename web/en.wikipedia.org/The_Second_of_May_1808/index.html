The Second of May 1808, also known as The Charge of the Mamelukes, is a painting by the Spanish artist Francisco Goya, completed in 1814. Together with its companion piece, The Third of May 1808, it commemorates Spanish resistance to Napoleon's armies during the occupation of Madrid in the Peninsular War. Both works hang in the Museo del Prado in Madrid.

Historical background

On 2 May 1808, fearing that the last members of the Spanish royal family would be removed to France, the people of Madrid rose against the French occupying forces of Marshal Murat. The uprising — the Dos de Mayo — was suppressed with great brutality by Imperial Guard cavalry, including the exotic Mameluke squadron, and followed by mass executions on the night of 2–3 May, the subject of Goya's companion canvas. The uprising ignited the Peninsular War across Spain.

The painting

Goya proposed the pair of paintings to the Spanish government in 1814, after the restoration of Ferdinand VII, offering "to perpetuate by the means of his brush the most notable and heroic actions of our glorious insurrection against the Tyrant of Europe." The Second of May depicts the chaotic street battle in the Puerta del Sol: Mameluke cavalrymen in turbans and curved scimitars clash at close quarters with Madrileños armed with knives, in a compressed, tumbling composition without a single heroic focus — both sides locked in mutual slaughter. One Spaniard drives a dagger into a Mameluke while stabbing his horse, at the center of a crowd scene that art historians note for its violence without glory.

Condition and legacy

The canvas was damaged in transit during the Spanish Civil War, when the Prado's treasures were evacuated, and visible restoration scars remain on its left side. With The Third of May, it stands among the most reproduced images of the Napoleonic Wars and a founding work of modern history painting's anti-heroic tradition.
