Crow

Kingdom: Animalia
Phylum: Chordata
Class: Aves
Order: Passeriformes
Family: Corvidae
Genus: Corvus

A crow is a bird of the genus Corvus, or more broadly a synonym for all of Corvus. The word "crow" is used as part of the common name of many species, such as the carrion crow (Corvus corone) and the American crow (Corvus brachyrhynchos). The larger species in the genus are called ravens, the smaller jackdaws, and intermediate forms rooks, though these distinctions are informal rather than taxonomic.

The carrion crow (Corvus corone).

Description

Crows are medium to large passerine birds with black or black-and-grey plumage, stout bills, and strong feet. Species range from the small jackdaws (about 34–39 cm long) to the common raven, one of the largest passerines. Crows are found nearly worldwide except for the southern tip of South America, Antarctica, and many oceanic islands.

Behaviour and intelligence

Crows and their relatives are renowned for intelligence: they solve multi-step problems, manufacture and use tools (famously the New Caledonian crow), recognise individual human faces, and display social learning and elaborate play. They are omnivorous and highly adaptable, feeding on invertebrates, carrion, eggs, grain, and human refuse. Many species are social, forming family groups and large communal roosts.

In culture

Crows appear prominently in folklore and mythology worldwide — as tricksters, omens, and messengers of gods (Odin's ravens Huginn and Muninn) — and have carried associations with both wisdom and death. A flock of crows is called a "murder".
