Williams Act

The Williams Act (Pub. L. 90-439, 82 Stat. 454, enacted July 29, 1968) is a United States federal law, named for its sponsor, Senator Harrison A. Williams of New Jersey, that amended the Securities Exchange Act of 1934 to regulate tender offers and large accumulations of corporate stock.

Purpose

In the 1960s, "Saturday night specials" — sudden cash tender offers made directly to shareholders — left investors pressured to decide quickly and in the dark. Congress sought to protect shareholders while remaining neutral between bidders and incumbent management.

Provisions

The Act added sections 13(d), 13(e), 14(d), 14(e) and 14(f) to the Exchange Act. Anyone acquiring more than 5% of a class of a public company's shares must file a Schedule 13D with the SEC disclosing their identity, source of funds and intentions. Tender offers must remain open for a minimum period, follow rules on withdrawal and proration, and bidders must disclose extensive information on Schedule TO. Section 14(e) prohibits fraud and misrepresentation in connection with tender offers.

Legacy

The Williams Act remains the core federal framework governing corporate takeovers in the United States, balancing investor protection against the market for corporate control.
