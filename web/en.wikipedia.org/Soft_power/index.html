Soft power

Soft power is the ability of a country or actor to influence others through attraction and persuasion rather than coercion (hard power: military force) or payment. The concept was coined by political scientist Joseph Nye of Harvard University, introduced in his 1990 book Bound to Lead and developed in Soft Power: The Means to Success in World Politics (2004). Nye summarized it as "the ability to get what you want through attraction rather than coercion or payments."

Sources

According to Nye, a country's soft power rests on three resources: its culture (in places where it is attractive to others), its political values (when it lives up to them at home and abroad), and its foreign policies (when they are seen as legitimate and having moral authority). Instruments include public diplomacy, international broadcasting, educational exchanges such as the Fulbright Program, popular culture exports (from Hollywood to the Korean Wave and "Cool Japan"), development assistance, and the perceived competence and benevolence of a country's institutions. Nye later proposed smart power — the effective combination of hard and soft power — and indices such as the Soft Power 30 have attempted to rank countries' intangible influence.

Criticism

Critics argue that soft power is difficult to measure, slow-acting, dependent on audiences' reception, and cannot substitute for hard power in existential conflicts; realists doubt attraction moves outcomes independently of material interests. Others note that governments do not fully control their cultural assets, that attraction can backfire, and that concepts such as China's "sharp power" — manipulation and censorship abroad — fall outside Nye's framework. Nonetheless the term has entered the standard vocabulary of international relations and foreign-policy planning worldwide.
