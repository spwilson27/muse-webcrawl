Ren (philosophy)

Ren 仁
TraditionConfucianism
TranslationsBenevolence, humaneness, humanity, goodness
Key textAnalects of Confucius
Related virtuesLi (禮), Yi (義), Xiao (孝)

Ren (Chinese: 仁; pinyin: rén) is a central virtue in Confucian philosophy, variously translated as "benevolence", "humaneness", "humanity" or "goodness". It denotes the humane quality of compassion and altruism that constitutes a person as fully human, and it stands at the heart of the teaching of Confucius (551–479 BCE), in whose Analects the character appears over a hundred times — more than any other concept.

Meaning

The Chinese character 仁 combines the radical for "person" (人) with the character for "two" (二), suggesting that ren is realised in relations between people: one becomes humane through how one treats others. Confucius never gave a single abstract definition, instead describing ren differently for different disciples. When Fan Chi asked about it, he answered "love others"; when Yan Hui asked, he replied that ren is "overcoming oneself and returning to ritual propriety" (li). The Golden Rule in its negative form — "do not impose on others what you yourself do not desire" — was offered as a practical method for practising ren throughout life.

Ren and li

Ren is closely paired with li (禮), ritual propriety. Ren is the inner substance of virtue; li is its outward form in conduct, ceremony and social roles. Confucius held that without ren, ritual becomes empty formalism, while ren without li lacks expression and discipline. Together with yi (righteousness), zhi (wisdom) and xin (trustworthiness), ren forms the core set of Confucian virtues cultivated by the junzi, or exemplary person.

Later interpretations

Mencius taught that ren is innate: the feeling of compassion — illustrated by the spontaneous alarm anyone feels seeing a child about to fall into a well — is the "sprout" of benevolence present in every heart. Later Neo-Confucians such as Zhu Xi defined ren as "the character of the mind and the principle of love", giving it a metaphysical scope encompassing all things. In modern scholarship, ren is often compared with Western notions of humaneness and virtue ethics, and some translators render it as "authoritative conduct" or "human-heartedness".
