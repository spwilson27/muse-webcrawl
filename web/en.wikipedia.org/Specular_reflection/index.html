Specular reflection

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Specular reflection, or regular reflection, is the mirror-like reflection of waves, such as light, from a surface. In this process, each incident ray is reflected at the same angle to the surface normal as the incident ray, on the opposite side of the surface normal in the plane formed by the incident ray and the normal. This is in contrast to diffuse reflection, where the radiation is scattered in many directions.

Law of reflection

The law of reflection states that the angle of reflection equals the angle of incidence, and that the incident direction, the surface normal, and the reflected direction are coplanar. When light encounters a boundary between two media, part of the wave is reflected and part is refracted; the proportions are given by the Fresnel equations. Specular reflection from metals and dielectrics underlies the operation of mirrors, which are typically glass coated with a reflective metal layer.

Specular versus diffuse reflection

Most real surfaces produce a mixture of specular and diffuse reflection. Smooth, polished surfaces — calm water, glass, polished metal — reflect specularly and form images, whereas rough surfaces scatter light. The degree of specularity depends on the surface roughness relative to the wavelength of the radiation. Specular highlights are a key visual cue for glossiness and are modeled in computer graphics by shading models such as the Phong reflection model and microfacet BRDFs.

Other waves

Specular reflection applies not only to visible light but to other electromagnetic waves and to acoustic and seismic waves. Radar returns, mirror-like reflections of radio waves from the ionosphere, and reflections used in ultrasound imaging all involve regular reflection from smooth interfaces.
