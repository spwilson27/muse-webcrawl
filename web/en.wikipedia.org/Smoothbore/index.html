Smoothbore

Interior of a smoothbore barrel, without rifling grooves

A smoothbore weapon is one that has a barrel without rifling — that is, the bore is not given helical grooves to spin the projectile. Smoothbore weapons range from early handheld firearms to modern shotguns, mortars and most tank guns. The opposite of a smoothbore is a rifled barrel, whose spiral grooves impart stabilising spin and generally greater accuracy with single projectiles.

History

All early firearms, from hand cannons through matchlock, flintlock and percussion muskets, were smoothbore; with a loose-fitting spherical ball, accuracy was poor beyond short ranges, which encouraged volley fire by massed infantry. Rifling, known from the 16th century, became practical for mass armies only in the mid-19th century with expanding bullets such as the Minié ball. Shotguns remained smoothbore because they fire multiple pellets, and some can also fire rifled slugs or use rifled choke tubes.

Modern use

Most modern battle tanks, such as those armed with the Rheinmetall 120 mm gun, use smoothbore cannon. They fire fin-stabilised armour-piercing projectiles (APFSDS), which are long rods that cannot be spin-stabilised effectively and actually perform better without spin; fins provide the stability instead. Smoothbore barrels are also cheaper to manufacture, wear less, and tolerate higher chamber pressures. Mortars are typically smoothbore and muzzle-loaded, firing fin-stabilised bombs.
