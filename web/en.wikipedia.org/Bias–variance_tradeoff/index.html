Bias–variance tradeoff

In statistics and machine learning, the bias–variance tradeoff describes the relationship between a model's complexity, the accuracy of its predictions, and how well it generalises to data it has not seen. The expected prediction error of a model can be decomposed into three parts: the square of the bias (error from wrong assumptions in the learning algorithm), the variance (error from sensitivity to fluctuations in the training set), and irreducible noise.

The decomposition

For a target value y and a prediction ŷ, the expected squared error satisfies E[(y − ŷ)²] = Bias(ŷ)² + Var(ŷ) + σ², where σ² is the variance of the noise in the observations. A model with high bias pays attention to the training data too little and systematically misses relevant patterns — underfitting. A model with high variance pays attention to the training data too much and models its random noise as well — overfitting.

The tradeoff

Classically, as model complexity increases, bias falls but variance rises, so total error is minimised at an intermediate complexity. Simple models such as linear regression on few features tend to have high bias and low variance; flexible models such as deep decision trees or k-nearest neighbours with small k tend to have low bias and high variance. Techniques such as regularisation, cross-validation, bagging and random forests are used to manage the tradeoff.

Double descent

Modern deep learning has complicated the classical picture: very large, heavily overparameterised neural networks can generalise well despite fitting their training data exactly. Empirically, test error may fall, rise near the interpolation threshold, and then fall again as model size grows — a phenomenon known as double descent.
