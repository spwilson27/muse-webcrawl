Thermae

In ancient Rome, thermae (from Greek θερμός, thermos, "hot") and balneae were facilities for bathing. Thermae usually refers to the large imperial bath complexes, while balneae were smaller-scale facilities, public or private, found in towns and villas. Bathing was a central social institution of Roman life: for a small fee, or free under imperial patronage, citizens washed, exercised, read, did business, and socialized in complexes that ranked among the largest buildings of the Roman world.

Layout and bathing routine

A typical great bath complex was arranged symmetrically around a sequence of heated rooms served by the hypocaust system, in which hot air from furnaces circulated beneath raised floors and through hollow wall tiles. The bather undressed in the apodyterium, exercised in the open palaestra, and progressed through the warm tepidarium to the hot, steamy caldarium, often with a dry sweating room (laconicum), before plunging into the cold frigidarium and, in the greatest complexes, an open-air swimming pool, the natatio. Libraries, lecture halls, gardens, and shops completed the precinct. Water arrived by aqueduct in enormous quantities, and drainage and furnace gangs of slaves kept the system running.

Great imperial thermae

Marcus Agrippa built Rome's first great thermae in 25 BC. Successors followed: the Baths of Nero, Titus, Trajan (on the Oppian Hill, designed by Apollodorus of Damascus), Caracalla (AD 212–216, accommodating some 1,600 bathers at once), and Diocletian (AD 298–306, the largest, for some 3,000 bathers). Their ruins — above all the Baths of Caracalla — still dominate parts of Rome, and the frigidarium of Diocletian's baths survives as the church of Santa Maria degli Angeli.

Social role and decline

The baths were democratic spaces where emperors advertised generosity and all classes mixed, spending the afternoon (hora balnearum was traditionally the eighth hour) in exercise, bathing, massage with oils, and conversation. With the cutting of Rome's aqueducts during the Gothic wars of the sixth century and the shrinking of the city, the great thermae fell out of use, though public bathing descended from them continued in the Byzantine and Islamic worlds as the hammam.
