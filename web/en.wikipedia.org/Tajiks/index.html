The Tajiks are an Iranian ethnic group native to Central Asia, principally inhabiting Tajikistan, northeastern Afghanistan, and parts of Uzbekistan, including the historic cities of Samarkand and Bukhara. They speak Tajik, a variety of the Persian language.

History

The Tajiks descend from ancient Eastern Iranian peoples of Central Asia, including the Sogdians and Bactrians. With the Arab conquests of the seventh and eighth centuries, the region was Islamised and gradually Persianised in language. Under the Samanid Empire (819–999), centred on Bukhara, a Persianate high culture flourished that Tajiks regard as the foundation of their national identity. Over subsequent centuries, Turkic peoples became politically dominant in the plains, while Persian-speaking Tajiks remained concentrated in cities and mountain valleys.

Distribution

In Tajikistan, Tajiks form roughly 85 percent of the population. In Afghanistan, they are the second-largest ethnic group, estimated at about a quarter of the population, concentrated in the northeast and the cities of Kabul and Herat — though there they are often counted as Persian-speaking "Farsiwan" and the boundary with other Persian speakers is fluid. Significant communities also live in Uzbekistan, Russia, and a growing diaspora worldwide.

Language and culture

Tajik is written in the Cyrillic script in Tajikistan, a legacy of Soviet rule; Afghan Tajiks use the Perso-Arabic script. The shared literary canon includes Ferdowsi's Shahnameh and the poetry of Rudaki, Rumi, and Omar Khayyam. Traditional culture features distinctive embroidered skullcaps (tubeteika), the chakan embroidery of the south, and the Pamiri peoples of Gorno-Badakhshan, who speak distinct Eastern Iranian languages and follow Ismaili Islam under the Aga Khan.
