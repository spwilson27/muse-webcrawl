Compton Gamma Ray Observatory

The Compton Gamma Ray Observatory (CGRO) was a space observatory designed to detect photons with energies from 20 keV to 30 GeV, in Earth orbit from 1991 to 2000. The observatory was part of NASA's Great Observatories series, along with the Hubble Space Telescope, the Chandra X-ray Observatory, and the Spitzer Space Telescope. It was named after Arthur Compton, who won the Nobel Prize in Physics for work on gamma-ray detection.

Instruments

CGRO carried four instruments covering six decades of the electromagnetic spectrum: the Burst and Transient Source Experiment (BATSE), which detected thousands of gamma-ray bursts and showed they were distributed isotropically across the sky, implying cosmological origin; the Oriented Scintillation Spectrometer Experiment (OSSE); the Imaging Compton Telescope (COMPTEL); and the Energetic Gamma Ray Experiment Telescope (EGRET), which produced the first all-sky survey above 100 MeV.

Discoveries

The observatory mapped the distribution of radioactive aluminium-26 in the Milky Way, discovered gamma-ray emitting pulsars and blazars, observed terrestrial gamma-ray flashes from thunderstorms, and detected anti-matter annihilation radiation from the galactic centre region.

End of mission

After the failure of one of its gyroscopes in December 1999, NASA decided to deorbit the satellite to avoid an uncontrolled re-entry. On 4 June 2000 CGRO made a controlled re-entry over the Pacific Ocean.
