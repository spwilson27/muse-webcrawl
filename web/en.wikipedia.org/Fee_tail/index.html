Fee tail

Entails kept landed estates intact within a family across generations.

The fee tail, or estate tail (from the Latin feodum talliatum, a "cut" or limited fee), was a form of land ownership in English common law under which land was held by a tenant and inherited only by specified descendants — usually the direct ("lineal") heirs of the original grantee — and could not freely be sold or devised by will. Created by a grant "to A and the heirs of his body", the fee tail was the legal engine of the entail, by which great families kept their estates intact for centuries.

History

The fee tail was regularised by the statute De Donis Conditionalibus (1285), part of the Statute of Westminster II, which enforced the donor's intention that land descend in the line specified, reverting to the donor's family if the line failed. Landowners soon sought ways to defeat entails so that land could be sold or settled afresh each generation; from the fifteenth century the courts permitted "barring the entail" through fictitious lawsuits — the common recovery, sanctioned in Taltarum's Case (1472) — and later through fines. The result was the strict settlement, under which each generation held only a life interest with the entail resettled on the next.

Varieties

A fee tail general descended to the grantee's lineal descendants of either sex, while a fee tail male or female restricted inheritance to one sex — the device behind the fictional predicament of the Bennet family in Jane Austen's Pride and Prejudice, whose estate is entailed away from five daughters to a male cousin. A fee tail special limited inheritance to the issue of a particular marriage.

Modern status

The fee tail has been abolished or rendered obsolete in most common-law jurisdictions. England and Wales prevented the creation of new entails by the Law of Property Act 1925 (converting them into equitable interests) and barred them entirely for deaths after 1996. In the United States most states abolished the fee tail in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries — Thomas Jefferson promoted abolition in Virginia as a blow against aristocracy — typically converting it into a fee simple or a life estate followed by a remainder in fee simple; a handful of states retain limited forms.
