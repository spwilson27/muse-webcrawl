Plagiarism

Plagiarism is the representation of another person's language, thoughts, ideas, or expressions as one's own original work. Although precise definitions vary, in many countries and cultures plagiarism is considered a violation of academic integrity and journalistic ethics, as well as social norms around learning, teaching, research, fairness, and respect. Plagiarism is typically not in itself a crime but, like copyright infringement, can be punished in court for violations related to copyright or moral rights; in academia and industry it is a serious ethical offense, sometimes resulting in expulsion, dismissal, or damage to reputation.


Copying another's work without attribution is the classic form of plagiarism.

Etymology and history

The word derives from the Latin plagiarius, "kidnapper", via plagium, "kidnapping". The Roman poet Martial (1st century AD) complained that another poet had "kidnapped" his verses, using the metaphor of stolen slaves. The term entered English around 1601, introduced by the dramatist Ben Jonson to describe literary theft. Concepts of individual authorship and intellectual property strengthened with printing, copyright law (beginning with the Statute of Anne in 1710), and the professionalization of scholarship, which made unattributed copying a distinct offence against norms rather than merely a compliment or a common practice, as imitation had largely been in earlier literary cultures.

Forms

Plagiarism includes verbatim copying without quotation or citation; close paraphrase that follows a source's structure and wording without attribution ("patchwriting"); submitting another's entire work as one's own — including work bought from essay mills or copied from other students; plagiarism of ideas, data, images, music, computer code, and translations; and self-plagiarism, the reuse of one's own previously published work without disclosure, which journals and universities may also sanction. Plagiarism should be distinguished from copyright infringement: copying public-domain text without attribution may be plagiarism but not infringement, while reproducing copyrighted text with attribution may infringe without being plagiarism.

In academia

Universities and schools typically define plagiarism in honour codes and academic-integrity policies, with penalties ranging from a failing grade on an assignment to expulsion. Detection relies on instructor recognition, source comparison, and text-matching software such as Turnitin and iThenticate, which compare submissions against large databases; such tools flag textual similarity, which is not by itself proof of plagiarism. Controversies recur over contract cheating, the use of paraphrasing tools, and, since the early 2020s, undisclosed use of generative artificial intelligence to produce coursework and publications.

In journalism, arts, and science

In journalism, plagiarism violates professional codes and has ended careers — prominent cases have involved reporters fabricating or lifting material at major newspapers. In the arts, accusations of plagiarism in music, literature, and film overlap with but differ from copyright litigation over substantial similarity. In science, plagiarism is classed as research misconduct alongside fabrication and falsification; documented cases have led to retractions and loss of funding. "Plagiarism scandals" involving politicians' dissertations have led to resignations in several European countries, notably Germany in 2011.

Prevention and norms

Attribution practices — quotation, citation styles such as APA and MLA, footnotes, and reference lists — allow writers to use others' work legitimately. Educators increasingly teach source use explicitly, set assessments designed to resist copying, and distinguish deliberate fraud from poor scholarly technique, particularly for students writing in a second language. Concepts of plagiarism vary across cultures and eras, and scholars debate how much the modern Western emphasis on individual originality should govern collaborative and communal knowledge practices.
