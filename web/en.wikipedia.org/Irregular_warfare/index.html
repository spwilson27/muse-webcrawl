Irregular warfare

Related terms: Guerrilla warfare, insurgency, unconventional warfare, asymmetric warfare

Irregular warfare (IW) is defined in United States joint doctrine as "a violent struggle among state and non-state actors for legitimacy and influence over the relevant populations". It contrasts with conventional (regular) warfare, in which states fight primarily to defeat an adversary's armed forces. Irregular warfare favours indirect and asymmetric approaches, though it may employ the full range of military and other capabilities.

Activities and concepts

US doctrine groups five activities under irregular warfare: counterterrorism, unconventional warfare, foreign internal defense, counterinsurgency, and stability operations. Irregular forces — insurgents, guerrillas, partisans, militias and terrorists — typically avoid decisive battle, blending with the civilian population and using ambush, sabotage, assassination and propaganda. The term "guerrilla" derives from the Peninsular War (1808–1814), when Spanish irregulars fought Napoleonic France; theorists such as Mao Zedong later systematised protracted guerrilla war.

History and modern usage

Irregular warfare is as old as war itself — from Scythian tactics against Persia to Lawrence of Arabia's Arab Revolt campaign. In the 21st century, conflicts in Iraq and Afghanistan pushed Western militaries to re-emphasise counterinsurgency and irregular warfare skills that had atrophied during the Cold War focus on conventional deterrence. "Hybrid warfare" and "grey-zone" conflict are related modern concepts describing campaigns that blend regular, irregular, and non-military means.
