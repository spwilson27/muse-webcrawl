Potassium-argon dating

Method: Radiometric dating

Isotopes: ⁴⁰K → ⁴⁰Ar

Half-life of ⁴⁰K: ~1.25 billion years

Range: ~100,000 years to billions of years

Potassium-argon dating, abbreviated K-Ar dating, is a radiometric dating method used in geochronology and archaeology. It is based on the radioactive decay of an isotope of potassium, ⁴⁰K, into the inert gas argon, ⁴⁰Ar. Potassium-40 decays with a half-life of about 1.25 billion years, roughly 89% of it to calcium-40 and about 11% to argon-40.

In volcanic rock, molten material contains no argon, because the gas escapes. When the rock solidifies, the "clock" starts: from then on, argon produced by decay is trapped in the crystal lattice and accumulates in proportion to the rock's age. Measuring the ratio of ⁴⁰Ar to remaining ⁴⁰K therefore gives the time since the rock cooled.

Applications

K-Ar dating is especially valuable for volcanic rocks and ash layers, and it famously dated the volcanic strata at Olduvai Gorge and other East African sites, establishing the great antiquity of early hominin fossils. It is useful from about 100,000 years ago to the age of the Earth itself.

Argon-argon dating

The related argon-argon (⁴⁰Ar/³⁹Ar) method converts some potassium in the sample to ³⁹Ar by neutron irradiation, allowing both isotopes to be measured in a single mass-spectrometer run. It offers greater precision and can reveal whether a sample has been disturbed or partially reset by heating.
