Compressed sensing

A sparse signal can be recovered from far fewer samples than Nyquist requires

Compressed sensing (also known as compressive sampling or sparse sampling) is a signal processing technique for efficiently acquiring and reconstructing a signal by finding solutions to underdetermined linear systems. It exploits the fact that many natural signals are sparse or compressible in some basis, so they can be recovered from far fewer measurements than suggested by the Shannon–Nyquist sampling theorem.

Theory

The field emerged in the mid-2000s from work by Emmanuel Candès, Justin Romberg, Terence Tao, and David Donoho. Recovery is possible when the measurement matrix satisfies conditions such as the restricted isometry property and is incoherent with the sparsity basis. Reconstruction is commonly performed by ℓ1-norm minimization (basis pursuit), which promotes sparsity, or by greedy algorithms such as orthogonal matching pursuit.

Applications

Compressed sensing is used in magnetic resonance imaging to shorten scan times, in single-pixel cameras, in radio astronomy, radar, seismology, and in analog-to-information conversion. In MRI, undersampling k-space and reconstructing with sparsity priors has enabled faster clinical scans, especially in pediatric imaging where reducing scan time reduces the need for sedation.
