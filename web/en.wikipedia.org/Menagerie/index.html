Menagerie

A menagerie is a collection of captive animals, frequently exotic, kept for display. It is a historical predecessor of the modern zoological garden, in which animals were typically kept in cages or small enclosures primarily to demonstrate the wealth, power and worldliness of their owners — often kings and aristocrats — rather than for public education or conservation.

History

Collections of exotic animals date to antiquity: Egyptian, Chinese and Aztec rulers all maintained animal collections, and the Tower of London menagerie, founded around 1204, kept lions, elephants and polar bears for over 600 years. The menagerie at Versailles, built for Louis XIV in the 1660s, became a model for European courts, with animals displayed in enclosures radiating from a central viewing pavilion. The Imperial Menagerie at Schönbrunn in Vienna, founded in 1752, survives as the world's oldest continuously operating zoo.

Travelling menageries

In the 18th and 19th centuries, travelling menageries toured Britain, continental Europe and North America, exhibiting animals from carts and temporary enclosures at fairs and market towns. In America these shows were absorbed into the travelling circus; showmen such as Isaac Van Amburgh built careers on performing with big cats.

Transition to zoos

From the early 19th century, collections increasingly opened to a paying public and were justified by scientific study — the Zoological Society of London's gardens (1828) being the landmark case. Over the following century the display-first menagerie gave way to the modern zoo, with larger, more naturalistic habitats and aims of education, research and, later, conservation.
