Henry's law

Gas dissolving in a liquid under pressure

In physical chemistry, Henry's law is a gas law stating that the amount of dissolved gas in a liquid is directly proportional to its partial pressure above the liquid. The proportionality factor is called the Henry's law constant. The law was formulated by the English chemist William Henry, who studied the topic in the early 19th century.

Formulation

A common form of the law is

p = kH · c

where p is the partial pressure of the gas above the solution, c is its concentration in the liquid, and kH is the Henry's law constant, which depends on the gas, the solvent, and the temperature. Alternative conventions define the constant as the reciprocal ratio (solubility form), so values must always be read with their units and definition.

Applications

Henry's law explains the carbonation of soft drinks, in which carbon dioxide is dissolved under pressure and escapes when the container is opened. In scuba diving it underlies decompression sickness: nitrogen dissolved in blood and tissues under pressure can form bubbles if pressure falls too quickly. Environmental scientists use it to model the exchange of gases such as oxygen and volatile pollutants between air and water. The law holds best for dilute solutions and gases that do not react chemically with the solvent; it is complemented by Raoult's law for the solvent component.
