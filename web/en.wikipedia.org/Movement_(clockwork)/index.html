Movement (clockwork)

Movement
TypeHorological mechanism
Power sourcesMainspring, weights, battery (quartz)
Also calledCalibre

In horology, a movement, also known as a caliber or calibre, is the mechanism of a watch or timepiece, as opposed to the case, which encloses and protects the movement, and the face, which displays the time. The term originated with mechanical timepieces, whose clockwork movements are made of many moving parts, but is also used for quartz movements, which have fewer moving parts.

A mechanical movement consists of an energy source (a mainspring barrel or, in clocks, descending weights), a gear train, an escapement that meters the release of energy, and a regulator — usually a balance wheel with hairspring in watches, or a pendulum in clocks, which oscillates at a fixed frequency and divides time into equal beats. Traditional wristwatch movements beat at frequencies such as 18,000 to 36,000 vibrations per hour (2.5–5 Hz).

Types

Manual movements must be wound by hand via the crown. Automatic (self-winding) movements harvest energy from the wearer's wrist motion through an oscillating weight (rotor). Quartz movements, introduced commercially in 1969 with the Seiko Astron, use a battery-driven quartz crystal vibrating at 32,768 Hz and a stepper motor; they are far more accurate and cheaper than mechanical movements, though generally less prized by collectors.

Complications

Features beyond hours, minutes and seconds are called complications: dates, chronographs, moon phases, alarms, tourbillons and minute repeaters. A "manufacture" movement is designed and made in-house by a watch brand rather than supplied by a third-party maker such as ETA, Sellita or Miyota.
