Transvestism

Cross-dressing has a long recorded history in theatre, ritual and everyday life.

Transvestism is the practice of dressing in a manner traditionally associated with a different gender, often termed cross-dressing. The word transvestite was coined in 1910 by the German sexologist Magnus Hirschfeld, who used it for people who habitually wore clothes of the opposite sex. The term later acquired clinical associations; in modern usage "cross-dresser" is generally preferred for the practice, which is distinct from being transgender.

History

Cross-dressing appears throughout recorded history: in classical mythology, in religious festivals, and on stage — female roles in ancient Greek theatre and in Shakespeare's England were played by men and boys, while in Japanese kabuki onnagata specialise in female roles. Some individuals in history, such as the Chevalier d'Éon in the 18th century, lived for long periods in another gender's dress. In the 19th and early 20th centuries many cities criminalised appearing in public in "the apparel of the opposite sex", laws that were enforced against a wide range of people.

Clinical history

Hirschfeld distinguished transvestism from homosexuality and from what he called transsexualism. Later psychiatry classified some forms under fetishism; the DSM-5 (2013) lists "transvestic disorder" only where cross-dressing causes significant distress or impairment, and the ICD-11 removed transvestic fetishism as a disorder. Most cross-dressing is today regarded as a form of gender expression rather than pathology.

Culture

Cross-dressing features in folk traditions such as mumming and pantomime dames, in comedy, and in subcultures such as drag performance, though drag is a theatrical art form and is not synonymous with everyday cross-dressing. Clothing norms themselves have shifted: trousers on women, once controversial, became unremarkable in most Western countries during the 20th century.
