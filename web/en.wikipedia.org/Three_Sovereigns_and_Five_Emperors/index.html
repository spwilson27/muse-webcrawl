The Three Sovereigns and Five Emperors (Chinese: 三皇五帝; pinyin: Sānhuáng Wǔdì) were a group of mythological rulers of ancient China who were credited, in traditional historiography, with founding Chinese civilisation. They were said to be morally perfect sage-kings whose inventions and teachings — agriculture, medicine, writing, the calendar, marriage and the family system — laid the foundations of Chinese culture. Their era precedes the Xia dynasty, the first dynasty of traditional Chinese history.

The Three Sovereigns

Lists vary between sources, but a common set gives Fuxi, the inventor of writing, hunting, fishing and the trigrams of the I Ching; Nüwa, his sister or wife, who created humanity and repaired the pillars of heaven; and Shennong, the "Divine Farmer", who taught agriculture and herbal medicine and tasted hundreds of herbs to test their properties. Other lists substitute figures such as Suiren, inventor of fire, or the Yellow Emperor among the Sovereigns.

The Five Emperors

The most common list of the Five Emperors is that of Sima Qian's Records of the Grand Historian (c. 91 BC): the Yellow Emperor (Huangdi), Zhuanxu, Emperor Ku, Emperor Yao and Emperor Shun. Huangdi is revered as the ancestor of the Han Chinese and credited with inventions from boats and carts to the compass. Yao and Shun became Confucian exemplars of virtue because each passed over his own son to choose the worthiest successor — Yao choosing Shun, and Shun in turn Yu the Great, tamer of the flood and founder of the Xia dynasty.

Historicity

Modern scholars regard the Sovereigns and Emperors as cultural heroes of legend rather than documented historical persons, though their stories may preserve memories of Neolithic tribal leaders and culture-bringers. Excavated texts, such as the Guodian Chu slips, show that alternative lists and genealogies circulated in the Warring States period before the Han-dynasty synthesis fixed the canonical sequence.
