Volcanism of Iceland

Iceland is one of the most volcanically active places on Earth, with eruptions on average every four to five years. The island sits astride the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, where the North American and Eurasian plates are pulling apart, and above a mantle plume or "hotspot" that supplies extra magma. The country has around 30 active volcanic systems.

Notable eruptions

The 1783–84 eruption of the Laki fissure was one of the largest lava eruptions in recorded history; its gas emissions caused famine in Iceland and climatic effects across the Northern Hemisphere. Other major events include the 1918 eruption of Katla, the 1973 eruption of Eldfell on Heimaey, which prompted an evacuation and attempts to cool advancing lava with seawater, the 2010 eruption of Eyjafjallajökull, whose ash cloud disrupted European aviation, the 2014–15 eruption of Bárðarbunga at Holuhraun, and the series of eruptions on the Reykjanes Peninsula beginning in 2021 after some 800 years of quiet.

Monitoring

Volcanic activity is monitored by the Icelandic Meteorological Office; geothermal energy from the volcanic systems supplies much of Iceland's heating and part of its electricity.
