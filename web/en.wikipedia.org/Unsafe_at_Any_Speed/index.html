Unsafe at Any Speed: The Designed-In Dangers of the American Automobile is a non-fiction book by consumer advocate Ralph Nader, first published in 1965. It is a pioneering work of investigative reporting and consumer advocacy that charged the American automobile industry with resisting the introduction of safety features and prioritising style and cost over occupant protection. The book became a best seller and is widely credited with prompting the passage of federal automobile safety legislation in the United States.

A first-generation Chevrolet Corvair, the car criticised in the book's first chapter.

Content

The opening chapter, "The Sporty Corvair — The One-Car Accident," attacks the Chevrolet Corvair's swing-axle rear suspension, which Nader argued made the car prone to sudden oversteer, "tuck-under" and rollover, and alleged that General Motors knew of the handling problem and declined to fit an inexpensive stabilising device. Later chapters criticise the industry's emphasis on styling, the dangers of dashboard design and glare, automatic transmission shift patterns that could confuse drivers, tire and rim failures, exhaust pollution, and the failure of state inspection regimes. Nader argued that the technology to build far safer cars already existed and was withheld for economic reasons.

Reception and the GM investigation of Nader

General Motors responded by attempting to discredit Nader: the company hired private detectives to follow him and investigate his private life, hoping to find compromising material. The surveillance came to light during congressional hearings, and GM president James Roche publicly apologised to Nader before a Senate subcommittee in March 1966. Nader sued GM for invasion of privacy and settled in 1970 for $425,000, using the proceeds to fund consumer advocacy organisations. The episode greatly amplified the book's publicity.

Legacy

Congressional hearings led by Senator Abraham Ribicoff culminated in the National Traffic and Motor Vehicle Safety Act and the Highway Safety Act of 1966, which established federal safety standards and the agency that became the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration. The Corvair was later substantially exonerated as to its handling by a 1971 federal study, which found its handling comparable to contemporaries, but the book's broader critique — and its demonstration that a lone advocate could challenge a major industry — made it a landmark of twentieth-century reform literature. Time magazine and the New York Times later listed it among the most influential non-fiction books of the century.
