The Interstate Commerce Commission (ICC) was a regulatory agency in the United States created by the Interstate Commerce Act of 1887. It was the first federal regulatory agency in the United States, originally established to regulate railroads (and later telephone companies and trucking) in the public interest, ensuring fair rates and eliminating rate discrimination.

History

The ICC emerged from decades of agitation by farmers and merchants — notably the Granger movement — against discriminatory railroad freight rates. The Hepburn Act of 1906 gave it real power to set maximum rates, and the Mann–Elkins Act of 1910 extended its authority. Its jurisdiction expanded to interstate trucking and buses under the Motor Carrier Act of 1935, and it also acquired roles in railroad consolidation, securities issuance and safety inspection.

Deregulation and abolition

The deregulation movement of the late 1970s sharply curtailed the ICC's role: the Railroad Revitalization and Regulatory Reform Act of 1976, the Motor Carrier Act of 1980 and the Staggers Rail Act of 1980 freed rates and entry in much of surface transport. The ICC Termination Act of 1995 abolished the commission effective January 1, 1996, transferring its remaining functions, chiefly over railroads, to the new Surface Transportation Board.
