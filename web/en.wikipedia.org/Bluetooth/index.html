Bluetooth

Bluetooth is a short-range wireless technology standard for exchanging data between fixed and mobile devices over short distances and building personal area networks (PANs). In the most widely used mode, transmission power is limited to 2.5 milliwatts, giving it a very short range of up to 10 metres (33 feet), though longer ranges are possible in other classes. It employs UHF radio waves in the ISM bands, from 2.402 GHz to 2.48 GHz. It is mainly used as an alternative to wired connections to exchange files between nearby portable devices and connect cell phones and music players with wireless headphones, and it is the dominant technology in the Internet of Things low-power sensor market via Bluetooth Low Energy.

The Bluetooth logo combines the runes for H and B, the initials of King Harald Bluetooth.

History and name

Bluetooth was developed beginning in 1994 at Ericsson in Lund, Sweden, where Jaap Haartsen and Sven Mattisson created the underlying short-link radio technology. The name was proposed in 1997 by Intel engineer Jim Kardach, who had been reading a novel about the Viking king Harald Bluetooth of Denmark and Norway; just as Harald united the tribes of Denmark, Bluetooth would unite communication protocols. The Bluetooth Special Interest Group, founded in 1998 by Ericsson, IBM, Intel, Nokia, and Toshiba, now has tens of thousands of member companies. The first consumer Bluetooth device, a hands-free mobile headset, shipped in 2000. The logo is a bind-rune merging the Younger Futhark runes Hagall (ᚼ) and Bjarkan (ᛒ), Harald's initials.

Technology

Bluetooth operates using frequency-hopping spread spectrum, dividing the band into 79 channels (in classic Bluetooth) and switching among them up to 1,600 times per second to reduce interference. Classic Bluetooth provides data rates from 1 Mbit/s (version 1.2) up to 3 Mbit/s with Enhanced Data Rate in version 2.0+EDR. Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE), introduced with version 4.0 in 2010, uses 40 channels with 2 MHz spacing, much lower energy consumption, and became the basis for beacons, wearables, and most IoT sensors. Later versions added higher throughput (Bluetooth 5 doubled BLE speed and quadrupled range), direction finding (5.1), LE Audio and the LC3 codec (5.2), and Auracast broadcast audio.

Devices communicate in a leader/follower (formerly master/slave) piconet structure, and must be paired before exchanging data, with modern pairing using elliptic-curve Diffie–Hellman key exchange for security.

Uses

Common applications include wireless headphones, speakers and earbuds; hands-free car systems; computer peripherals such as mice and keyboards; game controllers; fitness trackers and medical sensors; smart home devices; and indoor positioning beacons. Several billions of Bluetooth devices ship each year, making it one of the most successful wireless standards in history.

Health and security

Bluetooth uses very low transmission power, with non-ionising radiation far below mobile phone levels. Security vulnerabilities have periodically been identified, including Bluejacking (unsolicited messages), Bluesnarfing (data theft), and the BlueBorne attack family disclosed in 2017; keeping firmware updated and devices non-discoverable mitigates most risks.
