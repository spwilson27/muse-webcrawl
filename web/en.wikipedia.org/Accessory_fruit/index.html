Accessory fruit

Also calledFalse fruit, pseudocarp
Derived fromOvary plus other floral tissues (e.g. receptacle, hypanthium)
ExamplesApple, strawberry, fig, rose hip, cashew apple

An accessory fruit, also called a false fruit or pseudocarp, is a fruit in which a significant part of the flesh derives not from the ovary wall but from tissues adjacent to the carpel, such as the receptacle or the hypanthium (floral cup). The term contrasts with "true fruits", which form from the ovary alone.

Examples

In the apple and pear (pomes), the edible flesh develops from the hypanthium surrounding the ovary; the actual fruit is the papery core containing the seeds. In the strawberry, the fleshy part is the swollen receptacle, and the true fruits are the small achenes dotting its surface. The fig is a syconium in which an enclosed inflorescence forms the structure; the rose hip develops from the hypanthium; and the cashew "apple" is a swollen stalk above the true nut.

Terminology

Botanists use "accessory" for any non-ovarian tissue contributing to the fruit body. Older literature uses "false fruit" or "spurious fruit"; neither term implies anything inedible or inferior — many of the best-known commercial fruits are accessory fruits.
