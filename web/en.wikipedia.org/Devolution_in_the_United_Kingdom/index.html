Devolution in the United Kingdom is the statutory granting of powers from the Parliament of the United Kingdom to the Scottish Parliament, the Senedd (Welsh Parliament), and the Northern Ireland Assembly, together with their respective executive bodies. Unlike a federal system, devolution operates within a unitary state: the UK Parliament remains sovereign and could in principle legislate on devolved matters or abolish the devolved institutions, although conventions and referendum mandates make this politically unlikely.

History

Campaigns for home rule in Scotland, Wales, and Ireland date to the nineteenth century. Referendums in 1979 failed to establish assemblies in Scotland and Wales, but fresh referendums in 1997 approved a Scottish Parliament with tax-varying powers and a Welsh assembly. In 1998 the Scotland Act, the Government of Wales Act, and the Northern Ireland Act—implementing the Good Friday Agreement—created the modern devolved institutions, which first sat in 1999.

Devolved and reserved powers

Devolved matters typically include health, education, justice and policing (in Scotland and Northern Ireland), transport, housing, and agriculture, while foreign policy, defence, immigration, and most macroeconomic policy remain reserved to Westminster. The distribution of powers differs between the three settlements, and all three legislatures have gained additional powers over time, including greater fiscal powers in Scotland and Wales.

England and debates

England, with the great majority of the UK population, has no devolved parliament of its own; devolution within England has proceeded through metro mayors and combined authorities. The asymmetry fuels continuing debates, including the "West Lothian question," proposals for an English parliament, and calls for full independence in Scotland and, to a lesser extent, Wales.
