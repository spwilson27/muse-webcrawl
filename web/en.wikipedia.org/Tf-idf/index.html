tf–idf

In information retrieval, tf–idf (also TF*IDF, TFIDF, TF–IDF, or Tf–idf), short for term frequency–inverse document frequency, is a measure of importance of a word to a document in a collection or corpus, adjusted for the fact that some words appear more frequently in general. It is often used as a weighting factor in searches of information retrieval, text mining, and user modeling.

Definition

The tf–idf value increases proportionally to the number of times a word appears in the document (term frequency) and is offset by the logarithm of the ratio of the number of documents in the corpus to the number of documents containing the word (inverse document frequency). Variants of the tf weight are commonly combined with document-length normalization. A word that occurs often in one document but rarely in the corpus — like a technical term — receives a high weight, while a word common everywhere — like "the" — receives a low one.

History

Term frequency weighting was introduced in the 1950s by Hans Peter Luhn; the inverse document frequency concept appeared in Karen Spärck Jones's 1972 paper "A statistical interpretation of term specificity and its application in retrieval," which established the theoretical basis of the scheme. Gerard Salton and his SMART retrieval system popularized tf–idf in practical search engines of the 1970s and 1980s.

Applications

tf–idf underlies the vector space model of retrieval, in which documents and queries are vectors of tf–idf weights and relevance is estimated by cosine similarity. It is widely used for search ranking, document classification, stop-word detection, and text summarization, though modern retrieval systems increasingly combine it with probabilistic models such as BM25 and learned neural embeddings.
