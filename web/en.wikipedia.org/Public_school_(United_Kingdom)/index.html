Public school (United Kingdom)

In England and Wales, a public school is a type of fee-charging private school, traditionally for boys aged 13–18, defined by the Public Schools Act 1868 and membership of the Headmasters' and Headmistresses' Conference.

A public school in England and Wales is a type of fee-charging private school. The term historically meant a school open to the public (as opposed to private tuition or church schools restricted to a locality or denomination) that accepted paying pupils from anywhere in the country. In Scotland and in most other English-speaking countries, by contrast, "public school" usually means a state-funded school.

The Clarendon schools

The Public Schools Act 1868 reformed the governance of the nine schools investigated by the Clarendon Commission of 1861–1864: Eton, Harrow, Rugby, Winchester, Westminster, Charterhouse, Shrewsbury, St Paul's, and Merchant Taylors'. Many of these institutions are centuries old and predominantly boarding schools.

Characteristics and criticism

Public schools charge substantial fees, educate a small minority of children (roughly 7% of pupils attend private schools in the UK), yet their alumni are heavily represented in politics, the judiciary, the civil service, and other elite professions. This disproportion has long made the schools a subject of debate about privilege and social mobility. Distinctive features include boarding houses, prefect systems, school-specific slang and sports, and historically practices such as fagging.
