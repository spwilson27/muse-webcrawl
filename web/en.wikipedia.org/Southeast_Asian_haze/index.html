Southeast Asian haze
TypeRecurring transboundary air pollution
Main sourcesSlash-and-burn land clearing and peat fires, chiefly in Indonesia
Affected countriesIndonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Brunei, southern Thailand, Philippines
Worst episodes1997–98, 2013, 2015, 2019

The Southeast Asian haze is a recurring, large-scale air pollution episode affecting several countries in Southeast Asia, typically during the dry season between June and October. It is caused mainly by smoke from forest and peatland fires in Indonesia—many set to clear land for palm oil and pulpwood plantations—carried by prevailing winds to Malaysia, Singapore, and neighbouring states.

Major episodes

The 1997–98 haze, intensified by a strong El Niño drought, blanketed the region for months, caused an estimated US$9 billion in damage, and was linked to tens of thousands of respiratory hospitalisations. Severe recurrences followed in 2013 (when Singapore's Pollutant Standards Index hit a record 401), 2015 (fires burned some 2.6 million hectares in Indonesia and emitted, on peak days, more CO₂ than the entire EU economy), and 2019.

Health and environmental effects

The fine particulate matter (PM2.5) in haze smoke penetrates deep into the lungs and bloodstream; studies have attributed large numbers of premature deaths across the region to severe episodes. Peat fires also release vast stores of carbon and destroy habitat of endangered species such as orangutans.

Responses

ASEAN adopted the Agreement on Transboundary Haze Pollution in 2002, though it lacks enforcement mechanisms. Indonesia has imposed moratoria on new peatland clearing and stronger fire enforcement; Singapore's Transboundary Haze Pollution Act (2014) allows prosecution of companies responsible for fires. Despite progress, weak land governance and commodity demand keep the problem recurrent.
