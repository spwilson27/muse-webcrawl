Enoxaparin

Trade namesLovenox, Clexane, others
TypeLow-molecular-weight heparin (anticoagulant)
RoutesSubcutaneous injection; intravenous (some indications)
Elimination half-lifeAbout 4.5 hours
Legal statusPrescription only

Enoxaparin sodium, sold under the brand name Lovenox among others, is an anticoagulant medication (blood thinner). It is used to treat and prevent deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE) including during pregnancy and following certain types of surgery. It is also used in those with acute coronary syndrome (ACS) and heart attacks. It is given by injection just under the skin or into a vein.

Mechanism of action

Enoxaparin is a low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) prepared by depolymerising standard heparin. Like all heparins it binds antithrombin III, accelerating its inhibition of clotting factors. Because enoxaparin's chains are shorter, it preferentially inhibits factor Xa over thrombin (anti-Xa to anti-IIa ratio of roughly 3–4:1), and gives a more predictable dose response than unfractionated heparin, so routine laboratory monitoring is usually unnecessary.

Medical uses

Indications include prophylaxis of venous thromboembolism after orthopaedic or general surgery and in acutely ill medical inpatients; treatment of DVT and PE; management of unstable angina and non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction; and adjunctive therapy in ST-elevation myocardial infarction. Prophylactic doses are typically 40 mg once daily subcutaneously; treatment doses are weight-based (1 mg/kg twice daily or 1.5 mg/kg once daily).

Adverse effects

Common side effects include bleeding, low platelet counts, and pain at the injection site. Serious bleeding can often be partly reversed with protamine sulfate, though protamine neutralises only about 60% of enoxaparin's anti-Xa activity. Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia occurs less often than with unfractionated heparin. The drug is cleared by the kidneys, so doses are reduced in severe renal impairment.

History

Enoxaparin was first made in 1980 by the French company Choay (later part of Sanofi) and approved for medical use in the late 1980s–1990s. It became the most widely used LMWH worldwide and appears on the WHO Model List of Essential Medicines. A contamination incident involving a related heparin supply chain in 2008 led to tighter manufacturing controls.
