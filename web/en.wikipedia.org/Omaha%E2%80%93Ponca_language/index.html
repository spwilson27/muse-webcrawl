Omaha–Ponca is a Siouan language spoken by the Omaha people of Nebraska and the Ponca people of Nebraska and Oklahoma. It belongs to the Dhegiha branch of the Siouan family, closely related to Osage, Kansa, and Quapaw. The Omaha and Ponca dialects are mutually intelligible and are generally considered a single language with minor differences in vocabulary and pronunciation.

History

The Omaha and Ponca were historically a single people who separated before European contact; linguistic divergence between the two groups remained small. By the late twentieth century the number of fluent first-language speakers had declined sharply, and the language is now considered severely endangered. Tribal language programs in Nebraska and Oklahoma have worked to document Omaha–Ponca and to teach it to younger generations.

Phonology and grammar

Omaha–Ponca has a consonant inventory including aspirated and ejective stops, nasalized vowels, and a system of ablaut in verbs. Like other Siouan languages, it is verb-final (subject–object–verb word order) and uses a complex system of instrumental prefixes and positional articles. Nouns are marked with definite articles that encode the position or shape of the referent.

Revitalization

Documentation efforts include dictionaries, text collections, and recordings of elder speakers. The University of Nebraska and tribal colleges have offered language courses, and immersion programs have been established for children in both the Omaha and Ponca communities.
