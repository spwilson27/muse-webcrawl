The Battle of Washita River occurred on 27 November 1868 during the American Indian Wars, when Lieutenant Colonel George Armstrong Custer's 7th US Cavalry attacked the Southern Cheyenne village of Chief Black Kettle at dawn on the Washita River in present-day Roger Mills County, Oklahoma. The engagement formed part of General Philip Sheridan's winter campaign against the Southern Plains tribes.

Background

Black Kettle, a noted peace chief who had survived the Sand Creek massacre of 1864, had camped along the Washita with around 250 people. Sheridan ordered winter operations to force the tribes onto reservations, and Custer followed a trail through the snow to the village, unaware that many more Cheyenne, Arapaho, and Kiowa camps lay downstream.

The attack

At dawn Custer's four columns charged the village to the strains of "Garryowen". Black Kettle and his wife were shot dead as they tried to escape across the river. The cavalry killed an uncertain number of Cheyenne — army reports claimed 103 warriors, while Cheyenne accounts indicate fewer fighting men and a number of women and children — captured 53 women and children, and shot the village's pony herd. A detachment under Major Joel Elliott pursued fleeing Cheyenne and was surrounded and killed by warriors arriving from the downstream camps, forcing Custer to withdraw.

Legacy

The Washita fight remains controversial: celebrated at the time as a victory, it is now frequently described as an attack on a largely peaceful village. The site is preserved as the Washita Battlefield National Historic Site.
