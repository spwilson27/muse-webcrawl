The Shanghai massacre of 12 April 1927, also known as the April 12 Incident (四·一二事件) or the April 12 Purge, was a violent suppression of Chinese Communist Party (CCP) organizations and leftist elements in Shanghai by forces loyal to Kuomintang (KMT) leader Chiang Kai-shek. It marked the decisive rupture of the First United Front between the Nationalists and the Communists and the beginning of the Chinese Civil War.

Background

Since 1923, the KMT under Sun Yat-sen and then Chiang Kai-shek had cooperated with the CCP and received Soviet support to reunify China. During the Northern Expedition (1926–1928), Communist-led labour unions and peasant associations expanded rapidly. In March 1927, Shanghai's workers, organized by Communists including Zhou Enlai, launched a general strike and armed uprising that seized the Chinese-administered parts of the city from the warlord Sun Chuanfang before Chiang's National Revolutionary Army arrived.

Conservative factions within the KMT, Shanghai bankers and industrialists, foreign powers in the concessions, and the underworld Green Gang under Du Yuesheng all pressed Chiang to break with the Communists. On 12 April, Green Gang thugs wearing workers' armbands attacked union headquarters, while troops of the 26th Army under Bai Chongxi and Xue Yue disarmed the workers' pickets; crowds protesting the next day in Baoshan Road were fired upon.

The purge

Over the following days and weeks, thousands of Communists, union members, students, and suspected leftists were arrested, executed, or driven underground in Shanghai. Similar purges followed in Guangzhou (the Canton uprising's aftermath), Changsha, Xiamen, and other cities under KMT control, and the Wuhan-based left-KMT government of Wang Jingwei also split with the CCP in July 1927. Estimates of the dead in Shanghai range from several hundred to several thousand; across China in 1927–1928, tens of thousands of Communists and sympathizers were killed, and CCP membership collapsed from nearly 60,000 to about 10,000.

Consequences

The massacre ended the First United Front, forced the CCP underground and toward a strategy of rural base areas and armed insurrection — including the Nanchang and Autumn Harvest uprisings later in 1927 — and inaugurated a decade of civil war between the Nationalist government at Nanjing and the Communists, interrupted only by the Second United Front against Japan in 1937. Zhou Enlai narrowly escaped capture in Shanghai; many other Communist leaders were killed, among them union organizer Zhao Shiyan and, in the wider purge, Li Dazhao in Beijing.
