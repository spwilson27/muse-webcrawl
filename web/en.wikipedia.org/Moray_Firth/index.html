Moray Firth

Moray Firth
Location: northern Scotland, North Sea coast
Inner firths: Inverness Firth, Cromarty Firth, Dornoch Firth
Main city: Inverness
Known for: resident bottlenose dolphin population

The Moray Firth (Scottish Gaelic: An Cuan Moireach) is a roughly triangular inlet of the North Sea in the north of Scotland, north and east of Inverness. It is Scotland's largest firth and stretches from Duncansby Head near John o' Groats in the northeast, along the coasts of Highland, Moray, and Aberdeenshire, to Fraserburgh in the east. Its inner arms are the Beauly, Cromarty, and Dornoch firths.


The Kessock Bridge crossing the inner firth at Inverness

Geography and settlements

The firth is bounded by the coastlines of Caithness, Sutherland, Ross and Cromarty, Inverness-shire, Nairnshire, Moray, and Banff and Buchan. Principal settlements include Inverness at the head of the inner firth, Nairn, Lossiemouth, Buckie, Macduff, and Fraserburgh. Major rivers entering it include the Ness, Beauly, Conon, and Spey. The Chanonry Point lighthouse and Fort George, built after the Jacobite rising of 1745, are notable landmarks.

Economy and wildlife

Fishing—especially for herring in the 19th century—long dominated coastal towns, and the region became a center for North Sea oil fabrication at Nigg and offshore wind development in the 21st century, including the Beatrice and Moray East wind farms. The firth is internationally important for wildlife: it hosts one of the world's northernmost resident populations of bottlenose dolphins, as well as minke whales, harbour porpoises, grey and common seals, and large wintering flocks of wildfowl and waders. Much of the inner firth is protected as a Special Area of Conservation and Ramsar site.
