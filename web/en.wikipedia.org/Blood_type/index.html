Blood type

A blood type (also known as a blood group) is a classification of blood, based on the presence and absence of antibodies and inherited antigenic substances on the surface of red blood cells (RBCs). These antigens may be proteins, carbohydrates, glycoproteins, or glycolipids, depending on the blood group system. Some of these antigens are also present on the surface of other types of cells of various tissues. Several of these red blood cell surface antigens can stem from one allele (or an alternative gene) and collectively form a blood group system.

The ABO and Rh blood group systems are the most clinically important in transfusion medicine.

ABO system

The ABO system is the most important blood-group system in human blood transfusion. It comprises four common phenotypes: A, B, AB, and O. The A and B antigens are carbohydrate structures built on the H antigen; individuals with type O blood lack functional A and B transferase enzymes and display only the H antigen. Naturally occurring anti-A and anti-B antibodies are present in the plasma of individuals who lack the corresponding antigens, which is why transfusing ABO-incompatible blood can cause a rapid, potentially fatal haemolytic reaction.

Type O individuals are often called "universal donors" for red cell transfusion (especially O Rh-negative), while type AB individuals are "universal recipients" (especially AB Rh-positive), because AB plasma contains neither anti-A nor anti-B antibodies.

Rh system

The Rh system is the second most significant blood-group system in human transfusion. Its most important antigen is the D antigen; a person is described as Rh-positive if the D antigen is present and Rh-negative if it is absent. Unlike the ABO antibodies, anti-D antibodies are not naturally occurring and generally arise only after exposure to Rh-positive blood through transfusion or pregnancy. This sensitisation can cause haemolytic disease of the newborn in subsequent pregnancies, a condition now largely preventable by administering anti-D immunoglobulin (Rho(D) immune globulin) to Rh-negative mothers.

Other systems

Beyond ABO and Rh, the International Society of Blood Transfusion recognises dozens of blood group systems, including the Kell, Duffy, Kidd, Lewis, MNS, and Lutheran systems. Some have major clinical significance: the Kell antigen K is highly immunogenic, and Duffy antigens serve as receptors for the malaria parasite Plasmodium vivax, so Duffy-negative individuals are largely resistant to that form of malaria.

History

The ABO groups were discovered by the Austrian immunologist Karl Landsteiner in 1901, work for which he received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1930. The Rh factor was identified in 1940 by Landsteiner and Alexander S. Wiener, following experiments with rhesus macaque blood. Earlier attempts at blood transfusion, dating to the 17th century, frequently failed because the immunological basis of compatibility was unknown.
