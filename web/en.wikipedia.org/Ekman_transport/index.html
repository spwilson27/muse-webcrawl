Ekman transport

Ekman transport is the net motion of fluid in the surface layer of the ocean (or atmosphere) that results from a balance between wind stress, friction and the Coriolis force. It is named after the Swedish oceanographer Vagn Waldo Ekman, who developed the theory in 1905, building on observations made by Fridtjof Nansen during the Fram expedition, in which icebergs drifted at an angle to the right of the wind in the Arctic.

The Ekman spiral

Wind blowing over the sea drags the surface water with it. Because of the Coriolis effect, the surface current is deflected — to the right in the Northern Hemisphere and to the left in the Southern Hemisphere. Each successively deeper layer is dragged by the layer above and deflected further, while its speed decreases, producing a spiral pattern of velocity vectors called the Ekman spiral. The motion decays with depth and becomes negligible below the Ekman layer, typically some tens of metres deep.

Net transport and upwelling

Although the surface flow is deflected by about 45°, the depth-integrated (net) transport of the Ekman layer is at 90° to the wind direction — right in the Northern Hemisphere, left in the Southern. Where Ekman transport carries surface water away from a coast, deep water rises to replace it (upwelling), bringing nutrients that support rich fisheries off Peru, California, Namibia and the Canary Islands. Transport toward a coast causes downwelling. Convergence and divergence of Ekman transport in the open ocean produce Ekman pumping and suction, which help drive the great subtropical ocean gyres.
