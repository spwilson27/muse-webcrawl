Complement (linguistics)

In grammar and linguistics, a complement is a word, phrase, or clause that is necessary in order to complete the meaning of a given expression. Complements are often, but not always, arguments: they contrast with adjuncts, which are optional modifiers that can be omitted without rendering the sentence ungrammatical or incomplete.

Types of complements

Common kinds include object complements and subject complements. In "She made him happy," him is the object and happy is an object complement characterizing the object. In "She is happy," the adjective phrase happy is a subject complement (predicative complement) following a linking verb. Verbs, nouns, adjectives, and prepositions can all take complements: a verb may take a noun phrase or a finite or non-finite clause (that-clauses, to-infinitives); the noun belief takes a clause complement in "the belief that the earth is round."

In generative grammar

In phrase structure grammar, particularly X-bar theory, the complement is the sister constituent of a head within its phrase — the element most closely combined with the head, in contrast to specifiers and adjuncts. For example, in the verb phrase "eat the cake," the noun phrase the cake is the complement of the verb eat. In this technical usage, English objects are prototypical verbal complements.

Complements versus adjuncts

Several diagnostics distinguish complements from adjuncts: complements are typically obligatory for the head's meaning, cannot be stacked freely (a head usually takes at most one of each complement type), appear closer to the head than adjuncts, and are selected (subcategorized) by particular heads, whereas adjuncts combine with many heads.
