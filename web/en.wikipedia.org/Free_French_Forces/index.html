The Free French Forces (French: Forces françaises libres) were the armed forces of Free France, the government-in-exile led by General Charles de Gaulle during the Second World War. Formed after the fall of France in 1940, they fought alongside the Allies against the Axis and the Vichy regime, and in 1943 merged with other French forces to form the French Liberation Army.

Origins

On 18 June 1940, from London, de Gaulle broadcast his famous Appeal of 18 June, calling on French soldiers to continue the fight. Volunteers — initially only a few thousand men, including troops evacuated from Dunkirk and sailors of the French fleet in British ports — rallied to the Cross of Lorraine emblem. French Equatorial Africa's defection in August 1940 gave Free France territory and resources.

Campaigns

Free French units fought in Gabon, Eritrea, Syria (against Vichy forces), and North Africa. At the Battle of Bir Hakeim (1942), General Koenig's 1st Free French Brigade held off Axis forces for nearly two weeks, a resistance that became a rallying symbol. Free French naval and air units served worldwide, and French pilots flew with the RAF and, in the Normandie-Niemen regiment, on the Eastern Front.

Merger and liberation

After the Allied landings in North Africa in November 1942, the Free French merged with General Giraud's Army of Africa in 1943, forming the French Committee of National Liberation. Re-equipped French forces fought in Italy and then landed in Provence in August 1944; General Leclerc's 2nd Armoured Division liberated Paris and Strasbourg. By war's end, France fielded an army of well over a million soldiers, restoring its standing as a major Allied power.
