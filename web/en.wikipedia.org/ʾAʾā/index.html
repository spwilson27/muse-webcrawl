ʾAʾā

ʾAʾā is a Hawaiian term for a type of rough, clinkery basaltic lava flow. The word, written in Hawaiian orthography with ʻokina marking glottal stops, is one of two Hawaiian lava terms adopted into international geological vocabulary, the other being pāhoehoe, which denotes smooth, ropy lava.

Characteristics

An ʾaʾā flow has a rough, jagged surface composed of broken lava blocks called clinker. The flow advances with a relatively viscous interior beneath a fragmented crust; blocks tumble down the steep flow front and are overridden by the advancing lava. ʾAʾā flows typically move more slowly than pāhoehoe flows of comparable discharge and are associated with higher viscosity, higher effusion rates, and greater gas loss and crystallisation in the lava.

Formation

Pāhoehoe can transform into ʾaʾā downslope as it cools, loses gas, and is strained — for example where a channel steepens — but the transition is essentially irreversible: ʾaʾā does not change back into pāhoehoe. The surface rubble insulates the molten core, allowing ʾaʾā flows to travel considerable distances from their vents.
