Fuel injection

Fuel injection is the introduction of fuel into an internal combustion engine, most commonly automotive engines, by the means of an injector. Modern fuel injection systems are specifically designed for the type of fuel being used, and are controlled electronically to meter fuel precisely, improving power, efficiency, and emissions compared with carburettors.

Types

Major configurations include throttle-body injection (single-point), multi-point port injection, and direct injection, in which fuel is sprayed directly into the combustion chamber at high pressure. Diesel engines have always used direct injection because their fuel must be injected against high compression.

Operation

An electronic control unit (ECU) calculates the required fuel quantity from sensors measuring airflow, throttle position, engine speed, temperature, and exhaust oxygen content, then pulses the injectors for a precise duration. Rail pressure in modern common-rail diesel systems can exceed 2,000 bar.

History

Fuel injection was used in aircraft engines during World War II and in production cars from the 1950s (notably the Mercedes-Benz 300SL). Electronic systems appeared in the late 1960s, and by the 1990s fuel injection had almost entirely replaced carburettors in passenger cars.
