The paratope sits at the tip of each arm of a Y-shaped antibody.

A paratope, also called an antigen-binding site, is the part of an antibody that recognises and binds to an antigen. It is a small region — roughly 5 to 10 amino acids — at the tip of the antibody's variable region, formed jointly by the complementarity-determining regions (CDRs) of the heavy and light chains. The paratope binds to a specific corresponding region on the antigen called the epitope.

Structure

Antibodies are Y-shaped molecules; each of the two arms ends in a variable (V) domain pair, one from the heavy chain and one from the light chain. Loops of highly variable sequence — the CDRs, three on each chain — cluster at the tip to form the binding surface. Because the paratope's shape and charge distribution are complementary to those of its epitope, binding is often compared to a lock and key, though in practice the interaction involves induced fit and a network of hydrogen bonds, van der Waals forces and electrostatic interactions.

Specificity and diversity

The enormous diversity of paratopes is generated by V(D)J recombination of immunoglobulin gene segments, junctional diversity, and, after antigen encounter, somatic hypermutation during affinity maturation. This allows the immune system to produce antibodies capable of binding an immense range of epitopes. An antibody whose paratope binds a molecule that is itself an antibody variable region gives rise to anti-idiotypic responses, a feature used in some vaccine strategies.

Applications

Knowledge of paratope structure underlies antibody engineering, humanisation of monoclonal antibodies, and the design of binding proteins in diagnostics and therapy. The smallest functional unit retaining a complete paratope is a single-chain variable fragment (scFv).
