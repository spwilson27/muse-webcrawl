The VIX (ticker symbol for the CBOE Volatility Index) is a real-time index representing the market's expectation of future volatility of the S&P 500 Index over the next 30 calendar days, derived from the prices of S&P 500 index options with near-term expiration dates. It is calculated and published by Cboe Global Markets and is widely quoted by financial media as the market's "fear gauge".

Interpretation

The VIX is expressed as an annualized percentage: a VIX of 20 implies an expected move of about 20% over the coming year, or roughly 5.8% over the next 30 days (20% ÷ √12). The index is mean-reverting and strongly negatively correlated with stock prices: it typically trades between 10 and 20 in calm markets and spikes sharply during crises — exceeding 80 intraday during the 2008 financial crisis and again in March 2020.

History

The VIX was introduced in 1993, originally based on S&P 100 options; in 2003 Cboe, with Goldman Sachs, revised the methodology to the current model-free formula based on a wide strip of S&P 500 options, building on academic work by Demeterfi and others. VIX futures began trading in 2004 and VIX options in 2006, followed by exchange-traded products such as VXX, some of which featured in the February 2018 "Volmageddon" collapse of inverse-volatility funds.

Criticism

The VIX measures implied, not realized, volatility and tends on average to overstate subsequent realized volatility (the variance risk premium). Its derivatives can also be subject to feedback effects, and the settlement process of VIX futures has been the subject of academic and regulatory scrutiny regarding possible manipulation.
