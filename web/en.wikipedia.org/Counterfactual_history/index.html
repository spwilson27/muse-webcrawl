Counterfactual history

TypeHistoriographical method; speculative fiction genre
Also known asAlternate history, virtual history, allohistory
Noted practitionersNiall Ferguson, Robert Cowley (ed.), Geoffrey Hawthorn

Counterfactual history (also called virtual history) is a form of historiography that explores what might have happened if past events had unfolded differently. It proceeds by positing a plausible alteration at a decisive point — a "point of divergence" — and tracing the consequences, while ideally remaining constrained by the evidence and by what contemporaries actually considered possible.

Academic use

Supporters such as Niall Ferguson, in his edited volume Virtual History (1997), argue that all causal claims in history implicitly rest on counterfactual reasoning: to say an event caused an outcome is to imply matters would have differed without it. Counterfactuals are used seriously in cliometrics, political science, and the analysis of decisions such as the outbreak of the First World War. Critics, including E. H. Carr and E. P. Thompson, have dismissed the genre as a parlour game, while others warn that long chains of speculation quickly leave evidence behind.

In fiction

As fiction, alternate history is an established genre of speculative literature. Classic works include Ward Moore's Bring the Jubilee (Confederate victory), Philip K. Dick's The Man in the High Castle (Axis victory in the Second World War), and many works by Harry Turtledove. Common scenarios include the American Civil War, Nazi Germany, and the survival of empires or individuals.

Criteria

Scholars such as Geoffrey Hawthorn have proposed that good counterfactuals should be plausible, historically consistent at the branch point, and parsimonious in the changes they introduce — principles sometimes summarised as the "minimal rewrite rule".
