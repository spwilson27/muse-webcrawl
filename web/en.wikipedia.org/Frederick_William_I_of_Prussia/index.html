Frederick William I (German: Friedrich Wilhelm I.), known as the "Soldier King", was King in Prussia and Elector of Brandenburg from 1713 until his death in 1740. He transformed Prussia into a military and administrative power through drastic economies, centralised bureaucracy and a huge, rigorously drilled army.

Reforms

Austerely Calvinist and contemptuous of his father's Baroque court, Frederick William slashed court spending, sold crown luxuries and built an efficient fiscal administration (the General Directory, 1723). He founded schools, rebuilt Berlin and Potsdam, encouraged immigration, and reformed taxation and justice, abolishing torture in most cases.

The army

The Prussian army grew from about 38,000 to some 83,000 men — the fourth largest in Europe from only the thirteenth-largest population — sustained by the canton system of conscription (1733). He famously collected very tall soldiers for his regiment of "Potsdam Giants". Despite this investment he largely avoided war, using the army as an instrument of prestige and bargaining.

Family and succession

His harsh discipline of his son, the future Frederick the Great, culminated in the execution of the son's friend Hans Hermann von Katte in 1730 after a failed escape attempt. Despite their conflict, Frederick inherited in 1740 a full treasury, a professional civil service and Europe's best-drilled army.
