Joinery

Joinery is a part of woodworking that involves joining pieces of wood or lumber to produce more complex items. Some wood joints employ mechanical fasteners, bindings, or adhesives, while others use only wood elements (often called traditional joinery). The characteristics of wooden joints — strength, flexibility, toughness, appearance, etc. — derive from the properties of the materials involved and the purpose of the joint.

Types of joints

Common joints include the butt joint, the simplest joint in which pieces are simply joined end to end or edge to edge; the mitre joint, made by bevelled ends; the dovetail joint, valued for its resistance to being pulled apart; the mortise and tenon, in which a projecting tenon fits into a mortise hole; the dado and groove joints; the lap joint; the tongue and groove; and the box joint, a simpler alternative to the dovetail.

History

Wood joinery is an ancient craft. Mortise-and-tenon joints were used in Egyptian furniture and in prehistoric Chinese timber construction thousands of years ago. Traditional Japanese and Chinese joinery is especially noted for complex interlocking joints made without nails, screws, or glue, capable of being disassembled and re-assembled.

Modern practice

Modern joiners combine traditional hand techniques with power tools, adhesives, and engineered fasteners such as biscuits, dowels, and pocket screws. In building construction the term also covers the manufacture and installation of fitted woodwork such as doors, windows, stairs, and cabinetry.
