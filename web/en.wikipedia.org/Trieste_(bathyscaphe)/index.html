Trieste (bathyscaphe)

DesignerAuguste Piccard
Launched1953
OperatorUnited States Navy (from 1958)
RecordDive to Challenger Deep, 23 January 1960
StatusMuseum exhibit, Washington, D.C.

Trieste is a Swiss-designed, Italian-built deep-diving research submersible — a bathyscaphe — which reached a record depth of about 10,911 metres (35,797 ft) in the Challenger Deep of the Mariana Trench on 23 January 1960. The project was led by the Swiss scientist Auguste Piccard and his son Jacques Piccard; on the record dive the crew were Jacques Piccard and United States Navy Lieutenant Don Walsh.

Design

A bathyscaphe works like an underwater balloon. Trieste consisted of a large float filled with gasoline, which is lighter than water and nearly incompressible, providing buoyancy, above a heavy spherical steel pressure cabin that carried the two-man crew. Descent was controlled by iron-shot ballast held by electromagnets; releasing the shot — automatically if power failed — let the craft ascend. The cabin had a single small window of acrylic plastic and an entrance shaft through the float.

The record dive

The United States Navy bought Trieste in 1958 for deep-sea research under Project Nekton. On 23 January 1960, Piccard and Walsh descended for nearly five hours into Challenger Deep, the deepest known point of the world's oceans. At the bottom they observed surprisingly a small flatfish-like animal, evidence that life existed even at such pressure and darkness. The ascent took about three hours and twenty minutes. The dive was not repeated with a crewed vehicle for over fifty years, until James Cameron's solo descent in Deepsea Challenger in 2012.

Later service

Trieste went on to search for the wreck of the lost nuclear submarine USS Thresher in 1963. A heavily rebuilt successor, Trieste II, continued Navy deep-submergence work. The original craft is preserved at the National Museum of the United States Navy in Washington, D.C.
