Neoclassical economics

Neoclassical economics is an approach to economics in which the production, consumption and valuation (pricing) of goods and services are observed as driven by the supply and demand model. It emerged from the "marginalist revolution" of the 1870s and became the dominant framework of mainstream economics in the 20th century.

Origins

The marginalist theorists William Stanley Jevons, Carl Menger and Léon Walras independently argued that value derives not from the labour embodied in goods, as classical economists from Smith to Marx held, but from marginal utility — the additional satisfaction from consuming one more unit. Alfred Marshall's Principles of Economics (1890) synthesised marginal analysis with classical cost theory, introducing tools such as elasticity and consumer surplus. Vilfredo Pareto, John Hicks and Paul Samuelson later gave the theory rigorous mathematical form, culminating in the Arrow–Debreu model of general competitive equilibrium.

Core assumptions

  • Individuals have rational preferences among outcomes that can be ranked.
  • Individuals maximise utility and firms maximise profits.
  • People act independently on the basis of full and relevant information (an assumption later relaxed in information economics).

Scope and influence

Neoclassical theory underpins supply-and-demand analysis, the theory of the firm, welfare economics and its two fundamental theorems, and much of modern microeconomics. The mid-20th-century "neoclassical synthesis" merged it with Keynesian macroeconomics. Subsequent developments — game theory, behavioural economics, and new institutional economics — have relaxed its assumptions while often retaining its mathematical style.

Criticism

Critics from institutionalist, post-Keynesian, Austrian and ecological schools have challenged the realism of rational-agent assumptions, the treatment of uncertainty, and the framework's limited attention to power, institutions and environmental limits.
