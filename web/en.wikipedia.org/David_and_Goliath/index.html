David and Goliath

David and Goliath is the biblical account, told in the First Book of Samuel, of the battle between the young Israelite shepherd David and Goliath, the giant champion of the Philistines. The story, in which an unarmored youth defeats a heavily armed giant with a sling stone, has become a cultural archetype of the underdog's triumph.

The biblical account

According to 1 Samuel 17, the armies of Israel under King Saul faced the Philistines in the Valley of Elah. For forty days Goliath of Gath — described as six cubits and a span tall, clad in bronze armor — challenged Israel to settle the battle by single combat. David, who had come to bring food to his brothers, volunteered. Refusing Saul's armor, he took his sling and five smooth stones from a brook, struck Goliath in the forehead with the first stone, and then beheaded the fallen giant with Goliath's own sword. The Philistine army fled.

Textual questions

The story exists in two forms: the longer Masoretic text and a substantially shorter version in the Greek Septuagint, which many scholars regard as closer to an earlier edition. A passage in 2 Samuel 21 credits the killing of Goliath to Elhanan, son of Jaare-oregim, a discrepancy long debated by biblical scholars.

Cultural legacy

David and Goliath have been depicted in countless works of art, from Donatello's and Michelangelo's statues to Caravaggio's paintings. The phrase "David and Goliath" now commonly denotes any contest between a much weaker party and an overwhelming favorite, and it titles Malcolm Gladwell's 2013 book on the advantages of disadvantages.
