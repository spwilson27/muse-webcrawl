A geomagnetic reversal is a change in a planet's magnetic field such that the positions of magnetic north and magnetic south are interchanged (not to be confused with geographic north and geographic south). The Earth's field has alternated between periods of normal polarity, in which the predominant direction of the field is the same as the present direction, and reverse polarity, in which it is the opposite.

Magnetic stripes recorded in oceanic crust document past reversals and drove the acceptance of seafloor spreading.

These periods are called chrons. Reversal occurrence seems to be statistically random; intervals of constant polarity range from tens of thousands of years to tens of millions (the Cretaceous Normal Superchron lasted nearly 40 million years without a reversal). Over the last 83 million years there have been 183 reversals. The last complete reversal, the Brunhes–Matuyama reversal, occurred about 780,000 years ago; a brief near-reversal, the Laschamp event, occurred about 41,000 years ago.

Evidence and causes

Reversals are recorded in the magnetisation of volcanic lavas, sediments and — crucially for the plate-tectonics revolution — in the symmetric magnetic "stripes" of oceanic crust on either side of mid-ocean ridges. They arise from the self-sustaining dynamo in Earth's liquid outer core; numerical dynamo models spontaneously produce reversals, during which the dipole weakens and the field becomes more complex and multipolar before recovering with opposite polarity.

Effects

During a transition the field may weaken to a fraction of its normal strength, increasing cosmic-ray flux at the surface. Speculative links to extinctions have not been substantiated; human ancestors lived through several reversals. A weakened field could increase disruption to power grids and satellites from geomagnetic storms. The magnetic north pole is currently drifting and the dipole moment is decreasing, but this does not by itself imply an imminent reversal.
