Stippling

Stippling is the creation of a pattern simulating varying degrees of solidity or shading by using small dots. Such a pattern may occur in nature and these effects are frequently emulated by artists. In a drawing or painting, the dots are of pigment of a single colour, applied with a pen or brush; the denser the dots, the darker the apparent shade — or lighter, if the pigment is lighter than the surface. This is similar to — but distinct from — pointillism, which uses dots of different colours to simulate blended colours.

History

The technique became popular as a means of producing shaded line art illustrations for publication, because drawings created this way could be reproduced in simple black ink. The other common method is hatching, which uses lines instead of dots. Stipple can also be used in engraving or etching to create a mid-tone.

Technique

In traditional stippling, the artist works with a single medium, most often black ink on white paper. Density of dots controls tone: closely spaced dots read as dark areas, widely spaced dots as light areas. Because each dot is discrete, stippled images reproduce well in print, including newspapers, and the method is still used in scientific illustration, particularly in botany and zoology, where it gives precise, reproducible tonal rendering of specimens.

Applications

Stippling is used in forensic and medical illustration, in cartography (for example to indicate forested or sandy areas), and in wall decoration, where stippled paint finishes can soften the appearance of a room. In tattooing, dotwork is a related technique in which shading is built up entirely from individual needle dots. Digital artists reproduce the effect with brush tools and halftone-like filters.
