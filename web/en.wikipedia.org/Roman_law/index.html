Roman law is the legal system of ancient Rome, including the legal developments spanning over a thousand years of jurisprudence, from the Twelve Tables (c. 449 BC) to the Corpus Juris Civilis (AD 529–534) ordered by Eastern Emperor Justinian I. Roman law forms the basis for the civil law systems now used in most countries of the world, and its concepts and terminology permeate even common-law jurisdictions.

Development

Early law and the Twelve Tables

Early Roman law was customary and known only to the patrician priesthood. Under plebeian pressure, a board of ten men (decemvirs) produced the Twelve Tables (451–449 BC), traditionally regarded as the foundation of Roman law, covering procedure, property, family, delict, and sacred law.

Classical period

During the late Republic and Principate, jurisprudence became a sophisticated professional science. The praetor's edict developed the ius honorarium, supplementing and correcting the strict civil law; jurists such as Gaius, Papinian, Ulpian, Paul, and Modestinus systematized the law of persons, property, obligations, and actions. Gaius's Institutes (c. AD 161) organized law around persons, things, and actions.

Justinian's codification

Emperor Justinian I commissioned Tribonian and others to compile the Corpus Juris Civilis: the Codex (imperial enactments), the Digest or Pandects (juristic writings), the Institutes (a student textbook), and later the Novellae (new laws). This corpus preserved classical Roman law for posterity.

Concepts

Roman law distinguished ius civile (law for citizens), ius gentium (law of nations, applied to foreigners), and ius naturale (natural law). It developed enduring doctrines of ownership and possession, contract (including the formalism of stipulatio), delict, succession, and legal personality, as well as the adversarial court procedure with a private judge (iudex).

Legacy

Recovered in 11th-century Bologna, where glossators such as Irnerius revived its study, Roman law spread through European universities as the ius commune and shaped the great modern codifications, including the French Napoleonic Code (1804) and the German BGB (1900). Its maxims — such as audi alteram partem and the presumption of innocence in criminal contexts — remain part of legal culture worldwide.
