Dalton Minimum

Periodc. 1790–1830
Named forJohn Dalton
CyclesSolar cycles 5, 6, and 7
ComparableMaunder Minimum, Spörer Minimum

The Dalton Minimum was a period of low counted sunspot activity, representing low solar activity, named after the English meteorologist John Dalton, lasting from about 1790 to 1830 (or 1796 to 1820, according to some). Like the Maunder Minimum and Spörer Minimum before it, the Dalton Minimum coincided with a period of lower-than-average surface temperatures. Both solar cycles 5 and 6 had maximums far below average, and cycle 6 (ending in 1823) had a very low maximum sunspot count.

Climate context

The period fell within the later stages of the "Little Ice Age." The early 19th century saw severe winters and cool summers in Europe and North America, compounded by major volcanic eruptions — including the unidentified 1808/09 eruption and the colossal 1815 eruption of Mount Tambora, which caused the "Year Without a Summer" in 1816. Historians and climate scientists continue to debate the relative contributions of reduced solar output and volcanic aerosols to the cooling; the solar contribution alone is generally considered modest.

Observations

Sunspot records of the period come from astronomers such as Johann Kaspar Staudach and later Heinrich Schwabe, whose work from the 1820s onward led to the discovery of the Schwabe (11-year) solar cycle. Abundant aurorae and the behavior of the corona during eclipses were also noted. The Dalton Minimum is less deep than the Maunder Minimum: the Sun continued to show a clear (if weakened) cycle, and open solar flux did not collapse as completely.

Significance

The Dalton Minimum is frequently invoked in discussions of whether a modern "grand solar minimum" could offset anthropogenic warming. Research indicates that even a return to Maunder-like conditions would offset only a small fraction of projected greenhouse warming.
