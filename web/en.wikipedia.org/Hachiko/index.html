Hachikō


Hachikō's statue at Shibuya Station

Hachikō (ハチ公, November 10, 1923 – March 8, 1935) was a Japanese Akita dog remembered for his remarkable loyalty to his owner, Hidesaburō Ueno, for whom he continued to wait at Shibuya Station for more than nine years following Ueno's death in 1925. Hachikō has become a national symbol of loyalty in Japan, and his statue at Shibuya is one of Tokyo's most famous meeting points.

Life

Hachikō, a golden-brown Akita, was born on a farm near Ōdate in Akita Prefecture and was brought to Tokyo in 1924 by Ueno, a professor of agriculture at Tokyo Imperial University. Each day Hachikō would accompany Ueno to Shibuya Station and return in the evening to greet his train. On May 21, 1925, Ueno died of a cerebral haemorrhage while at work and never returned. Hachikō was given away but kept escaping back to Ueno's house and then to the station, where every evening he waited for the train, a vigil he maintained for nine years, nine months and fifteen days, cared for by station staff and commuters.

Recognition

One of Ueno's former students published articles about the dog in 1932, bringing him national fame; he was given the honorific suffix -kō. In 1934 a bronze statue by Teru Andō was erected at Shibuya Station in Hachikō's presence. The original statue was recycled for the war effort, but a replacement by the sculptor's son was installed in 1948. Hachikō died on March 8, 1935; his remains are preserved at the National Museum of Nature and Science in Ueno, Tokyo, and his story has been retold in films including Hachikō Monogatari (1987) and the American remake Hachi: A Dog's Tale (2009).
