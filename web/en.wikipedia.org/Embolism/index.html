Embolism

Embolism
SpecialtyVascular medicine, cardiology, hematology
TypesThromboembolism, fat, air, cholesterol, amniotic fluid, septic
ComplicationsInfarction, stroke, pulmonary embolism, ischemia

An embolism is the lodging of an embolus — a detached intravascular mass of material — within a blood vessel, causing partial or complete blockage of blood flow. The embolus may be a blood clot (thrombus), a globule of fat, a bubble of air or other gas, amniotic fluid, or a piece of atheromatous plaque. The term was coined by the German pathologist Rudolf Virchow, who investigated how clots formed in veins travel to distant sites.

Classification

Embolisms are classified both by the material involved and by the circulation affected. Thromboembolism is the commonest type: a thrombus formed in a deep vein, usually of the leg, may break off and travel through the right side of the heart into the pulmonary arteries, causing a pulmonary embolism. Clots forming in the left side of the heart — for example in atrial fibrillation — enter the systemic circulation and may cause stroke, or infarction of the limbs, kidneys, or bowel.

Fat embolism can follow long-bone fractures or orthopedic surgery. Air embolism may result from trauma, surgical procedures, or rapid decompression in divers. Amniotic fluid embolism is a rare but often catastrophic complication of childbirth. A paradoxical embolism occurs when a venous clot crosses a right-to-left shunt, such as a patent foramen ovale, into the arterial circulation.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms depend entirely on the vessel obstructed and the tissue downstream. Pulmonary embolism typically causes breathlessness, chest pain and rapid heart rate; arterial embolism to a limb causes pain, pallor, pulselessness and paresthesia; cerebral embolism presents as stroke. Small emboli may be clinically silent.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis relies on imaging — CT pulmonary angiography, ventilation–perfusion scanning, echocardiography, and Doppler ultrasound of the legs — together with blood tests such as D-dimer. Treatment centres on anticoagulation with heparin and oral anticoagulants to prevent clot extension, and, for large or life-threatening emboli, thrombolytic drugs, catheter-directed removal, or surgical embolectomy. Prevention in hospital patients uses early mobilization, compression stockings, and prophylactic anticoagulation.
