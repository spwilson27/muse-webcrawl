Georges Sagnac (14 October 1869 – 26 February 1928) was a French physicist who lent his name to the Sagnac effect, a phenomenon encountered in interferometry that underlies modern ring laser gyroscopes and fibre-optic gyroscopes.

Work

Sagnac studied at the Sorbonne in Paris, working first on X-rays; he was among the earliest to observe secondary X-ray emission, the phenomenon now called X-ray fluorescence. He is best remembered for his 1913 experiment with a rotating interferometer: when a beam of light is split and sent in opposite directions around a closed loop on a rotating platform, the two beams return with a phase difference proportional to the rate of rotation. Sagnac interpreted the result as evidence for a luminiferous ether, though it is fully consistent with special relativity.

Legacy

The Sagnac effect is now a working principle of precision navigation: ring laser and fibre-optic gyroscopes in aircraft, ships, and spacecraft measure rotation by exploiting the same phase shift Sagnac first observed. The large Michelson–Gale–Pearson experiment of 1925 used the effect to measure the rotation of the Earth itself.
