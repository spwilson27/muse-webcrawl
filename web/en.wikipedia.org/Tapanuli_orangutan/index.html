The Tapanuli orangutan (Pongo tapanuliensis) is a species of orangutan restricted to South Tapanuli in the island of Sumatra, Indonesia. It was described as a distinct species only in 2017 — the first new great ape species named since the bonobo in 1929 — and with fewer than 800 individuals it is the rarest of all great apes.

Discovery and taxonomy

The isolated population in the Batang Toru forest, south of Lake Toba, was noted in the 1930s and rediscovered in 1997, but was long treated as part of the Sumatran orangutan (Pongo abelii). A 2017 study combining genomics, morphology (including a distinctive skull from a male killed in 2013), and behavioural evidence — such as the male "long call" — showed that the Batang Toru population is in fact more closely related to the Bornean orangutan (Pongo pygmaeus) than to its Sumatran neighbours, having diverged some 3.4 million years ago. Its recognition raised the number of living orangutan species to three and of great ape species to eight.

Description and behaviour

Tapanuli orangutans resemble Sumatran orangutans but have frizzier hair, smaller heads, and flatter faces; dominant males have prominent moustaches and flat cheek pads (flanges). They feed heavily on unusual foods such as conifer seeds and caterpillars. Like other orangutans they are largely solitary and arboreal, with slow reproduction — females give birth roughly once every eight to nine years.

Conservation

The species was listed as Critically Endangered on the IUCN Red List in 2017. Its tiny, fragmented range is threatened by habitat loss, hunting, a planned hydroelectric dam in the Batang Toru ecosystem, and the population's division into three sub-populations by roads and development.
