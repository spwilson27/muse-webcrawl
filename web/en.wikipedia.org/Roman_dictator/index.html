Roman dictator

A Roman dictator was an extraordinary magistrate of the Roman Republic appointed by a consul in times of severe crisis, vested with supreme authority for a strictly limited term — traditionally six months or the duration of the emergency, whichever was shorter. Unlike later uses of the word, the classical dictatorship was a constitutional office, and dictators were expected to lay it down as soon as the crisis passed.

Appointment and powers

On the Senate's advice, a consul named (dicere) the dictator, usually an ex-consul, who in turn appointed a deputy, the magister equitum ("master of the horse"). The dictator's imperium overrode that of all other magistrates; 24 lictors attended him, and within Rome his decisions were initially beyond appeal. Each appointment was made for a specific task (causa): conducting a war, holding elections, suppressing sedition, or even ceremonial duties such as driving the annual nail (clavi figendi causa).

History

The office was created, according to tradition, in 501 BC, a decade after the Republic's founding; the first dictator is given as Titus Larcius. Famous dictators include Cincinnatus, who famously returned to his farm after defeating the Aequi in 458 BC, and Fabius Maximus, whose delaying strategy against Hannibal earned him the name "Cunctator" (the Delayer). After the Second Punic War the office fell into abeyance for over a century.

The late Republic

Sulla revived the dictatorship in 82 BC in an unlimited form (dictator legibus scribundis et rei publicae constituendae), using it to restructure the state and proscribe his enemies before resigning in 79 BC. Julius Caesar assumed the dictatorship repeatedly and in 44 BC became dictator perpetuo — dictator in perpetuity — weeks before his assassination. After his death, Mark Antony abolished the office by law, and Augustus later refused the title.
