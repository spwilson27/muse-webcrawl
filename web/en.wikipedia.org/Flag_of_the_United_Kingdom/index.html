The national flag of the United Kingdom is the Union Flag, commonly known as the Union Jack. It combines the heraldic crosses of the kingdoms of England, Scotland, and Ireland: the red cross of St George for England, the white saltire of St Andrew for Scotland, and the red saltire of St Patrick for Ireland. Wales is not represented in the flag, as the principality had been incorporated into the Kingdom of England before the first Union Flag was created.

History

The first Union Flag was created by royal proclamation of James VI and I on 12 April 1606, joining the English and Scottish crosses following the Union of the Crowns. This flag was primarily for use at sea. After the Acts of Union 1707 it became the flag of the Kingdom of Great Britain. The present design dates from the union with Ireland on 1 January 1801, when the saltire of St Patrick was added. The design is deliberately asymmetrical: in the upper hoist quarter, the white saltire of St Andrew lies above the red saltire of St Patrick, giving Scotland precedence; flying the flag upside down is traditionally considered a distress signal and a grave discourtesy.

Terminology

Strictly, "Union Jack" originally referred to the flag when flown from the jack staff of a warship, while "Union Flag" is the general term; the Flag Institute and Parliament have endorsed the popular use of both names in all contexts. Unlike some national flags, the Union Flag is not codified in statute as the national flag of the United Kingdom, holding its status by royal proclamation and customary use.

Use and derivatives

The Union Flag appears in the cantons of the flags of several Commonwealth realms and territories, including Australia, New Zealand, Fiji, and Tuvalu, and forms the basis of the British ensigns (Red, White, and Blue) used at sea. Within the UK it is flown from government buildings on designated days, and it has become a ubiquitous emblem in fashion, design, and popular culture.
