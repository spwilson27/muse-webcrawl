Noun class

In linguistics, a noun class is a particular category of nouns. A noun may belong to a given class because of the characteristic features of its referent, such as sex, animacy, shape, but such designations are often clearly conventional. Some authors use the term "grammatical gender" as a synonym of "noun class", but others consider these different concepts; noun class systems with many classes are found especially in African, Australian Aboriginal and some other language families.

Bantu noun classes

The Bantu languages are famous for their extensive noun class systems. Swahili, for example, is analysed as having around 15 classes, marked by prefixes on the noun and by concord agreement on verbs, adjectives and demonstratives: the m-/wa- class for persons (mtu "person", watu "people"), the ki-/vi- class for things and languages (kiswahili), the m-/mi- class for plants and natural things, and others for animals, abstracts, infinitives and locations. Singular and plural are typically separate classes that pair with each other.

Other systems

The Australian language Dyirbal has four noun classes, famously analysed by George Lakoff in Women, Fire, and Dangerous Things: one class groups men and most animals; another women, fire, water and fighting; another edible plants; and a residue class. Many languages of the Caucasus, such as Chechen, also have multiple noun classes, while Tamil distinguishes rational from non-rational nouns. In Indo-European languages, systems of two or three genders (masculine, feminine, neuter) are comparatively small class systems.

Function

Noun classes organise the vocabulary semantically, drive agreement across the sentence, and can disambiguate reference. Class assignment is mostly inherent to each noun but can sometimes shift meaning — for example, by placing a noun in an augmentative or diminutive class.
