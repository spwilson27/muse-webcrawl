Neo-Confucianism

Neo-Confucianism is a moral, ethical and metaphysical Chinese philosophy influenced by Confucianism, which attained prominence during the Song and Ming dynasties and had a major influence on East Asian social and political organization. It emerged as a reaction both to the dominant influence of Buddhism and Daoism in intellectual life and to the perceived fragmentation of Confucian thought since the Han dynasty.

Neo-Confucian thinkers such as Zhu Xi (1130–1200) built a comprehensive philosophical system around the concepts of li (理, "principle" or "pattern") and qi (氣, "material force"). Zhu Xi's synthesis, known as the Cheng–Zhu school, became the orthodox interpretation of Confucianism and the basis of the civil service examinations from the Yuan dynasty until the early twentieth century.

Origins

The movement's roots lie in the Tang dynasty essays of Han Yu and Li Ao, who sought to revive Confucianism against Buddhist dominance. During the Northern Song, the "Five Masters" — Zhou Dunyi, Shao Yong, Zhang Zai, Cheng Hao and Cheng Yi — laid the metaphysical foundations, drawing on the Yijing and classical texts such as the Great Learning and the Doctrine of the Mean.

Zhu Xi and the Cheng–Zhu school

Zhu Xi systematized earlier thought, holding that all things are composed of li and qi: li is the universal pattern that makes each thing what it is, while qi is the material energy through which pattern is realized. He championed the "investigation of things" (gewu) as the path to moral self-cultivation and elevated four texts — the Great Learning, Doctrine of the Mean, Analects and Mencius — as the "Four Books", the canonical curriculum of Confucian learning.

Wang Yangming and the School of Mind

In the Ming dynasty, Wang Yangming (1472–1529) developed the Lu–Wang school, arguing that li is not external to the mind but inherent within it (xin ji li, "the mind is principle"). He taught the unity of knowledge and action and the doctrine of innate knowledge (liangzhi), holding that moral truth becomes real only when enacted.

Influence

Neo-Confucianism spread throughout East Asia: it became the state ideology of Joseon-dynasty Korea, deeply shaped Tokugawa Japan, and was adopted in Vietnam. It underpinned educational systems, family ethics and governance across the region for centuries, and continues to inform modern discussions of East Asian values and philosophy.
