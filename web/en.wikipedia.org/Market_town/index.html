Market town

A market town is a settlement most often in Europe that obtained by custom or royal charter, in the Middle Ages, a market right, which allowed it to host a regular market; this distinguished it from a village or city. In Britain a market town is a term widely used to describe a town that was granted this right.

History

By the time of the Norman Conquest, markets were a feature of Roman and Saxon settlements. From the 12th century onward, kings and lords granted market charters in large numbers: more than 2,000 markets were established in England between 1199 and 1350. A town needed the charter to hold its market legally and to collect tolls from traders. Market towns typically stood a day's journey — roughly six to ten miles — from their neighbours, so that surrounding farmers could travel in, trade, and return home the same day.

Characteristics

Market towns commonly possess a market square or wide high street accommodating stalls, a market cross, and buildings such as a town hall, corn exchange, coaching inns and shops serving a rural hinterland. Many retain weekly markets, livestock markets or farmers' markets today, and the term is also used in real-estate and tourism descriptions to evoke a small, prosperous rural town with good amenities.

Outside Britain

Analogous statuses existed across Europe: the German Marktgemeinde and Austrian market municipalities, French bourgs, and the market towns (köping) of Sweden. Ireland has many towns whose market functions date to Anglo-Norman charters.
