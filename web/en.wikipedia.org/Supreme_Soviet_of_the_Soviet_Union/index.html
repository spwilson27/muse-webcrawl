Supreme Soviet of the Soviet Union

The Supreme Soviet of the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics was the legislative body of the Soviet Union, formally its highest organ of state authority under the constitutions of 1936 and 1977. In practice, real power rested with the Communist Party of the Soviet Union, and for most of its existence the Supreme Soviet ratified decisions made elsewhere.

Structure

The Supreme Soviet was bicameral. The Soviet of the Union was elected on the basis of population (one deputy per 300,000 people), while the Soviet of Nationalities represented the union republics, autonomous republics and other national units. Both chambers had equal rights, and a law required approval by each. Deputies were elected for four-year (later five-year) terms in elections with a single approved candidate for most of Soviet history.

Powers and practice

Formally, the Supreme Soviet alone could amend the constitution, adopt the state budget and plans, admit new republics, and elect the Presidium and the Council of Ministers. Because it met for only two short sessions a year, its standing Presidium issued decrees (ukases) between sessions; the Chairman of the Presidium served as the USSR's formal head of state, a role held at times by figures such as Mikhail Kalinin and Leonid Brezhnev.

Final years

Under Mikhail Gorbachev's reforms, the 1989 partially free elections created a new Congress of People's Deputies, which elected a reconstituted Supreme Soviet with genuine legislative debate. As republics declared sovereignty, the body's authority drained away, and it ceased to function with the dissolution of the Soviet Union in December 1991.
