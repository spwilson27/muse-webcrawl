Continental rise

The continental rise is a gently sloping accumulation of sediment found at the base of the continental slope, between the slope and the flat abyssal plain of the deep ocean floor. It marks the true geological edge of a continent, where continental crust gives way to oceanic crust beneath a thick wedge of deposited material.

Formation

Sediments eroded from the land are carried across the continental shelf and funneled down submarine canyons by turbidity currents. As these dense, sediment-laden flows slow on reaching the gentler gradient, they drop their load, building overlapping deep-sea fans. Over millions of years the fans merge into the smooth apron of the rise, which can be hundreds of kilometres wide and several kilometres thick off large river systems such as the Amazon, Ganges–Brahmaputra (Bengal Fan) and Mississippi.

Characteristics

The rise typically begins at depths of about 3,000–5,000 metres. Its surface gradient is far gentler than that of the continental slope — commonly cited as less than 1:1000 — and it is shaped by contour currents that redistribute fine sediment along the margin. Not all coasts have a rise: at active margins, such as those ringing much of the Pacific, an oceanic trench takes its place.

Significance

The thick sediments of continental rises are of interest for petroleum geology, since buried organic-rich layers and porous fan deposits can form source rocks and reservoirs. Under the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea, the rise also matters legally: the outer limit of a state's continental shelf is defined partly by the "foot of the continental slope" at the rise's upper edge.
