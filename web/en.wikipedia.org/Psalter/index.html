A psalter is a volume containing the Book of Psalms, often with other devotional material bound in as well, such as a liturgical calendar and litany of the saints. Until the emergence of the book of hours in the later Middle Ages, psalters were the books most widely owned by wealthy lay persons and were commonly used for learning to read. Many psalters were richly illuminated, and they include some of the most stunning surviving examples of medieval book art.

Contents and versions

The core of a psalter is the 150 psalms in one of the Latin versions then in use—principally the Gallican Psalter, Jerome's revision based on the Septuagint, which dominated in the medieval West. Psalters were often divided for liturgical recitation across the week according to the Divine Office (the "three fifties" division was also common). Appendices commonly included the biblical canticles, the Te Deum, the Athanasian Creed, a litany, and prayers for the dead.

Notable psalters

Famous illuminated psalters include the Utrecht Psalter (9th century), with its vigorous pen drawings; the St. Albans Psalter (c. 1130), made for the anchoress Christina of Markyate; the Luttrell Psalter (c. 1320–1340), famous for its marginal scenes of everyday life; and the Très Riches Heures tradition of lavish French manuscripts. In the Byzantine world, "aristocratic" psalters such as the Paris Psalter and marginal psalters like the Chludov Psalter are key monuments of Orthodox art.

Use and influence

In monasteries the entire Psalter was recited weekly in the Office, and monks were expected to know it by heart. For laypeople, owning a psalter was a mark of devotion and status; children learned their letters from its pages, which is why "psalter" and "primer" are historically linked. After the Reformation, metrical psalters—versified psalms for congregational singing, such as the Genevan Psalter and the Scottish Psalter of 1650—became central to Reformed worship, while the Anglican tradition retained the prose psalter in the Book of Common Prayer, in Coverdale's translation.
