Plan of Iguala

Proclaimed24 February 1821
PlaceIguala, New Spain (now Guerrero, Mexico)
AuthorAgustín de Iturbide
ResultIndependence of Mexico

The Plan of Iguala (Spanish: Plan de Iguala), also known as the Plan of the Three Guarantees, was a proclamation issued on 24 February 1821 by Agustín de Iturbide, commander of royalist forces in southern New Spain, during the final phase of the Mexican War of Independence. It proposed a political settlement that united royalists and insurgents and achieved Mexican independence within months.

The Three Guarantees

The plan rested on three guarantees, symbolized by the colours of the flag of the Army of the Three Guarantees: Religion (white) — Roman Catholicism would remain the sole permitted religion; Independence (green) — Mexico would become an independent constitutional monarchy, offering the crown first to Ferdinand VII of Spain or another Bourbon prince; and Union (red) — all inhabitants, Spaniards and American-born alike, would be equal citizens. The army enforcing the guarantees was formed by Iturbide's troops together with the insurgent forces of Vicente Guerrero, who accepted the plan after the embrace of Acatempan.

Outcome

The plan swept the country with little fighting. The last viceroy, Juan O'Donojú, accepted its terms in the Treaty of Córdoba (24 August 1821), and the Army of the Three Guarantees entered Mexico City on 27 September 1821. Spain's refusal to supply a king led to Iturbide's own coronation as Emperor Agustín I in 1822; his empire fell the following year, but independence itself endured.
