Cygnus X-1

Cygnus X-1 (abbreviated Cyg X-1) is a galactic X-ray source in the constellation Cygnus and was the first such source widely accepted to be a black hole. It was discovered in 1964 during a rocket flight and is one of the strongest X-ray sources seen from Earth, producing a peak X-ray flux rivalling that of the Crab Nebula.

The system

Cygnus X-1 belongs to a high-mass X-ray binary system, located about 6,000 light-years from the Sun, that includes a blue supergiant star designated HDE 226868. The two objects orbit each other every 5.6 days. The compact object is too massive — recent measurements put it at roughly 21 solar masses — to be a neutron star, and is therefore identified as a stellar-mass black hole. Material stripped from the supergiant forms an accretion disc around the black hole, heated to millions of degrees and emitting the observed X-rays.

Identification as a black hole

In 1974 Stephen Hawking and Kip Thorne made a famous wager on whether Cygnus X-1 contained a black hole; Hawking conceded the bet in 1990 as the observational evidence became overwhelming. The system also launches relativistic jets, making it a microquasar, and measurements of its distance by radio parallax have refined estimates of its mass and spin, which appears to be close to the maximum possible for a black hole.
