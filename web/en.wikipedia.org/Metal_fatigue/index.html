Metal fatigue

Metal fatigue is the weakening of a metal caused by repeatedly applied loads, leading to progressive and localised structural damage and the growth of cracks. A fatigue failure can occur at stresses well below the ultimate tensile strength or yield strength of the material, because microscopic cracks form at stress concentrators such as surface scratches, sharp corners, holes and inclusions, and grow a little with each cycle until the remaining cross-section can no longer carry the load and the part breaks suddenly.

Stages

Fatigue life is usually divided into crack initiation, in which small cracks nucleate at the surface or at defects; crack propagation, describable by Paris' law relating growth per cycle to the stress-intensity range; and final fracture. The number of cycles to failure at a given stress amplitude is plotted on an S-N (Wöhler) curve. Some metals, notably steels, show an endurance limit below which fatigue life is effectively infinite; aluminium alloys do not, and continue to lose life at low stress.

History

The phenomenon was recognised in the 19th century after railway axle failures; August Wöhler's systematic testing for the railways established the S-N approach. Famous fatigue disasters include the de Havilland Comet airliner break-ups in 1954, traced to cracks starting at square window corners, and the Alexander L. Kielland platform capsizing in 1980.

Design against fatigue

Engineers reduce fatigue damage by avoiding sharp notches, polishing surfaces, using shot peening or other compressive residual stresses, choosing materials and geometries with good fatigue data, inspecting for cracks, and applying cumulative-damage rules such as Miner's rule to variable-amplitude loading.
