Whaling

Whaling is the hunting of whales for their products, principally meat, blubber (rendered into whale oil) and baleen. People have hunted whales since prehistoric times; industrial whaling in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, using steam ships, explosive harpoons and factory ships, drove many whale populations to the brink of extinction.

History

Basque whalers pioneered commercial whaling in the Bay of Biscay from the eleventh century and carried the trade to Newfoundland and Spitsbergen. Dutch, British and American fleets followed, hunting right and sperm whales for oil used in lamps and machinery; New Bedford, Massachusetts, and Nantucket became centres of the American industry, immortalised in Herman Melville's Moby-Dick (1851). The invention of the explosive harpoon gun by Svend Foyn in the 1860s and the development of pelagic factory ships opened the rich Antarctic grounds, where blue, fin, sei and minke whales were taken in vast numbers; over 300,000 blue whales alone were killed in the Southern Ocean.

Regulation

The International Whaling Commission (IWC), established in 1946, eventually set a moratorium on commercial whaling that took effect in 1986. Norway and Iceland continued commercial whaling under objection or reservation, and Japan conducted whaling under a scientific permit programme until it withdrew from the IWC in 2019 and resumed commercial whaling in its own waters. Aboriginal subsistence whaling continues in Alaska, Greenland, Russia and elsewhere, and the Faroe Islands maintain a controversial drive hunt of pilot whales.

Conservation

Some populations, such as humpback whales, have recovered strongly since the moratorium, while others, notably the North Atlantic right whale, remain critically endangered. Whale watching has become an economically significant alternative to whaling in many countries.
