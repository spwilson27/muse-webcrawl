Lord of the Isles

Lord of the Isles (Scottish Gaelic: Triath nan Eilean or Rìgh Innse Gall) is a title of Scottish nobility with origins in the medieval Hebrides. For roughly four centuries, from the 12th century until 1493, the Lords of the Isles ruled the Hebrides and parts of the western Scottish mainland as a largely independent Gaelic-Norse maritime lordship, at times rivalling the power of the kings of Scots and of England. Their court was centred on Finlaggan on the island of Islay.

Origins

The lordship grew out of the Norse-Gaelic Kingdom of the Isles. Its founder is generally taken to be Somerled (died 1164), a warlord of mixed Norse and Gaelic descent who reunified the southern Hebrides and Kintyre and died invading mainland Scotland. Somerled's descendants, the Clann Somhairle, divided his territories among three branches: the MacDougalls of Lorne, the MacRorys, and the MacDonalds of Islay, the last of whom would come to hold the lordship itself.

The MacDonald lordship

John of Islay (Eòin Mac Dòmhnuill), who flourished in the mid-14th century, was the first to style himself Dominus Insularum — Lord of the Isles. Through marriage to Amy MacRory he absorbed the MacRory lands, and his second marriage to Margaret Stewart, daughter of Robert II, eventually brought his descendants the earldom of Ross. At its height the lordship stretched from Lewis in the north to the Isle of Man in the south, and across to Ross and Lochaber on the mainland.

The Lords maintained their own council, chancery and court at Finlaggan, issued charters in Latin and Gaelic, and conducted diplomacy as near-sovereigns. In 1462 John II concluded the Treaty of Westminster-Ardtornish with Edward IV of England, agreeing to help conquer Scotland and divide it — a treasonable bargain whose exposure gravely weakened the lordship.

Forfeiture and aftermath

James III forced John II to surrender Ross and other mainland territories in 1476. After his son Angus Óg's turbulent career and murder in 1490, James IV declared the lordship itself forfeit in 1493, ending four centuries of semi-independent rule. Attempts by Donald Dubh to revive the title in the 1540s failed. The title was later revived as a subsidiary dignity: since 1878 "Lord of the Isles" has been one of the titles borne by the heir apparent to the British throne (as Duke of Rothesay), in right of the Scottish crown.

List of Lords of the Isles

  • Somerled (died 1164), king of the southern Isles
  • Ragnall (Reginald) mac Somairle
  • Donald mac Ragnall, eponym of Clan Donald
  • Angus Mor MacDonald (died c. 1293)
  • Alexander Og MacDonald (died c. 1299)
  • Angus Og MacDonald (died c. 1330), ally of Robert the Bruce
  • John of Islay (died 1386), first styled Lord of the Isles
  • Donald of Islay (died 1423), fought at the Battle of Harlaw (1411)
  • Alexander of Islay, Earl of Ross (died 1449)
  • John II MacDonald, Earl of Ross (1434–1503), last Lord; forfeited 1493

Culture

The lordship was a great patron of Gaelic culture, supporting hereditary families of poets (the MacMhuirichs), harpers, physicians and jurists, and maintaining close ties with Ireland. Its castles — Finlaggan, Dunaverty, Aros, and others — and its war galleys, the birlinns modelled on Norse longships, symbolised a sea-kingdom straddling the Irish Sea world.
