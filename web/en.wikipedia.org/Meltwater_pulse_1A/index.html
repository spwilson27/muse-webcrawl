Meltwater pulse 1A

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Meltwater pulse 1A (MWP1a) was a period of rapid post-glacial sea level rise, of approximately 13.5–20 metres (44–66 ft), beginning about 14,650 years ago and lasting roughly 300–500 years, at the start of the Bølling–Allerød interstadial warming. It is widely considered the most important meltwater pulse of the last deglaciation.

Sea level rise

Estimates of the rate of sea level rise during MWP1a reach 30–60 millimetres per year, far faster than modern rates. Evidence comes from fossil coral reefs in Barbados, Tahiti, and other sites, dated using uranium-thorium methods, which record the drowning of reef terraces as the sea rose.

Source of the meltwater

The origin of the water has been debated. Studies using sea-level fingerprinting suggest a substantial contribution from the Antarctic Ice Sheet in some reconstructions, while others attribute most of the rise to the Laurentide and Fennoscandian ice sheets of the Northern Hemisphere. A significant contribution from Antarctica would imply that hemisphere's ice was highly sensitive to warming at the end of the last glacial period.

Context

MWP1a followed meltwater pulse 1A0 and preceded the Younger Dryas cold reversal. A later, smaller event, meltwater pulse 1B, occurred around 11,300 years ago during the early Holocene.
