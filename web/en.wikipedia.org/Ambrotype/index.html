Ambrotype

The ambrotype (from Greek ambrotos, "immortal") is a photographic process on glass, invented by James Ambrose Cutting in 1854 and popular in the mid-1850s to mid-1860s. An ambrotype is a thin collodion negative on a glass plate which, when backed with a dark material — black paint, cloth, paper or a second dark glass — appears as a positive image. The underexposed negative's silver image appears light against the dark background.

Process and use

The photographer coated a glass plate with collodion containing iodide salts, sensitised it in silver nitrate, exposed it in the camera while still wet, and developed it immediately — the same wet-plate collodion process used for tintypes and negatives. Ambrotypes were cheaper than daguerreotypes, had a soft, matte appearance without the daguerreotype's mirror glare, and were usually sold in small hinged cases, sometimes hand-tinted. They were the dominant portrait format of the American Civil War era until the cheaper tintype and the paper carte de visite displaced them.

Identification

Ambrotypes are one-of-a-kind images on glass; unlike tintypes they will look like a negative when held against a white background if the dark backing is removed. Many survive in family collections as fragile, cased portraits.
