Contactless payment

Contactless payment systems are credit cards, debit cards, key fobs, smart cards, smartphones and other devices, including wearables, that use radio-frequency identification (RFID) or near-field communication (NFC) for making secure payments. The embedded integrated circuit chip and antenna enable consumers to wave the card, fob or handheld device over a reader at the point of sale terminal, typically for low-value transactions without entering a PIN.

Technology

Contactless cards communicate using the ISO/IEC 14443 standard at 13.56 MHz over a range of a few centimetres. Transactions use the EMV framework with dynamic cryptograms, so card data cannot simply be cloned from a single intercepted exchange. Mobile wallets such as Apple Pay, Google Pay and Samsung Pay tokenise card numbers and authenticate the user with biometrics or a passcode.

History and adoption

Early systems included Mobil's Speedpass (1997) and transit fare cards such as London's Oyster card and Hong Kong's Octopus card. Bank-issued contactless cards spread from the late 2000s; transport acceptance, notably by Transport for London from 2014, drove habitual use in the United Kingdom. Transaction limits without PIN verification vary by country — for example, £100 in the UK following increases in 2020 and 2021 — and adoption accelerated during the COVID-19 pandemic for hygiene reasons.

Security

Because no PIN is required below the floor limit, a lost card can be used for small purchases until reported; issuers mitigate this with cumulative spend counters that periodically demand chip-and-PIN verification, and with fraud guarantees. Fears about "skimming" of cards in pockets are generally considered low-risk because of the very short range and encrypted, one-time transaction data.
