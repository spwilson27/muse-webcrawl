Blockbusting

Blockbusting is a business practice in the United States in which real estate agents and speculators induce white homeowners to sell their houses at a loss by implying that racial minorities are moving into the neighbourhood, then resell the properties at much higher prices to minority buyers. The practice was a major driver of racial turnover in American cities in the mid-20th century.

History

Blockbusting flourished after the Second World War as African Americans moved to northern and western cities during the Second Great Migration, facing severe housing shortages in segregated neighbourhoods. Speculators would, for example, hire Black women to push prams through white neighbourhoods or distribute flyers, stoking fears of falling property values, then buy cheaply and sell dear. Whole districts in cities such as Chicago, Detroit, Baltimore and Philadelphia turned over in a few years.

Legal response

The Fair Housing Act of 1968 made blockbusting illegal in the United States, prohibiting representations about the entry of minority residents made for the purpose of inducing a sale. Many states and municipalities enacted their own bans, and some communities adopted anti-solicitation rules and sign bans to slow panic selling.
