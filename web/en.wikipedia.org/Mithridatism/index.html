Mithridatism

Mithridatism is the practice of protecting oneself against poisoning by gradually self-administering non-lethal amounts of a poison, thereby building tolerance or immunity to it. The word derives from Mithridates VI, the king of Pontus (reigned 120–63 BC), who is said to have so feared assassination by poison that he regularly ingested small doses to become immune.

History

According to legend, when Mithridates was defeated by Pompey and attempted suicide by poison, the attempt failed because of his acquired immunity, and he had to order a soldier to kill him with a sword instead. Mithridates also developed a purported universal antidote, mithridatium, a complex preparation of dozens of ingredients that was later elaborated into theriac and remained in pharmacopoeias for nearly two thousand years.

Practice and limits

The approach can work, in varying degrees, for some substances to which the body develops metabolic tolerance or an antibody response — historical examples include arsenic eaters and snake handlers who self-administer venom, and it loosely parallels the principle of venom immunotherapy used to produce antivenom in animals. It is ineffective and dangerous for many toxins: heavy metals and numerous poisons accumulate in the body or damage organs even at sub-lethal doses, and tolerance to one poison confers no protection against others. Modern toxicology regards deliberate self-poisoning as hazardous, though controlled dose escalation underlies legitimate practices such as drug desensitization and allergen immunotherapy.

In culture

The concept recurs in fiction — A. E. Housman's poem "Terence, this is stupid stuff" retells the Mithridates story, and a self-immunizing character appears in The Princess Bride with the fictional iocane powder.
