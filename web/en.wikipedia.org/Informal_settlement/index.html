Informal settlement

An informal settlement is a residential area where housing has been constructed on land that the occupants have no legal claim to, or that does not comply with planning and building regulations. Such settlements — commonly called shanty towns, squatter settlements, favelas (Brazil), barriadas (Peru) or gecekondular (Turkey) — typically lack secure tenure and basic services such as clean water, sanitation, electricity and paved roads.

Informal settlements often grow on marginal land at the edge of rapidly expanding cities.

Causes and scale

Informal settlements arise where rapid urbanization and population growth outpace the supply of affordable formal housing, drawing rural migrants and the urban poor to occupy vacant or marginal land — hillsides, floodplains, railway margins — close to employment. According to UN-Habitat, more than one billion people, roughly a quarter of the world's urban population, live in slums and informal settlements, with the largest numbers in sub-Saharan Africa and South and East Asia.

Conditions and policy responses

Residents face overcrowding, insecure tenure and eviction risk, poor sanitation, contaminated water, and vulnerability to fire, flooding and disease. Policy responses have shifted over time from demolition and forced eviction toward in-situ upgrading — providing infrastructure, services and tenure security in place — and toward sites-and-services schemes and participatory planning. Scholars such as Hernando de Soto have argued that granting property titles can unlock residents' economic potential, though critics dispute the magnitude of such effects.
