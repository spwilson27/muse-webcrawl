Court of Appeal (England and Wales)

Court of Appeal
Established: 1875
Location: Royal Courts of Justice, Strand, London
Composition: Lord and Lady Justices of Appeal
Appeals to: Supreme Court of the United Kingdom
Heads: Master of the Rolls (Civil Division); Lord Chief Justice (Criminal Division)

The Court of Appeal of England and Wales is, together with the High Court and the Crown Court, one of the Senior Courts of England and Wales. It is the highest court within that group, hearing appeals from the High Court and county courts in civil matters, and from the Crown Court in criminal matters. Only the Supreme Court of the United Kingdom sits above it. It was established in 1875 by the Supreme Court of Judicature Act 1873, which reorganized the English court system.

Divisions

The court has two divisions. The Civil Division, headed by the Master of the Rolls, hears appeals in civil cases, including from the High Court, county courts, and certain tribunals. The Criminal Division, headed by the Lord Chief Justice, hears appeals against conviction and sentence from the Crown Court; it may quash convictions, order retrials, and adjust sentences. Its decisions also consider references by the Attorney General on points of law and unduly lenient sentences.

Judges and procedure

The court's judges are the Lord Justices of Appeal and Lady Justices of Appeal, together with ex officio members including the Lord Chief Justice, the Master of the Rolls, and the heads of the High Court divisions. Cases are usually heard by three judges. Permission to appeal is normally required, filtering out unmeritorious cases. Judgments of the Court of Appeal bind lower courts and, in the civil division, the court itself under the doctrine of precedent, subject to limited exceptions laid down in Young v Bristol Aeroplane Co.

Role

As the busiest appellate court in the jurisdiction, the Court of Appeal produces many of the precedents governing English common law, and its civil jurisprudence is influential across the Commonwealth.
