Edward the Black Prince

Edward the Black Prince (15 June 1330 – 8 June 1376), born Edward of Woodstock, was the eldest son of Edward III and heir apparent to the English throne for most of his life. Celebrated in his lifetime as the flower of English chivalry, he commanded at the battles of Crécy and Poitiers during the Hundred Years' War, governed Aquitaine, and died before he could inherit the crown.

Crécy and Poitiers

Edward was knighted on landing in Normandy in 1346 and fought in the front line at the Battle of Crécy at the age of sixteen. A decade later, commanding the English forces in south-western France, he defeated and captured King John II at the Battle of Poitiers (1356), one of the most celebrated victories of the war, which led to the Treaty of Brétigny.

Prince of Aquitaine

Granted Aquitaine as a principality in 1362, Edward ruled from Bordeaux with his wife Joan of Kent. His intervention in Castile in 1367, crowned by victory at Nájera, restored Pedro the Cruel but left him indebted and ill. Mounting taxation and the resumption of war with France eroded his position, and he resigned his principality and returned to England in 1371.

Death and reputation

Edward died in 1376, predeceasing his father by a year; the crown passed to his young son, Richard II. His funerary achievements — helm, shield, gauntlets, and surcoat — are preserved at Canterbury Cathedral. The epithet "Black Prince" is first recorded in the 16th century and may refer to his black armour or shield, or to his severity in war.
