Charon (mythology)

In Greek mythology, Charon or Kharon (Greek: Χάρων) is the ferryman of Hades who carries the souls of the newly deceased across the rivers Styx and Acheron that divide the world of the living from the world of the dead. A son of Erebus and Nyx, Charon is depicted as a surly, aged boatman who demands payment — the obol, a coin placed in the mouth of the dead — and refuses passage to those who cannot pay or who did not receive proper burial, leaving them to wander the banks for a hundred years.

In ancient literature

Charon appears in Aristophanes' comedy The Frogs (405 BC), where Dionysus rows with him, and is developed fully in Virgil's Aeneid (Book 6), where he initially objects to carrying the living Aeneas until the Sibyl shows him the golden bough. Dante made him a demonic figure in the Inferno, beating lagging souls with his oar — a description echoed by Michelangelo's painting of Charon in the Sistine Chapel's Last Judgment. Living heroes who compel passage include Heracles, Orpheus, Theseus, and Odysseus.

Legacy

The custom of Charon's obol persisted in Greek and Roman burial practice, with coins archaeologically attested in graves. In Etruscan religion his counterpart Charun was a menacing demon with a hammer. Pluto's largest moon, discovered in 1978, was named Charon after the ferryman — a name that also echoed the discoverer's wife, Charlene.
