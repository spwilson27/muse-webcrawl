Epicurus

Epicurus (Greek: Ἐπίκουρος, Epíkouros; 341–270 BC) was an ancient Greek philosopher who founded Epicureanism, one of the most influential schools of Hellenistic philosophy. In 307 BC he established his school, "The Garden", in Athens, admitting women and slaves in defiance of convention. Of his voluminous writings — some 300 rolls, according to Diogenes Laërtius — only a few letters and maxims survive intact, though his system is expounded in Lucretius's poem De rerum natura.

Philosophy

Epicurus taught that the goal of life is ataraxia (freedom from mental disturbance) and aponia (absence of bodily pain), achieved through modest pleasures, friendship and philosophical reflection — not the indulgence later suggested by the word "epicurean". His physics was atomistic, following Democritus, with the addition of a slight spontaneous "swerve" (clinamen) in atomic motion to preserve free will. His theology held that the gods exist but take no interest in human affairs, dissolving fear of divine punishment; his famous fourfold remedy, the tetrapharmakos, held that God is not to be feared, death is nothing to us, the good is easy to obtain, and the terrible is easy to endure.

Death

Epicurus died in 270 BC, aged 72, of complications from kidney stones, writing on his final day that he counted it a happy day despite his pain.
