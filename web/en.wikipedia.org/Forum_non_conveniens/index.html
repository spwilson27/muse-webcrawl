Forum non conveniens

Forum non conveniens (Latin for "an inconvenient forum") is a doctrine in the common law of conflict of laws under which a court, although it has jurisdiction over a case, may dismiss or stay the action in favour of a more appropriate court in another jurisdiction. The doctrine aims to prevent forum shopping and to ensure that litigation takes place where it has the closest connection and can be conducted most conveniently and justly.

Doctrine

The doctrine has its roots in Scots law and was developed in the courts of Scotland, England, and the United States. In Spiliada Maritime Corp v Cansulex Ltd (1987), the House of Lords set out the modern English test: the court asks first whether there is another available forum that is clearly or distinctly more appropriate — the "natural forum" with which the action has its most real and substantial connection — and second, whether justice nonetheless requires the case to proceed in England.

In the United States, the Supreme Court's decisions in Gulf Oil Corp. v. Gilbert (1947) and Piper Aircraft Co. v. Reyno (1981) established a balancing of private-interest factors (access to evidence, availability of witnesses, enforceability of judgment) and public-interest factors (court congestion, the local interest in deciding local controversies). Under Piper, a foreign plaintiff's choice of forum receives less deference, and dismissal is proper where an adequate alternative forum exists abroad. Federal cases may instead be transferred between districts under 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a), leaving forum non conveniens mainly for cases that belong in a foreign court or in state systems that retain the doctrine.

Limitations

Within the European Union, the Brussels I Regulation largely displaced the doctrine: in Owusu v Jackson (2005) the European Court of Justice held that a court of a member state could not decline jurisdiction in favour of a non-member state on forum non conveniens grounds. Many civil-law systems do not recognise the doctrine at all, regarding jurisdictional rules as mandatory. Critics also argue that dismissal can disadvantage plaintiffs from developing countries suing multinational defendants, a controversy prominent in litigation following the Bhopal disaster, which was dismissed from United States courts to India on forum non conveniens grounds.
