Color space

A color space is a specific organization of colors: a mathematical model, usually three- or four-dimensional, in which each color is represented by a unique set of numbers (coordinates). Combined with a mapping to an absolute reference space, a color space allows colors to be reproduced consistently in analogue and digital media. Spaces are often defined relative to the CIE 1931 XYZ or CIELAB reference spaces established by the International Commission on Illumination.

Common color spaces

RGB spaces are additive and device-oriented: sRGB is the standard for the web and consumer displays; Adobe RGB (1998) offers a wider gamut for print work; ProPhoto RGB covers nearly all perceivable colors. CMYK is the subtractive space of process printing. Broadcast video uses luma/chroma spaces such as YCbCr, while HSV and HSL reparameterise RGB into intuitive hue, saturation and lightness coordinates for user interfaces.

Gamut and conversion

The range of colors a space or device can represent is its gamut; no practical device reproduces all visible colors. Converting between spaces requires profile-based color management — typically ICC profiles and rendering intents that govern how out-of-gamut colors are clipped or compressed. A color space together with its white point, primaries and transfer function fully determines the meaning of its coordinate values.

History

Quantitative color spaces began with the CIE 1931 experiments, which measured how observers matched colors with mixtures of three primaries and produced the first mathematically defined space. Later perceptually uniform spaces such as CIELAB (1976) attempted to make equal numerical distances correspond to equal perceived differences.
