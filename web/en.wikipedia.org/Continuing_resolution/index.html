Continuing resolution

A continuing resolution (often abbreviated to CR) is a type of appropriations legislation in the United States. An appropriations bill is a bill that appropriates (gives to, sets aside for) money to specific federal government departments, agencies and programs. When Congress and the President cannot agree on regular appropriations by the start of the federal fiscal year on October 1, a continuing resolution may be enacted to provide temporary funding, generally at the previous year's level, for a set period of time.

Operation

Continuing resolutions are usually limited in duration — lasting from a few days to several months — and typically maintain funding at the rate of operations of the prior fiscal year, with restrictions on starting new programs or increasing production rates. They may include "anomalies" adjusting funding for specific programs. If neither regular appropriations nor a continuing resolution is enacted, a funding gap occurs, resulting in a partial government shutdown of non-essential functions.

History

Although intended as an emergency stopgap, continuing resolutions have become a routine feature of the federal budget process; in many years since the modern budget process began in the 1970s, some or all of the government has operated under continuing resolutions for part of the fiscal year. Full-year continuing resolutions, covering the entire fiscal year, have occasionally been enacted when agreement on regular bills proved impossible.
