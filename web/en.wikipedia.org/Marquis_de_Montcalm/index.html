Louis-Joseph de Montcalm

Louis-Joseph de Montcalm-Gozon, Marquis de Montcalm de Saint-Veran (February 28, 1712 – September 14, 1759) was a French soldier best known as the commander of the French forces in North America during the Seven Years' War (whose North American theatre is known in the United States as the French and Indian War). He was mortally wounded at the Battle of the Plains of Abraham outside Quebec City in 1759.

Montcalm, commander of French forces in North America during the Seven Years' War.

Early life and career

Montcalm was born at the Château de Candiac near Nîmes into an old noble family of Rouergue. He entered the French army at the age of fifteen and served in the War of the Polish Succession and the War of the Austrian Succession, in which he was wounded several times and rose to the rank of colonel. He was captured at the Battle of Piacenza in 1746 but exchanged, and later promoted to brigadier and then maréchal de camp.

North America

In 1756 Montcalm was appointed commander of French troops in North America. He won a series of striking victories: the capture of Fort Oswego in 1756, the capture of Fort William Henry in 1757 (followed by a notorious massacre of British prisoners by France's Indigenous allies, an event Montcalm tried to prevent), and the defence of Fort Carillon in 1758, where his small force repulsed a much larger British army under James Abercrombie.

Montcalm's relations with the governor general of New France, the Marquis de Vaudreuil, were strained; the two men disagreed over strategy, and Montcalm regarded the Canadian militia and reliance on Indigenous allies with professional suspicion. In 1759 the British launched a major expedition up the St. Lawrence River under Major-General James Wolfe.

Battle of the Plains of Abraham and death

After a summer-long siege of Quebec, Wolfe's troops scaled the cliffs below the city and formed up on the Plains of Abraham on September 13, 1759. Montcalm engaged them and was decisively defeated in a battle lasting less than half an hour. Both Wolfe and Montcalm were mortally wounded; Montcalm died early the next morning. His reputed dying words — a stoic acceptance that he would not live to see the surrender of Quebec — became part of Canadian and French legend. Quebec capitulated on September 18, and New France passed to Britain with the Treaty of Paris in 1763.

Legacy

Montcalm remains a central figure in the history of Quebec and Canada, and monuments to him and Wolfe stand together in Quebec City. Historians have debated his generalship and his bitter rivalry with Vaudreuil, but his Canadian campaign is generally considered skilful given his limited resources.
