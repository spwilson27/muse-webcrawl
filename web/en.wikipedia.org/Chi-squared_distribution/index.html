Chi-squared distribution

In probability theory and statistics, the chi-squared distribution (also written chi-square or χ² distribution) with k degrees of freedom is the distribution of a sum of the squares of k independent standard normal random variables. It is a special case of the gamma distribution and one of the most widely used probability distributions in inferential statistics.

Definition

If Z₁, …, Zk are independent standard normal variables, then the quantity Q = Z₁² + … + Zk² follows the chi-squared distribution with k degrees of freedom. Its probability density function involves the gamma function, with mean equal to k and variance equal to 2k.

Applications

The chi-squared distribution underlies the chi-squared test for the independence of categorical variables and goodness-of-fit tests, which compare observed frequencies with those expected under a hypothesis. It is also used to construct confidence intervals for a population variance and arises in the sampling distributions behind Student's t-distribution and the F-distribution.

History

The distribution was first described by the German geodist Friedrich Robert Helmert in 1876 and was rediscovered and popularized by Karl Pearson around 1900, whose name is associated with the chi-squared test.
