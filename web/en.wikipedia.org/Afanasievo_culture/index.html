Afanasievo culture

PeriodEarly Bronze Age, c. 3300–2500 BC
RegionMinusinsk Basin and Altai Mountains, southern Siberia; extensions into Mongolia and Xinjiang
EconomyCattle, sheep and horse pastoralism
Genetic profileClosely related to the Yamnaya culture of the Pontic steppe

The Afanasievo culture (also spelled Afanasevo or Afanasyevo) is an early Bronze Age archaeological culture of southern Siberia, dating to roughly 3300–2500 BC. It takes its name from a burial site near the village of Afanasyeva Gora in the Minusinsk Basin of the upper Yenisei River, with sites extending through the Altai Mountains into eastern Kazakhstan, Mongolia (the Chemurchek culture) and possibly Xinjiang.

The Afanasievo people practised stockbreeding of cattle, sheep and horses, with some hunting and possibly limited farming; they lived in small settlements, used wheeled vehicles, worked copper and later bronze, and buried their dead in kurgan-like mounds, often crouched within stone enclosures and accompanied by distinctive pointed-bottom pottery with incised decoration.

Origins and significance

The culture is remarkable for appearing some 2,000 kilometres east of the closely similar Yamnaya culture of the Pontic-Caspian steppe. Ancient DNA studies (2015 onwards) confirmed that Afanasievo individuals were genetically nearly indistinguishable from Yamnaya, indicating a rapid long-distance migration of steppe pastoralists into the Altai around 3000 BC. Because of this, the Afanasievo culture is widely associated with the spread of an early Indo-European language into Central Asia, and has often been linked to the ancestors of the Tocharians, an Indo-European people later attested in the Tarim Basin.
