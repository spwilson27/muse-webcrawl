Spinodal decomposition

Spinodal decomposition is a mechanism by which a single thermodynamic phase spontaneously (i.e., without nucleation) separates into two phases. Decomposition occurs when there is no thermodynamic barrier to phase separation, in contrast to nucleation and growth, where a metastable phase persists until fluctuations create nuclei of the new phase large enough to grow. Spinodal decomposition is a consequence of the instability of a homogeneous mixture inside the spinodal region of a phase diagram, where the curvature of the free energy with respect to composition is negative.

Theory

Within the spinodal, small composition fluctuations are amplified rather than damped, because diffusion effectively runs "uphill" — from regions of lower to higher concentration — lowering the total free energy. The theory was developed by John W. Cahn and John E. Hilliard; the Cahn–Hilliard equation describes the kinetics, predicting the growth of a characteristic wavelength of composition modulation that coarsens over time, producing an interconnected, bicontinuous microstructure with a well-defined length scale.

Examples and applications

Spinodal decomposition occurs in alloys (such as Alnico and Cu–Ni–Fe), in polymer blends, and in glasses; the Vycor process exploits spinodal decomposition of borosilicate glass followed by leaching to make high-silica porous glass. Spinodally decomposed microstructures can give exceptional combinations of strength and magnetic or electrical properties, and the process is used deliberately in materials design and studied in geology in minerals such as feldspars that show exsolution textures (perthite).
