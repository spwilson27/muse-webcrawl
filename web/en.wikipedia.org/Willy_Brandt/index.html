Willy Brandt

Willy Brandt (born Herbert Ernst Karl Frahm; 18 December 1913 – 8 October 1992) was a German politician and statesman who was leader of the Social Democratic Party of Germany (SPD) from 1964 to 1987 and served as Chancellor of the Federal Republic of Germany (West Germany) from 1969 to 1974. He received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1971 for his Ostpolitik, the policy of normalising relations with East Germany and other Eastern Bloc countries.

Early life and exile

Born in Lübeck, Brandt joined socialist youth organisations as a teenager. After the Nazis came to power in 1933 he fled to Norway, adopting the name Willy Brandt as a pseudonym, and later worked as a journalist in Spain and Sweden, opposing Nazism from exile. He returned to Germany after the Second World War.

Political career

Brandt served as governing mayor of West Berlin from 1957 to 1966, winning wide recognition for his calm leadership during the building of the Berlin Wall in 1961. He became foreign minister and vice-chancellor in the grand coalition of 1966, and Chancellor in 1969 after the SPD formed a coalition with the FDP.

Chancellorship and Ostpolitik

Domestically Brandt promised reform under the slogan "dare more democracy." His foreign policy of Ostpolitik produced treaties with the Soviet Union and Poland (1970), the Four Power Agreement on Berlin (1971), and the Basic Treaty with East Germany (1972). His kneeling at the memorial to the Warsaw Ghetto Uprising in December 1970 — the Kniefall von Warschau — became an iconic gesture of German contrition. Brandt resigned in May 1974 after his personal aide Günter Guillaume was unmasked as an East German spy.

Later life

Brandt remained SPD chairman until 1987, led the Socialist International, and chaired the Brandt Commission on international development, whose 1980 report popularised the North–South divide. He welcomed German reunification in 1990 and died in Unkel in 1992.
