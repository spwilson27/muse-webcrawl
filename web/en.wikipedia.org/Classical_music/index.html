Classical music

Classical music generally refers to the formal musical tradition of the Western world, considered distinct from popular and folk music. It is characterized by written notation, complex forms, and a tradition of performance practice transmitted through conservatories and concert institutions. The term, borrowed from classical antiquity, came into use in the early 19th century as the idea of a permanent canon of masterworks took hold.

Periods

The tradition is conventionally divided into periods: Medieval (to c. 1400), Renaissance (c. 1400–1600), Baroque (c. 1600–1750; Bach, Handel, Vivaldi), Classical (c. 1750–1820; Haydn, Mozart, Beethoven), Romantic (c. 1820–1910; Schubert, Brahms, Wagner, Tchaikovsky), and the diverse modern and contemporary eras from 1900 onward, encompassing modernism, minimalism, and spectralism.

Characteristics and institutions

Classical works are usually composed for specific combinations of instruments—symphony orchestra, string quartet, solo piano—and in forms such as the symphony, concerto, sonata, opera, and oratorio. Performers interpret detailed scores, balancing fidelity to the text with personal expression. Major institutions include orchestras, opera houses, chamber ensembles, and festivals; training is centred on technique, theory, and repertoire.

Reception

Classical music occupies a small but durable share of the recording and streaming market, and debates continue about audience demographics, diversity of the canon, and the role of new composition. Recordings and broadcasts have made the repertoire globally accessible, and the tradition has been adopted and transformed by composers worldwide.
