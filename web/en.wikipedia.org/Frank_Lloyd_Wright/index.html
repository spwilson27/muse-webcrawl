Frank Lloyd Wright

Frank Lloyd Wright (1867–1959), regarded as the greatest American architect of his era.

Frank Lloyd Wright (8 June 1867 – 9 April 1959) was an American architect, designer, writer and educator. He designed more than 1,000 structures over a career spanning 70 years and pioneered the concept of organic architecture — buildings harmonised with their environment and with humanity — exemplified by Fallingwater (1939), partly designated, along with seven other Wright works, a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2019.

Career

Wright was born in Richland Center, Wisconsin, and worked in Chicago under Louis Sullivan, whom he called his "Lieber Meister". In the 1900s he developed the Prairie School style of houses — low, horizontal, open-planned, with overhanging eaves — seen in the Robie House (1910). His Unity Temple (1908) was a landmark of early modernism in reinforced concrete.

After personal scandal and the tragic 1914 fire and murders at his Taliesin estate in Wisconsin, Wright's career revived dramatically in the 1930s with Fallingwater, the Johnson Wax Headquarters, and his affordable Usonian houses for middle-class families. Late masterworks include Taliesin West in Arizona and the Solomon R. Guggenheim Museum in New York, completed the year of his death.

Legacy

The American Institute of Architects recognised Wright in 1991 as "the greatest American architect of all time". His ideas — open plans, integration of building and landscape, and total design of interiors and furniture — profoundly shaped modern architecture worldwide.
