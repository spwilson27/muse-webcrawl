Balance wheel

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A balance wheel, or balance, is the timekeeping device used in mechanical watches and some clocks, analogous to the pendulum in a pendulum clock. It is a weighted wheel that rotates back and forth, returned toward its centre position by a spiral torsion spring, known as the balance spring or hairspring. Together, the wheel and spring form a harmonic oscillator whose period of oscillation is (nearly) constant, regulating the release of the escapement and thus the rate of the timepiece.

Operation

Most modern balances oscillate at 18,000 to 36,000 beats per hour (2.5–5 Hz). With each swing, the wheel allows the gear train to advance by a fixed amount through the escapement, and in turn receives a small impulse that replaces energy lost to friction. Robert Hooke and Christiaan Huygens independently introduced the balance spring in the 1670s, dramatically improving the accuracy of watches and making practical portable timekeeping possible; the priority dispute between them is famous in the history of science. Later refinements included temperature-compensated bimetallic rims, timing screws, overcoil hairsprings, and alloys such as Nivarox and silicon that resist magnetism and temperature change.

Errors and adjustment

The rate of a balance is affected by temperature (through the elasticity of the spring and the diameter of the wheel), position (gravity acting on imperfect poise), magnetism, and the amplitude of oscillation. Watchmakers adjust rate by moving a regulator that changes the effective length of the hairspring, or, in free-sprung balances, by turning screws on the rim that change the wheel's moment of inertia. Despite the dominance of quartz movements since the 1970s, the balance wheel remains the heart of mechanical watchmaking and a symbol of the craft.
