Dividend

A dividend is a distribution of profits by a corporation to its shareholders, after which the stock exchange decreases the price of the stock by the dividend to remove volatility. When a corporation earns a profit, its board of directors may reinvest the profit in the business (retained earnings) or distribute it. Dividends are usually paid in cash, but may also be issued as shares ("scrip dividends") or other property.

Payment process

The board declares a dividend with a set record date and payment date. The ex-dividend date is the day on which the stock starts trading without the right to the upcoming dividend. Dividend yield is the annual dividend divided by the share price; the payout ratio is the share of net income paid as dividends.

History

The Dutch East India Company paid regular dividends from 1610, sometimes in spices, and is credited with the first recorded dividend payments. Dividend policy became a central topic in corporate finance: Miller and Modigliani's dividend irrelevance theorem (1961) argued that in perfect markets payout policy does not affect firm value, while signalling theories hold that dividend changes convey information about managers' expectations. Lintner's 1956 study found that firms smooth dividends, adjusting them only gradually toward target payout ratios.

Taxation

Dividends are taxed as income in most jurisdictions, sometimes at preferential rates or with imputation credits (franking) to offset corporate tax, as in Australia. In the United States, "qualified dividends" are taxed at capital-gains rates.

Alternatives

Share repurchases (buybacks) are an alternative way to return cash to shareholders, and have grown relative to dividends since the 1980s, especially in the US.
