Mini

The Mini is a small, two-door, four-seat car, developed by the British Motor Corporation (BMC) and its successors, from 1959 through 2000. Minus a brief hiatus, original Minis were built for four decades and sold during six, from the last year of the 1950s into the last year of the 20th century, in fastback, hatchback, saloon, estate, van and pickup-bodied variants.

Design and development

The Mini was designed in response to fuel rationing caused by the 1956 Suez Crisis. Leonard Lord, head of BMC, tasked Alec Issigonis with designing a proper miniature car that could carry four adults within a tiny footprint. Issigonis mounted the four-cylinder A-series engine transversely, drove the front wheels through a gearbox built into the sump, and used compact rubber-cone suspension designed by Alex Moulton. The space-saving layout placed the wheels at the corners and allowed 80 per cent of the car's floorpan to be used for passengers and luggage.

Performance versions

Formula One racing car designer John Cooper, a friend of Issigonis, saw racing potential in the little car. The resulting Mini Cooper and Cooper S were giant-killers, winning the Monte Carlo Rally in 1964, 1965 and 1967, and dominating British saloon car racing.

Cultural impact

The Mini became an icon of 1960s British popular culture, bought by film stars, musicians and royalty alike, and starred in the 1969 film The Italian Job. In 1999 it was voted the second most influential car of the 20th century, behind the Ford Model T. Production of the classic Mini ended in October 2000, by which time some 5.3 million had been built. The name was revived by BMW for the modern Mini Hatch range launched in 2001.
