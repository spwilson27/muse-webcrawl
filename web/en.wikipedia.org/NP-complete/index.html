NP-complete

In computational complexity theory, a problem is NP-complete when it is both in NP and NP-hard. The set of NP-complete problems is often cited as the most important complexity class, because a polynomial-time algorithm for any one of them would yield polynomial-time algorithms for all problems in NP — resolving the famous P versus NP problem.

Definition

A decision problem is NP-complete if (1) it belongs to NP — its solutions can be verified in polynomial time — and (2) every problem in NP can be reduced to it in polynomial time. The concept was introduced by Stephen Cook in his 1971 paper "The complexity of theorem-proving procedures", which proved that the Boolean satisfiability problem (SAT) is NP-complete; Leonid Levin independently developed the theory in the Soviet Union.

Examples

Richard Karp's 1972 paper showed 21 classic problems to be NP-complete, including 3-SAT, the clique problem, Hamiltonian path, vertex cover, and subset sum. Later additions include the travelling salesman decision problem, graph colouring, bin packing, and many scheduling, routing, and puzzle problems (such as generalised Sudoku and Minesweeper consistency).

Dealing with NP-complete problems

Because no efficient exact algorithm is known, practitioners use approximation algorithms, heuristics, parameterised algorithms, and exponential algorithms that are fast enough in practice, such as modern SAT solvers.
