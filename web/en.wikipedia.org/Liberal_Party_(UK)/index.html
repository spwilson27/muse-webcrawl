The Liberal Party was one of the two major political parties in the United Kingdom, along with the Conservative Party, in the 19th and early 20th centuries. Founded in 1859 from a coalition of Whigs, Peelites and Radicals, it was in power for much of the period between 1868 and 1922 under leaders including William Ewart Gladstone, the Earl of Rosebery, Henry Campbell-Bannerman, H. H. Asquith and David Lloyd George.

History

Gladstonian liberalism

Gladstone's governments (1868–74, 1880–85, 1886, 1892–94) pursued free trade, fiscal retrenchment, disestablishment of the Irish Church, secret ballots, and elementary education reform. The party split in 1886 over Irish Home Rule, when Joseph Chamberlain's Liberal Unionists broke away.

New Liberalism and the People's Budget

After a decade out of office, the Liberals won a landslide in 1906. The governments of Campbell-Bannerman and Asquith, with Lloyd George as Chancellor of the Exchequer, introduced old-age pensions (1908), labour exchanges and National Insurance (1911). Lloyd George's "People's Budget" of 1909 provoked a constitutional crisis resolved by the Parliament Act 1911, which curtailed the veto of the House of Lords.

Decline

The First World War split the party between Asquith and Lloyd George, whose coalition won the 1918 "coupon election." Labour displaced the Liberals as the main party of the left, and by the 1950s the party was reduced to a handful of MPs. A modest revival under Jo Grimond and Jeremy Thorpe followed, and in 1981 the Liberals entered the SDP–Liberal Alliance. In 1988 the party merged with the Social Democratic Party to form the Liberal Democrats. A small continuation Liberal Party was refounded by members who opposed the merger.

Ideology

Classical Liberalism emphasised individual liberty, free markets and limited government; the "New Liberalism" of the Edwardian era, influenced by thinkers such as T. H. Green and L. T. Hobhouse, accepted state intervention to secure real freedom through welfare provision.
