Type: Ocean circulation
Driven by: Surface wind stress
Key features: Ekman transport, Sverdrup balance, western boundary currents

Wind-driven circulation

Wind-driven circulation is ocean circulation forced primarily by the stress exerted by surface winds on the sea surface. It dominates the upper ocean, producing the great subtropical and subpolar gyres, the Ekman layer transport in the surface, and intense western boundary currents such as the Gulf Stream and the Kuroshio.

Ekman dynamics

Friction between wind and water generates an Ekman spiral in the upper tens of metres: surface water moves at an angle to the wind, and the net transport over the Ekman layer is directed 90° to the right of the wind in the Northern Hemisphere (to the left in the Southern). Convergence and divergence of this transport push water down (Ekman pumping) or draw it up (Ekman suction), driving vertical motion into the interior ocean.

Sverdrup balance and gyres

In the ocean interior away from boundaries, the flow approximately satisfies the Sverdrup balance, in which the curl of the wind stress is balanced by meridional motion through the effect of the variation of the Coriolis parameter with latitude (the beta effect). In the subtropical North Atlantic and North Pacific, negative wind-stress curl drives a broad equatorward interior flow, which must be returned in narrow, fast western boundary currents — a phenomenon called western intensification, explained theoretically by Henry Stommel (1948) and Walter Munk (1950).

Significance

Wind-driven circulation redistributes heat, salt, nutrients and dissolved gases across ocean basins, shapes regional climate, and interacts with the deeper thermohaline circulation. It is a central topic of physical oceanography and is simulated in every ocean general circulation model.
