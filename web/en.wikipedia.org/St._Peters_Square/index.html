St. Peter's Square

St. Peter's Square, Vatican City, seen from the dome of the basilica.

St. Peter's Square (Italian: Piazza San Pietro) is a large plaza located directly in front of St. Peter's Basilica in Vatican City, the papal enclave in Rome. It was designed by Gian Lorenzo Bernini between 1656 and 1667, under the direction of Pope Alexander VII, as an appropriate forecourt where the greatest possible number of people could see the Pope give his blessing.

Design

The square consists of two parts: a trapezoidal space immediately before the basilica, framed by converging colonnades, and a vast elliptical space beyond. The ellipse is encircled by two semicircular colonnades of four rows of Tuscan columns, comprising 284 columns and 88 pilasters, surmounted by 140 statues of saints. Bernini likened the colonnades to the welcoming arms of the Church embracing the faithful.

The obelisk and fountains

At the centre of the ellipse stands an ancient Egyptian obelisk, originally erected in the Circus of Nero and moved to its present position in 1586 under Pope Sixtus V by the engineer Domenico Fontana. Two fountains flank the obelisk, one by Carlo Maderno (1613) and a matching one by Bernini (1675). Markers in the pavement indicate the centres of the colonnades, from which the four rows of columns align into a single row.

Use

The square is the setting for papal audiences, major liturgical celebrations and canonizations, and can hold hundreds of thousands of people. The Apostolic Palace overlooks it, and the Pope's weekly Angelus address is given from a window there. Visitors usually enter from the Via della Conciliazione, the broad avenue constructed in the 20th century. The square lies near other great gathering spaces such as London's St Paul's Cathedral precincts in the geography of European pilgrimage.
