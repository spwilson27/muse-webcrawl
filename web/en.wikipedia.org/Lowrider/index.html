Lowrider

A lowrider is a customised car with a lowered body, characterised by elaborate paintwork, plush interiors, wire wheels and, most distinctively, hydraulic or air-bag suspension systems that allow the driver to raise and lower the car and make it "hop" or dance. Lowrider culture originated among Mexican-American (Chicano) communities in the southwestern United States in the years after World War II, particularly in Los Angeles, and its motto is often given as "low and slow" — the opposite ethos to hot-rodding.

History and culture

From the late 1940s, young Chicano drivers in East Los Angeles began lowering cars such as Chevrolet sedans by cutting coils or weighting the trunk, dressing them with candy-apple and metal-flake paint, pinstriping and murals. In the late 1950s Ron Aguirre's X-Sonic Corvette used hydraulics adapted from aircraft parts, allowing instant ride-height adjustment — partly in response to a 1958 California law against cars riding too low. Car clubs, cruising boulevards like Whittier Boulevard, and Lowrider magazine (founded 1977) built a distinctive cultural movement blending Mexican-American identity, Catholic imagery and pachanga aesthetics.

Influence

Lowriding spread across the United States and internationally — to Japan, Brazil and Europe — and influenced hip-hop culture, fashion and art. Anti-cruising ordinances targeted the scene in several cities, but lowrider shows, museums and community events have kept the tradition a celebrated expression of Chicano artistry.
