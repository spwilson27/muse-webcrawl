Front projection effect

In-camera visual effects technique for filmmaking

The front projection effect is an in-camera visual effects technique in filmmaking in which actors perform in front of a previously filmed or photographed background projected onto a highly reflective screen, so that foreground and background are combined optically during the original photography rather than in post-production.

How it works

A projector is aligned with the camera through a two-way mirror (beam splitter): the camera photographs the scene through the mirror while the projector casts the background image onto the same axis. The screen is made of a retroreflective material such as Scotchlite, which returns light almost entirely toward its source. Because the projected light bounces straight back to the camera lens, the background appears brightly and evenly lit, while the dimmer spill on the actors is overwhelmed by the studio lighting — and the actors' shadows fall out of the camera's line of sight.

History and use

The technique was developed in the 1950s and refined for 2001: A Space Odyssey (1968), whose "Dawn of Man" sequences used large 8×10-inch transparencies projected onto a 110-foot screen. Front projection offered sharper results and interactive lighting compared with rear projection, and was used in films such as Superman (1978), whose flying scenes employed the related Zoptic system. It has largely been superseded by blue-screen and digital compositing, though modern LED-volume stages are a conceptual descendant.
