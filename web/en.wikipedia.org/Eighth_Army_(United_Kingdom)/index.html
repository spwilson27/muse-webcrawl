Eighth Army (United Kingdom)

The Eighth Army was a field army formation of the British Army during the Second World War, famous for its role in the North African campaign.

The Eighth Army was a field army formation of the British Army during the Second World War. It was made up of units from many Commonwealth nations, including Australia, India, New Zealand, South Africa and the United Kingdom, as well as Free French and Polish forces. The army gained fame during the Western Desert campaign in North Africa and the subsequent Italian campaign.

Formation and the desert war

The Eighth Army was formed in September 1941 in Egypt from the Western Desert Force. Its first commander was Lieutenant-General Sir Alan Cunningham, followed by Neil Ritchie and Claude Auchinleck. It fought Erwin Rommel's Afrika Korps in a series of sweeping desert battles including Operation Crusader and the First Battle of El Alamein.

Montgomery and El Alamein

In August 1942 General Bernard Montgomery took command. His victory at the Second Battle of El Alamein (October–November 1942) proved the turning point of the North African war. The Eighth Army then advanced some 2,400 km across Libya into Tunisia, fighting at the Mareth Line and Wadi Akarit before the Axis surrender in May 1943.

Italy

The army landed in Sicily in July 1943 (Operation Husky) and then on the Italian mainland, advancing up the eastern side of the peninsula under Montgomery and later Oliver Leese and Richard McCreery. It fought at the Sangro, in the breaking of the Gothic Line, and in the final spring offensive of 1945.

Legacy

The "Desert Rats" nickname attached to its 7th Armoured Division, and the army's multi-Commonwealth character made it one of Britain's most celebrated formations of the war. Veterans are commemorated by the Eighth Army Veterans Association and memorials at El Alamein.
