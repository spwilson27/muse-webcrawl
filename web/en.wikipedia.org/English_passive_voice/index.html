English passive voice

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The passive voice in English is a grammatical "voice" in which the subject of a clause is the patient — the recipient of the action — rather than the agent performing it. In the active sentence "The cat chased the mouse", the subject is the chaser; in the passive counterpart "The mouse was chased (by the cat)", the subject is the one chased, and the agent may be omitted entirely or relegated to an optional by-phrase.

Formation

The standard English passive is formed with a form of the auxiliary be (or, especially in informal registers, get) plus the past participle of the main verb: "The window was broken", "He got fired". Passives are available in all tenses and aspects ("has been seen", "is being watched", "will be announced"). Typically only transitive verbs passivise, though prepositional passives extend the construction to some intransitives: "This bed has been slept in". Double passives and passives of ditransitives ("She was given a prize") are a particular feature of English.

Use

The passive is used when the patient is the topic of discourse, when the agent is unknown, obvious or unimportant — as in much scientific writing — or, less creditably, to obscure responsibility, as in the notorious "Mistakes were made". Style guides once commonly condemned the passive wholesale; modern guidance, such as that of linguists writing on usage, recognises it as an indispensable information-packaging device, making up a substantial minority of verbs in informative prose.

History and criticism

Passives have existed in English since Old English. Nineteenth- and twentieth-century prescriptivists frequently misidentified other constructions as passive and warned against its overuse; corpus linguists have shown the passive's frequency varies systematically by genre, being highest in academic and official prose.
