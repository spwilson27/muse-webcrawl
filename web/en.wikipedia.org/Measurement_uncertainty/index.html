Measurement uncertainty is the doubt that exists about the result of any measurement. Formally, it is a non-negative parameter characterizing the dispersion of the values attributed to a measurand, based on the information used. No measurement is exact, so a complete measurement result pairs a value with an uncertainty that indicates the range within which the true value is believed to lie.

Concepts

The modern framework, codified in the Guide to the Expression of Uncertainty in Measurement (GUM, first published 1993), distinguishes random and systematic effects but evaluates all uncertainty components as standard deviations. Type A evaluations use statistical analysis of repeated observations; Type B evaluations use other information such as calibration certificates, instrument specifications and experience.

Propagation

When a result is computed from several input quantities, their standard uncertainties are combined using the law of propagation of uncertainty, accounting for sensitivity coefficients and correlations. The combined standard uncertainty is multiplied by a coverage factor, typically k = 2, to give an expanded uncertainty corresponding to a coverage probability of about 95 percent.

Significance

Uncertainty statements are essential for comparing results, demonstrating traceability to national standards, and deciding whether products meet specifications, for example in calibration laboratories, analytical chemistry and manufacturing quality control. Uncertainty is distinct from error: error is a single (unknowable) difference, while uncertainty is a quantified range.
