Soundex is a phonetic algorithm for indexing names by sound, as pronounced in English. The goal is for homophones to be encoded to the same representation so that they can be matched despite minor differences in spelling. The algorithm mainly encodes consonants; a vowel will not be encoded unless it is the first letter.

The algorithm

A Soundex code consists of a letter followed by three digits. The first letter of the name is retained; subsequent letters are mapped to digits by group (b, f, p, v → 1; c, g, j, k, q, s, x, z → 2; d, t → 3; l → 4; m, n → 5; r → 6), vowels and h, w, y are dropped, adjacent duplicate codes are merged, and the result is padded with zeros or truncated to four characters. Thus "Robert" and "Rupert" both encode as R163.

History and use

Patented by Robert C. Russell and Margaret King Odell in 1918 and 1922, Soundex became famous through its use by the United States Census Bureau to index census returns, notably the 1880–1930 censuses, enabling genealogists to find names despite inconsistent spelling. Variants and successors include the refined Soundex, NYSIIS, and Metaphone, designed to handle non-English names and finer distinctions.
