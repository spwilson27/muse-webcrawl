Neolithic Revolution
Periodc. 10,000 BCE onwards
Key developmentTransition to agriculture and sedentism
Early centresFertile Crescent, China, Mesoamerica, Andes, New Guinea
Term coinedV. Gordon Childe (1920s–30s)

Early farming settlements transformed human society.

The Neolithic Revolution, or the (First) Agricultural Revolution, was the wide-scale transition of many human cultures during the Neolithic period from a lifestyle of hunting and gathering to one of agriculture and settlement, making an increasingly large population possible. The term was popularised by the archaeologist V. Gordon Childe.

Sequence and causes

Farming began independently in several regions after the end of the last ice age. In the Fertile Crescent, wheat, barley, sheep, and goats were domesticated by around 9000 BCE; rice and millet in China; maize in Mesoamerica; and potatoes in the Andes. Proposed causes include the warmer, more stable Holocene climate, population pressure, and coevolution between humans and domesticates. Sedentary Natufian communities in the Levant harvested wild cereals before full domestication.

Consequences

Agriculture produced food surpluses that supported larger populations, specialised crafts, social stratification, and eventually cities, writing, and states. It also brought costs: poorer and narrower diets, heavier labour, new zoonotic diseases, and greater inequality, leading some scholars to call it a mixed blessing in human history. Farming spread from its centres by migration and adoption, reaching Europe over several millennia.
