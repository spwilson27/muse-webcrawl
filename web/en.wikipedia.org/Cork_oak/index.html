Cork oak

Scientific name: Quercus suber

Family: Fagaceae

Native range: Southwest Europe and northwest Africa

Conservation status: Least Concern

The cork oak (Quercus suber), commonly called the cork tree, is a medium-sized evergreen oak tree in the section Quercus sect. Cerris. It is the primary source of cork for wine bottle stoppers and other uses, such as cork flooring and cricket ball cores. It is native to southwest Europe and northwest Africa, in the western Mediterranean Basin.

Description

The tree typically grows to about 20 metres (66 ft) tall, though in its native environment it is often shorter. Its most distinctive feature is its thick, rugged bark, rich in suberin, which can be stripped from the tree roughly every nine to twelve years without killing it. The leaves are 4 to 7 cm long, dark green above and paler below, with toothed margins. The acorns are 2 to 3 cm long, maturing in about six months.

Ecology

Cork oak forests, known as montado in Portugal and dehesa in Spain, are important ecosystems supporting endangered species such as the Iberian lynx and the Spanish imperial eagle. The trees prefer sandy, lime-free soils and mild winters with moderate rainfall.

Cork production

Portugal produces about half of the world's cork, followed by Spain. A tree must be roughly 25 years old before its bark can be harvested for the first time; the first two harvests generally yield lower-quality cork, while later strippings produce the prime material used for stoppers. A single tree can be harvested over a lifespan of 150 to 250 years, yielding many tonnes of cork over its life.
