Empiricism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In philosophy, empiricism is an epistemological view that states that knowledge comes only or primarily from sensory experience and empirical evidence. It is one of several competing views within epistemology, along with rationalism and skepticism. Empiricism emphasises the central role of empirical evidence in the formation of ideas, rather than innate ideas or traditions. Empiricists may argue, however, that traditions arise due to relations of previous sense experiences.

Historically, empiricism was associated with the "blank slate" concept (tabula rasa), according to which the human mind is "blank" at birth and develops its thoughts only through experience.

British empiricism

The classic empiricists of early modern Britain were John Locke, George Berkeley and David Hume. Locke's Essay Concerning Human Understanding (1689) argued against innate ideas and held that all ideas derive from sensation and reflection. Berkeley radicalised the doctrine into idealism — "to be is to be perceived" — denying the existence of mind-independent material substance. Hume drew skeptical conclusions: our beliefs in causation, the external world and the self rest, he argued, on custom and habit rather than reason, and his analysis of induction posed a problem that philosophy has wrestled with ever since. Earlier figures such as Francis Bacon, who championed inductive method in science, and Thomas Hobbes, were also foundational to the tradition.

Later developments

In the 19th century, John Stuart Mill systematised empiricism in his logic, and phenomenalist versions were developed by Ernst Mach and others. In the 20th century, the logical positivists of the Vienna Circle tried to ground meaningful statements in verification by experience, while W. V. O. Quine's naturalised epistemology rejected the sharp analytic–synthetic distinction and portrayed knowledge as a web of belief answerable to experience as a whole. Empiricism remains the default outlook of the scientific method: hypotheses must be tested against observation and experiment.

Related positions

Empiricism contrasts with rationalism, which assigns a greater role to innate knowledge and deductive reason (as in Descartes, Spinoza and Leibniz), though most philosophers combine elements of both. Kant's critical philosophy famously attempted a synthesis, holding that while all knowledge begins with experience, it does not follow that it all arises from experience.
