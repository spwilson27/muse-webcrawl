Ethnic nationalism

Ethnic nationalism, also known as ethnonationalism, is a form of nationalism in which the nation and nationality are defined in terms of ethnicity, with emphasis on ethnic self-determination and on real or imagined common descent, language, and culture. It is conventionally contrasted with civic nationalism, in which the nation is defined by shared citizenship and political values regardless of ethnicity.

Characteristics

Historian Anthony D. Smith described ethnic nations as named human populations with shared ancestry myths, histories, and cultures, having an association with a specific territory and a sense of solidarity. Ethnic nationalism tends to define membership by birth and blood (jus sanguinis), and its states or movements may privilege the titular ethnic group in law and policy, sometimes at the expense of minorities.

History

The concept draws on Herder's celebration of the Volk and its language, and the German romantic nationalism of the 19th century, in contrast to the revolutionary civic nationalism associated with France and the United States (a distinction systematised by Hans Kohn). Ethnic nationalism drove many state-formation and secession movements in Central and Eastern Europe, and after the Cold War it underlay conflicts in the former Yugoslavia and the Caucasus.

Criticism

Critics argue that the ethnic/civic dichotomy is oversimplified—all nationalisms blend cultural and political elements—and that ethnic definitions of the nation invite exclusion, discrimination, and irredentism. Supporters reply that ethnic nationhood can protect small cultures and legitimate collective self-determination.
