Child welfare

Child welfare is a broad field of services designed to protect children and encourage family stability. The primary missions include investigating alleged child abuse and neglect, providing services to families who need assistance in the protection and care of their children, arranging for children to live with relatives or foster families when they cannot live safely at home, and arranging adoption for children who cannot return home.

Scope of services

Child welfare systems typically encompass child protection investigations, family preservation and support services, foster care, kinship care, residential and group care, independent living services for older youth, and adoption services. Public agencies administer most formal services, often contracting with private nonprofit organisations.

History

Organised child welfare developed in the 19th century with orphanages, "orphan trains" in the United States, and societies for the prevention of cruelty to children, such as the New York Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Children (founded 1874). The 20th century brought public responsibility through laws mandating reporting, juvenile courts, and eventually foster care as the preferred alternative to institutions. The UN Convention on the Rights of the Child (1989) framed the modern rights-based approach.

Approaches internationally

Countries differ in emphasis: Anglo-American systems are often described as "child protection" oriented, focusing on risk and investigation, while many continental European and Nordic systems emphasise "family service" — offering support to families before problems escalate. All systems wrestle with balancing child safety against family autonomy.
