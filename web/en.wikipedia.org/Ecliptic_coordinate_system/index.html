Ecliptic coordinate system

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The ecliptic coordinate system is a celestial coordinate system that uses the ecliptic — the apparent path of the Sun across the celestial sphere over the course of a year — as its fundamental plane. It is especially useful for describing the positions and motions of Solar System bodies, which move near the ecliptic plane.

Coordinates

The system's two angular coordinates are ecliptic longitude (λ), measured eastward along the ecliptic from the March (vernal) equinox through 360°, and ecliptic latitude (β), measured perpendicular to the ecliptic from 0° at the plane to +90° at the north ecliptic pole and −90° at the south ecliptic pole. A third coordinate, distance, may be added for full three-dimensional positions.

Reference frames

Because the equinox precesses, ecliptic coordinates are specified for a particular epoch, such as J2000.0. The system may be geocentric (measured from Earth) or heliocentric (from the Sun). The north ecliptic pole lies in the constellation Draco, near the Cat's Eye Nebula.

Conversion

Ecliptic coordinates convert to equatorial coordinates through rotation by the obliquity of the ecliptic, ε ≈ 23.44°. The system was the dominant coordinate framework in ancient and medieval astronomy, used by Ptolemy in the Almagest, and remains standard in planetary science and astrology's zodiacal divisions.
