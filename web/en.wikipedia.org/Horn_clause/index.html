Horn clause

Horn clauseField: mathematical logic, logic programming
Named after: Alfred Horn
Property: at most one positive literal

In mathematical logic, a Horn clause is a clause (a disjunction of literals) with at most one positive literal. Horn clauses are named after the logician Alfred Horn, who pointed out their significance in 1951. They form the logical foundation of logic programming languages such as Prolog.

Types

A Horn clause with exactly one positive literal is a definite clause, written as an implication — a rule "head if body". A clause with no positive literal is a goal clause (a query or denial), and a clause with a single positive literal and no negative literals is a fact. For example, the rule "if X is mortal when X is human" has one positive conclusion and one negative condition in clause form.

Properties

Satisfiability of Horn formulas is decidable in linear time, and Horn clauses enjoy a least-model property: the intersection of models of a set of definite clauses is again a model, yielding a unique minimal model computed by forward chaining. Resolution restricted to Horn clauses underlies SLD resolution in Prolog and the database query language Datalog.
