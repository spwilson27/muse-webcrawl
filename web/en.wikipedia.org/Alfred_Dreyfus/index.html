Alfred Dreyfus (9 October 1859 – 12 July 1935) was a French artillery officer of Jewish ancestry who became the central figure of the Dreyfus affair, the political scandal that convulsed the French Third Republic from 1894 until his full exoneration in 1906.

The affair

In 1894 a cleaning woman recovered from the German embassy in Paris a torn memorandum (the bordereau) offering French military secrets. Suspicion fell on Dreyfus, whose handwriting was judged similar. Convicted of treason by a court-martial on flimsy and partly forged evidence, he was publicly degraded — his insignia torn off and sword broken — and deported for life to the penal colony of Devil's Island in French Guiana.

In 1896 Lieutenant Colonel Georges Picquart discovered that the real traitor was Major Ferdinand Walsin Esterhazy, but the army high command suppressed the evidence and forged documents to protect the original verdict. The case split France between "Dreyfusards" and anti-Dreyfusards and became a battleground over antisemitism, nationalism and the army's honour. Émile Zola's open letter J'Accuse…! (1898) reignited the campaign for a retrial.

Rehabilitation

A second court-martial at Rennes in 1899 again found Dreyfus guilty "with extenuating circumstances"; he accepted a presidential pardon but continued to fight for full vindication. On 12 July 1906 the Court of Cassation annulled the verdict and Dreyfus was reinstated in the army, promoted and made a knight of the Legion of Honour. He served in the First World War and retired as a lieutenant colonel.
