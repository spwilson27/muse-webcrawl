David Vetter

David Phillip Vetter (21 September 1971 – 22 February 1984) was an American boy born with severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID), a hereditary disease that leaves the immune system almost entirely nonfunctional. To protect him from germs, he lived from infancy inside a sterile plastic isolator, becoming famous worldwide as the "bubble boy." His case became a landmark in immunology and medical ethics.

Life in the bubble

Vetter's older brother had died of SCID, and doctors at Texas Children's Hospital in Houston prepared a sterile environment before David's birth, placing him in the isolator seconds after delivery. Everything entering the bubble — food, clothing, books, and a specially built NASA spacesuit that let him leave the hospital briefly as a small child — had to be sterilized. He was baptized with sterilized holy water and for years communicated with his family only through the isolator's plastic walls and attached gloves.

Bone marrow transplant and death

In October 1983 Vetter received a bone marrow transplant from his sister Katherine, in the hope that new treatment methods (treating the marrow to remove T cells) would succeed. The transplant initially seemed promising, but the marrow contained traces of the dormant Epstein–Barr virus, undetectable by the screening of the time, which triggered uncontrolled B-cell lymphoproliferation. Vetter left the isolator for treatment and died on 22 February 1984, aged twelve, of Burkitt's lymphoma.

Legacy

Vetter's case advanced the scientific understanding of the immune system and helped pave the way for newborn screening and gene therapy for SCID. It also raised enduring ethical questions about experimental treatment, quality of life, and the psychological effects of extreme isolation on children.
