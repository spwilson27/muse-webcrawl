Hunger Winter

Dutch Hunger Winter
Hongerwinter, winter of 1944–45
A famine in the German-occupied Netherlands during the Second World War; some 4.5 million people affected.

The Dutch Hunger Winter (Dutch: Hongerwinter) was a famine that took place in the German-occupied Netherlands, especially in the densely populated western provinces, during the winter of 1944–45, near the end of World War II.

Causes

After the failure of Operation Market Garden in September 1944, the Dutch government-in-exile called a railway strike to aid the Allied advance. The German authorities retaliated with an embargo on food transport to the western Netherlands. When the embargo was partially lifted, an exceptionally harsh winter froze canals and waterways, halting barge traffic. Official rations in the western cities fell below 500 kilocalories per day by early 1945.

Effects

An estimated 18,000–22,000 people died of starvation and related causes. City dwellers made "hunger trips" to the countryside to barter valuables for food; tulip bulbs and sugar beets were widely eaten. Relief came with Allied operations Manna and Chowhound — food drops by British and American bombers in late April 1945 — and liberation in May.

Legacy in science

The Hunger Winter has been intensively studied by epidemiologists because its sharp beginning and end created a natural experiment: people conceived or born during the famine show elevated rates of obesity, cardiovascular disease and schizophrenia decades later, and even epigenetic differences — evidence that prenatal nutrition can have lifelong effects.
