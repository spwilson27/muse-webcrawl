Nontrinitarianism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Nontrinitarianism is a form of Christianity that rejects the mainstream Christian doctrine of the Trinity — the teaching that God is one being in three coeternal, consubstantial persons: the Father, the Son (Jesus Christ) and the Holy Spirit. Nontrinitarians hold a variety of differing views about the nature of God, Jesus and the Holy Spirit, and have existed from Christianity's earliest centuries to the present day.

History

Early alternatives to what became Trinitarian orthodoxy include adoptionism, which held that Jesus was adopted as God's Son; modalism (Sabellianism), which taught that Father, Son and Spirit are modes of a single person; and Arianism, which held that the Son was created by and subordinate to the Father — condemned at the Council of Nicaea in 325. During the Reformation, anti-Trinitarian currents re-emerged among Socinians in Poland and Transylvania and in early Unitarian churches. Michael Servetus, who rejected the Trinity, was executed in Geneva in 1553.

Contemporary groups

Modern nontrinitarian denominations and movements include Unitarian Universalists (whose Christian roots run through historical Unitarianism), Jehovah's Witnesses, who teach that Jesus is God's created firstborn son and the archangel Michael; the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, whose doctrine of the Godhead holds Father, Son and Holy Ghost to be three distinct beings united in purpose; Oneness Pentecostals, who hold a modalistic view; and groups such as the Christadelphians and Iglesia ni Cristo. Because they reject a doctrine most Christian churches consider definitional, nontrinitarian groups are often regarded as outside the Christian mainstream by Trinitarian churches, though all of these movements self-identify as Christian.
