Dirac delta function

The Dirac delta is represented as an infinitely tall spike at the origin with unit area.

In mathematics and physics, the Dirac delta function (δ function) is a generalized function (or distribution) introduced by physicist Paul Dirac. It models an idealized point mass or point charge: it is zero everywhere except at a single point, where it is infinite, yet its total integral over the whole line equals one.

Definition and properties

The delta function is informally defined by

δ(x) = 0 for x ≠ 0;   δ(0) = ∞;   ∫ δ(x) dx = 1

Its defining practical property is the sifting property: for any well-behaved function f,

∫ f(x) δ(x − a) dx = f(a)

Strictly, δ is not a function but a distribution, made rigorous in the theory of distributions of Laurent Schwartz. It can be obtained as a limit of sequences of ordinary functions — such as narrow Gaussian or rectangular pulses — whose width tends to zero while the area stays equal to one.

Applications

The delta function is fundamental in engineering and physics. It is the impulse response kernel in linear systems theory: the response of a system to δ is its Green's function, and any input can be decomposed into a continuum of impulses. In quantum mechanics it appears in position eigenstates and potential models (the delta potential well). In probability, it represents a distribution concentrated at a single value, and in signal processing it models ideal sampling, related to the Dirac comb.

History

Ideas resembling the delta function appear in the work of Fourier, Cauchy, and Kirchhoff, and Oliver Heaviside's operational calculus used impulse functions informally. Dirac introduced δ in his 1927 work and his 1930 book The Principles of Quantum Mechanics. Mathematicians initially objected to its informality until distribution theory provided a rigorous foundation in the 1940s.
