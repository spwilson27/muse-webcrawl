Riff

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A riff is a repeated chord progression or refrain in music, typically found in classical music, popular music, jazz, and rock. It is a pattern or melody that is often the structural basis of a piece: riffs are the "main hook" of many songs, repeated throughout as a foundation over which solos and vocals are layered. The term is probably an abbreviation of "rhythmic figure" or a derivation from "refrain".

In jazz and blues

In jazz, big-band arrangers such as those writing for Count Basie's orchestra built entire compositions from short riff patterns played by the horn sections, often in call-and-response. Blues music standardized the twelve-bar progression, over which guitarists developed signature riffs. Charlie Christian and other early electric guitarists brought riff-based playing to prominence in the 1940s.

In rock music

Rock and heavy metal are defined by the guitar riff: examples include the opening figures of "Smoke on the Water" by Deep Purple, "Whole Lotta Love" by Led Zeppelin, "Back in Black" by AC/DC, and "Satisfaction" by the Rolling Stones. A distinction is drawn between a riff, which supports the song rhythmically, and a lick, a stock melodic phrase used in solos.

Related terms

An ostinato is the classical-music equivalent: a persistently repeated figure. A vamp is a short repeated accompaniment figure, often at the start of a jazz or popular song, played until a cue. In film and advertising, a short distinctive motif may instead be called a sting or a hook.
