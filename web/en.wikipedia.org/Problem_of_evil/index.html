Problem of evil

The problem of evil is the philosophical question of how to reconcile the existence of evil and suffering with an omnipotent, omnibenevolent, and omniscient God. There are currently differing definitions of these concepts. The problem was first formulated by the Greek philosopher Epicurus, and has been discussed extensively since. Besides the philosophy of religion, the problem of evil is also important to the fields of theology and ethics.

Formulations

The logical problem of evil claims that the existence of God and the existence of evil are logically incompatible. The evidential problem argues that the amount, kinds, and distribution of suffering — such as the agony of animals in natural disasters ("natural evil") as distinct from wrongdoing by agents ("moral evil") — make God's existence improbable, even if not impossible. J. L. Mackie and William Rowe are prominent modern proponents of these arguments.

Defences and theodicies

A defence seeks only to show that God and evil are compatible; Alvin Plantinga's free will defence argues that God could not create free creatures who never choose evil, and that even omnipotence cannot do the logically impossible. A theodicy attempts to give God's actual reasons: the Augustinian tradition traces evil to the Fall and the misuse of free will; the Irenaean or "soul-making" theodicy developed by John Hick holds that suffering is necessary for moral and spiritual growth; process theology limits God's power; sceptical theism holds that humans cannot grasp God's reasons.

Related issues

Related debates concern the problem of hell, the suffering of non-human animals, and parallels in other religions, as well as non-theistic framings of suffering in Buddhism and other traditions.
