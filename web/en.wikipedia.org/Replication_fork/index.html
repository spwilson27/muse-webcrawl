Replication fork

The replication fork is a structure that forms within the nucleus during DNA replication. It is created by helicases, which break the hydrogen bonds holding the two DNA strands together in a double helix. The resulting structure has two branching "prongs", each one made up of a single strand of DNA, that serve as templates for the leading and lagging strands.

Structure and function

At the fork, the replisome — a complex of proteins including helicase, primase, DNA polymerases, single-strand binding proteins and topoisomerases — unwinds the parental duplex and synthesises new DNA. DNA polymerase can only synthesise in the 5′→3′ direction, so the leading strand, whose template runs 3′→5′ toward the fork, is made continuously. The lagging strand is synthesised discontinuously as short Okazaki fragments, each begun by an RNA primer and later joined by DNA ligase after primer removal.

Fork progression and problems

Replication forks typically emanate bidirectionally from an origin of replication until they meet a fork travelling in the opposite direction. Obstacles such as DNA damage, tightly bound proteins, transcription complexes and difficult-to-replicate sequences can stall forks; cells respond with fork-protection and checkpoint pathways (involving ATR kinase and proteins such as BRCA1 and BRCA2) that stabilise the fork and permit restart. Unrepaired fork collapse generates double-strand breaks and contributes to genome instability in cancer.
