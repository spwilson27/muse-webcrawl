Cliché

A cliché is an element of an artistic work, saying, or idea that has become overused to the point of losing its original meaning or effect, even to the point of being trite or irritating. Typically a cliché was originally fresh or striking, but repeated use drained it of force. The word is borrowed from French, where in printers' jargon a cliché was a stereotype plate — a block cast to reproduce a page or image again and again — a metaphor for mechanical repetition.

Usage

The term is used across literature, film, and everyday speech. Stock phrases such as "at the end of the day" or "think outside the box", stock characters such as the absent-minded professor, and stock plot devices such as the deus ex machina may all be called clichés. What counts as clichéd is partly a matter of audience and era: an image fresh to one generation may be threadbare to the next.

Attitudes toward clichés

Writing teachers generally advise avoiding clichés in favour of precise, original language, arguing that ready-made phrases let writers substitute familiarity for thought. Defenders note that clichés survive because they are efficient and vivid, and that in speech they function as social shorthand. Some writers deliberately subvert clichés, or use them ironically, while movements such as postmodernism have played with them as material. Linguists also observe that many idioms dismissed as clichés are simply conventional expressions of the language.
