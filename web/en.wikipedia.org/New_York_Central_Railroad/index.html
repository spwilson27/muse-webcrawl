The New York Central Railroad (reporting mark NYC) was one of the largest railroads in the United States, operating from 1853 until 1968 across the Great Lakes and Mid-Atlantic regions, with routes extending into Canada. Its "Water Level Route"—following the Hudson River and the shore of Lake Erie—offered a low-gradient path between New York City and Chicago in competition with the Pennsylvania Railroad. The Central was famous for its luxury passenger trains, its Hudson and Niagara steam locomotives, and its construction and ownership of Grand Central Terminal in Manhattan.

History

The railroad was formed in 1853 by the consolidation of ten short lines across New York State between Albany and Buffalo. Cornelius Vanderbilt gained control in the 1860s, merging it with his Hudson River Railroad to create through service between New York City and the Great Lakes. The system expanded by merger and lease to include the Lake Shore and Michigan Southern, the "Big Four" (Cleveland, Cincinnati, Chicago and St. Louis), and lines into Canada. The Vanderbilt interests controlled the railroad for generations.

Passenger service

The 20th Century Limited, inaugurated in 1902 between New York and Chicago, was among the most famous trains in the world, celebrated for its red carpet treatment at Grand Central. The Empire State Express and the streamlined fleet of the 1930s–40s, styled by Henry Dreyfuss, epitomised the golden age of American rail travel. After the Second World War, competition from motorcars and airlines eroded passenger traffic despite modernisation under president Robert R. Young and his successor Alfred E. Perlman.

Merger and legacy

In 1968 the New York Central merged with its great rival, the Pennsylvania Railroad, to form the Penn Central, which became the largest bankruptcy in American history to that point in 1970. Much of the Central's network passed to Conrail in 1976 and was later divided between CSX and Norfolk Southern. The Hudson and Harlem commuter lines survive under Metro-North Railroad, and Grand Central Terminal remains one of New York's great landmarks.
