Calcitonin

Calcitonin
Type: peptide hormone
Length: 32 amino acids
Produced by: parafollicular (C) cells of the thyroid
Gene: CALCA (chromosome 11)

Calcitonin is a 32-amino-acid peptide hormone produced in humans primarily by the parafollicular cells (C cells) of the thyroid gland. It is involved in regulating levels of calcium and phosphate in the blood, acting broadly in opposition to parathyroid hormone (PTH). It was discovered in the early 1960s by Douglas Harold Copp and colleagues, who first named it after its calcium-lowering effect.

Function

Calcitonin lowers blood calcium by inhibiting the activity of osteoclasts, the cells that break down bone and release calcium into the circulation, and by reducing calcium reabsorption in the kidneys, increasing its excretion in urine. Its secretion is stimulated by rising blood calcium concentrations. In humans its physiological role appears modest compared with that of PTH and vitamin D, since patients who have the thyroid removed do not develop major calcium abnormalities.

Clinical uses

Pharmaceutical calcitonin — usually salmon calcitonin, which is more potent and longer-acting than the human form — has been used to treat Paget disease of bone, hypercalcaemia, and postmenopausal osteoporosis, as well as pain from osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures. Its use has declined with the availability of bisphosphonates.

Diagnostic use

Serum calcitonin is a tumour marker for medullary thyroid carcinoma, a cancer arising from the C cells themselves, and is used in screening families with multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2 (MEN 2).
