Microcredit

Microcredit is the extension of very small loans (microloans) to impoverished borrowers who lack collateral, steady employment or a verifiable credit history, and therefore cannot access conventional banking. It is the best-known part of microfinance, which also includes microsavings, microinsurance and money transfer services.

Origins

The modern microcredit movement is associated with Muhammad Yunus, who began lending small sums to poor villagers in Bangladesh in the 1970s and founded the Grameen Bank in 1983. Yunus and the bank jointly received the Nobel Peace Prize in 2006 "for their efforts to create economic and social development from below." Earlier precedents include the Irish loan funds of the eighteenth century and Friedrich Raiffeisen's credit cooperatives in Germany.

Methods

Microcredit programs often lend to groups — typically groups of women — who guarantee one another's loans, substituting social collateral for physical collateral. Repayment rates in well-run programs have historically been high. Institutions range from nonprofits to regulated microfinance banks such as BRAC, ASA and BancoSol.

Criticism and evidence

Microcredit has been criticized for high interest rates, borrower over-indebtedness (as in the 2010 Andhra Pradesh crisis), and limited transformative impact. Randomized evaluations across several countries have generally found modest effects: microcredit expands business activity for some borrowers but does not, on average, dramatically raise incomes or reduce poverty. Supporters respond that financial access has intrinsic value and benefits particular groups such as existing entrepreneurs.
