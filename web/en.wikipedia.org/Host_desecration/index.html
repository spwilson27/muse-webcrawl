Host desecration is the alleged act of sacrilege against a consecrated Eucharistic host. In medieval and early modern Europe, accusations of host desecration — a form of blood libel — were levelled principally against Jews, on the claim that they stole and tortured consecrated wafers believed to be the body of Christ. Such accusations repeatedly triggered trials, executions and massacres.

Background

After the Fourth Lateran Council (1215) affirmed transubstantiation, devotion to the consecrated host intensified, and stories of "bleeding hosts" spread. The first major accusation followed the Paris case of 1290; major episodes included Deggendorf (1338), Brussels (1370) and Passau (1477), often followed by expulsions and the establishment of pilgrimage cults.

Consequences

Accused individuals were typically tortured into confessions and burned, and entire communities were expelled or destroyed. Historians treat the accusations as fabrications driven by religious anti-Judaism, economic motives and social tensions. The cults associated with several cases were formally suppressed by the Catholic Church in the 20th century.

Modern usage

The term is also used for actual acts of vandalism or protest involving consecrated hosts, which remain criminal offences in some jurisdictions and a source of controversy over blasphemy laws.
