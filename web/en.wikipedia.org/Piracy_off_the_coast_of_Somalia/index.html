Piracy off the coast of Somalia

Piracy off the coast of Somalia occurs in the Gulf of Aden, Guardafui Channel, Somali Sea, and Somali territorial waters and other surrounding places. It initially posed a threat to international fishing vessels and to the delivery of food aid shipments by the World Food Programme. Since 2005, many international organizations have expressed concern over the rise in piracy acts, which increased transportation costs and interrupted the delivery of food aid. The problem peaked around 2010–2011 and declined sharply after 2012.

Origins

Following the collapse of the Somali central government in 1991 under Siad Barre, foreign vessels began illegal fishing in Somali waters and dumping toxic waste. Somali fishermen, lacking a coast guard, organized themselves as volunteer patrols to protect their waters; some of these groups gradually shifted to demanding "fines" and then ransoms from foreign vessels, and eventually to attacking shipping generally.

Scale of the problem

At the height of the crisis, in 2011, Somali pirates launched 237 attacks and hijacked 28 ships, holding hundreds of hostages. Ransoms rose from an average of about US$150,000 per ship in 2005 to more than US$5 million by 2010; the total economic cost to the shipping industry, including rerouting, insurance, security, and naval deployments, was estimated in the billions of dollars annually.

Notable incidents included the hijacking of the supertanker Sirius Star in 2008, the Maersk Alabama hijacking in April 2009—resolved by United States Navy SEAL snipers—and the seizure of the sailing yacht Quest in 2011, in which four American hostages were killed.

International response

The United Nations Security Council adopted a series of resolutions, beginning with Resolution 1816 in 2008, authorizing foreign navies to enter Somali territorial waters and use "all necessary means" to repress piracy. Multinational forces included the European Union Naval Force (Operation Atalanta), NATO's Operation Ocean Shield, and the US-led Combined Task Force 151. China, India, Japan, Russia, and other states also deployed warships independently.

Shipping companies adopted Best Management Practices: higher speeds, evasive maneuvering, razor wire, citadels, and embarked private armed security teams. By 2013, successful hijackings had fallen to near zero; no large commercial vessel was successfully hijacked for several years.

Prosecution and imprisonment

Captured pirates were prosecuted in Kenya, the Seychelles, Mauritius, and in courts in Europe and the United States under transfer agreements. At its peak, more than a thousand suspected pirates were held in prisons in some twenty countries.

Recent developments

A resurgence of attacks was reported in late 2023 and 2024, including the hijacking of the bulk carrier Ruen in December 2023—the first successful Somali pirate hijacking of a commercial vessel since 2017—amid reduced naval presence and regional instability. International naval forces responded with renewed patrols and rescues.

Impact on Somalia

Piracy brought ransom money into coastal towns such as Eyl, Hobyo, and Harardhere, distorting local economies, but it also drew international attention to illegal fishing and waste dumping. Development programs, the rebuilding of Somali security forces, and alternative livelihood schemes were promoted as long-term remedies.
