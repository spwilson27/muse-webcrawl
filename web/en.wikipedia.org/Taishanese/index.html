Taishanese

Taishanese (Chinese: 台山話; pinyin: Táishānhuà), also known as Toishanese or Hoisanese, is a Yue Chinese dialect spoken in Taishan (Toishan), a county-level city in Jiangmen, Guangdong province, China. It is part of the Siyi (Sei Yap, "Four Counties") group of Yue dialects.

History

Taishanese was historically the most widely spoken Chinese variety in North America. From the mid-19th century, large numbers of emigrants from Taishan and the surrounding Siyi counties left for the United States, Canada, and other destinations, driven by poverty, the Taiping Rebellion era turmoil, and opportunities created by the California Gold Rush and the construction of the transcontinental railroads. As a result, Taishanese (along with closely related Siyi varieties) became the dominant spoken Chinese language in American Chinatowns well into the mid-20th century, before immigration patterns shifted and Cantonese and later Mandarin became more prominent.

Phonology

Like other Yue varieties, Taishanese is tonal. It preserves many features of Middle Chinese, including final consonants -p, -t, -k, -m, -n, and -ng. It differs noticeably from standard Cantonese in pronunciation: for example, many syllables that begin with certain initials in Cantonese are pronounced differently, and the tone system, while related, is not identical. Mutual intelligibility with Cantonese is partial; speakers often describe Taishanese as sounding like a "country" relative of Cantonese.

Usage today

Taishanese remains widely spoken in Taishan itself and in overseas communities with roots in the Siyi region, including Chinatowns in San Francisco, New York, Vancouver, and Toronto. However, its use among younger generations of the diaspora is declining as Mandarin and standard Cantonese gain ground through education and media.
