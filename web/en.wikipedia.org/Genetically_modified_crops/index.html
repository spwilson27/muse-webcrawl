Genetically modified crops

Genetically modified crops (GM crops) are plants used in agriculture whose DNA has been modified using genetic engineering methods. In most cases the aim is to introduce a new trait that does not occur naturally in the species, such as resistance to certain pests, diseases or herbicides, or improved nutritional content.

History and extent

The first commercially grown GM crop was the Flavr Savr tomato, approved in the United States in 1994. Large-scale planting began in 1996, led by herbicide-tolerant soybean and insect-resistant maize and cotton expressing the Bt toxin from the bacterium Bacillus thuringiensis. By the late 2010s GM crops were grown on roughly 190 million hectares in about 29 countries, with the United States, Brazil, Argentina, Canada and India the largest producers. Soybean, maize, cotton and canola account for nearly all of the area.

Traits and examples

Commercial traits are dominated by herbicide tolerance (notably glyphosate tolerance) and insect resistance, often "stacked" in a single variety. Other examples include virus-resistant papaya, which saved the Hawaiian papaya industry from ringspot virus; Golden Rice, engineered to produce beta-carotene as a source of vitamin A; and drought-tolerant maize.

Regulation and debate

GM crops are regulated differently worldwide: the United States regulates the product's characteristics, while the European Union applies a process-based, precautionary framework and requires labelling. Scientific consensus assessments by bodies such as the US National Academies and the World Health Organization hold that approved GM foods are no riskier than conventional foods, while debate continues over environmental effects, herbicide use, corporate control of seed, and consumer choice.
