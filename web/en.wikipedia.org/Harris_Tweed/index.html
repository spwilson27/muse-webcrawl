Harris Tweed (Scottish Gaelic: Clò na Hearadh) is a tweed cloth that is handwoven by islanders at their homes in the Outer Hebrides of Scotland, finished in the Outer Hebrides, and made from pure virgin wool dyed and spun in the Outer Hebrides. This definition is protected by the Harris Tweed Act 1993, which strictly outlines the conditions in which the cloth can genuinely be called Harris Tweed.

History

Crofters in the Hebrides had long woven heavy woollen cloth (called clò mòr, "the big cloth") for their own use. In the 1840s, Lady Catherine Herbert, widow of the Earl of Dunmore, promoted the cloth among the aristocracy as durable sporting wear, and demand from the landed gentry transformed the craft into an industry. The Harris Tweed Association was formed in 1909 to protect the product, adopting the famous Orb trademark — a Maltese cross surmounted by a crown — in 1910. Litigation and the adaptation to single-width Hattersley looms reshaped production during the 20th century.

Production

Production today follows a chain established by law and custom: island mills dye, blend, card and spin pure virgin wool into yarn; the yarn is delivered to independent self-employed weavers, who weave it on treadle looms in their own loom sheds; the cloth returns to the mills for finishing (washing, cropping, pressing) and is stamped with the Orb mark by the Harris Tweed Authority only after inspection. Patterns traditionally reflect the Hebridean landscape — herringbones, checks and flecks in earth greens, browns, peat and heather tones.

Uses and culture

Harris Tweed is used for jackets, suits, caps, bags, shoes and furnishings, and has been adopted by fashion houses and brands from Chanel to Nike. Output fluctuates with fashion cycles but the cloth remains a flagship product of the Hebridean economy and a symbol of protected geographical craft.
