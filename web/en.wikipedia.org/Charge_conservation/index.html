Charge conservation


Charge conservation: the total electric charge in an isolated system never changes.

Charge conservation is the principle that the total electric charge in an isolated system never changes. The net quantity of electric charge — the amount of positive charge minus the amount of negative charge in the universe — is always conserved. Charge can be transferred between bodies and can be created or destroyed only in equal-and-opposite pairs, as when an electron and a positron are produced or annihilate.

Continuity equation

Locally, charge conservation is expressed by the continuity equation ∂ρ/∂t + ∇·J = 0, relating the charge density ρ and the current density J. In circuit theory the same idea appears as Kirchhoff's current law: the sum of currents entering a node equals the sum leaving it.

Theoretical basis

By Noether's theorem, charge conservation follows from the gauge invariance of electromagnetism: the invariance of physical laws under local phase changes of the quantum wavefunction implies a conserved current whose charge is electric charge. Experiments have tested charge conservation to extraordinary precision; the electron is stable against charge-violating decay on timescales exceeding 1028 years.

History

The principle emerged in the 18th century from the work of experimenters such as Benjamin Franklin, who observed that rubbing processes create equal amounts of positive and negative charge, and was placed on a firm mathematical footing with Maxwell's equations in the 19th century.
