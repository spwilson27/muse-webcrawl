Downshifting

Downshifters often trade income for time and a simpler life

Downshifting (also known as downscaling or the simple living movement, related to voluntary simplicity) is a social behaviour or trend in which individuals live simpler lives in order to escape from what is sometimes called the obsessive materialism of industrial societies. Individuals who downshift seek to reduce stress, working hours and consumption, accepting a lower income in exchange for more leisure time, personal fulfilment and often a lower environmental impact.

History and motivations

The term gained currency in the 1990s, influenced by books such as Duane Elgin's Voluntary Simplicity (1981) and Juliet Schor's The Overworked American (1992). Commentators link the trend to the "rat race" of long working hours and consumer debt, and to earlier philosophies of simple living from Epicurus and Diogenes to Henry David Thoreau. Typical motivations include spending more time with family, escaping job stress and burnout, pursuing hobbies, improving health, and reducing one's carbon footprint.

Forms of downshifting

Downshifting ranges from modest steps — cutting working hours, going part-time, working from home or changing to a less pressured job — to radical changes such as moving from a city to the countryside, taking up smallholding or homesteading, or early semi-retirement. Related concepts include the FIRE movement (financial independence, retire early), slow living, degrowth and career breaks or sabbaticals.

Criticism

Critics argue that downshifting is a privilege of higher earners who have assets and safety nets, that it can reduce pension savings and career prospects, and that widespread downshifting could reduce tax revenues and economic output. Others respond that even small reductions in work and consumption can benefit health and wellbeing.
