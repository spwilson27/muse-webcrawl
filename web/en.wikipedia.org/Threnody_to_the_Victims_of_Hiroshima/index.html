Threnody to the Victims of Hiroshima (Polish: Tren – ofiarom Hiroszimy) is a musical composition for 52 string instruments by the Polish composer Krzysztof Penderecki, written in 1960. One of the most famous works of the post-war avant-garde, it is a landmark of sonorism, using extended instrumental techniques and massive tone clusters to produce an intensely expressive soundscape.

Composition and title

Penderecki composed the piece at the age of 26. Its original title was simply 8'37", referring to its duration in the manner of John Cage. After hearing an early performance, Penderecki decided to dedicate it to the victims of the atomic bombing of Hiroshima, saying that he wanted the threnody — a song of lamentation — to stand as a firm protest against war. The retitled work won the UNESCO International Rostrum of Composers prize in 1961 and brought Penderecki to international prominence.

Music

The score employs unconventional notation, including proportional notation in which passages are timed in seconds rather than bars. Players are instructed to use extreme techniques: playing between the bridge and tailpiece, bowing on the tailpiece, tapping the instruments, playing the highest possible pitches, and producing quarter-tone clusters by massing adjacent strings. The result moves from near-silence through siren-like glissandi to overwhelming cluster chords, often interpreted as evoking air-raid sirens, screams and the chaos of the bombing — associations Penderecki embraced through the dedication even though the music was not originally programmatic.

Reception and use in media

The Threnody has become a staple of the contemporary repertoire and has been widely used in film and television, including excerpts in The Exorcist (1973), The Shining (1980) and Children of Men (2006), and in the television series Twin Peaks: The Return (2017), cementing its reputation as music of dread and catastrophe.
