Educational accreditation

Educational accreditation is a quality assurance process under which services and operations of educational institutions or programs are evaluated and verified by an external body to determine whether applicable and recognised standards are met. If standards are met, accredited status is granted by the appropriate agency. In most countries accreditation is performed by governmental or quasi-governmental bodies; in the United States it is carried out by private, non-profit accrediting agencies overseen by the Department of Education and the Council for Higher Education Accreditation.

PurposeQuality assurance in education
TypesInstitutional, programmatic (specialised)
ProcessSelf-study, peer review, site visit, periodic renewal
ConsequencesEligibility for funding, transfer of credit, licensure

Accreditation certifies that an institution meets recognised standards.

United States

In the US, institutional accreditors (historically called "regional" accreditors) accredit entire colleges and universities, while programmatic accreditors review individual fields such as nursing, engineering (ABET), law (ABA), and medicine (LCME). Accreditation is voluntary in principle, but it is a prerequisite for students to receive federal financial aid under Title IV, so in practice it is essential for most institutions.

Process

A typical cycle involves an institutional self-study against the accreditor's standards, a site visit by a peer-review team, a decision to grant, renew, defer, or withdraw accreditation, and periodic reaffirmation, often every five to ten years. Loss of accreditation can lead to closure, as credits from unaccredited institutions may not transfer and degrees may not be recognised for professional licensure.

Criticism

Critics argue accreditation can be slow to respond to innovation, imposes heavy compliance costs, and has at times failed to prevent institutional failures or poor student outcomes. Reform proposals include greater focus on outcomes data and streamlined review.
