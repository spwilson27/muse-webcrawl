History of coffee


An Ottoman coffeehouse of the 16th century

The history of coffee traces the cultivation and consumption of coffee from its origins in the highlands of Ethiopia, through the coffeehouses of the Arab and Ottoman worlds, to its global spread as one of the world's most traded commodities. Coffee drinking dates back at least to the 15th century in Yemen, and by the 17th century coffeehouses had become centres of social and intellectual life across the Middle East and Europe.

Origins and legend

The coffee plant Coffea arabica is native to the montane forests of south-western Ethiopia, where the fruit was traditionally chewed or mixed with fat. A popular legend tells of Kaldi, a 9th-century goatherd who noticed his goats became lively after eating the berries and reported this to monks, who used the drink to stay awake during prayers. Whatever its truth, Sufi communities in Yemen were drinking coffee (qahwa) by the 15th century, using it to aid concentration during night devotions, and cultivation spread through the Arabian Peninsula. The port of Mocha in Yemen gave its name to a famed variety.

Coffeehouses of the Islamic world

By the early 16th century coffee had reached Mecca, Cairo and, by the 1550s, Istanbul, where the first coffeehouses (qahveh khaneh) opened. Coffeehouses became venues for conversation, chess, music and political discussion, periodically attracting official suspicion and attempted bans. Coffee spread through the Ottoman Empire to the Balkans and North Africa.

Arrival in Europe

Venetian merchants brought coffee to Europe in the early 17th century; Pope Clement VIII is said to have "baptised" it, approving its use. The first European coffeehouses opened in Venice (1645), Oxford (1650) and London (1652). London's coffeehouses — such as those frequented by merchants in the City — incubated institutions like Lloyd's of London (insurance) and the stock exchange. In Vienna, coffee culture flourished after 1683, legendarily from beans left by the retreating Ottoman army. Coffeehouses became associated with Enlightenment sociability and were dubbed "penny universities" in England.

Colonial cultivation

European powers broke the Arab monopoly by smuggling out viable seeds and plants. The Dutch cultivated coffee in Java from the 1690s; a single plant from Amsterdam's botanical garden, gifted to Louis XIV, became ancestor to much of the Americas' coffee after Gabriel de Clieu carried a seedling to Martinique in 1723. Coffee plantations spread through the Caribbean, Brazil and Spanish America, worked overwhelmingly by enslaved Africans. Brazil became the dominant producer in the 19th century. In Africa and Asia, colonial regimes imposed coffee cultivation in Kenya, Uganda, Vietnam and elsewhere, often through forced labour.

Industrialisation and mass consumption

The 19th and 20th centuries brought vacuum packaging, commercial roasting brands, instant coffee (developed in the early 1900s and popularised in the world wars), the espresso machine (patented in Turin in 1884 and refined by Achille Gaggia in 1948), and decaffeination. Coffee leaf rust devastated Ceylon's plantations in the 1870s, pushing the island toward tea, and remains a recurring threat. The robusta species (Coffea canephora) came to dominate instant coffee and espresso blends; Vietnam became the largest robusta producer.

Modern coffee culture

From the 1960s–70s, the "specialty coffee" movement emphasised origin, freshness and brewing craft; chains such as Starbucks (founded 1971 in Seattle) globalised café culture. Fair trade and sustainability certification emerged in response to price crises affecting smallholders, who produce most of the world's coffee. Coffee is now grown in more than 70 countries and is among the most valuable agricultural commodities traded worldwide.
