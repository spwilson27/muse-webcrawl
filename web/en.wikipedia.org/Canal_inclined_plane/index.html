Canal inclined plane

A canal inclined plane is a type of cable railway used on canals to raise or lower boats between stretches of water at different levels, as an alternative to a flight of locks. The boat is floated into a water-filled caisson (or, in smaller "dry" designs, onto a cradle) which is then hauled up or lowered down a railed slope by cables and a winding engine, with a counterbalancing caisson or counterweights assisting the movement.

Advantages and history

Inclined planes consume far less water than locks — an important consideration where summit-level water is scarce — and can move boats more quickly than a long flight of locks. They were built on several early canals: notable early examples served the Ketley Canal (1788) and the Shropshire Canal in England, and the Morris Canal in New Jersey used 23 inclined planes to overcome a change in elevation of nearly 300 metres, carrying boats in wheeled cradles. Large planes were also built in Belgium (Ronquières, 1968) and France, while the Foxton inclined plane in Leicestershire (1900) was intended to speed traffic past the Foxton locks but proved short-lived.

Examples

Famous surviving and rebuilt examples include the Elbląg Canal in Poland, where boats are carried overland on rail-mounted cradles across a height difference of about 100 metres; the Saint-Louis-Arzviller inclined plane in France (1969), which replaced 17 locks; the Ronquières inclined plane in Belgium, with a slope over 1,400 metres long; and the Big Chute Marine Railway in Ontario, Canada, a related open-carriage design. Inclined planes are closely related to boat lifts, which raise caissons vertically rather than on a slope.
