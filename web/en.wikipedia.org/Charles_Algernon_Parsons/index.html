Sir Charles Algernon Parsons, OM, KCB, FRS (13 June 1854 – 11 February 1931) was an Anglo-Irish engineer best known for his invention of the modern steam turbine, a revolution in marine propulsion and electrical power generation. His work made cheap and plentiful electricity possible and transformed naval warfare and ocean travel.

Early life and the turbine

Parsons was the youngest son of the astronomer William Parsons, 3rd Earl of Rosse, builder of the great "Leviathan" telescope. He was educated at Trinity College Dublin and St John's College, Cambridge, graduating as eleventh wrangler in mathematics in 1877. After an apprenticeship at the Elswick works of W. G. Armstrong in Newcastle, he joined Clarke, Chapman and Co. in Gateshead, where in 1884 he patented his multi-stage axial-flow steam turbine. Early Parsons turbo-generators, of just 7.5 kilowatts, were soon supplying electricity to Newcastle — among the first public supplies in Britain.

In 1889 he founded C. A. Parsons and Company at Heaton, Newcastle, to build turbines and dynamos. When a dispute over patent rights with Clarke Chapman temporarily deprived him of his turbine patents, he developed the radial-flow design, and after recovering the original patents in 1894 he built ever-larger machines; by 1899 a 1-megawatt turbo-alternator was operating at Elberfeld in Germany.

Turbinia and marine propulsion

Parsons demonstrated the turbine at sea in the experimental yacht Turbinia (1894). After solving cavitation problems — work that led him to found the first cavitation tunnel — Parsons famously gatecrashed the Spithead naval review for Queen Victoria's Diamond Jubilee in 1897, dashing Turbinia at over 34 knots between the lines of warships, outrunning every vessel sent to intercept her. The Admiralty was converted: turbine propulsion was adopted for destroyers, then for HMS Dreadnought (1906) and the great liners Lusitania and Mauretania.

Later work and honours

Parsons also pursued optical manufacture — his firm ground the mirrors of large telescopes, a craft he learned at his father's estate — and conducted experiments on sound amplification (the auxetophone) and on attempts to synthesize diamond at high pressure. He was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1898, knighted in 1911, and appointed to the Order of Merit in 1927. He died aboard the liner Duchess of Richmond at Kingston, Jamaica, in 1931. Parsons turbines, built by his company and licensees, came to generate a large share of the world's electricity, and steam turbines remain the prime movers of most thermal and nuclear power stations today.
