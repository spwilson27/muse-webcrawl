The People's Commissariat for Internal Affairs (Russian: Народный комиссариат внутренних дел, Narodnyy komissariat vnutrennikh del), abbreviated NKVD, was the interior ministry of the Soviet Union from 1934 to 1946. It combined ordinary policing with the secret police functions of its predecessor, the OGPU, and became the principal instrument of Joseph Stalin's Great Purge and of political repression in the USSR.

History

The all-union NKVD was created in July 1934, absorbing the OGPU secret police as its Main Directorate of State Security (GUGB). Under Genrikh Yagoda, and then Nikolai Yezhov (1936–1938), the NKVD carried out the Great Terror (Yezhovshchina), arresting millions and executing an estimated 750,000 people in 1937–38 alone under quota-driven orders such as NKVD Order No. 00447. Extra-judicial sentencing was performed by three-man committees called troikas.

Under Lavrentiy Beria (1938–1946), the NKVD administered the Gulag camp system, organised mass deportations of entire nationalities, and in 1940 murdered some 22,000 Polish officers and intelligentsia in the Katyn massacre. During the Second World War it ran internal troops, border guards, and "blocking detachments", and conducted espionage and partisan operations behind German lines through its foreign intelligence directorate.

Structure and functions

The NKVD encompassed the regular police (militsiya), traffic police, border and internal troops, fire brigades, the Gulag administration of corrective-labour camps, and state security. Its foreign intelligence arm ran networks that recruited the "Cambridge Five" in Britain and penetrated the Manhattan Project.

Dissolution

In 1946 all People's Commissariats were renamed ministries; the NKVD became the Ministry of Internal Affairs (MVD), while state security functions passed to the MGB, later reorganised in 1954 as the KGB.
