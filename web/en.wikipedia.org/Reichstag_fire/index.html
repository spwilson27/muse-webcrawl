Date27 February 1933
LocationReichstag building, Berlin, Germany

The Reichstag fire (German: Reichstagsbrand) was an arson attack on the Reichstag building, home of the German parliament in Berlin, on Monday, 27 February 1933, precisely four weeks after Adolf Hitler was sworn in as Chancellor of Germany. The fire destroyed the main chamber and much of the building.

The fire and arrests

Marinus van der Lubbe, a young Dutch council communist, was arrested inside the burning building and confessed to starting the fire. Hitler and his cabinet, claiming the fire was part of a communist plot to seize power, persuaded President Paul von Hindenburg to issue the Reichstag Fire Decree on 28 February 1933. The decree suspended most civil liberties in Germany — including habeas corpus, freedom of expression, and freedom of assembly — and allowed the mass arrest of Communist Party members and the suppression of opposition newspapers, days before the March 1933 election.

The Leipzig trial

Van der Lubbe was tried in Leipzig together with three Bulgarian communists, among them Georgi Dimitrov, and the German communist Ernst Torgler. Dimitrov's celebrated self-defence embarrassed the prosecution, and only van der Lubbe was convicted; he was executed in January 1934. The others were acquitted for lack of evidence.

Historical debate

Historians continue to debate whether van der Lubbe acted alone or whether Nazi agents set or helped set the fire. The majority view holds that van der Lubbe was the sole arsonist, while a minority argues for Nazi involvement. Regardless of its authorship, the fire was the pivotal pretext by which the Nazi government suspended democracy and consolidated dictatorial power, culminating in the Enabling Act of March 1933. Van der Lubbe's conviction was posthumously annulled by a German court in 2008 under a law overturning unjust Nazi-era verdicts.
