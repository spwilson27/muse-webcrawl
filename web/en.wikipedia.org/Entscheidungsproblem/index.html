The Entscheidungsproblem (German for "decision problem") is a challenge posed by David Hilbert and Wilhelm Ackermann in 1928. It asks for an algorithm that, given a statement of first-order logic, decides whether the statement is universally valid — true in every structure satisfying the axioms. Hilbert regarded a positive solution as a capstone of his programme for the foundations of mathematics.

Negative solution

In 1936, Alonzo Church and Alan Turing independently proved that no such algorithm exists. Church used his lambda calculus to show the undecidability of the problem; Turing, in "On Computable Numbers", introduced the abstract machines now called Turing machines and proved that the halting problem — deciding whether a given machine ever halts — is unsolvable, from which the undecidability of the Entscheidungsproblem follows. The equivalence of their formalisms underlies the Church–Turing thesis on the nature of effective computation.

Historical context

The result, together with Gödel's incompleteness theorems of 1931, overturned the expectation that all mathematical truth could be captured by a single complete formal system. Hilbert's earlier problems had already yielded partial results: the decision problem had been solved positively for restricted fragments of logic, such as monadic predicate logic, and those decidable fragments remain important in computer science and automated reasoning.

Legacy

Beyond logic, the work of Church and Turing founded theoretical computer science: the idea of a universal machine that can simulate any other machine became the conceptual basis of the stored-program computer. Decidability and undecidability remain central organising questions in logic, verification and complexity theory.
