Acadia (French: Acadie) was a colony of New France in northeastern North America, comprising parts of what are now the Maritime provinces of Canada and the U.S. state of Maine. Founded in 1604, its capital was Port-Royal (now Annapolis Royal, Nova Scotia). The name is thought to derive from Arcadia, the Greek idyllic land, applied by explorer Giovanni da Verrazzano to the Atlantic coast in the 16th century.

History

French settlement began in 1604 on Saint Croix Island and in 1605 at Port-Royal, where settlers under Pierre Dugua, Sieur de Mons and Samuel de Champlain established a permanent habitation. The Acadians developed a distinctive society based on dyked-marshland agriculture along the Bay of Fundy, building aboiteaux to reclaim tidal marshes for farming.

Acadia changed hands repeatedly in Anglo-French wars. By the Treaty of Utrecht (1713), mainland Acadia (peninsular Nova Scotia) was ceded to Britain, while Île Royale (Cape Breton) and Île Saint-Jean (Prince Edward Island) remained French until 1763.

The Expulsion

During the French and Indian War, British authorities, doubting Acadian neutrality, carried out the Expulsion of the Acadians (1755–1764), known in French as le Grand Dérangement. More than 11,000 Acadians were deported to the Thirteen Colonies, Britain and France; thousands died of disease and shipwreck. Many exiles eventually settled in Spanish Louisiana, where their descendants became the Cajuns. Others returned to the Maritimes after 1764, settling mainly in what is now New Brunswick.

Legacy

Today, Acadian identity is centred in New Brunswick, Canada's only officially bilingual province, and in francophone communities of Nova Scotia and Prince Edward Island. The Acadian flag, the feast of the Assumption (15 August) as National Acadian Day, and Henry Wadsworth Longfellow's poem Evangeline (1847) are enduring symbols of Acadian culture.
