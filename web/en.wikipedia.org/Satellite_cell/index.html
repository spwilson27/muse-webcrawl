Satellite cell

Satellite cells, also called myosatellite cells, are small multipotent stem cells found in mature skeletal muscle, wedged between the basal lamina and the plasma membrane (sarcolemma) of individual muscle fibers. First described by Alexander Mauro in 1961 using electron microscopy, they are the principal source of new muscle nuclei for postnatal muscle growth and regeneration.

Function

In resting muscle, satellite cells are quiescent and express the transcription factor Pax7. Upon injury, exercise, or other stimuli, they activate, proliferate as myoblasts, and either fuse with existing muscle fibers to donate new nuclei or fuse with one another to form new myotubes, repairing the damage. A fraction of the progeny return to quiescence, replenishing the stem cell pool — a self-renewal capacity essential for repeated rounds of repair.

Significance

Satellite cell dysfunction contributes to age-related muscle loss (sarcopenia) and to the pathology of muscular dystrophies such as Duchenne muscular dystrophy. They are a major focus of regenerative medicine research, though attempts at satellite-cell transplantation therapy face challenges of survival and migration in host tissue. Related satellite (glial) cells also exist around neuronal cell bodies in peripheral ganglia, sharing the name but not the function.
