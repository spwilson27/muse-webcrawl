The Amazon rainforest, also called Amazonia or the Amazon jungle, is a moist broadleaf tropical rainforest in the Amazon biome that covers most of the Amazon basin of South America. This basin encompasses 7,000,000 km² (2,700,000 sq mi), of which the rainforest covers approximately 6,000,000 km² (2,300,000 sq mi). The majority of the forest is contained within Brazil, with 60% of the rainforest, followed by Peru with 13%, and smaller amounts in Colombia, Venezuela, Ecuador, Bolivia, Guyana, Suriname, and French Guiana.

The Amazon represents over half of the planet's remaining rainforests, and comprises the largest and most biodiverse tract of tropical rainforest in the world, with an estimated 16,000 tree species and 390 billion individual trees. It is often popularly described as the "lungs of the Earth", although scientists note that most of its oxygen production is consumed within the forest itself.

Etymology

The name Amazon is said to derive from a war Francisco de Orellana fought with the Tapuyas and other tribes. The women of the tribe fought alongside the men, as was their custom. Orellana derived the name from the mythical Amazons of Greek mythology, described by Herodotus and Diodorus.

History

Based on archaeological evidence from excavations at Caverna da Pedra Pintada, human inhabitants first settled in the Amazon region at least 11,200 years ago. Subsequent development led to late-prehistoric settlements along the periphery of the forest, such as in the Andes. For a long time it was thought that the Amazon rainforest was only ever sparsely populated, but recent research has uncovered evidence of large-scale pre-Columbian social formations, including earthworks, geoglyphs, and fertile anthropogenic soils known as terra preta.

In 1500 the Portuguese Pedro Álvares Cabral reached Brazil, and in 1541–42 the Spanish explorer Francisco de Orellana completed the first navigation down the length of the Amazon River. The rubber boom of the late 19th and early 20th centuries brought wealth to cities such as Manaus and Iquitos, followed by decline when plantation rubber from Southeast Asia undercut Amazonian production.

Biodiversity

The Amazon is home to about 10% of the world's known species. It contains roughly 2.5 million insect species, tens of thousands of plants, and some 2,000 birds and mammals. Species richness is highest in the western Amazon near the Andes. Notable animals include the jaguar, giant otter, Amazon river dolphin, harpy eagle, poison dart frogs, and anacondas. One in ten known species in the world lives in the Amazon rainforest, constituting the largest collection of living plants and animal species in the world.

Climate

The basin's climate is warm and humid, with average annual temperatures around 26–27 °C and very high rainfall — annual precipitation averages about 2,300 mm, though parts of the western Amazon receive much more. The forest itself drives much of this rainfall: water vapour released by trees forms "flying rivers" that carry moisture across the basin and to agricultural regions further south.

Deforestation

Deforestation in the Amazon accelerated dramatically from the 1960s onward, driven by cattle ranching, soybean cultivation, logging, road building, and mining. Roughly 17–20% of the original forest had been cleared by the early 21st century. Scientists have warned that the forest may approach a tipping point — commonly placed near 20–25% deforestation — beyond which large areas could transition toward savanna, releasing enormous stores of carbon and disrupting regional rainfall. Rates of loss fell sharply in Brazil between 2004 and 2012 under enforcement policies, rose again in the late 2010s, and declined once more in the early 2020s.

Conservation

Conservation efforts include networks of protected areas and Indigenous territories, which consistently show lower deforestation rates than surrounding lands. International mechanisms such as the Amazon Fund support monitoring and sustainable development. Indigenous peoples, numbering around 1.5 million in hundreds of distinct groups, are widely regarded as central to the forest's protection.
