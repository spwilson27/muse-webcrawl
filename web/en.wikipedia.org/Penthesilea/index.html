Penthesilea

In Greek mythology, Penthesilea was an Amazonian queen, daughter of Ares and Otrera. She came to the aid of Troy during the Trojan War and was killed in single combat by Achilles. Her story, not told in the Iliad, was narrated in the lost epic Aethiopis attributed to Arctinus of Miletus and survives in later summaries and vase painting.

Myth

Penthesilea came to Troy partly to seek purification, having accidentally killed her sister Hippolyta during a hunt, and was cleansed by King Priam. In battle she slew many Greek warriors, but was confronted by Achilles, who killed her. When he removed her helmet, he was struck by her beauty and grieved — in some versions falling in love with her corpse. The Greek Thersites mocked him for this and was killed by Achilles in turn.

In art and literature

The duel of Achilles and Penthesilea was a favourite subject of Greek vase painters, notably Exekias, and appears in sculpture on the throne of Zeus at Olympia. In medieval and later literature Penthesilea was counted among the Nine Worthies' female counterparts and appears in works such as Boccaccio's De mulieribus claris and Christine de Pizan's Book of the City of Ladies. Heinrich von Kleist's drama Penthesilea (1808) gives the myth a tragic psychological reinterpretation in which the queen kills Achilles, mistaking combat for love. Her name is also given to an asteroid, 271 Penthesilea.
