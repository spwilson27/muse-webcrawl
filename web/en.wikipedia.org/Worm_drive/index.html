Worm drive

A worm (screw) driving a worm wheel.

A worm drive is a gear arrangement in which a worm — a screw-like gear — meshes with a worm wheel, a gear similar in appearance to a spur gear. The arrangement provides very large speed reductions in a compact space and is used where high torque and smooth, quiet operation are required.

Operation

Each turn of the worm advances the wheel by one tooth per thread start, so a single-start worm driving a 40-tooth wheel gives a 40:1 reduction. Worms may have one or several thread starts; more starts raise the ratio less but improve efficiency. The axes of worm and wheel are typically at 90 degrees and non-intersecting.

Self-locking

A distinctive property of many worm drives is that the wheel cannot drive the worm: friction and the shallow lead angle make the drive "self-locking" or irreversible. This is exploited in hoists and lifts, where the load cannot drive the motor backwards when power is removed, though vibration can sometimes overcome the effect and designers may not rely on it for safety-critical holding.

Applications and drawbacks

Worm drives are used in elevators and escalators, conveyor systems, steering mechanisms, guitar tuning machines, rudder controls, and presses. Their principal drawback is efficiency loss — sliding contact between worm and wheel wastes energy as heat, and efficiencies below 50% are common in high-ratio single-start sets. Wheels are therefore often made of bronze against hardened steel worms to reduce friction and wear.
