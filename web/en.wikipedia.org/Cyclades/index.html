The Cyclades (Greek: Κυκλάδες, Kykládes) are an island group in the Aegean Sea, southeast of mainland Greece; they form a rough circle — the name derives from the Greek word for "circle" — around the sacred island of Delos. The group comprises about 220 islands, of which roughly 33 are inhabited.

History

The Cyclades were home to the Cycladic civilisation of the Early Bronze Age (c. 3200–2000 BC), famed for its abstract marble figurines. In antiquity the islands were associated with the sanctuary of Apollo on Delos and were members of the Delian League. Over the centuries they passed under Hellenistic, Roman, Byzantine, Venetian (Duchy of the Archipelago), and Ottoman rule before joining the modern Greek state in the 19th century.

Geography and culture

The islands are peaks of submerged mountains, mostly extensions of the Attic-Cycladic metamorphic belt. They are known for their whitewashed cubist architecture, blue-domed churches, windmills, and dry, terraced landscapes. Tourism is the main industry; Mykonos and Santorini (Thera) are among Greece's most visited destinations. Notable products include marble from Naxos and Paros and the volcanic wines of Santorini.
