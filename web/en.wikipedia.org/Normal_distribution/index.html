Normal distribution

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In statistics, a normal distribution (also known as a Gaussian distribution or bell curve) is a type of continuous probability distribution for a real-valued random variable. Its probability density function is

f(x) = (1 / (σ√(2π))) · e−(x−μ)² / (2σ²)

where μ is the mean and σ is the standard deviation. The distribution is symmetric about its mean, and fully described by these two parameters. The special case μ = 0, σ = 1 is the standard normal distribution.

Importance

The normal distribution's ubiquity rests on the central limit theorem: sums of many independent random variables tend toward a normal distribution regardless of the variables' own distributions. Consequently, measurement errors, biological traits such as height, test scores, and many other natural and social quantities are approximately normal. About 68% of values fall within one standard deviation of the mean, 95% within two, and 99.7% within three (the "68–95–99.7 rule").

History

Abraham de Moivre introduced the distribution in 1738 as an approximation to the binomial distribution. Carl Friedrich Gauss used it in his 1809 analysis of astronomical measurement errors — hence "Gaussian" — and Pierre-Simon Laplace proved the first version of the central limit theorem. The term "normal" was popularised by Karl Pearson and others in the late nineteenth century; statisticians have since cautioned that the name does not imply that other distributions are abnormal.

Properties

The normal distribution belongs to the exponential family, has zero skewness and (excess) zero kurtosis in its standard parameterisation, and is closed under linear transformations: sums of independent normal variables are normal. It has the maximum entropy of any distribution with a given mean and variance. Its cumulative distribution function has no elementary closed form and is expressed through the error function, erf.

Generalizations

Extensions include the multivariate normal distribution for random vectors, the log-normal distribution (whose logarithm is normal), truncated and censored normal distributions, and normal mixture models. Deviations from normality in real data — heavy tails, skewness — are an important concern in fields such as finance, where treating returns as normal can badly underestimate risk.
