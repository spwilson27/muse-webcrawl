Imperial Diet (Holy Roman Empire)

The Imperial Diet (Latin: Dieta Imperii or Comitium Imperiale; German: Reichstag) was the deliberative body of the Holy Roman Empire. It was not a legislature in the modern sense — its members were the Imperial Estates (imperial princes, counts, and free imperial cities) rather than elected deputies. It developed from the royal court assemblies (Hoftage) of the early Middle Ages.

Composition

By the early modern period, the Diet was organized into three colleges:

  • The Council of Electors (Kurfürstenrat), composed of the prince-electors entitled to elect the emperor.
  • The Council of Princes (Fürstenrat), composed of secular and ecclesiastical princes, and the counts and lords, who voted in benches.
  • The Council of Imperial Cities (Städterat), representing the free imperial cities; its vote was for long considered advisory.

History

The Diet met at various cities over the centuries. The Diet of Worms (1521) famously summoned Martin Luther to recant his writings. The reforms of 1495 under Emperor Maximilian I, proclaimed at the Diet of Worms of that year, established the Ewiger Landfriede (Perpetual Public Peace) and gave the Diet a more regular structure. After the Peace of Westphalia (1648), the Diet sat permanently at Regensburg from 1663 — the Perpetual Diet (Immerwährender Reichstag) — until the dissolution of the Holy Roman Empire in 1806.

Functions

The Diet deliberated on matters affecting the Empire as a whole: taxation for imperial wars (especially against the Ottoman Empire), imperial reform, religious settlements, and the admission of new estates. Its decisions, agreed with the emperor, were published as Imperial Recesses (Reichsabschiede).
