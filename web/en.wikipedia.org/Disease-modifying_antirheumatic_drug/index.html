Disease-modifying antirheumatic drug

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs) comprise a category of otherwise unrelated drugs defined by their use in rheumatoid arthritis to slow down disease progression. The term is often used in contrast to nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), which refer to agents that treat the inflammation but not the underlying cause, and steroids, which blunt the immune response but are insufficient to slow the progression of the disease.

The term "DMARD" was first used to mean a drug that could modify the course of the disease, as evidenced by a reduction in joint damage on imaging. Historically the group included antimalarials such as hydroxychloroquine, sulfasalazine, methotrexate, gold salts, D-penicillamine and leflunomide. Methotrexate is now widely regarded as the first-line DMARD for rheumatoid arthritis because of its efficacy, tolerability and low cost.

Classification

DMARDs are commonly divided into conventional synthetic DMARDs (csDMARDs), such as methotrexate, sulfasalazine, leflunomide and hydroxychloroquine; biologic DMARDs (bDMARDs), which are genetically engineered molecules such as tumour necrosis factor (TNF) inhibitors including adalimumab, etanercept and infliximab; and targeted synthetic DMARDs (tsDMARDs), notably the Janus kinase inhibitors such as tofacitinib, baricitinib and upadacitinib.

Mechanism and use

Different DMARDs act through different mechanisms: methotrexate is a folate antagonist with anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive effects; TNF inhibitors neutralise a key inflammatory cytokine; Janus kinase inhibitors block intracellular signalling pathways used by several cytokines. Unlike analgesics and NSAIDs, DMARDs take weeks to months to exert their full effect, but early initiation is associated with better long-term outcomes and reduced joint erosion.

Adverse effects

Because many DMARDs suppress the immune system, patients require regular monitoring for infections, liver toxicity and blood count abnormalities. Methotrexate is teratogenic and is contraindicated in pregnancy. Biologic agents carry a risk of reactivating latent tuberculosis, and Janus kinase inhibitors have been associated with increased risks of cardiovascular events and malignancy in some populations.
