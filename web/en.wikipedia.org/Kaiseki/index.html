Kaiseki

Kaiseki (懐石) or kaiseki-ryōri (懐石料理) is a traditional multi-course Japanese dinner, regarded as the pinnacle of Japanese haute cuisine. The term also refers to the collection of skills and techniques used in its preparation, analogous to Western haute cuisine. Kaiseki emphasises seasonal ingredients (shun), meticulous preparation, and artistic presentation on carefully chosen tableware.

Origins

Kaiseki traces its roots to the simple meal served at the Japanese tea ceremony (chanoyu), perfected in the 16th century by the tea master Sen no Rikyū. The name literally means "bosom stone", from the practice of Zen monks holding a warm stone against the stomach to stave off hunger. Over time, in Kyoto — the imperial capital — the austere tea meal evolved into an elaborate banquet cuisine for the aristocracy, while a related style, kaseki written 会席, developed for social banquets with sake.

Course structure

A classic kaiseki meal in Kyoto proceeds through a sequence of courses, typically including: sakizuke (an appetiser akin to an amuse-bouche), hassun (a seasonal platter), mukōzuke (sashimi), takiawase (simmered vegetables), futamono (a soup), yakimono (grilled fish), su-zakana (a palate cleanser), shiizakana (a substantial dish), and finally gohan (rice), kō no mono (pickles) and tome-wan (miso soup), concluding with mizumono (a seasonal dessert, often fruit).

Philosophy

Central principles include shun — using ingredients at the peak of their brief season — and presenting a balance of tastes, textures, colours and cooking methods. Dishes are served on plates and bowls selected to complement the food and the season, and garnishes such as edible leaves and flowers are common.
