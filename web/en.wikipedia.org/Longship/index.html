Longship

A reconstructed longship under sail

Longships (Old Norse: langskip) were a type of specialised Scandinavian warship with a long history in Scandinavia, whose epitome is found in the form of the dragon ship. Their development occurred over several centuries, and their appearance in the Viking Age enabled the Norse to travel, raid, trade and settle across Europe and into the North Atlantic.

Design

Longships were built clinker-style with overlapping oak planks riveted together, on a single sturdy keel plank extended into curving stems and sterns. They were light, flexible, shallow-draught vessels that could sail in a metre of water, be beached, and even carried overland. Propulsion was by a single square sail on one mast, supplemented by oars. Types ranged from small karvi with 13 pairs of oars, through the snekkja with 20, to great skeid warships with 30 or more pairs.

History and archaeology

The longship evolved from earlier Scandinavian rowing boats such as the Hjortspring boat. Buried ships at Oseberg, Gokstad and Tune, and the five scuttled ships found at Skuldelev in Denmark, provide the main archaeological evidence. The largest known, Ormen Lange ("the Long Serpent"), attributed to Olaf Tryggvason, was said to have 34 pairs of oars. Longships reached Iceland, Greenland and, around AD 1000, North America (Vinland), and their design influenced shipbuilding throughout northern Europe.
