Gastronomy

Gastronomy is the study of the relationship between food and culture, the art of preparing and serving rich or delicate and appetising food, the cooking styles of particular regions, and the science of good eating. A practitioner devoted to food and culinary culture is known as a gastronome, while a gastronomist combines theory and practice in the field.

History

The term entered modern usage through Jean Anthelme Brillat-Savarin's The Physiology of Taste (1825), which explored eating as a cultural and physiological phenomenon. Nineteenth-century French writers and chefs such as Carême and Escoffier codified haute cuisine, while the 20th century brought gastronomic criticism, restaurant guides — most famously the Michelin Guide — and eventually nouvelle cuisine and molecular gastronomy, in which chefs such as Ferran Adrià applied scientific techniques to cooking.

Scope

Gastronomy encompasses ingredients and their production, cooking techniques, flavour perception, dining rituals, and the historical and regional development of cuisines. UNESCO has inscribed several food cultures on its Intangible Cultural Heritage list, including the "gastronomic meal of the French" in 2010.

Molecular gastronomy

A subdiscipline pioneered by physicist Hervé This and Nicholas Kurti investigates the physical and chemical transformations of ingredients during cooking, giving rise to techniques such as spherification, foams and low-temperature cooking in avant-garde restaurants.
