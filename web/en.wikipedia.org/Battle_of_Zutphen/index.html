Battle of Zutphen

The Battle of Zutphen was fought on 22 September 1586 near Zutphen in the Netherlands, during the Eighty Years' War between the Dutch Republic and Spain. An English and Dutch force under Robert Dudley, Earl of Leicester, attempted to intercept a Spanish supply convoy destined for the besieged town of Zutphen, held by the Spanish under the Duke of Parma. The engagement is best remembered for the mortal wounding of the English courtier, poet and soldier Sir Philip Sidney, whose death became a legend of Elizabethan chivalry.

Background

Under the Treaty of Nonsuch (1585), Elizabeth I sent an English expeditionary force to aid the Dutch rebels. In 1586 Alexander Farnese, Duke of Parma, Spain's commander in the Netherlands, laid siege to Zutphen, a strategic town on the IJssel. Leicester marched to relieve it and, learning that a large Spanish convoy was bringing supplies to the town, moved to intercept it in the morning mist of 22 September.

The battle

The English horse, including Sidney, charged the Spanish escort several times in fog so thick that opponents could barely be distinguished. Sidney, who had lent part of his armour to a friend and rode without cuisses (thigh armour), was shot through the thigh; his horse bolted beneath him before he was carried from the field. The convoy nonetheless fought its way through into Zutphen, and the allies failed to break the supply line. Sidney died of his wound, which turned gangrenous, at Arnhem on 17 October 1586, aged 31.

Legacy

According to the famous anecdote, the wounded Sidney gave his water bottle to a dying soldier with the words "Thy necessity is yet greater than mine." Zutphen cemented Sidney's posthumous fame across Europe; he was given a state funeral at St Paul's Cathedral. Militarily the battle changed little: Zutphen remained in Spanish hands until Maurice of Nassau captured it in 1591.
