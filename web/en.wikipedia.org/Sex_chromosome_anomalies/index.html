Sex chromosome anomalies (also called sex chromosome aneuploidies) are conditions arising from an abnormal number of sex chromosomes — extra or missing X or Y chromosomes — usually caused by nondisjunction during meiosis. Because of X-inactivation, which silences all but one X chromosome in each cell, most sex chromosome aneuploidies are relatively mild compared with autosomal aneuploidies, and many affected individuals are never diagnosed. Taken together they occur in roughly 1 in 400 to 500 births, making them among the most common chromosomal abnormalities in humans.

Main types

  • Turner syndrome (45,X) — a female with a single X chromosome; features include short stature, webbed neck, streak ovaries with infertility, and congenital heart defects. One of the few complete monosomies compatible with life.
  • Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY) — a male with an extra X; tall stature, small testes, infertility, gynaecomastia, and variable learning difficulties. Described by Harry Klinefelter in 1942.
  • 47,XYY syndrome — a male with an extra Y; usually tall with few or no symptoms. Early claims linking it to criminality were based on biased samples and have been discredited.
  • Triple X syndrome (47,XXX) — a female with an extra X; often tall and with mild or no symptoms; most cases go undiagnosed.
  • 48,XXYY, 48,XXXY, 49,XXXXY and mosaics — rarer, generally more pronounced phenotypes.

Management

There is no cure for the chromosomal constitution itself, but many manifestations are treatable: growth hormone and oestrogen replacement in Turner syndrome, testosterone in Klinefelter syndrome, early speech, occupational and educational support, and fertility assistance or donor options. Prenatal diagnosis is possible by chorionic villus sampling or amniocentesis, and non-invasive prenatal screening increasingly detects sex chromosome aneuploidies incidentally, raising counselling and ethical questions because phenotypes are often mild.
