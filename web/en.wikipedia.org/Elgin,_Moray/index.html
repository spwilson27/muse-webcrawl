Elgin, historic cathedral city and royal burgh in Moray

Elgin (Scottish Gaelic: Eilginn) is a town and formerly a royal burgh and cathedral city in Moray, Scotland, lying on the River Lossie about 35 miles (56 km) east of Inverness. It is the administrative and commercial centre of Moray and one of Scotland's smaller cities, a status it holds by virtue of its medieval cathedral.

History

Elgin was created a royal burgh in the twelfth century, in the reign of David I. Its cathedral, founded in 1224, was one of the most beautiful in Scotland — known as the "Lantern of the North" — until it was burned in 1390 by Alexander Stewart, the "Wolf of Badenoch", in a feud with the Bishop of Moray, and again attacked in 1402. The ruins, including the chapter house and carved stones such as the Bishop's Chair, remain a major landmark; the Pictish Elgin Pillar stands nearby.

The town's eighteenth- and nineteenth-century prosperity, built on agriculture, textiles and trade, produced many of its fine stone buildings. Elgin's railway station opened in 1852 on the line between Aberdeen and Inverness.

Economy and culture

Elgin is a centre of the Speyside whisky industry: the Glen Moray distillery stands in the town, and many distilleries lie nearby. Other employers include food production (shortbread and oatcakes), cashmere textiles, education and the public sector; RAF Lossiemouth is a few miles north. Elgin City F.C. plays at Borough Briggs. Elgin Museum, founded in 1843, is among Scotland's oldest independent museums and displays Pictish stones and fossils, including the Elgin reptiles from local Permian and Triassic sandstones.
