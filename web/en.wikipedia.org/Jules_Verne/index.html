Jules Verne

Jules Gabriel Verne (/vɜːrn/; French: [ʒyl ɡabʁijɛl vɛʁn]; 8 February 1828 – 24 March 1905) was a French novelist, poet, and playwright. His collaboration with the publisher Pierre-Jules Hetzel led to the creation of the Voyages extraordinaires, a series of bestselling adventure novels including Journey to the Center of the Earth (1864), Twenty Thousand Leagues Under the Seas (1870), and Around the World in Eighty Days (1872).

Life

Born in Nantes, Verne was sent to Paris to study law but devoted himself to writing. His first success came with Five Weeks in a Balloon (1863), which began his long association with Hetzel. Verne's novels, grounded in careful research and contemporary science, imagined submarines, aircraft, and space travel decades before they became practical, earning him — alongside H. G. Wells — recognition as a founding father of science fiction.

Legacy

Verne is one of the most translated authors in the world. His works have been adapted into countless films and plays; the Nautilus and its Captain Nemo, and the unflappable Phileas Fogg, remain cultural icons. He spent his later years in Amiens, where he served as a city councillor, and died there in 1905.
