Probability

Probability is the branch of mathematics concerning events and numerical descriptions of how likely they are to occur. The probability of an event is a number between 0 and 1; the larger the probability, the more likely an event is to occur. A simple example is the tossing of a fair (unbiased) coin, where heads and tails are equally likely and each has probability 1/2.

History

The mathematical study of probability arose from gambling problems addressed in the 1654 correspondence of Blaise Pascal and Pierre de Fermat, prompted by questions from the Chevalier de Méré. Christiaan Huygens published the first book on the subject in 1657. Jacob Bernoulli's Ars Conjectandi (1713) and Laplace's Théorie analytique des probabilités (1812) systematised the theory; Andrey Kolmogorov gave it a measure-theoretic axiomatic foundation in 1933.

Formal treatment

In Kolmogorov's framework, a probability space consists of a sample space Ω, a σ-algebra of events, and a measure P satisfying: P(A) ≥ 0 for all events A; P(Ω) = 1; and countable additivity for disjoint events. A random variable is a measurable function from the sample space to the real numbers, characterised by its probability distribution.

Interpretations

Philosophers and statisticians debate what probability statements mean. Frequentists interpret probability as the long-run relative frequency of an event; Bayesians interpret it as a degree of belief, updated via Bayes' theorem; the classical interpretation assigns equal probabilities to symmetric outcomes; propensity theories treat probabilities as objective tendencies of physical systems.

Applications

Probability underlies statistics, risk assessment, finance, insurance, gambling, quantum mechanics (via the Born rule), statistical mechanics, and machine learning. Key results include the law of large numbers and the central limit theorem.
