Chewing gum is a soft, cohesive substance designed to be chewed without being swallowed. Modern chewing gum is composed of gum base, sweeteners, softeners/plasticizers, flavors, colors, and, typically, a hard or powdered polyol coating. Its texture is reminiscent of rubber because of the physical-chemical properties of its polymer, plasticizer, and resin components, which contribute to its elastic-plastic, sticky, chewy characteristics.

History

The custom of chewing gum-like substances is ancient: Neolithic people in northern Europe chewed birch bark tar 5,000–6,000 years ago, and ancient Greeks chewed mastic, while the Maya and Aztecs chewed chicle from the sapodilla tree. In 1848 John B. Curtis began selling spruce gum, the first commercial chewing gum in the United States. In 1869 dentist William Finley Semple patented an early gum intended to clean teeth, and in 1871 Thomas Adams patented a machine to manufacture gum from chicle, founding a major industry. William Wrigley Jr. built an empire in the 1890s with Juicy Fruit and Spearmint. Bubble gum, which stretches without breaking and allows bubbles, was invented in 1928 by Walter Diemer, an accountant at Fleer, whose pink Dubble Bubble became iconic — the pink colour simply because it was the only dye on hand.

Composition

Traditional gum base used natural latexes such as chicle, jelutong, and sorva; most modern gum bases are food-grade synthetic polymers such as polyisobutylene, styrene-butadiene rubber, and polyethylene, blended with waxes and resins. Sweeteners include sugar, corn syrup, or, in sugar-free gums, sugar alcohols such as xylitol and sorbitol, often with high-intensity sweeteners like aspartame or sucralose.

Health effects

Sugar-free gum sweetened with xylitol is recognized to reduce the risk of dental caries when chewed after meals, because chewing stimulates saliva that neutralizes acids and clears food debris. Nicotine gum is used as an aid to smoking cessation. Excessive chewing has occasionally been linked to jaw muscle fatigue, and large quantities of sorbitol can have a laxative effect.

Environmental and social issues

Because gum base is a non-biodegradable plastic, discarded gum is a persistent litter problem; cities spend heavily removing it from pavements. Singapore famously restricted the sale of chewing gum in 1992, largely over litter and vandalism, later partially relaxing the ban for therapeutic gum.
