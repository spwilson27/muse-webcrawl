Architecture is the art and technique of designing and building, as distinguished from the skills associated with construction. It is both the process and the product of sketching, conceiving, planning, designing and constructing buildings or other structures. The term derives from the Latin architectura, from the Greek arkhitéktōn ("chief builder").

Definitions

The Roman architect Vitruvius, in De architectura (1st century BC), held that a building must satisfy three principles: firmitas (firmness/durability), utilitas (commodity/usefulness) and venustas (delight/beauty). These remain a touchstone of architectural theory. Modern definitions also encompass the design of the total built environment, from the macro-level of urban planning and landscape architecture to the micro-level of furniture and products.

History

Building first evolved from the dynamics between needs (shelter, security, worship) and means (available building materials and attendant skills). Monumental architecture appeared in Neolithic settlements; the ziggurats of Mesopotamia and the pyramids of Egypt (c. 2600 BC) are among the earliest large-scale works. Classical Greek and Roman architecture codified the orders — Doric, Ionic and Corinthian — which were revived in Renaissance architecture from the 15th century, led by Brunelleschi and codified by Alberti and Palladio.

Gothic architecture dominated much of medieval Europe with pointed arches, rib vaults and flying buttresses. In the Islamic world, architecture developed distinctive forms including domes, minarets and geometric ornament. East Asian traditions developed timber-frame systems with tiled roofs, exemplified by Chinese and Japanese temples and palaces.

Modern architecture

The Industrial Revolution introduced cast iron, steel and reinforced concrete, enabling skyscrapers and wide-span structures. Modernism in the early 20th century — the Bauhaus, Le Corbusier, Frank Lloyd Wright, Ludwig Mies van der Rohe — rejected ornament in favour of functionalism and new materials. Postmodernism from the 1960s reintroduced historical reference and complexity, while contemporary practice explores parametric design, sustainability and computer-aided fabrication.

Theory and practice

Architects work with clients, engineers and planners to produce designs that are functional, safe and aesthetically considered. Professional practice is regulated in most countries, requiring accredited education, internship and licensure. Contemporary concerns include energy efficiency, adaptive reuse, accessibility and resilience to climate change.
