The Potala Palace is a vast dzong fortress-palace in Lhasa, Tibet, named after Mount Potalaka, the mythical abode of the bodhisattva Avalokiteśvara. It was the winter palace of the Dalai Lamas from 1649 until 1959, when the 14th Dalai Lama fled to India. Today it is a museum and, since 1994, a UNESCO World Heritage Site, and it remains the pre-eminent symbol of Tibetan Buddhism and of Lhasa.

History

A palace first stood on Marpo Ri ("Red Hill") in the 7th century, built by the Tibetan emperor Songtsen Gampo; only fragments, including the Chagpori cave chapels, survive. The present palace was begun in 1645 by the 5th Dalai Lama, Lozang Gyatso, whose minister Sangay Gyatso completed the White Palace by 1649 and added the Red Palace (1690–94), concealing the Dalai Lama's death for twelve years until the work was finished. The palace thereafter served as the seat of Tibet's government and the residence of successive Dalai Lamas.

Architecture and contents

Rising thirteen storeys and containing more than 1,000 rooms, 10,000 shrines, and some 200,000 statues, the Potala is among the largest palace complexes in the world. Its massive sloping walls, up to 5 metres thick at the base, are built of stone, earth, and — by legend — copper poured into the foundations against earthquakes. The White Palace houses living quarters, offices, and seminary rooms; the Red Palace is devoted to religious purposes, containing the gilded burial stupas of eight Dalai Lamas, the most splendid being that of the 5th, encrusted with jewels and gold. The palace preserves an immense collection of murals, thangkas, scriptures, porcelain, jade, and imperial gifts from Chinese emperors.

Present day

Since 1980 the palace has been maintained as a state museum under Chinese administration, with major restoration programmes in 1989–94 and 2002–09. Pilgrims continue to circumambulate and visit its chapels. To protect the structure, visitor numbers are limited by a daily quota and timed ticketing.
