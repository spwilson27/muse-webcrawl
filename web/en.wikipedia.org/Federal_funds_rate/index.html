Federal funds rate

The effective federal funds rate since 1954: peaks around 1981, long declines after 2008, and a rapid rise in 2022–2023.

The federal funds rate is the target interest rate set by the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) of the Federal Reserve System, at which commercial banks borrow and lend their excess reserves to each other overnight. It is the principal tool of monetary policy in the United States. The effective federal funds rate (EFFR) is the volume-weighted median of actual overnight transactions, published each business day by the Federal Reserve Bank of New York.

Mechanism

Banks hold reserve balances at Federal Reserve Banks. Those with surplus balances lend them overnight to those needing to meet reserve and liquidity requirements, at the federal funds rate. Since 2008 the FOMC has announced a target range for the rate, and the Federal Reserve steers the effective rate within the range mainly by setting the interest rate paid on reserve balances and the rate on its overnight reverse repurchase agreement facility — so-called administered rates — supplemented when necessary by open market operations.

Effect on the economy

The federal funds rate anchors all short-term interest rates in the dollar financial system. When the Fed raises it, borrowing costs for households and businesses rise — through the prime rate, credit cards, and auto loans — demand cools, and inflation pressure eases; when it cuts the rate, the opposite occurs. Because the United States dollar is the world's main reserve currency, changes in the rate also move global bond yields, exchange rates, and emerging-market borrowing costs.

History

The rate reached its all-time high under Federal Reserve Chairman Paul Volcker, averaging about 19% in June 1981 during the campaign against double-digit inflation. It fell almost continuously thereafter, reaching 1% in 2003, and was cut to a target range of 0–0.25% in December 2008 during the financial crisis — the zero lower bound — where it stayed until December 2015. It returned to zero in March 2020 in response to the COVID-19 pandemic, then rose at the fastest pace in four decades in 2022–2023, to a range of 5.25–5.50%, before the FOMC began easing again in late 2024.
