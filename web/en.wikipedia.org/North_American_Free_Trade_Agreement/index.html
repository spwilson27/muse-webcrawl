North American Free Trade Agreement

The North American Free Trade Agreement (NAFTA; Spanish: Tratado de Libre Comercio de América del Norte, TLCAN; French: Accord de libre-échange nord-américain, ALÉNA) was an agreement signed by Canada, Mexico, and the United States that created a trilateral trade bloc in North America. It came into force on 1 January 1994.

The leaders of Canada, Mexico, and the United States at the signing

Provisions and effects

NAFTA eliminated most tariffs and other barriers to trade and investment among the three countries, with phased implementation over 15 years, and added side agreements on labour and environmental standards. Trade among the partners roughly quadrupled, supply chains — especially in automobiles — became deeply integrated, and the agreement is credited with boosting Mexican exports and U.S. agricultural sales, while critics blamed it for manufacturing job losses in the United States and harm to Mexican small farmers.

Replacement

During the 2016 U.S. presidential campaign NAFTA was attacked as a "bad deal", and the administration of Donald Trump renegotiated it. The resulting United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement (USMCA) was signed in 2018 and replaced NAFTA on 1 July 2020.
