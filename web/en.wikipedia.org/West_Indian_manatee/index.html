West Indian manatee

Scientific nameTrichechus manatus
SubspeciesFlorida manatee (T. m. latirostris); Antillean manatee (T. m. manatus)
SizeUp to c. 3.5–4 m; 200–600 kg
StatusVulnerable (IUCN)

The West Indian manatee (Trichechus manatus) is a large, slow-moving aquatic mammal of warm coastal waters of the western Atlantic, from Florida and the Gulf of Mexico through the Caribbean to northeastern Brazil. It is one of three living manatee species and comprises two subspecies, the Florida manatee and the Antillean manatee.

Description and behaviour

Manatees are grey-brown, fully aquatic herbivores with paddle-like flippers, a rounded spoon-shaped tail, and dense bones that act as ballast. They graze on seagrasses and other aquatic plants for many hours a day and surface regularly to breathe. They have poor tolerance of cold and gather at warm-water refuges such as Florida springs and power-plant outflows in winter.

Conservation

Historically hunted, manatees are now protected, but they remain threatened by collisions with boats — a leading cause of death in Florida — habitat loss, cold stress and harmful algal blooms (red tide). The Florida population recovered enough for the species to be downlisted from endangered to threatened under the US Endangered Species Act in 2017, though a mass starvation event in the Indian River Lagoon in 2020–2022 killed well over a thousand animals.
