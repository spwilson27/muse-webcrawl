Sumer

The Ziggurat of Ur, built during the Third Dynasty of Ur.

Sumer (from Akkadian Šumeru; Sumerian Ki-en-gi) is one of the earliest civilizations, located in the southern part of Mesopotamia (modern southern Iraq) between the Tigris and Euphrates rivers. Beginning in the late 5th millennium BCE, Sumerian city-states such as Uruk, Eridu, Ur, Lagash and Kish pioneered urban life, irrigation agriculture and, around 3400–3300 BCE, writing in the form of the cuneiform script. The Sumerian language, a language isolate, is the earliest known written language.

History

Sumerian history is conventionally divided into the Ubaid and Uruk periods, the Early Dynastic era of competing city-states, the Akkadian Empire under Sargon (c. 2334–2279 BCE), which first unified Sumer and Akkad, and the Neo-Sumerian Third Dynasty of Ur (c. 2112–2004 BCE), a final flowering of Sumerian culture under kings such as Ur-Namma and Shulgi. Thereafter Sumerian ceased to be spoken, though it long survived as a liturgical and scholarly language; political power passed to Babylonian and other kingdoms.

Achievements

The Sumerians are credited with the wheel, the plough, irrigation systems, the sexagesimal (base-60) system of mathematics and timekeeping, monumental temple towers (ziggurats), law collections such as the Code of Ur-Namma, and a rich literature including the Epic of Gilgamesh in its earliest forms, temple hymns and proverbs.
