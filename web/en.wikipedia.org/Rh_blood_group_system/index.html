Rh blood group system

The Rh blood group system is a human blood group system. It contains proteins on the surface of red blood cells. After the ABO blood group system, it is most likely to be involved in transfusion reactions. The Rh blood group system consists of 49 defined blood group antigens, among which the five antigens D, C, c, E, and e are the most important. There is no d antigen. Rh(D) status of an individual is normally described with a positive or negative suffix after the ABO type (e.g., someone who is A Positive has the A antigen and the Rh(D) antigen, whereas someone who is A Negative lacks the Rh(D) antigen).

History

The term "Rh" was originally an abbreviation of "Rhesus factor". It was discovered in 1937 by Karl Landsteiner and Alexander S. Wiener, who, at the time, believed it to be a similar antigen found in rhesus macaque red blood cells. It was subsequently discovered that the human factor is not identical to the rhesus monkey factor, but by then "Rh" was already in wide use. In 1940, Landsteiner and Wiener reported that serum from rabbits and guinea pigs immunised with rhesus red cells agglutinated most human red cells, and the clinical importance of the factor for transfusion and pregnancy soon became clear.

Nomenclature

Two main terminologies describe the system: the Fisher–Race nomenclature, which postulates three closely linked genes (C/c, D/d, E/e), and the Wiener nomenclature, based on a single gene with multiple alleles. In practice, the genetics are explained by two genes, RHD and RHCE, located on chromosome 1. The D antigen is encoded by RHD; people who are Rh-negative most commonly lack the RHD gene entirely.

Clinical significance

Antibodies to Rh antigens can cause haemolytic transfusion reactions and, importantly, haemolytic disease of the newborn: an Rh-negative mother sensitised by a previous pregnancy with an Rh-positive fetus may produce anti-D antibodies that cross the placenta and attack the red blood cells of an Rh-positive fetus in a subsequent pregnancy. This is preventable by administering anti-D immunoglobulin (such as Rho(D) immune globulin) during and after pregnancy.

Distribution

The frequency of Rh-negative individuals varies widely among populations: roughly 15 percent of people of European ancestry are Rh-negative, the proportion is lower in most African populations and only about 1 percent or less in East Asian populations. The Basques have one of the highest known frequencies of Rh-negative blood.
