Point process

In probability and statistics, a point process or point field is a collection of mathematical points randomly located on a mathematical space such as the real line or Euclidean space. Point processes can be used as mathematical models of phenomena or objects representable as points in some type of space — for example, the positions of trees in a forest, arrivals of customers, or locations of earthquakes.

Definitions

A point process can be defined as a random counting measure: a random measure that assigns to each set the (random) number of points of the process falling in it. It is called simple if no two points coincide with probability one.

Poisson point process

The best-known example is the Poisson point process, in which numbers of points in disjoint regions are independent Poisson random variables. It models purely random, unclustered patterns and appears in queueing theory, telecommunications, and spatial statistics. The one-dimensional homogeneous Poisson process is the classic model of event arrivals in time.

Other types

Other families include Cox processes (doubly stochastic Poisson processes), cluster processes such as the Neyman–Scott process, hard-core and Gibbs processes with repulsion between points, and determinantal point processes, used in spatial statistics, ecology, forestry, and machine learning.
