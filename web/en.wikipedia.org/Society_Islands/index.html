The Society Islands (French: Îles de la Société; Tahitian: Tōtaiete mā) are an archipelago in the South Pacific Ocean that forms part of French Polynesia. Politically divided into the Windward Islands and the Leeward Islands, the group includes Tahiti, Mo'orea, Bora Bora, Raiatea, Huahine, and Maiao, and contains the majority of French Polynesia's population.

Geography

The islands are volcanic, formed over a hotspot, with rugged interiors rising from coral reefs and lagoons. Tahiti, the largest island, carries Mount Orohena (2,241 m), the highest point in French Polynesia, and the capital Papeete. The Windward group (Tahiti, Mo'orea, and nearby atolls) lies east of the Leeward group (Raiatea, Taha'a, Bora Bora, Huahine, Maupiti).

History

Settled by Polynesian voyagers around the turn of the second millennium CE, the islands were a centre of Polynesian religion and voyaging culture; Raiatea's great marae Taputapuātea is now a UNESCO World Heritage Site. European contact began in the eighteenth century — Samuel Wallis reached Tahiti in 1767, followed by Bougainville and Cook, who named the group (probably after the Royal Society). Tahiti's Pomare dynasty and later French protectorates led to annexation by France in the nineteenth century.

Economy and culture

Tourism is central to the economy, especially around Bora Bora and Mo'orea; black pearl farming and agriculture are also significant. The islands are famed for Polynesian dance, tattooing traditions, and outrigger canoe racing.
