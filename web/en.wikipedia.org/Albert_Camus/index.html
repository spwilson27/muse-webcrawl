Albert Camus

Born: 7 November 1913, Mondovi, French Algeria
Died: 4 January 1960, Villeblevin, France
Notable works: The Stranger, The Myth of Sisyphus, The Plague
Awards: Nobel Prize in Literature (1957)

Albert Camus (7 November 1913 – 4 January 1960) was a French philosopher, author, dramatist, and journalist. He is best known for his philosophical concept of the absurd and for novels including The Stranger (1942), The Plague (1947), and The Fall (1956). He received the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1957 "for his important literary production, which with clear-sighted earnestness illuminates the problems of the human conscience in our times".

Life

Camus was born in French Algeria to a working-class pied-noir family; his father was killed in the First World War in 1914. He studied philosophy at the University of Algiers, where illness (tuberculosis) ended his hopes of a football career. During the Second World War he joined the French Resistance and edited the clandestine newspaper Combat. After the war he rose to prominence in Parisian intellectual life, though he fell out bitterly with Jean-Paul Sartre over communism.

Philosophy

In The Myth of Sisyphus (1942), Camus argued that human life is defined by the absurd — the confrontation between our desire for meaning and the silent indifference of the universe — and that the proper response is lucid revolt rather than suicide or false hope. The Rebel (1951) extended the theme into politics, opposing totalitarian violence. Although often labelled an existentialist, Camus rejected the term.

Death and legacy

Camus died in a car accident in January 1960, aged 46; the manuscript of his unfinished novel The First Man was found in the wreckage and published posthumously in 1994. His works remain among the most widely read of 20th-century French literature.
