Hurricane Katrina was a devastating tropical cyclone that caused 1,392 fatalities and about $125 billion in damage, particularly in the city of New Orleans and surrounding areas, in August 2005. It is tied with Hurricane Harvey (2017) as the costliest Atlantic hurricane on record. Katrina formed over the Bahamas on August 23, 2005, crossed South Florida as a Category 1 hurricane, then strengthened rapidly over the Gulf of Mexico to Category 5 intensity with maximum sustained winds of 175 mph (280 km/h), before weakening and making its final landfall as a Category 3 storm near Buras, Louisiana, on August 29.

Flooding of New Orleans

Although the storm surge was most extreme on the Mississippi Gulf Coast—where communities such as Gulfport, Biloxi, and Waveland were devastated—the catastrophe in New Orleans resulted chiefly from the failure of the federally built levee and floodwall system. More than 50 breaches occurred, including on the 17th Street, London Avenue, and Industrial canals, inundating roughly 80% of the city, in places to depths of several metres. Tens of thousands of residents who had not evacuated were stranded, many gathering at the Louisiana Superdome and the Ernest N. Morial Convention Center amid shortages of food, water, and sanitation. Subsequent investigations, including by the American Society of Civil Engineers, found design and construction flaws in the flood protection system.

Response and criticism

The disaster response by local, state, and federal authorities was widely criticised as slow and poorly coordinated. The Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) and its director Michael D. Brown became focal points of blame; Brown resigned in September 2005. Mayor Ray Nagin and Governor Kathleen Blanco also faced scrutiny over evacuation timing and deployment of resources. Over a million people were displaced from the Gulf Coast—the largest diaspora in US history—and New Orleans's population fell by more than half, from about 484,000 before the storm to roughly 230,000 a year later, recovering only gradually in subsequent decades.

Aftermath

In Katrina's wake, Congress reformed federal disaster law through the Post-Katrina Emergency Management Reform Act of 2006, and the Army Corps of Engineers constructed a $14.5 billion upgraded levee and surge-barrier system around New Orleans. The storm reshaped debate about coastal land loss in Louisiana, racial and economic inequality in disaster vulnerability, and the influence of climate change on hurricane intensity. The name Katrina was retired from the Atlantic naming lists and replaced with Katia.
