Fog bow

A fog bow, sometimes called a white rainbow, is an atmospheric optical phenomenon similar to a rainbow, produced when sunlight is refracted and reflected by the tiny water droplets in fog rather than by raindrops. Because the droplets are very small — typically less than 0.05 millimeters across — diffraction broadens and overlaps the colors, so a fog bow appears almost entirely white, sometimes with faint reddish outer and bluish inner edges.

Formation

Fog bows appear opposite the Sun when it is low (less than about 35–40 degrees above the horizon) and shines on a bank of fog in front of the observer. Like a rainbow, the bow forms at an angular radius of roughly 42 degrees around the antisolar point, though the band is broader and paler. From mountains or aircraft, a full circular fog bow may be seen, sometimes accompanied by a glory around the observer's shadow.

Appearance and observation

The white arc can look ghostly and is easily missed in bright conditions; it is most often reported over misty fields, sea fog, and mountain ridges in the early morning or late afternoon. Mariners historically called it the "sea dog."

Related phenomena

Cloud bows form in clouds by the same mechanism, and similar white bows can occur in moonlight, though they are much fainter. The size of the bow's band varies with droplet size, making it a rough indicator of the fog's microphysics.
