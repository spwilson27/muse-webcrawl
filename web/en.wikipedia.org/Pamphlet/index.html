Pamphlet

Pamphlet
FormatUnbound booklet, typically 5–48 pages
ConstructionSingle sheet folded, or sheets stapled/saddle-stitched
EtymologyFrom Pamphilus, seu de Amore, a popular 12th-century poem

A pamphlet is an unbound book — that is, without a hard cover or binding. Pamphlets may consist of a single sheet of paper printed on both sides and folded in half, in thirds, or in fourths, called a leaflet, or they may be made up of a few pages folded in half and stapled at the fold, called a booklet. Under UNESCO's definition, a pamphlet is a non-periodical printed publication of 5 to 48 pages.

History

Cheap to produce and distribute, pamphlets became a central medium of political and religious controversy from the Reformation onward: Luther's pamphlets flooded Germany in the 1520s, English pamphlet wars raged during the Civil War, and pamphleteering flourished in the American and French Revolutions. Famous examples include Thomas Paine's Common Sense (1776), which sold over 100,000 copies in the American colonies, and Jonathan Swift's A Modest Proposal (1729). Collections such as the Thomason Tracts preserve tens of thousands of seventeenth-century English pamphlets.

Modern use

Today the term most often denotes small informational booklets used in marketing, public health, education, and activism, as well as the samizdat and zine traditions that descend from the pamphlet format. Bibliographers note that pamphlets' ephemerality makes surviving early examples rare and valuable.
