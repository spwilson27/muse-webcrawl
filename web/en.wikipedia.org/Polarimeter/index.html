Polarimeter

A polarimeter is a scientific instrument used to measure optical rotation: the angle of rotation caused by passing linearly polarized light through an optically active substance.

Some chemical substances are optically active, and linearly polarized (uniformly oriented) light will rotate either to the left (counter-clockwise) or right (clockwise) when passed through these substances. The amount by which the light is rotated is known as the angle of rotation, and is a characteristic property of the substance, depending also on its concentration, the path length of the sample tube, the temperature and the wavelength of the light used.

Construction and principle

A typical polarimeter consists of a light source (historically a sodium lamp giving the yellow D line at 589 nm), a polarizer that produces linearly polarized light, a sample tube holding the substance under test, and a rotatable analyser through which the observer (or a photodetector) views the field. By turning the analyser until a reference condition — such as equal illumination of a split (half-shadow) field — is restored, the observer measures the angle through which the substance has rotated the plane of polarization.

Applications

Polarimetry is widely used in the sugar industry, where instruments known as saccharimeters measure the concentration of sugar solutions; in chemistry, for identifying and characterising chiral compounds and determining enantiomeric excess; and in pharmaceuticals and food science, for quality control of optically active products.

History

The rotation of polarized light by quartz was discovered by François Arago in 1811, and Jean-Baptiste Biot showed that organic liquids such as turpentine also rotate light. Louis Pasteur's celebrated 1848 work on tartaric acid crystals used optical activity to establish the foundations of stereochemistry. Modern automatic polarimeters use photoelectric detection and the Faraday effect to measure rotations with great precision.
