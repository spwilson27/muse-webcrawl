Waziristan is a mountainous region in northwestern Pakistan, bordering Afghanistan, covering the former tribal agencies of North Waziristan and South Waziristan. It is inhabited mainly by Pashtun tribes, principally the Wazir and Mahsud, and is famous for a long history of fierce independence and resistance to outside rule. Until 2018 the area was part of the Federally Administered Tribal Areas (FATA); it is now administered as districts of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa province.

Geography

The region is a rugged land of barren hills, deep ravines and seasonal torrent beds, lying between the Kurram River to the north and the Gomal River to the south. The Tochi and Gomal passes connect it to Afghanistan. Agriculture is sparse, and settlements are often fortified tower houses in tribal territory.

History

British forces mounted repeated expeditions into Waziristan from the 1860s onward, facing celebrated resistance figures such as the Faqir of Ipi, who waged guerrilla war from the 1930s into the 1950s. Under British rule and after 1947 under Pakistan, the area was governed through political agents and tribal jirgas under the Frontier Crimes Regulation. Following the U.S.-led invasion of Afghanistan in 2001, Waziristan became a refuge for Taliban and al-Qaeda-linked militants; the Pakistani military launched major operations, notably Operation Zarb-e-Azb in North Waziristan (2014), which displaced hundreds of thousands of residents. In 2018 the 25th Amendment merged FATA into Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, formally ending the region's separate status.

Society

Pushtunwali, the traditional Pashtun code centred on honour, hospitality and the jirga (council of elders), remains influential. The region has historically had low literacy and limited infrastructure, though road, school and development projects have expanded since the merger.
