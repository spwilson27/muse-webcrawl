Gamma matrices

FieldQuantum field theory; representation theory
Introduced byPaul Dirac (1928)
Defining relation{γμ, γν} = 2ημν I4
Common representationsDirac, Weyl (chiral), Majorana

In mathematical physics, the gamma matrices, also called the Dirac matrices, are a set of conventional matrices with specific anticommutation relations that generate a matrix representation of the Clifford algebra Cl1,3(R). They were introduced by Paul Dirac in 1928 in constructing the Dirac equation, a relativistic wave equation for spin-1/2 particles such as the electron.

Definition

The four gamma matrices γ0, γ1, γ2, γ3 satisfy the anticommutation relation {γμ, γν} = γμγν + γνγμ = 2ημν I4, where η is the Minkowski metric. A fifth matrix, γ5, defined as the product iγ0γ1γ2γ3, anticommutes with each γμ and plays a central role in describing chirality and weak interactions.

Representations

In the Dirac representation, γ0 is built from the identity and zero blocks while the spatial γi use the Pauli matrices. In the Weyl (chiral) representation, γ5 is diagonal, separating left- and right-handed spinor components. The Majorana representation makes all gamma matrices purely imaginary. Traces of products of gamma matrices, computed with their standard identities, are a workhorse of perturbative quantum electrodynamics.
