Excitotoxicity

FieldNeuroscience, neurotoxicology
Principal agentGlutamate
Key receptorsNMDA, AMPA, kainate
Associated withStroke, traumatic brain injury, epilepsy, neurodegenerative disease

Excitotoxicity is the pathological process by which neurons are damaged and killed by excessive stimulation from excitatory neurotransmitters, principally glutamate. When glutamate receptors — especially the NMDA receptor — are overactivated, large amounts of calcium ions (Ca²+) flood into the cell. The calcium influx triggers a cascade of destructive enzymes, free-radical production, and mitochondrial failure that can culminate in cell death by necrosis or apoptosis.

Mechanism

Under normal conditions glutamate is rapidly cleared from the synapse by transporter proteins. After ischaemia, trauma, or seizure, energy failure disables these transporters (and can reverse them), extracellular glutamate rises, and surrounding neurons are exposed to sustained stimulation. NMDA-receptor overactivation is central because of its high calcium permeability; downstream activation of nitric oxide synthase, phospholipases, and proteases such as calpain damages membranes, proteins, and DNA. The concept was established by John Olney, who coined the term in 1969 after observing that monosodium glutamate destroyed neurons in the mouse retina and brain, and by earlier work of D. R. Lucas and J. P. Newhouse (1957).

Clinical significance

Excitotoxicity is implicated in the secondary injury that follows stroke and traumatic brain injury — the expanding wave of cell death beyond the initial lesion — and in status epilepticus, spinal cord injury, and some neurodegenerative conditions including Alzheimer's, Huntington's, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis. Therapeutic attempts to block it have been difficult: NMDA antagonists such as memantine are approved for Alzheimer's disease, and riluzole (which reduces glutamate release) for ALS, but many candidate neuroprotectants that succeeded in animals failed in human stroke trials, partly owing to the narrow time window and partly to the side effects of interfering with normal excitatory transmission.
