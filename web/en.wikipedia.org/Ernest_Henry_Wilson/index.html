Ernest Henry "Chinese" Wilson (15 February 1876 – 15 October 1930) was an English plant explorer and botanist who introduced some 2,000 Asian plant species to cultivation in the West. Frequently described as one of the foremost plant hunters of his generation, he spent more than a decade travelling in China and later collected in Japan, Korea, and Formosa (Taiwan).

Expeditions to China

Wilson trained at Birmingham Botanical Gardens and Kew before being hired in 1899 by the nursery firm of Veitch to travel to western China in search of the dove tree (Davidia involucrata). On his first two expeditions for Veitch (1899–1905) he introduced hundreds of species, including the dove tree, the kiwi fruit vine (Actinidia deliciosa, then grown as a curiosity), and many rhododendrons, maples, and primulas.

From 1907 he collected for the Arnold Arboretum of Harvard University under Charles Sprague Sargent. His most celebrated introduction came in 1910 with the regal lily (Lilium regale), whose bulbs he gathered in the Min River valley of Sichuan; he broke his leg badly on that expedition and walked with a limp thereafter. His three-volume Plantae Wilsonianae documented the Arboretum's Chinese collections.

Later career and death

Wilson became keeper of the Arnold Arboretum in 1927 and wrote popular accounts of his travels, including China, Mother of Gardens (1929). He and his wife Helen were killed in a car accident near Worcester, Massachusetts, in October 1930. More than 60 plants bear the epithet wilsonii or wilsoniae in his honour, and gardens in the West remain full of his introductions — lilies, azaleas, dogwoods, and the paperbark maple among them.
