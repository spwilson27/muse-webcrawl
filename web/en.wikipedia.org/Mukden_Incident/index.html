Mukden Incident

Date18 September 1931
LocationNear Mukden (Shenyang), Manchuria, China
Staged byOfficers of the Japanese Kwantung Army
ResultJapanese invasion and occupation of Manchuria; creation of Manchukuo

The Mukden incident was a false flag event staged by Japanese military personnel as a pretext for the 1931 Japanese invasion of Manchuria. On September 18, 1931, Lieutenant Suemori Kawamoto of the Imperial Japanese Army detonated a small quantity of dynamite close to a railway line owned by Japan's South Manchuria Railway near Mukden (now Shenyang). The explosion was so weak that it failed to destroy the track and a train passed over it minutes later. The Imperial Japanese Army accused Chinese dissidents of the act and responded with a full invasion, leading to the occupation of Manchuria, which the Japanese then established as the puppet state of Manchukuo six months later. The deception was exposed by the Lytton Report of 1932, leading Japan to withdraw from the League of Nations.

Background

Japan had held extensive rights in southern Manchuria since the Russo-Japanese War, guarded by the Kwantung Army. Friction with the Chinese warlord Zhang Xueliang's administration, the effects of the Great Depression, and ambitions of army officers such as Kanji Ishiwara and Seishiro Itagaki for a resource-rich continental base led hard-liners to plan an incident that would justify seizing the whole region.

Events and invasion

After the explosion at the Liutiaohu section of track, Japanese troops attacked the Chinese barracks at Beidaying and seized Mukden's arsenal and airfield. Acting without orders from Tokyo — and in defiance of the civilian government's policy of non-expansion — the Kwantung Army fanned out across Manchuria; by February 1932 the entire region was under Japanese control. China appealed to the League of Nations and the United States, which responded with diplomatic protest and the Stimson Doctrine of non-recognition of territorial changes made by force.

Aftermath

The League's Lytton Commission concluded that Japanese forces had not acted in self-defence and that Manchukuo was not a genuine independence movement; when the League Assembly adopted the report in February 1933, Japan withdrew from the League. The incident is regarded by historians as a milestone on the road to the Second Sino-Japanese War (1937–1945) and the Pacific War, and 18 September is commemorated in China as a day of national humiliation.
