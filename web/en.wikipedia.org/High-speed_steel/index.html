High-speed steel
Alloy of iron with tungsten, molybdenum, chromium, vanadium and sometimes cobalt; hardness retained to ~600 °C.

High-speed steel (HSS or HS) is a subset of tool steels, commonly used in tool bits and cutting tools. Its defining property is the ability to retain hardness at the high temperatures generated during high-speed machining — a property known as red hardness — allowing cutting tools to operate far faster than those of plain carbon steel.

History

High-speed steel was developed around 1900 by Frederick Winslow Taylor and Maunsel White, who discovered that heating tungsten-bearing tool steel to unusually high temperatures dramatically improved its performance. Tools of the new alloy could cut so fast that they glowed dull red yet kept their edge, revolutionising machining productivity.

Composition and grades

Typical HSS alloys contain large proportions of tungsten or molybdenum, together with chromium and vanadium. Common grades include M2, a molybdenum-based general-purpose grade used for drills and milling cutters, and T1, the original tungsten grade. Cobalt-bearing grades such as M35 and M42 offer still greater hot hardness for demanding applications.

Uses

HSS is used for drill bits, end mills, taps, saw blades, lathe tools and broaches. Although cemented carbides outperform it in many production settings, HSS remains valued for its toughness, ease of sharpening and lower cost.
