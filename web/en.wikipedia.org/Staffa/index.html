Staffa (from Old Norse stafi, "pillar island") is an uninhabited island of the Inner Hebrides off the west coast of Scotland. It is famous for its dramatic volcanic geology — great hexagonal basalt columns formed by a Palaeogene lava flow — and above all for Fingal's Cave, a sea cave whose columnar walls and natural acoustics have inspired artists and composers since the eighteenth century.

Geology

Staffa is the emergent tip of a basalt plateau built up by lava flows of the North Atlantic Igneous Province some 55–58 million years ago, the same eruptive episode that formed the Giant's Causeway in Northern Ireland and the island of Fingal's Cave's parent flows on Mull. As the thick lava cooled slowly, it contracted and cracked into the regular, mostly six-sided columns visible around the island's cliffs and in the roof of Fingal's Cave, which is about 72 metres long.

History and culture

The island was brought to wide attention in 1772 by the naturalist Joseph Banks, and visits by Sir Walter Scott, William Wordsworth, Jules Verne, Queen Victoria and Felix Mendelssohn followed. Mendelssohn's 1829 visit, and the sound of waves in the cave, inspired his overture The Hebrides ("Fingal's Cave"). A small population of crofting tenants — 16 people in 1772 — dwindled through the nineteenth century, and the island has been uninhabited since then. It came into the care of the National Trust for Scotland in 1986 and is a National Nature Reserve, noted for its breeding puffins and other seabirds.

Access

Boat trips to Staffa run in season from Fionnphort and Ulva Ferry on Mull and from Oban; landings are weather-dependent, and visitors can walk along the column tops to the mouth of Fingal's Cave.
