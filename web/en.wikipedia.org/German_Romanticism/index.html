German Romanticism

German Romanticism was the dominant intellectual and artistic movement in the German-speaking world from the late 18th century into the mid-19th century. Against the rationalism of the Enlightenment and the classical ideals of Weimar Classicism, the Romantics prized feeling, imagination, nature, the fragmentary and the infinite longing expressed in the concept of Sehnsucht.

Early Romanticism (Jena)

The movement began in the 1790s around the circle at Jena, including the brothers August Wilhelm and Friedrich Schlegel, Novalis (Friedrich von Hardenberg), Ludwig Tieck and the philosopher F. W. J. Schelling. The journal Athenäum (1798–1800) published their programme of "progressive universal poetry". Novalis's image of the "blue flower" in his novel Heinrich von Ofterdingen became an emblem of Romantic longing.

Later Romanticism (Heidelberg and Berlin)

The Heidelberg circle of Achim von Arnim and Clemens Brentano collected German folk songs in Des Knaben Wunderhorn (1805–1808), and the Brothers Grimm gathered their famous fairy tales, contributing to a growing sense of German cultural identity during the Napoleonic era. E. T. A. Hoffmann's tales of the uncanny influenced literature across Europe, while painters such as Caspar David Friedrich created iconic images of solitary figures before vast landscapes.

Music and philosophy

German Romanticism profoundly shaped music, from Beethoven and Schubert through Weber, Schumann, Mendelssohn, Brahms and Wagner, and its ideas drew on Kant, Fichte and Schelling's philosophy of nature. Its themes — the sublime, the folk spirit, the medieval past and the fusion of the arts — spread throughout European culture.
