Civilian Conservation Corps

The Civilian Conservation Corps (CCC) was a voluntary government jobs program that operated from 1933 to 1942 in the United States for unemployed, unmarried men ages 18–25 and eventually expanded to include men ages 17–28. The CCC was a major part of President Franklin D. Roosevelt's New Deal that supplied manual labor jobs related to the conservation and development of natural resources in rural lands owned by federal, state, and local governments. The CCC was designed to supply jobs for young men and to relieve families who had difficulty finding jobs during the Great Depression, while simultaneously implementing a general natural resource conservation program in every state and territory.

Operations

Established by the Emergency Conservation Work Act signed on 31 March 1933, the CCC enrolled its first men within weeks. Enrollees lived in camps administered by the Army, worked under the direction of the Departments of Agriculture and the Interior, and were paid $30 a month, of which $25 was sent home to their families. Robert Fechner directed the program throughout most of its existence. At its peak in 1935 the CCC operated some 2,600 camps employing about 500,000 men.

Accomplishments

Over nine years, more than three million men participated. The CCC planted more than three billion trees to help reforest America, constructed trails and shelters in more than 800 parks nationwide, updated forest fire fighting methods, and built a network of service buildings and public roadways in remote areas. CCC crews fought fires, battled soil erosion on millions of acres, stocked fish, built flood-control works, and developed much of the infrastructure still used in national and state parks. The program also provided education and vocational training to enrollees, many of whom learned to read through camp classes.

Public response and legacy

The CCC was one of the most popular New Deal programs, drawing support across party lines. Although segregated in practice, with separate camps for Black enrollees and limited enrolment for Native Americans through a distinct Indian Division, it supplied vital income to families across the country. Congress declined to make it permanent, and it was liquidated in 1942 as employment shifted to war production. Its model inspired later programs including the Peace Corps, AmeriCorps, and state conservation corps.
