Yupik languages

The Yupik languages are a family of languages spoken by the Yupik peoples of western and south-central Alaska and the Russian Far East. They belong to the Eskimo branch of the Eskimo–Aleut language family, alongside the Inuit languages, from which they diverged roughly a thousand years ago. The Yupik languages are polysynthetic and ergative, building very long, complex words from roots and many suffixes.

Languages

The Yupik family is usually divided into the following languages:

  • Central Alaskan Yup'ik — the largest indigenous language in Alaska by number of speakers, with a number of dialects including General Central Yup'ik, Chevak Cup'ik, and Nunivak Cup'ig.
  • Alutiiq (Pacific Gulf Yupik, Sugpiaq) — spoken on the Alaska Peninsula, Kodiak Island, and the Kenai Peninsula coast.
  • Central Siberian Yupik (Yuit) — spoken on St. Lawrence Island, Alaska, and across the Bering Strait in Chukotka, Russia.
  • Naukan Yupik — spoken on the Chukotka Peninsula of Russia; severely endangered.
  • Sirenik Yupik — formerly spoken in the village of Sireniki, Russia; its last native speaker died in 1997, and its classification within Yupik is debated.

Grammar

Yupik languages are highly polysynthetic: a single word can express what English requires a whole sentence to say, by combining a root with derivational "postbases" and inflectional endings. Nouns and verbs inflect for person and number, distinguishing singular, dual, and plural. The languages are ergative–absolutive in their case alignment and have basic word order subject–object–verb.

Status

Central Alaskan Yup'ik remains one of the most widely spoken Indigenous languages in Alaska, though the proportion of children learning it has declined; it is taught in immersion and bilingual programs in the Yukon–Kuskokwim Delta. The Siberian varieties are endangered, with Naukan reduced to a few dozen mostly elderly speakers. All Yupik languages are official languages of Alaska under a 2014 state law.
