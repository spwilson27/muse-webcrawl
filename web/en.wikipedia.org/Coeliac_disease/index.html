Coeliac disease is a long-term autoimmune disorder, primarily affecting the small intestine, in which eating gluten — a group of proteins found in wheat, barley and rye — triggers an immune response that damages the intestinal lining. It occurs in people genetically predisposed by the HLA-DQ2 or HLA-DQ8 genes and affects roughly one in a hundred people, though many cases remain undiagnosed.

Signs and symptoms

Classic symptoms include chronic diarrhoea, abdominal distension, malabsorption, weight loss and, in children, failure to thrive. Many patients, however, present with non-classical features — iron-deficiency anaemia, fatigue, osteoporosis, mouth ulcers, or the intensely itchy blistering skin rash dermatitis herpetiformis — and a substantial minority have no symptoms at all ("silent" coeliac disease) despite ongoing intestinal damage.

Mechanism

In the small intestine, the enzyme tissue transglutaminase modifies gluten peptides (such as gliadin), which are then presented by HLA-DQ2/DQ8 molecules to T cells. The resulting inflammation flattens the villi that absorb nutrients — villous atrophy — and deepens the intestinal crypts, impairing absorption of iron, folate, calcium and fat-soluble vitamins.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis usually begins with serology — antibodies to tissue transglutaminase (tTG-IgA) — while the patient is still eating gluten, and is confirmed in adults by duodenal biopsy; genetic testing can rule the condition out. The only effective treatment is a strict, lifelong gluten-free diet, avoiding all wheat, barley and rye — including products such as hollandaise-style sauces thickened with flour — which allows the intestinal lining to heal and greatly reduces the risk of complications, including rare intestinal lymphomas. The Dutch paediatrician Willem Karel Dicke established the role of wheat during the Dutch famine of 1944–45, when coeliac children improved while bread was scarce.
