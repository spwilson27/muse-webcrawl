Osmoregulation

Osmoregulation is the active regulation of the osmotic pressure of an organism's body fluids, detected by osmoreceptors, to maintain the homeostasis of the organism's water content; that is, it maintains the fluid balance and the concentration of electrolytes (salts in solution) to keep the body fluids from becoming too diluted or concentrated. Osmotic pressure is a measure of the tendency of water to move into one solution from another by osmosis.

Osmoregulators and osmoconformers

Osmoregulators tightly regulate internal solute concentrations despite the environment, while osmoconformers allow their internal osmolarity to match that of their surroundings; many marine invertebrates are osmoconformers. Freshwater fish constantly gain water by osmosis and excrete large volumes of dilute urine while actively absorbing salts at the gills. Marine bony fish face the opposite problem: they lose water to the sea, drink seawater, and excrete excess salt through chloride cells in the gills. Some animals, like sharks, retain urea so that their tissues are slightly hyperosmotic to seawater.

In mammals

In humans, the kidneys are the principal osmoregulatory organs. When blood osmolarity rises, osmoreceptors in the hypothalamus trigger thirst and the release of antidiuretic hormone (ADH, vasopressin), which increases water reabsorption in the collecting ducts of the nephron. When osmolarity falls, ADH release is suppressed and more dilute urine is produced. The renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system regulates sodium balance and blood volume, which are closely linked to water balance.

In plants

Plants regulate water and solute content through stomatal control of transpiration, vacuolar storage of ions, and the accumulation of compatible solutes such as proline under salt or drought stress. Mangroves and other halophytes exclude or excrete salt via glands and specialised roots.
