Continuity equation

A continuity equation in physics is an equation that describes the transport of some quantity. It is particularly simple and powerful when applied to a conserved quantity, but it can be generalized to apply to any extensive quantity. Since mass, energy, momentum, electric charge, and other natural quantities are conserved under their respective appropriate conditions, a variety of physical phenomena may be described using continuity equations.

General form

For a quantity with density ρ and flux j, the differential form of the continuity equation states that the rate of increase of the quantity within a volume equals the net inflow through its surface:

∂ρ/∂t + ∇·j = σ

where σ represents generation or destruction of the quantity (a source or sink term); for a strictly conserved quantity, σ = 0.

Applications

In fluid dynamics, the continuity equation expresses conservation of mass: ∂ρ/∂t + ∇·(ρu) = 0, which for incompressible flow in a pipe reduces to the statement that the product of cross-sectional area and velocity is constant, so fluid speeds up in a constriction. In electromagnetism, the same structure expresses conservation of electric charge, linking charge density to current density. Analogous equations govern probability in quantum mechanics (via the probability current) and heat and species transport in engineering.

Relation to conservation laws

Continuity equations are the local differential expression of conservation laws, connected through Noether's theorem to symmetries of the underlying physics. Their integral form, obtained by applying the divergence theorem, states the same balance for finite volumes and is the basis of finite-volume numerical methods.
