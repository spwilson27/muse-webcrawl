Legionella pneumophila

Legionella pneumophila is an aerobic, pleomorphic, flagellated, non-spore-forming, Gram-negative bacterium of the genus Legionella. It is the primary human pathogen in the genus and the causative agent of Legionnaires' disease, also known as legionellosis.


L. pneumophila under electron microscopy

Discovery

The bacterium was identified in 1977 by researchers at the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention investigating an outbreak of pneumonia among attendees of an American Legion convention at the Bellevue-Stratford Hotel in Philadelphia in the summer of 1976, which sickened 221 people and killed 34. The organism was isolated by Joseph McDade and Charles Shepard.

Pathogenesis

L. pneumophila is a facultative intracellular parasite. In the environment it invades free-living amoebae; in humans it infects alveolar macrophages, where it uses a type IV secretion system (Dot/Icm) to create a protective vacuole and replicate. Infection follows inhalation of contaminated aerosols from sources such as cooling towers and showers. Risk factors include older age, smoking, chronic lung disease, and immunosuppression.

Identification and treatment

The organism is fastidious and requires buffered charcoal yeast extract agar for culture. Most cases are diagnosed by a urinary antigen test detecting serogroup 1. Legionnaires' disease is treated with macrolides (such as azithromycin) or respiratory fluoroquinolones (such as levofloxacin).
