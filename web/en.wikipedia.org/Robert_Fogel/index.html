Robert William Fogel (July 1, 1926 – June 11, 2013) was an American economic historian and scientist, awarded the 1993 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences "for having renewed research in economic history by applying economic theory and quantitative methods in order to explain economic and institutional change." Together with Douglass North, with whom he shared the prize, he is regarded as a founder of cliometrics, also called the new economic history.

Work

Fogel's first major book, Railroads and American Economic Growth (1964), used counterfactual analysis to argue that railroads were not indispensable to American economic growth, since canals and roads could have supplied much of the same transport benefit. His most controversial work, Time on the Cross (1974), written with Stanley Engerman, applied quantitative methods to American slavery, arguing that slavery was profitable, that slave material conditions compared less unfavourably with those of free industrial workers than had been assumed, and that slavery was not economically moribund in 1860. The book provoked intense scholarly and public debate over both its findings and its methods.

In later work, collected in works such as The Escape from Hunger and Premature Death, 1700–2100 (2004), Fogel studied long-run improvements in nutrition, body size, health and longevity—what he termed "technophysio evolution"—and their contribution to economic growth.

Career

Fogel studied at Cornell University and Johns Hopkins University, where he received his PhD in 1963. He taught at the University of Chicago, Harvard University, and Cambridge, and spent his later career at Chicago's Booth School of Business.
