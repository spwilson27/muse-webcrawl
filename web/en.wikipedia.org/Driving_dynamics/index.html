Driving dynamics

Driving dynamics, also known as vehicle dynamics in its applied, driver-facing sense, is the study and description of how a motor vehicle responds to driver inputs while moving — accelerating, braking, steering and cornering. The term is used both in engineering, where it concerns the mathematical modelling of vehicle motion, and in motoring journalism, where it describes the subjective feel of a car's handling.

Fundamentals

A moving vehicle is governed by the forces generated at the four tyre contact patches. Longitudinal forces arise from acceleration and braking; lateral forces from steering. Because the tyres can only generate a limited total force — often visualised as a friction circle — combined manoeuvres such as braking while turning trade off against one another.

Key concepts include weight transfer, in which load shifts toward the rear under acceleration, toward the front under braking, and toward the outside wheels in a corner; slip angles, the difference between the direction a tyre points and the direction it actually travels; and the balance between understeer, where the front tyres saturate first and the car runs wide, and oversteer, where the rear saturates first and the tail steps out.

Factors affecting dynamics

Weight distribution, centre of gravity height, suspension geometry, wheelbase, track width, tyre construction and compound, aerodynamic downforce and electronic aids such as stability control all shape a vehicle's dynamic character. Drivetrain layout — front-wheel drive, rear-wheel drive or all-wheel drive — influences traction and balance.

Testing and evaluation

Manufacturers evaluate dynamics through instrumented tests such as steady-state cornering, lane-change manoeuvres (including the "moose test"), slaloms and laps of proving grounds such as the Nürburgring, as well as extensive subjective assessment by test drivers.
