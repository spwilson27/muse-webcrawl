Freon

Freon is a registered trademark of the Chemours Company (originally DuPont) that has become a genericised name for a family of haloalkane refrigerants, especially chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) and hydrochlorofluorocarbons (HCFCs). Individual products are designated by numbers, such as Freon-12 (dichlorodifluoromethane, CCl2F2), Freon-11 (trichlorofluoromethane), and Freon-22 (chlorodifluoromethane, an HCFC).

History

CFC refrigerants were developed in the late 1920s by a team at Frigidaire/General Motors led by Thomas Midgley Jr., who demonstrated their safety in 1930 by inhaling the gas and blowing out a candle flame. Non-toxic, non-flammable, and chemically stable, Freon compounds replaced dangerous early refrigerants such as ammonia, methyl chloride, and sulphur dioxide in refrigerators and air conditioners, and were also widely used as aerosol propellants and solvents.

Environmental phase-out

In 1974, Mario Molina and F. Sherwood Rowland showed that CFCs could reach the stratosphere and release chlorine that catalytically destroys ozone; the discovery of the Antarctic ozone hole in 1985 confirmed the danger. The Montreal Protocol (1987) mandated the phase-out of CFCs, completed in developed countries by 1996, and HCFCs such as Freon-22 are also being phased out. Successor hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs, such as R-134a) spare the ozone layer but are potent greenhouse gases, and are themselves being reduced under the Kigali Amendment in favour of alternatives such as hydrofluoroolefins and natural refrigerants.
