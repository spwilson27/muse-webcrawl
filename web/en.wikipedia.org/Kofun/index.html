Kofun
Japanese古墳 ("old tomb")
Periodc. 3rd–6th centuries CE (Kofun period)
Typical formKeyhole-shaped burial mound
LargestDaisenryō Kofun (attributed to Emperor Nintoku), ~486 m long

Kofun are megalithic tombs built in Japan between the 3rd and 6th centuries CE, giving their name to the Kofun period. Many are enormous earthen mounds surrounded by moats, most characteristically in a keyhole shape (zenpō-kōen-fun) — square at one end and circular at the other.

Construction and contents

Mounds were covered with stones and decorated with haniwa, terracotta figures of people, animals, houses and warriors placed on and around them. Inside, stone burial chambers held wooden coffins, bronze mirrors, weapons, armour, jewellery and horse trappings, reflecting the power of regional kings and the Yamato court.

Major examples

The Daisenryō Kofun in Sakai, Osaka, attributed to Emperor Nintoku, is among the largest tombs ever built, about 486 metres long. The Mozu-Furuichi Kofun Group was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2019. The Hashihaka Kofun near Nara is among the earliest large keyhole tombs.

Historical significance

The spread and standardisation of keyhole tombs tracks the consolidation of the Yamato state. Because most imperial kofun are administered by the Imperial Household Agency and cannot be excavated, many questions about their occupants remain debated.
