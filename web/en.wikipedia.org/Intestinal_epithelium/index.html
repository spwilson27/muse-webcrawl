Intestinal epithelium

The intestinal epithelium is the single-cell layer of epithelium that forms the lining of the lumen of the small and large intestines. Composed of simple columnar epithelium, it renews itself every few days — one of the fastest rates of turnover in the body — from stem cells located in the intestinal crypts (crypts of Lieberkühn).

Structure

In the small intestine the epithelium is thrown into finger-like projections called villi, and its cells bear dense microvilli (the brush border), enormously increasing the surface area for absorption. In the colon there are no villi, but deeper crypts adapted for water absorption and mucus secretion.

Cell types

  • Enterocytes — the dominant absorptive cells, with a brush border of digestive enzymes.
  • Goblet cells — secrete mucus that lubricates and protects the surface.
  • Paneth cells — at the crypt base; secrete antimicrobial peptides such as defensins and support the stem-cell niche.
  • Enteroendocrine cells — release hormones and peptides (e.g., secretin, cholecystokinin, GLP-1) regulating digestion.
  • M cells — overlie Peyer's patches and sample antigens for the immune system.
  • Tuft cells — chemosensory cells involved in responses to parasites.

Functions

The epithelium digests and absorbs nutrients, water and electrolytes; acts as a selective barrier sealed by tight junctions against luminal microbes and toxins; secretes mucus, enzymes and antimicrobial substances; and participates in immune surveillance through intraepithelial lymphocytes and associated lymphoid tissue. Disruption of the barrier or turnover is implicated in coeliac disease, inflammatory bowel disease and colorectal cancer.
