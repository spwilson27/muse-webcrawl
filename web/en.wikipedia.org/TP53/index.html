TP53

TP53 (tumor protein p53) is a gene on human chromosome 17 that encodes the transcription factor p53, often called the "guardian of the genome". It is the most frequently mutated gene in human cancer, altered in roughly half of all tumours.

Function

In normal cells p53 levels are kept low by the ubiquitin ligase MDM2. DNA damage, oncogene activation or other stresses stabilise p53, which then activates genes that pause the cell cycle (such as p21), coordinate DNA repair, or trigger senescence or apoptosis if damage is irreparable. This prevents the propagation of mutations.

Role in cancer

Most cancer-associated TP53 mutations are missense changes in the DNA-binding domain that abolish its tumour-suppressor activity, and some mutant proteins gain new oncogenic functions. Because loss of p53 permits genomic instability, tumours bearing TP53 mutations are often more aggressive. Germline mutations cause Li–Fraumeni syndrome, a strong hereditary predisposition to early cancers.

History and research

p53 was discovered in 1979 as a protein bound to SV40 large T antigen, and was initially mistaken for an oncogene before its true suppressor role was established around 1989. Restoring p53 function — with drugs such as MDM2 inhibitors or mutant-p53 reactivators — remains a major goal of cancer drug development.
