Twenty-foot equivalent unit

The twenty-foot equivalent unit (abbreviated TEU or teu) is an inexact unit of cargo capacity, often used to describe the capacity of container ships and container terminals. It is based on the volume of a 20-foot-long (6.1 m) intermodal container, a standard-sized metal box that can be easily transferred among different modes of transportation, such as ships, trains, and trucks.

Definition

A standard 20-foot container measures 20 feet long, 8 feet wide, and typically 8 feet 6 inches high, with an internal volume of roughly 1,172 cubic feet (33.2 m³) and a maximum gross mass of about 67,200 pounds (30,480 kg). Because containers also come in other lengths — most commonly 40 feet — capacity is expressed in TEU, with a 40-foot container counting as two TEU, or one forty-foot equivalent unit (FEU).

The unit is inexact because containers vary in height (notably "high cube" containers of 9 feet 6 inches) and because capacity figures may be quoted as nominal slots rather than actual loadable volume. Nevertheless, TEU remains the standard measure of container traffic: port throughput statistics, shipping line fleet capacities, and trade volumes are routinely reported in TEU. The largest container ships in service have capacities exceeding 24,000 TEU.

History

Containerization was pioneered in the 1950s by American trucking magnate Malcolm McLean, whose company Sea-Land Service began carrying truck trailers on ships in 1956. International standardization of container dimensions under the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) in the late 1960s fixed the 20-foot and 40-foot lengths, and the TEU emerged as the industry's common yardstick.
