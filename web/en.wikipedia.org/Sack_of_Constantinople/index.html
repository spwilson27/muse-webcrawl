Sack of Constantinople

Date12–13 April 1204
LocationConstantinople, Byzantine Empire
Part ofFourth Crusade
ResultCrusader victory; establishment of the Latin Empire
CommandersEnrico Dandolo; Boniface of Montferrat; Baldwin of Flanders

The Sack of Constantinople occurred in April 1204 during the Fourth Crusade, when a Crusader and Venetian army captured, looted and occupied the capital of the Byzantine Empire. It was the culmination of a crusade diverted first to Zara and then to Constantinople by promises of payment from the Byzantine prince Alexios IV Angelos. The sack is regarded as one of the most infamous episodes of the Crusades and a decisive blow to relations between the Latin West and the Orthodox East.

Background

The crusade, proclaimed by Pope Innocent III, depended on Venetian shipping contracted at a price the crusaders could not fully pay. To settle the debt the fleet attacked the Christian city of Zara in 1202, and in 1203 it sailed to Constantinople to install Alexios IV, who promised money, supplies and military aid. When Alexios was overthrown and murdered in early 1204, the crusaders resolved to conquer the city for themselves, agreeing in advance to divide the empire.

The sack

After a first assault failed on 9 April, the crusaders breached the sea walls along the Golden Horn on 12 April 1204. Three days of systematic plundering followed. Churches and monasteries were stripped of relics, gold and silver; the great bronze horses of the Hippodrome were carried off to St Mark's Basilica in Venice, where they remain known as the Horses of Saint Mark. Contemporary accounts, including that of the Byzantine historian Niketas Choniates, describe widespread murder, rape and destruction; the crusader Geoffrey of Villehardouin justified the seizure as lawful conquest. The amount of loot was enormous and was formally divided between the crusaders and Venice under the treaty known as the Partitio terrarum imperii Romaniae.

Aftermath

Count Baldwin of Flanders was crowned the first Latin Emperor in May 1204, but the Latin Empire never controlled much of the former Byzantine territory, where successor states arose at Nicaea, Epirus and Trebizond. In 1261 Michael VIII Palaiologos of Nicaea recovered Constantinople. The sack permanently embittered Greek–Latin relations; in 2001 Pope John Paul II expressed sorrow for the events of 1204 during a visit to Greece.
