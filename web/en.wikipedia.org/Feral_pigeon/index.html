Feral pigeon

Feral pigeons in an urban plaza

Feral pigeons (Columba livia domestica or Columba livia forma urbana), also known as city doves, city pigeons, or street pigeons, are descendants of domestic pigeons that have returned to the wild. The domestic pigeon was originally bred from the wild rock dove, which naturally inhabits sea cliffs and mountains. Feral pigeons find the ledges of buildings to be a substitute for sea cliffs, have become adapted to urban life, and are abundant in towns and cities throughout the world.

Description

Feral pigeons are essentially the same size and shape as the original wild rock dove, but often display far greater variation in colour and pattern than their wild ancestors. The blue-barred pattern which the original wild rock dove displays is generally less common in more urban areas. Urban pigeons tend to be darker in colour than those in rural areas.

Behaviour

Pigeons mate for life and are continuous breeders, laying eggs throughout the year when conditions are favourable. They feed on seeds, grains and human food scraps. Their droppings, which can accumulate on buildings, are a common complaint in cities, and large flocks are sometimes regarded as a nuisance, leading many municipalities to adopt control measures such as proofing buildings against perching, restricting feeding, or introducing birds of prey.

Predators and health

Urban pigeons are preyed upon by peregrine falcons, sparrowhawks and other raptors that have colonised cities. Although pigeons can carry diseases transmissible to humans, such as histoplasmosis and psittacosis, the risk to the general public is generally considered low.
