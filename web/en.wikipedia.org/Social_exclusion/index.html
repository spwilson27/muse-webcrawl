Social exclusion

Social exclusion
FieldSociology; social policy
Origin of termFrance, 1970s (René Lenoir)
AntonymSocial inclusion

Social exclusion or social marginalisation is the process by which individuals or groups are wholly or partially shut out of the social, economic, political, and cultural systems that determine a person's integration in society. Unlike poverty, which focuses on material resources, exclusion is multidimensional and relational, encompassing unemployment, poor housing, low skills, family breakdown, and lack of civic participation.

History of the concept

The term originated in France in the 1970s — René Lenoir's Les Exclus (1974) described categories of people falling outside social insurance — and was adopted by the European Union in the 1980s and 1990s as a central policy concept. The British government created a Social Exclusion Unit in 1997.

Dimensions and causes

Exclusion can arise from disability, race and ethnicity, age, gender, homelessness, addiction, or criminal records, and from structural factors such as deindustrialisation and welfare retrenchment. Hilary Silver distinguished three paradigms — solidarity, specialisation, and monopoly — through which exclusion is understood in different political traditions.

Responses

Policy responses emphasise social inclusion: employment programmes, area-based regeneration, education and early-years intervention, and anti-discrimination protections. Critics note that the language of exclusion can individualise structural problems or label communities as deficient.
