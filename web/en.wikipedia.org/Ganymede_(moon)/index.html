Ganymede is a satellite of Jupiter and the largest and most massive moon in the Solar System. With a diameter of about 5,268 km, it is larger than the planet Mercury, though less massive. It is the ninth-largest object in the Solar System and the largest without a substantial atmosphere.

Discovery and naming

Ganymede was discovered by Galileo Galilei in January 1610, along with the other Galilean moons Io, Europa and Callisto. It is named after Ganymede, the cup-bearer to the Greek gods in mythology.

Composition and structure

Ganymede is composed of roughly equal amounts of silicate rock and water ice. Evidence from the Galileo spacecraft and the Hubble Space Telescope indicates a saltwater ocean lying perhaps 100 km beneath the icy crust, potentially containing more water than all of Earth's surface oceans combined. Ganymede is the only moon known to generate its own internal magnetic field, attributed to convection in a liquid iron core.

Surface

The surface shows two main terrain types: dark, heavily cratered regions dating to about four billion years ago, and somewhat younger, lighter terrain marked by grooves and ridges formed by tectonic activity in the ice. A very thin atmosphere of oxygen has been detected, too tenuous to support life as we know it.

Exploration

Ganymede was visited by Pioneer 10, Voyager 1 and 2, Galileo (which orbited Jupiter 1995–2003) and Juno. The European Space Agency's JUICE spacecraft, launched in 2023, is scheduled to arrive at Jupiter in 2031 and eventually enter orbit around Ganymede, the first spacecraft to orbit a moon of another planet.
