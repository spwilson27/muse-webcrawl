Sacred Cenote

The Sacred Cenote (Cenote Sagrado) at Chichén Itzá, Yucatán.

The Sacred Cenote (Spanish: Cenote Sagrado, "sacred well") is a natural limestone sinkhole at the pre-Columbian Maya site of Chichen Itza in the Yucatán Peninsula of Mexico. A cenote is a water-filled sinkhole formed by the collapse of limestone bedrock. The Sacred Cenote measures about 60 metres (200 ft) in diameter, with steep walls dropping some 27 metres (89 ft) to green water below.

Ritual use

The ancient Maya revered cenotes as portals to the underworld, Xibalba, and as sources of water and communication with the rain god Chaac. Pilgrims came to the Sacred Cenote from across the Maya world to cast offerings into its waters, particularly during droughts, when human sacrifices were also made to petition for rain. A raised sacbé (causeway) connects the cenote to the main plaza of Chichen Itza.

Exploration

Between 1904 and 1907, American consul and amateur archaeologist Edward Herbert Thompson dredged the cenote, recovering thousands of objects of gold, jade, copper, pottery, copal incense, and carved bone, along with human skeletal remains. Thompson shipped much of the material to the Peabody Museum at Harvard University, provoking a decades-long ownership dispute with Mexico; Harvard returned part of the collection in the 1940s and more in 2008. Later expeditions in the 1960s, sponsored by Mexican institutions, recovered additional artifacts and clarified the stratigraphy of offerings, which spanned the site's occupation and continued after its decline.
