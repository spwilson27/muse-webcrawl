Philippe Pétain

Henri Philippe Benoni Omer Pétain (24 April 1856 – 23 July 1951), generally known as Philippe Pétain and Marshal Pétain, was a French general who attained the position of Marshal of France at the end of World War I, during which he became known as "the Lion of Verdun" for commanding the French forces in the Battle of Verdun. From 1940 to 1944, he was the chief of state of Vichy France during World War II — a role for which he was tried and convicted of treason after the war.

Early career and World War I

Pétain, the son of a farmer in the Pas-de-Calais, graduated from Saint-Cyr in 1878 and pursued a slow career as an infantry officer and instructor, noted for his doctrine of firepower ("le feu tue" — "fire kills") in an army committed to the offensive. On the verge of retirement as a colonel in 1914, he rose rapidly in the First World War, and in February 1916 took command of the defence of Verdun, organising the supply lifeline of the Voie Sacrée and rotating divisions through the battle (the noria), vowing "on ne passe pas" — "they shall not pass". In May 1917, after the failed Nivelle offensive and widespread mutinies, he became commander-in-chief, restored discipline with a mix of restraint, leave, better conditions and a defensive strategy pending American manpower and tanks. He was made Marshal of France in November 1918.

Between the wars

Pétain remained France's most prestigious soldier, commanded French forces in the Rif War in Morocco (1925), served briefly as minister of war (1934) and as ambassador to Franco's Spain (1939).

Vichy France

Recalled as deputy premier in May 1940 during the German invasion, Pétain, aged 84, favoured an armistice, which was signed on 22 June 1940. On 10 July the National Assembly voted him full powers; as Chief of the French State, based at Vichy, he presided over the authoritarian "National Revolution" (motto Travail, Famille, Patrie), collaboration with Nazi Germany, anti-Jewish statutes enacted on the regime's own initiative, roundups and deportations, and — after November 1942 — full German occupation. He was taken to Germany in 1944 and returned to France in April 1945.

Trial and death

Tried by the High Court in 1945, Pétain was convicted of intelligence with the enemy and sentenced to death; Charles de Gaulle commuted the sentence to life imprisonment in view of his age and his First World War service. He died in detention at the citadel of Port-Joinville on the Île d'Yeu in 1951, aged 95. His reputation remains a subject of enduring controversy in France, where his WWI prestige and his Vichy record are weighed against each other, and periodic campaigns seek his reburial at Verdun.
