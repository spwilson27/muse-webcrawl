Proto-Germanic language

Proto-Germanic (abbreviated PGmc; also called Common Germanic) is the reconstructed common ancestor of all Germanic languages. It is not directly attested by any coherent texts but has been reconstructed using the comparative method. Proto-Germanic developed from Proto-Indo-European in northern Europe and is generally held to have been spoken from roughly 500 BCE until about 500 CE, when it began to split into the East, North, and West Germanic branches.

Phonology

The defining phonological change separating Proto-Germanic from Proto-Indo-European is the consonant shift described by Grimm's law, which turned the Indo-European voiceless stops into fricatives and the voiced stops into voiceless stops, supplemented by Verner's law, which accounts for apparent exceptions when the preceding syllable was unstressed. Proto-Germanic fixed stress on the initial syllable of a word, a change that caused extensive reduction of unstressed endings in its daughter languages.

Morphology

Proto-Germanic retained much of the inflectional complexity of Proto-Indo-European, with nouns declined for four cases (nominative, accusative, genitive, dative) and three numbers, and verbs distinguished by tense (present and past) and mood. It innovated the dental preterite — the weak verb past tense in a dental suffix, the ancestor of English -ed — and divided adjectives into strong and weak declensions.

Descendants

East Germanic, represented by Gothic, is attested earliest in substantial texts (Wulfila's Bible translation, 4th century CE) but is now extinct. North Germanic gave rise to Old Norse and the modern Scandinavian languages, while West Germanic produced Old English, Old Frisian, Old Saxon, and Old High German, ancestors of English, Dutch, and German.
