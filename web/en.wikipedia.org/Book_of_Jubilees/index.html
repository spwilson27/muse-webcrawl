Book of Jubilees

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Book of Jubilees is an ancient Jewish religious work of 50 chapters (1,341 verses), considered canonical by the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church as well as by Haymanot Judaism, a denomination observed by members of the Ethiopian Jewish community known as Beta Israel, where it is known as the Book of Division (Ge'ez: መጽሐፈ ኩፋሌ, maṣḥafa kufale). It is also known to be used by some members of the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church.

Jubilees is considered one of the pseudepigrapha by Protestant, Roman Catholic, and Eastern Orthodox Churches. It is also not considered canonical within Judaism outside of the Beta Israel. It was well known to early Christians, as evidenced by the writings of Epiphanius, Justin Martyr, Origen, Diodorus of Tarsus, Isidore of Alexandria, Isidore of Seville, Eutychius of Alexandria, John Malalas, George Syncellus, and George Kedrenos.

Content

Jubilees covers much of the same ground as Genesis and part of Exodus, but often with additional detail, and addressing the entire period of creation up to God's handing the Law to Moses on Mount Sinai. In Jubilees, as in the Book of Enoch, the fallen angels play a significant role. The fallen angels are angels who were sent to Earth to teach men the law, but who then began to sin.

The book divides history into periods of 49 years, the "jubilee" of the title, each of which is divided into seven "weeks" of seven years. This chronology is used to date events and to insist on the observance of the solar calendar of 364 days. The narrative emphasizes the laws of Sabbath, circumcision, dietary restrictions, and the festivals, presenting them as having been observed by the patriarchs long before Moses.

Manuscripts and text

The text survives in its entirety only in the Ge'ez language, in numerous Ethiopian manuscripts. Fragments in Hebrew were found among the Dead Sea Scrolls at Qumran, confirming the antiquity of the work and its importance to the community there; Jubilees is among the most frequently attested works in the Qumran library. Greek fragments and a Latin translation of roughly a third of the book also survive.

Origins and dating

Scholars generally date the book to the second century BCE, roughly 160–150 BCE, in the Maccabean period. Robert Henry Charles argued that the author was a Pharisee and that the book represented the tradition of meticulous, legalistic Judaism. More recent scholarship has connected the work to sectarian circles related to those that produced parts of the Dead Sea Scrolls.
