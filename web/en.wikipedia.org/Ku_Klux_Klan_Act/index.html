Ku Klux Klan Act


The Ku Klux Klan Act of 1871 is formally the Civil Rights Act of 1871.

The Ku Klux Klan Act, formally the Civil Rights Act of 1871, is a United States federal law enacted on 20 April 1871 during the Reconstruction era, as the third of the Enforcement Acts passed to protect the civil rights of African Americans in the former Confederate states against violence by the Ku Klux Klan and similar groups.

Provisions

The Act made it a federal crime for persons acting "under color of law" to deprive others of rights secured by the Constitution, and gave victims a civil cause of action — the provision now codified at 42 U.S.C. § 1983, which became a principal vehicle for modern civil-rights litigation against state officials. Another section, now § 1985(3), targets private conspiracies to deprive persons of equal protection. The Act also authorised the President to suspend habeas corpus and use federal troops to suppress conspiracies; President Ulysses S. Grant invoked these powers in South Carolina, where federal prosecutions helped break the first Klan.

Legacy

Largely dormant after Reconstruction, § 1983 was revived by the Supreme Court in Monroe v. Pape (1961) and remains a cornerstone of American civil-rights law, while § 1985(3) was applied to private conspiracies in Griffin v. Breckenridge (1971).
