Mach's principle

Mach's principle
OriginatorErnst Mach (named by Albert Einstein)
FieldTheoretical physics, cosmology
Core ideaLocal inertia is determined by the large-scale distribution of matter in the universe

In theoretical physics, particularly in discussions of gravitation theories, Mach's principle (or Mach's conjecture) is the name Albert Einstein gave to an imprecise hypothesis often credited to the physicist and philosopher Ernst Mach. The idea is that the existence of absolute rotation — for example, the bulging of a rotating sphere or the swinging plane of a Foucault pendulum — is not meaningful in itself; rather, local inertial frames are determined by the distribution and motion of matter in the universe as a whole.

Newton's bucket: Mach argued the water's concavity reflects rotation relative to the distant stars, not absolute space.

History

Mach criticised Newton's notion of absolute space in his Science of Mechanics (1883), using Newton's rotating bucket argument: Mach suggested that the inertial effects Newton attributed to absolute space might instead be caused by the mass of the rest of the universe. Einstein was deeply influenced by this idea while developing general relativity and coined the term "Mach's principle" in 1918, hoping his theory would embody it — an expectation later debated, since general relativity admits solutions, such as empty universes and the Gödel universe, that are not obviously Machian.

Interpretations

Because Mach never stated the principle precisely, many distinct formulations exist — Einstein's biographer Abraham Pais counted 21 versions. Related effects in general relativity, such as frame-dragging (the Lense–Thirring effect), in which rotating masses drag local inertial frames, are often seen as partial realisations of Machian ideas. Alternative theories of gravity, notably Brans–Dicke theory, were constructed to be more fully Machian than general relativity.
