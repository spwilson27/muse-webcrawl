Rano Raraku

LocationEaster Island (Rapa Nui), Chile
TypeVolcanic crater and moai quarry
MaterialConsolidated volcanic ash (tuff)
ProtectionRapa Nui National Park; UNESCO World Heritage Site

Rano Raraku is a volcanic crater formed of consolidated volcanic ash, or tuff, on the lower slopes of Terevaka on Easter Island (Rapa Nui) in Chilean Polynesia. It was the principal quarry for the island's famous monolithic statues, the moai, for roughly 500 years, until the early eighteenth century.

The quarry

About 95 percent of the island's moai were carved from Rano Raraku's distinctive yellow-brown tuff. Nearly 400 statues remain in and around the crater in every stage of production — some still attached to the rock face, others finished and partly buried upright on the outer slopes, their bodies hidden by accumulated sediment so that only the heads show. The largest moai ever begun, nicknamed "El Gigante," lies unfinished in the quarry; at about 21.6 metres long it would have weighed an estimated 145–165 tonnes.

Crater lake and later use

The crater contains one of Easter Island's three freshwater crater lakes, fringed with totora reeds. After statue carving ceased, the Rano Raraku moai became associated with the island's later birdman (tangata manu) era, and petroglyphs of birdman motifs are carved on some statues. The site lies within Rapa Nui National Park, a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1995.
