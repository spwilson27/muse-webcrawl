Dacite

Dacite is a volcanic rock formed by rapid solidification of lava that is high in silica and low in alkali metal oxides. It has a fine-grained (aphanitic) to porphyritic texture and is intermediate in composition between andesite and rhyolite. It is the volcanic equivalent of granodiorite.

Composition and formation

Dacite consists mostly of plagioclase feldspar and quartz, with biotite, hornblende and pyroxene as minor constituents. In the QAPF classification it is defined by its content of quartz and feldspar; on the TAS diagram it falls in the O3 field. Dacitic magma is viscous, so dacite eruptions tend to be explosive or to extrude slowly as thick lava flows and domes. The name comes from Dacia, the Roman province between the Danube and the Carpathians where the rock was first described.

Occurrence

Dacite is characteristic of subduction-zone volcanism above oceanic plates. Notable dacite eruptions include the 1980 eruption of Mount St. Helens, whose initial blast was followed by dacite dome growth, the 1991 eruption of Mount Pinatubo, and the long-lived lava dome at Soufrière Hills, Montserrat. Dacite is also found at Mount Unzen in Japan, in the Taupo Volcanic Zone of New Zealand, and around the Aegean and the Andes.
