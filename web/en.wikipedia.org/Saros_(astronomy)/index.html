The saros is a period of exactly 223 synodic months — approximately 6,585.32 days, or about 18 years, 11 days and 8 hours — after which the Sun, Earth and Moon return to nearly the same relative geometry, so that a nearly identical eclipse occurs. The cycle was known to Babylonian astronomers and has been used for eclipse prediction for more than two thousand years.

Why the cycle works

The period combines three lunar cycles almost exactly: 223 synodic months (new moon to new moon) equal about 242 draconic months (returns to the same node) and 239 anomalistic months (returns to perigee). Because the Moon is again near a node and at a similar distance, an eclipse takes place with similar character — a total eclipse tends to be followed, one saros later, by another total eclipse.

The awkward remainder of roughly one-third of a day means that after one saros an eclipse occurs about 8 hours later in the day, so it is visible from a longitude about 120° farther west. After three saros periods (the exeligmos, about 54 years) the eclipse returns to roughly the same longitude.

Eclipse series

Eclipses are grouped into numbered saros series. Each series begins with a sequence of partial eclipses near one pole, progresses through central (total or annular) eclipses, and ends with partial eclipses at the opposite pole after roughly 1,226–1,550 years and 69–87 eclipses. At any given time about 40 saros series are active. The Greek Antikythera mechanism of the 2nd century BCE included a dial for predicting eclipses by the saros, and the cycle's name (from the Babylonian šar) was applied to it by Edmond Halley in 1691.
