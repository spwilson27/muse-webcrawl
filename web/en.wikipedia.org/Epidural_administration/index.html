Epidural administration is a route of administering medication into the epidural space, the potential space surrounding the dural sac of the spinal cord, outside the dura mater. Medication placed there — most often local anaesthetics, frequently combined with opioids — bathes the spinal nerve roots as they leave the cord, producing segmental analgesia or anaesthesia. It is one of the most widely used techniques in anaesthesia and pain medicine.

Technique

With the patient sitting or lying on their side and their back flexed, the clinician inserts a large-bore (Tuohy) needle between the vertebrae, usually in the lumbar region for labour, and advances it using a "loss of resistance" technique to identify the epidural space. A fine catheter is typically threaded through the needle and left in place, allowing repeated doses or a continuous infusion. A test dose is given to check that the catheter is not inside a blood vessel or the subarachnoid space. Unlike spinal anaesthesia, the dura is not intentionally pierced, and onset of block is slower and can be extended indefinitely via the catheter.

Uses

Epidural analgesia is best known for pain relief in labour and childbirth, where a low-dose infusion relieves pain while preserving the mother's ability to move and push. Epidurals are also used as the sole anaesthetic or an adjunct to general anaesthesia for surgery on the lower abdomen, pelvis, and legs; for postoperative pain control after major thoracic and abdominal operations; for rib-fracture analgesia; and, in chronic pain practice, for epidural steroid injections for radicular ("sciatic") pain.

Risks and complications

Common effects include a fall in blood pressure, shivering, itching from opioids, urinary retention, and temporary leg weakness. Uncommon complications include accidental dural puncture with a post-dural-puncture headache, inadequate or patchy block, and catheter migration. Rare but serious complications include epidural haematoma, epidural abscess, nerve injury, and local anaesthetic systemic toxicity. Absolute contraindications include patient refusal, infection at the insertion site, and uncorrected coagulopathy.
