Fabian strategy

The Fabian strategy is a military strategy in which pitched battles and frontal assaults are avoided in favour of wearing down the enemy through a war of attrition and indirection — harassing operations, skirmishes and denial of supplies — while avoiding decisive engagement. It is named after Quintus Fabius Maximus Verrucosus, the Roman dictator appointed after the defeats at Trasimene and Cannae during the Second Punic War.

History

Faced with Hannibal's superior Carthaginian army in Italy, Fabius shadowed the invaders, cut off their foragers and refused open battle, aiming to exhaust them over time. His delaying tactics earned him the cognomen Cunctator ("the Delayer") — initially a term of mockery, later of praise. The strategy was controversial in Rome, but was vindicated as attrition weakened Hannibal's position.

Later usage

The Fabian approach has been invoked for many later campaigns of evasion and attrition, including George Washington's conduct during much of the American Revolutionary War. The gradualist socialist Fabian Society adopted Fabius's name for its own strategy of slow, incremental political change.
