Arthur Schopenhauer (22 February 1788 – 21 September 1860) was a German philosopher best known for his 1818 work The World as Will and Representation, which argues that the world is fundamentally driven by a blind, insatiable metaphysical will. His pessimistic philosophy influenced thinkers and artists including Nietzsche, Wagner, Tolstoy and Freud.

Life

Schopenhauer was born in Danzig (now Gdańsk) into a wealthy merchant family. After his father's death he gave up a commercial apprenticeship to study, attending the universities of Göttingen, Berlin and Jena. He received his doctorate in 1813 with the dissertation On the Fourfold Root of the Principle of Sufficient Reason. He lived most of his later life in Frankfurt am Main, supported by his inheritance.

Philosophy

Developing Kant's distinction between appearance and the thing-in-itself, Schopenhauer held that the inner nature of reality is Will — a striving, purposeless force manifested in nature and in human desire. Because willing is endless, human life oscillates between suffering and boredom. Aesthetic contemplation, compassion and ascetic denial of the will offer temporary or lasting release. His thought drew on Plato, Kant and Indian philosophy, particularly the Upanishads and Buddhism.

Influence

Ignored for much of his life in favour of Hegelian idealism, Schopenhauer gained fame late in life after the publication of Parerga and Paralipomena (1851). His influence on European culture in the late nineteenth century was immense, spanning philosophy, literature and music.
