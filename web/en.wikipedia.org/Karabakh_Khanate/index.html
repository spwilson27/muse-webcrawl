Karabakh Khanate

The Karabakh Khanate was a semi-independent Turkic khanate in the Karabakh region of the South Caucasus, established in the mid-18th century amid the collapse of Afsharid Persia and existing until its annexation by the Russian Empire in the early 19th century. Its territories correspond to parts of modern Azerbaijan and Armenia, including the highlands of Nagorno-Karabakh.

History

Panah Ali Khan of the Turkic Javanshir clan founded the khanate around 1748, building the fortress of Panahabad — later Shusha — as his capital in 1750–52. His son Ibrahim Khalil Khan expanded its influence, alternately resisting and accommodating Persia's Qajar dynasty; in 1795 and 1797 Shusha withstood sieges by Agha Mohammad Khan Qajar. During the Russo-Persian War, Ibrahim Khalil Khan signed the Treaty of Kurakchay (1805), placing the khanate under Russian suzerainty; he was killed by Russian troops in 1806. The khanate was formally abolished in 1822, and the Treaties of Gulistan (1813) and Turkmenchay (1828) confirmed Russian rule over the region.

Society

The population mixed Turkic Muslims in the lowlands and towns with Armenian Christians in the mountainous east, where five Armenian melikdoms (the Khamsa) retained a degree of autonomy inside the khanate. Shusha became a notable centre of Azerbaijani culture, music and carpet weaving.
