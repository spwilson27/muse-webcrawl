The Battle of the Catalaunian Plains, also known as the Battle of Châlons or the Battle of Maurica, took place on 20 June 451 AD between a coalition led by the Roman general Flavius Aëtius and the Visigothic king Theodoric I on one side, and the Hunnic confederation commanded by Attila on the other. It was one of the most important battles of late antiquity, marking the high point of Attila's invasion of Gaul and its subsequent failure.

Background

In 451 Attila invaded Gaul, claiming he sought the Visigothic kingdom at Toulouse and, in some accounts, the hand of Honoria, sister of the western emperor Valentinian III, with half the empire as dowry. After the Huns sacked Metz and besieged Orléans, Aëtius — the effective ruler of the western empire — assembled an army of Roman regulars and federate allies, above all the Visigoths of Theodoric, whose kingdom Attila most directly threatened.

Battle

The principal source, Jordanes' Getica, describes a vast and bloody struggle on a plain, with a ridge as the tactical prize. The Visigoths held the right wing, Aëtius the left with the Romans, and the less reliable Alans were placed in the centre. Theodoric was killed leading his men — trampled or struck down, accounts differ — but his son Thorismund rallied the Visigoths, who drove the Huns back to their wagon camp. Casualties were enormous by any estimate; Jordanes' figures run into the hundreds of thousands and are certainly exaggerated, but the slaughter was remembered for generations.

Aftermath

Attila withdrew from Gaul, his army weakened but intact; Aëtius, perhaps preferring a surviving Hunnic counterweight to an over-mighty Visigothic kingdom, did not annihilate him. Attila invaded Italy the following year before dying in 453. The battle has often been portrayed as a decisive defence of Roman and Christian civilisation, though modern historians treat its meaning more cautiously; it remains one of the last great victories of the Western Roman Empire, which fell a generation later in 476.
