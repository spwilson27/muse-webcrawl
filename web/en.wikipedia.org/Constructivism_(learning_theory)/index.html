Constructivism (learning theory)

Constructivism is a theory of learning holding that learners actively construct their own understanding and knowledge of the world through experience and reflection, rather than passively receiving information. New information is integrated with prior knowledge (assimilation) or prompts a restructuring of existing mental schemas (accommodation).

Major theorists

Jean Piaget described cognitive development as proceeding through stages as children build schemas. Lev Vygotsky emphasised the social dimension: learning occurs first between people, and concepts such as the zone of proximal development highlight what a learner can achieve with guidance. John Dewey's experiential education and Jerome Bruner's discovery learning are also central influences.

Classroom implications

Constructivist teaching favours inquiry-based and problem-based learning, projects, discussion, and scaffolding by the teacher as facilitator rather than lecturer. Assessment stresses authentic tasks and formative feedback. Critics argue that minimally guided discovery can overload beginners and that explicit instruction is sometimes more efficient, prompting hybrid approaches.

Variants

Psychological (cognitive) constructivism focuses on individual mental construction; social constructivism stresses language, culture, and collaboration. Radical constructivism, associated with Ernst von Glasersfeld, treats knowledge as a viable model of experience rather than a mirror of objective reality.
