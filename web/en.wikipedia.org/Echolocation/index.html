Animal echolocation, also called bio sonar, is a biological active sonar used by several animal species. Echolocating animals emit calls out into the environment and listen to the echoes of those calls that return from various objects near them. They use these echoes to locate and identify the objects.

Echolocation is used for navigation, foraging (including hunting), and communication in various environments. It evolved independently in bats and toothed whales (odontocetes), and in simpler forms in oilbirds, cave swiftlets, and some small mammals.

Bats

Most microbats produce ultrasonic calls through the larynx and emit them through the mouth or nose. Call frequencies range from about 14 to over 100 kHz, well beyond human hearing. By varying call duration, bandwidth, and repetition rate, bats can resolve targets with millimetre precision and distinguish texture and wingbeats of insect prey. Experiments by Donald Griffin and Robert Galambos in the 1940s first demonstrated the phenomenon.

Toothed whales

Odontocetes, including dolphins, porpoises, and sperm whales, generate clicks in nasal passages and focus them through the melon, a fatty organ in the forehead. Echoes are received primarily through the lower jaw. Dolphin biosonar can discriminate objects differing in size by fractions of a millimetre.

Evolution and countermeasures

Bat echolocation likely evolved in the early Eocene, driving an evolutionary arms race with prey: some moths evolved ears tuned to bat calls, and tiger moths produce clicks that may jam bat sonar. Human sonar technology was developed independently of, and parallel to, biological echolocation.
