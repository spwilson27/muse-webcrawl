Ear piercing

Ear piercing
Type: Body piercing
Common locations: Earlobe, helix, tragus, conch, daith
Jewellery: Studs, hoops, barbells
Healing time: 6–8 weeks (lobe); longer for cartilage

Ear piercing is the practice of making a hole through part of the ear, usually the earlobe, so that jewellery such as earrings can be worn. It is one of the oldest and most widespread forms of body modification, practised by many cultures throughout history for both women and men.

Ear piercings can be placed in the lobe or in various parts of the ear cartilage.

History

Evidence of pierced ears dates back thousands of years: the mummified body of Ötzi the Iceman (c. 3400–3100 BC) bore ear piercings, and earrings are depicted in ancient Egyptian, Persian and Greek art. In many cultures ear piercing has had ritual, religious, or status significance, and in Hindu tradition the karnavedha ceremony is one of the standard rites of passage for children.

Types

The most common piercing is through the soft earlobe. Cartilage piercings include the helix (outer rim), forward helix, tragus, antitragus, conch, rook, daith and snug, while the industrial piercing joins two points of cartilage with a single barbell. Multiple lobe piercings and stretched (gauged) lobes are also common.

Procedure and care

Piercings are made either with a hollow piercing needle, preferred by professionals, or with a spring-loaded piercing gun, generally considered unsuitable for cartilage. Aftercare involves cleaning with saline solution and avoiding unnecessary handling. Risks include infection, allergic reactions to nickel, keloid scarring, and, for cartilage piercings, slower healing.
