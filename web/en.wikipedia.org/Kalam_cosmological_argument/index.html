Kalam cosmological argument

The Kalam cosmological argument is a philosophical argument for the existence of God that traces its roots to medieval Islamic scholastic theology (kalām). Its best-known modern formulation, defended by the philosopher William Lane Craig, runs:

  1. Whatever begins to exist has a cause of its existence.
  2. The universe began to exist.
  3. Therefore, the universe has a cause of its existence.

Proponents further argue that this cause must be a personal, timeless, spaceless and immaterial creator — that is, God.

Historical development

The argument grew out of the mutakallimūn tradition of Islamic theology and was developed by thinkers such as al-Kindī, al-Ghazālī and others, who argued that an actual infinite regress of past events is impossible and that the universe must therefore have had a beginning. Similar reasoning appears in the work of the Christian philosopher John Philoponus and was later taken up by Bonaventure. Craig revived and popularised the argument in his 1979 book The Kalām Cosmological Argument.

Support for the premises

Defenders support the second premise both philosophically — arguing that an actual infinite cannot exist and cannot be formed by successive addition — and scientifically, citing Big Bang cosmology and the second law of thermodynamics as evidence that the universe began a finite time ago. The first premise is defended as a basic causal intuition: things do not pop into existence uncaused out of nothing.

Criticism

Critics dispute both premises and the theological conclusion. Objections include the possibility of actual infinites in mathematics, interpretations of quantum mechanics in which events occur without deterministic causes, cosmological models without an absolute beginning, and the charge that even if the universe had a cause, identifying that cause with a personal God requires further argument. The argument remains one of the most debated in contemporary philosophy of religion.
