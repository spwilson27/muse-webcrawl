Geology of Mars

The geology of Mars is the scientific study of the surface, crust, and interior of the planet Mars. It emphasizes the composition, structure, history, and physical processes that have shaped the planet. The field is comparable to terrestrial geology in its methods, drawing on remote sensing from orbiters, in-situ analysis by landers and rovers, and the study of Martian meteorites found on Earth.

The surface of Mars, shaped by impacts, volcanism, and the action of water and wind.

Surface features

Mars displays a striking dichotomy between its smooth, low northern plains and its heavily cratered southern highlands. The Tharsis region hosts enormous shield volcanoes, including Olympus Mons, the tallest volcano in the Solar System, while the great canyon system Valles Marineris stretches more than 4,000 kilometres across the equator. Polar caps of water ice and frozen carbon dioxide grow and shrink with the seasons.

Water and climate history

Orbital and rover observations of dried river valleys, lakebed sediments, and hydrated minerals indicate that early Mars was warmer and wetter, with liquid water flowing on its surface roughly four billion years ago. The Curiosity and Perseverance rovers have documented sedimentary rocks deposited in ancient lakes, evidence central to the search for past habitability.

Interior

Data from NASA's InSight lander, which recorded marsquakes between 2018 and 2022, revealed a large liquid core and a crust between roughly 20 and 70 kilometres thick. Mars lacks an internally generated global magnetic field today, though magnetized crustal rocks record an ancient dynamo.
