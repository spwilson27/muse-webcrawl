Kazakh famine of 1930–1933

The Kazakh famine of 1930–1933, known in Kazakhstan as the Asharshylyk ("Great Famine"), was a man-made famine in the Kazakh Autonomous Socialist Soviet Republic, then part of the Soviet Union. Caused by forced collectivisation and the confiscation and slaughter of livestock that destroyed the nomadic pastoral economy, it killed an estimated 1.3 to 2.3 million people — roughly a third or more of all ethnic Kazakhs — the highest proportional death toll of any Soviet famine. More than a million survivors fled, many across the border into China.

Causes

Under Filipp Goloshchyokin, first secretary in Kazakhstan from 1925, Soviet authorities launched a campaign of forced sedentarisation and collectivisation of Kazakh nomadic herders, sometimes called a "Little October". Livestock — the basis of the nomadic economy — was confiscated and redistributed; between 1928 and 1932 the republic's herds collapsed by roughly 90 percent as animals were seized, slaughtered or starved. Grain procurement quotas were imposed even on pastoral regions, and resistance was met with arrests and deportations.

Course of the famine

Mass starvation spread from 1930 and peaked in 1932–33. Whole districts were emptied as Kazakhs fled in search of food — to other Soviet republics, Siberia and across the border into Xinjiang, China. Disease accompanied hunger, and the dead numbered well over a million; Kazakhs became a minority in their own republic, a demographic position not reversed until the 1990s. Goloshchyokin was removed in 1933 and livestock collectivisation was partially relaxed, easing the catastrophe.

Memory and assessment

The famine was long suppressed in Soviet historiography. Since independence, Kazakhstan has built memorials and observes 31 May as a day of remembrance for victims of famine and political repression. Historians debate its classification: many describe it as a consequence of criminal policy and ethnic catastrophe, while the Kazakhstani state has generally been cautious about applying the term genocide, a question scholars continue to discuss.
