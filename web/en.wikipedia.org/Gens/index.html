In ancient Rome, a gens (plural gentes) was a clan or extended family group consisting of all those individuals sharing the same nomen and claiming descent from a common ancestor. Membership of a gens was an important marker of social identity, and the gens name — the nomen gentilicium — formed the middle element of the standard Roman three-part name (tria nomina), as in Gaius Julius Caesar, of the gens Julia.

Structure and function

A gens was divided into branches or families (stirpes), often distinguished by a cognomen, such as the Cornelii Scipiones within the gens Cornelia. The gens collectively maintained religious rites (sacra gentilicia), burial places, and mutual obligations among members; in early Roman law, inheritance from a member who died intestate without close heirs passed to the gens. Patrician gentes (gentes maiores) claimed precedence over plebeian ones (gentes minores).

Notable gentes

Famous gentes include the Julii, Cornelii, Claudii, Aemilii, Fabii, and Valerii, which produced many of Rome's leading statesmen across the Republic and Empire. Individuals could enter a gens by birth, adoption, or (for freed slaves) manumission, taking the nomen of the family that freed them.
