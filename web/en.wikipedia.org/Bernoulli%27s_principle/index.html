Bernoulli's principle

Bernoulli's principle is a key concept in fluid dynamics that relates pressure, speed, and elevation: an increase in the speed of a fluid occurs simultaneously with a decrease in static pressure or a decrease in the fluid's potential energy. The principle is named after the Swiss mathematician and physicist Daniel Bernoulli, who published it in his book Hydrodynamica in 1738; the more general form of the equation was derived by Leonhard Euler in 1752.

The Bernoulli equation

For steady, incompressible, inviscid flow along a streamline, Bernoulli's equation states that the sum

p + ½ρv² + ρgh

is constant along the streamline, where p is pressure, ρ is fluid density, v is flow speed, g is gravitational acceleration, and h is elevation. The middle term is the dynamic pressure. The equation expresses conservation of mechanical energy per unit volume: pressure energy, kinetic energy, and gravitational potential energy trade off against one another.

Applications

Bernoulli's principle underlies the Venturi effect used in carburettors, aspirators, and flow measurement; the operation of pitot tubes that measure airspeed; atomizers and spray devices; and aspects of lift on airfoils, where faster flow over the upper surface of a wing corresponds to lower pressure. It also explains phenomena such as the inward pull of a shower curtain and the behaviour of curveballs, though popular explanations sometimes misapply it — for example, lift cannot be explained by Bernoulli alone without also invoking Newton's third law and the turning of the airflow.

Limitations

The simplified equation assumes steady flow, negligible viscosity, and incompressibility, and applies along a streamline. Extensions exist for compressible flow, unsteady potential flow, and flows with added energy input such as pumps.
