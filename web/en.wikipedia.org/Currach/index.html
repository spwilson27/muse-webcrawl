Currach

A currach (Irish: curach) is a type of Irish boat with a frame of wooden laths covered originally with animal hides and today usually with canvas coated in tar. Light, seaworthy and cheap to build, currachs were used for centuries for fishing and transport along the west coast of Ireland, especially around the Aran Islands and in Counties Kerry, Clare, Galway, Mayo and Donegal.

Construction

The traditional currach has a framework of hazel or ash laths and stringers, over which hides — ox hides in earlier times — were stretched and treated; modern boats use canvas waterproofed with tar. The boats lack a keel, have a distinctive upswept bow, and are rowed with oars that have a thin blade and a peg (the "grummet" arrangement). Sizes range from small two-person river currachs to large sea-going versions.

History

Hide boats are described in early Irish literature and in accounts of voyages such as that of Saint Brendan, whose legendary Atlantic crossing inspired modern re-creations, including Tim Severin's 1976–77 Brendan voyage. Related craft include the Welsh coracle and the Scottish curragh. Currach racing remains popular, with regattas held along the west coast, and the boats still appear in lobster and crab fishing.
