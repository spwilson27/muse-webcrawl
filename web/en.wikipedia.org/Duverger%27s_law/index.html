Duverger's law

Duverger's law is a principle in political science which states that plurality-rule elections structured within single-member districts tend to favour a two-party system. It is named after French sociologist and political scientist Maurice Duverger, who set out the principle in the 1950s.

Mechanisms

Duverger identified two effects. The mechanical effect arises because plurality rules systematically under-represent third parties: a party winning 20 per cent of the vote evenly spread across districts may win no seats at all. The psychological effect follows from the first: voters, anticipating this, desert smaller parties to avoid "wasting" their vote, and politicians and donors similarly coordinate on the two leading contenders.

Converse and qualifications

Duverger also observed that proportional representation tends to favour multiparty systems. The law is a tendency rather than an iron law: regional parties can thrive under plurality rules (as in Canada and India), and social cleavages, federalism, and election rules at other levels mediate its strength.

Influence

The principle is one of the most famous regularities in comparative politics and has been formalised in later work on strategic voting and party-system equilibrium.
