Négritude

Négritude is a framework of critique and a literary and ideological movement developed by francophone African and Caribbean intellectuals, writers, and politicians in the 1930s. Its founders — Martinican poet Aimé Césaire, Guianese poet Léon Damas, and Senegalese poet and future president Léopold Sédar Senghor — aimed to raise and celebrate black consciousness and African cultural values in reaction against French colonial rule and the policy of assimilation.

Origins

The movement grew out of encounters between colonial students in Paris in the early 1930s, influenced by the Harlem Renaissance, surrealism, and Marxist and anti-colonial thought. Césaire coined the word négritude in his poem Cahier d'un retour au pays natal (1939), giving voice to a reclaimed black identity against the dehumanisation of colonialism.

Philosophy

Négritude celebrated the distinctiveness of African culture, rhythm, intuition, and communal values, in contrast to what its proponents saw as the cold rationality of Europe. Senghor famously summarised the contrast by saying that emotion is black just as reason is Hellenic. The journal Présence Africaine, founded in 1947 by Alioune Diop, became an important organ of the movement.

Criticism

The movement was later criticised — by Wole Soyinka, Frantz Fanon in part, and others — for essentialism and for accepting colonial stereotypes in inverted form. Soyinka remarked that a tiger does not proclaim its tigritude. Nevertheless, Négritude remains a foundational influence on postcolonial literature and African philosophy.
