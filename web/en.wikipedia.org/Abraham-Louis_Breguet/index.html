Abraham-Louis Breguet (10 January 1747 – 17 September 1823) was a Swiss horologist who made many innovations in the course of a career in France and Switzerland that made him the founder of the Breguet watch company and one of the most famous watchmakers of all time. Often called the father of modern horology, he invented or perfected numerous mechanisms and also transformed the aesthetics of watchmaking with his elegant, legible designs — guilloché dials, blued steel "pomme" hands and slim cases that still define the Breguet style.

Life and career

Born in Neuchâtel, Breguet was apprenticed at Versailles and in Paris, where he studied mathematics and opened his own workshop on the Quai de l'Horloge, Île de la Cité, in 1775. His perfectionism won him the patronage of the French court: Marie Antoinette commissioned watches from him, including the fabulously complicated No. 160 "Marie-Antoinette" pocket watch, completed after both their deaths. Forced to flee revolutionary France for Switzerland in 1793, he returned in 1795 and rebuilt the business.

Inventions

  • The tourbillon (patented 1801), a rotating cage for the escapement and balance that averages out gravity errors
  • The self-winding perpétuelle watches
  • The Breguet overcoil (raised terminal curve of the balance spring, 1795)
  • The first wristwatch, made for Caroline Murat, Queen of Naples (1810–1812)
  • The pendule sympathique, a clock that wound and set a companion watch
  • Shock-protection (pare-chute), constant-force escapements and early chronographs

Breguet's clients included Napoleon, George III, the Tsar Alexander I and much of European royalty. He became a member of the Académie des sciences and horologer to the French navy. The company he founded remains a prestigious Swiss watch brand within the Swatch Group.
