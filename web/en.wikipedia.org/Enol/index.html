Enol

In organic chemistry, an enol is an alkene (olefin) with a hydroxyl group attached to one end of the alkene double bond: the structure C=C–OH. The term is a contraction of "ene" (for the double bond) and "-ol" (for the alcohol). Enols are closely related to ketones and aldehydes through the process of keto–enol tautomerism.

Keto–enol tautomerism

Most carbonyl compounds exist predominantly in the keto form, with the enol form present in small amounts; the two forms interconvert by migration of an α-hydrogen atom and relocation of the double bond. For simple ketones such as acetone, the enol fraction is tiny (about one molecule in ten million), but for β-dicarbonyl compounds such as acetylacetone and ethyl acetoacetate the enol form is stabilised by conjugation and intramolecular hydrogen bonding and can predominate.

Enolates

Deprotonation of a carbonyl compound at the α-position gives an enolate, an anion stabilised by resonance between carbon and oxygen. Enolates are important nucleophiles in carbon–carbon bond-forming reactions such as the aldol reaction and alkylation reactions. Silyl enol ethers are protected, isolable enol equivalents widely used in synthesis.

Significance

Enol chemistry underlies many fundamental reactions, including halogenation at the α-position, racemisation of α-stereocentres, and the rare enol tautomers of nucleobases, which can cause mispairing in DNA replication.
