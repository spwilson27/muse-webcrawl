Friedrich Gustav Jakob Henle (19 July 1809 – 13 May 1885) was a German physician, pathologist and anatomist credited with major advances in microscopic anatomy (histology). His name survives in the loop of Henle of the kidney nephron, and his 1840 essay On Miasmata and Contagia is regarded as a foundational statement of the germ theory of disease, anticipating the work of his student Robert Koch.

Life and career

Henle studied medicine at Heidelberg and Bonn, receiving his doctorate in 1832. At Bonn he was influenced by the physiologist Johannes Peter Müller, whose assistant he became in Berlin. After a period of political imprisonment for Burschenschaft (student fraternity) activities, he became professor of anatomy at Zurich in 1840, moved to Heidelberg in 1844, and in 1852 took the chair at Göttingen, where he remained until his death.

Work

Henle's Handbuch der systematischen Anatomie des Menschen (1856–1873) was the first comprehensive treatise of histology as an organised science, describing the structure of epithelia, blood vessels, and the kidney. In 1862 he described the hairpin loop in the renal medulla that now bears his name. His 1840 miasma essay set out criteria — later developed by Koch as "Koch's postulates" — for proving that a microorganism causes a disease. Henle also classified epithelial tissues and recognised that the body surfaces are covered by epithelium.
