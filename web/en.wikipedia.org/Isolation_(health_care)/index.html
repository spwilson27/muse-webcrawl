Isolation (health care)

Isolation precautions separate infectious patients to prevent transmission.

In health care facilities, isolation represents one of several measures that can be taken in implementing infection control: the prevention of communicable diseases from being transmitted from a patient to other patients, health care workers and visitors, or from outsiders to a particular patient (reverse isolation).

Types of precautions

Standard precautions apply to all patients. Transmission-based precautions add measures according to the route of spread: contact precautions (gloves, gowns, dedicated equipment) for organisms such as MRSA; droplet precautions (surgical masks) for influenza and similar infections; and airborne precautions (fit-tested respirators and negative-pressure rooms) for tuberculosis, measles and varicella. Patients with severely weakened immune systems may be placed in protective (reverse) isolation.

Practice and effects

Isolation may involve single rooms, cohorting of patients with the same infection, and restrictions on movement within a facility. While essential for outbreak control, isolation can have adverse effects on patients' mental health and on the frequency of clinical observation, and is therefore applied proportionately and reviewed.
