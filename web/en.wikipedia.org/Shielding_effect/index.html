Shielding effect


Inner electrons partially shield outer electrons from the nuclear charge.

In chemistry, the shielding effect (also called screening) describes the reduction of the effective attraction between the atomic nucleus and an outer electron caused by the presence of inner-shell electrons. The effective nuclear charge experienced by a valence electron, Zeff, is therefore smaller than the actual nuclear charge Z: Zeff = Z − S, where S is the screening constant.

Cause

In a multi-electron atom, electrons in inner shells lie between the nucleus and the valence shell. Because they carry negative charge, they repel the outer electrons and partially cancel the attractive force of the nucleus. Electrons in the same shell shield one another only weakly, whereas electrons in shells closer to the nucleus shield much more effectively. Penetration of the valence orbital toward the nucleus reduces shielding: s orbitals penetrate most, followed by p, d, and f orbitals, which is why, within a shell, s electrons experience the largest effective nuclear charge.

Slater's rules

Slater's rules, proposed by John C. Slater in 1930, provide an empirical scheme for estimating the screening constant S by assigning each other electron a contribution depending on its shell. These estimates explain periodic trends in atomic radius, ionization energy, and electronegativity.

Consequences

Shielding is central to the trends of the periodic table. Across a period, electrons are added to the same shell and shield each other poorly, so Zeff rises and atoms contract. Down a group, new shells are added and shielding increases, so atoms grow despite the greater nuclear charge. Poor shielding by filled d and f subshells produces the transition-metal contraction and the lanthanide contraction.
