Right ventricle

The right ventricle is one of the four chambers of the heart. It receives deoxygenated blood from the right atrium through the tricuspid valve and pumps it through the pulmonary valve into the pulmonary artery, towards the lungs for oxygenation.

The right ventricle (lower left in this view) forms most of the heart's anterior surface.

Structure

The right ventricle is triangular in shape and is divided functionally into inflow, apical trabecular and outflow (infundibular) portions. Its wall is much thinner than that of the left ventricle—about 3–5 mm—because the pulmonary circulation it supplies is a low-pressure circuit. The interior bears muscular ridges called trabeculae carneae, and papillary muscles anchor the tricuspid valve leaflets via the chordae tendineae; a distinctive moderator band carries part of the right bundle branch to the anterior papillary muscle.

Function

In each cardiac cycle the right ventricle ejects the same stroke volume as the left ventricle, but at much lower pressure (peaking around 25 mmHg). Right ventricular function is sensitive to increases in afterload, as in pulmonary hypertension, which can lead to right ventricular hypertrophy, dilation and ultimately right-sided heart failure.

Clinical significance

Diseases affecting the right ventricle include arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy, congenital defects such as tetralogy of Fallot, and acute strain in massive pulmonary embolism. Function is commonly assessed by echocardiography and cardiac MRI.
