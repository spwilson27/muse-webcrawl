Calculus

Newton (left) and Leibniz (right), whose independent inventions of calculus sparked a famous priority dispute.

Calculus is the mathematical study of continuous change, in the same way that geometry is the study of shape and algebra is the study of generalisations of arithmetic operations. Originally called infinitesimal calculus, it has two major branches: differential calculus, concerning instantaneous rates of change and the slopes of curves, and integral calculus, concerning the accumulation of quantities and the areas under or between curves. These two branches are related by the fundamental theorem of calculus.

History

Precursors appear in the ancient world: Eudoxus' method of exhaustion, Archimedes' quadrature results, and the work of Indian mathematicians such as Madhava of the Kerala school, who developed series expansions in the 14th–15th centuries. Modern calculus was independently invented in the late 17th century by Isaac Newton (fluxions, developed in the 1660s–70s and published in the Principia, 1687) and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (whose differential notation, published from 1684, is still standard). Their rivalry produced a bitter priority dispute between English and Continental mathematics. In the 19th century, Cauchy, Weierstrass and others gave calculus a rigorous foundation in limits and real analysis.

Principles

The derivative of a function measures its instantaneous rate of change; the definite integral measures accumulation and area. The fundamental theorem states that differentiation and integration are inverse operations. Extensions include multivariable calculus, differential equations, vector calculus (gradient, divergence, curl; the theorems of Green, Stokes and Gauss), and the calculus of variations.

Applications

Calculus underlies classical mechanics, electromagnetism, statistics, economics and engineering; it describes everything from the stability of the atomic nucleus models to epidemic growth of diseases like bubonic plague, and it supplies the optimisation machinery (gradient descent) on which deep learning depends.
