Pierre Levegh

Pierre Levegh (born Pierre Eugène Alfred Bouillin, 22 December 1905 – 11 June 1955) was a French racing driver, ice hockey player, and sailor. He is remembered principally for the 1955 Le Mans disaster, in which his Mercedes-Benz 300 SLR was launched into the crowd, killing Levegh and more than 80 spectators — the deadliest accident in motor racing history.

Career

Levegh took his racing name in memory of his uncle, a pioneering driver who died in 1904. A talented all-round sportsman, he played ice hockey and competed in sailing before concentrating on motor sport after the Second World War. Driving a Talbot-Lago, he achieved fame at the 1952 24 Hours of Le Mans by attempting to drive the entire race single-handed; he led until the final hour, when engine failure — after a missed gear change by the exhausted driver — ended his run.

Death at Le Mans, 1955

For 1955 Levegh joined the Mercedes-Benz team. During the race, Lance Macklin's Austin-Healey swerved in front of him to avoid Mike Hawthorn's slowing Jaguar. Levegh's Mercedes struck the Austin-Healey and was launched into the spectator area, disintegrating and catching fire. Levegh and more than 80 spectators were killed. The disaster prompted sweeping safety reforms and led Switzerland to ban motor racing for decades.
