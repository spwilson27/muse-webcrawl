Amir Khusrau

Amir Khusrau (1253 – October 1325), sometimes spelled Amir Khusro, was an Indo-Persian Sufi singer, musician, poet and scholar who lived under the Delhi Sultanate. He is regarded as an iconic figure in the cultural history of the Indian subcontinent and is often called the "father of qawwali" and the "parrot of India" (Tuti-e-Hind).

Life

Khusrau was born in Patiyali in present-day Uttar Pradesh to a father of Turkic origin and an Indian mother. He was a prolific poet who wrote in Persian and Hindavi, and a disciple of the Sufi saint Nizamuddin Auliya of Delhi. He served as court poet under a succession of Delhi sultans, including Jalaluddin Khalji, Alauddin Khalji and Ghiyasuddin Tughlaq.

Works

Khusrau's enormous output includes five divans of Persian poetry, romantic masnavis such as Khamsa-e-Khusrau (a response to Nizami's Khamsa), and historical works including the Qiran-us-Sa'dain and Tughlaq Nama. He is credited with composing verses in Hindavi that blend Persian and local vocabulary, contributing to the development of Urdu and Hindi literary traditions.

Music

Tradition credits Khusrau with enriching Hindustani classical music by introducing Persian and Arabic elements, laying the groundwork for forms such as qawwali, tarana and khayal, and with innovations in musical instruments. Modern scholars debate the extent of these attributions, but his central place in the musical culture of South Asia is undisputed.

Legacy

Khusrau died in 1325, a few months after his master Nizamuddin Auliya, and is buried beside him in the Nizamuddin Dargah complex in Delhi, which remains a major pilgrimage and musical centre.
