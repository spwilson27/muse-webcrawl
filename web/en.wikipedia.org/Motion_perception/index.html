Motion perception

Motion perception is the process of inferring the speed and direction of elements in a scene based on visual, vestibular and proprioceptive inputs. Although this process appears straightforward to most observers, it has proven to be a difficult problem from a computational perspective, and difficult to explain in terms of neural processing.

Types of motion processing

Motion perception is customarily divided into first-order motion perception, in which movement is detected from changes in luminance across space and time, and second-order motion perception, in which movement is inferred from changes in contrast, texture, or flicker that involve no net change in luminance. The perception of motion also underlies phenomena such as apparent motion (the phi phenomenon exploited by film and animation), induced motion, and motion aftereffects such as the waterfall illusion.

Neural basis

In the primate visual system, direction-selective neurons in area V1 provide the initial encoding of local motion, while area MT (V5) integrates these signals to represent the global motion of objects. Damage to MT can produce akinetopsia, or motion blindness, in which a person perceives the world in a series of static snapshots. The visual system must also solve the aperture problem and distinguish object motion from self-motion signaled by optic flow.

Eye movements

Motion perception is closely coupled to eye movements: smooth pursuit holds a moving target on the fovea, and the brain uses extraretinal signals (copies of motor commands) to discount retinal motion caused by the observer's own movements.
