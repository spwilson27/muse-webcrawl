Proteostasis

The proteostasis network maintains the cell's protein complement.

Proteostasis (protein homeostasis) is the network of biological pathways that controls the synthesis, folding, trafficking, aggregation, and degradation of proteins within and outside cells. Healthy proteostasis ensures that proteins adopt and maintain their functional conformations and that damaged or misfolded proteins are refolded or cleared.

Components

The proteostasis network integrates the ribosome and chaperone systems (including heat shock proteins), the ubiquitin-proteasome system, autophagy and lysosomal degradation, and stress responses such as the unfolded protein response in the endoplasmic reticulum. Its total capacity is sometimes called the proteostasis boundary, and it is regulated by transcription factors such as HSF1.

Disease and ageing

Proteostasis capacity declines with age, and its failure is a hallmark of ageing. Collapse of proteostasis contributes to protein-misfolding diseases including Alzheimer's, Parkinson's, Huntington's disease, and amyloidosis, in which proteins aggregate into toxic assemblies. Pharmacological "proteostasis regulators" that boost chaperones or degradation pathways are an active strategy for treating such diseases.
