Nine Ministries

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Nine Ministries (Chinese: 九卿; pinyin: jiǔ qīng), also rendered as the Nine Courts, were the principal central government agencies of imperial China. Together with the Three Departments and Six Ministries of the mature imperial system, the Nine Ministries formed the administrative core through which the emperor governed the empire from the Han dynasty onward, though their precise functions and titles changed considerably over the centuries.

History

The origins of the Nine Ministries lie in the court offices of the Zhou and Qin periods. The Han dynasty formalised the system: each ministry was headed by an official of ministerial rank (卿, qīng) receiving a salary of "fully 2,000 shi" of grain, and subordinate to the Three Ducal Ministers and, in practice, to the imperial secretariat. As the Three Departments and Six Ministries system developed under the Sui and Tang dynasties, the Nine Ministries lost much of their policy-making role and became largely executive and operational agencies of the court.

The nine ministries

  • Ministry of Ceremonies (太常, Tàicháng) — imperial ancestral temples, state rituals, and sacrifices.
  • Ministry of Imperial Household or of the Palace (光祿勳, Guānglùxūn) — palace guards, imperial banquets, and court attendants.
  • Ministry of Guards (衛尉, Wèiwèi) — security of the palace gates and the imperial armouries.
  • Ministry of Coachmen (太僕, Tàipú) — imperial stables, carriages, and the postal relay system in some periods.
  • Ministry of Justice (廷尉 / 大理, Tíngwèi / Dàlǐ) — judicial review and the imperial prison; later organised as the Court of Judicial Review.
  • Ministry of Guests (大鴻臚, Dàhónglú) — reception of foreign envoys and tributary missions, and registration of the nobility.
  • Ministry of the Imperial Clan (宗正, Zōngzhèng) — genealogy and affairs of the imperial family.
  • Ministry of Finance or of Agriculture (大司農, Dàsīnóng) — state granaries, land tax revenues, and price stabilisation.
  • Ministry of the Treasury (少府, Shàofǔ) — the emperor's privy purse, palace workshops, and taxes on mountains, seas, and markets.

Later development

Under the Ming and Qing dynasties the Nine Courts survived in reduced form — the Court of Imperial Sacrifices, Court of Imperial Entertainments, Court of State Ceremonial, Imperial Equipage, Court of Judicial Review, and others — while real administrative power rested with the Six Ministries (Personnel, Revenue, Rites, War, Justice, and Works) and, under the Qing, the Grand Council.
