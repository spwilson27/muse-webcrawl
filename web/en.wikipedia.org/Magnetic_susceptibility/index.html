Magnetic susceptibility

Magnetic susceptibility (denoted χ) is a dimensionless quantity that measures how much a material becomes magnetised in an applied magnetic field — that is, the degree to which its magnetisation M responds to the field strength H: M = χH. It is defined in SI units as the ratio of magnetisation (magnetic moment per unit volume) to the applied magnetising field.

Sign and magnitude

Materials with negative susceptibility are diamagnetic: they are weakly repelled by a field and have small negative values of χ (for example, water ≈ −9.05 × 10⁻⁶). Materials with small positive susceptibility are paramagnetic, attracted weakly to a field; their susceptibility follows Curie's law, decreasing inversely with temperature. Ferromagnetic materials such as iron have very large, field-dependent susceptibilities and exhibit hysteresis, so χ is not a single constant. Superconductors are perfect diamagnets with χ = −1 in SI units.

Measurement

Susceptibility can be measured with a Gouy balance or Faraday balance (based on the force on a sample in a field gradient), an Evans balance, or a SQUID magnetometer. In geophysics, susceptibility measurements of rocks aid mineral exploration and palaeomagnetic studies; in chemistry, the Evans NMR method estimates the number of unpaired electrons in a compound.

Relation to permeability

The relative permeability μr is related to susceptibility by μr = 1 + χ. Volume susceptibility is dimensionless in SI; in cgs units the definition differs, and values differ by a factor of 4π between the systems — a frequent source of confusion.
