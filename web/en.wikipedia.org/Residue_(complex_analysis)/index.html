Residue (complex analysis)

Image placeholder: residue.jpg — Contour around an isolated singularity

In complex analysis, the residue of a meromorphic function at an isolated singularity is the unique complex number, often denoted Res(f, a), such that integrating the function around a small circle about the singularity gives 2πi times the residue. It is the central quantity in the residue theorem, one of the most powerful tools in the subject.

Definition

If f is holomorphic in a punctured neighbourhood of a, it has a Laurent series expansion about a:

f(z) = Σ an(z − a)n

The residue is the coefficient a−1 of the (z − a)−1 term. All other powers integrate to zero around a closed contour, which is why this single coefficient alone determines the integral:

∮ f(z) dz = 2πi · Res(f, a)

Computing residues

At a simple pole, the residue may be found from Res(f, a) = limz→a (z − a)f(z). For a pole of order m, the residue is obtained by differentiating m − 1 times: Res(f, a) = (1/(m−1)!) limz→a dm−1/dzm−1 [(z − a)m f(z)]. For a quotient g/h where h has a simple zero at a, the residue is g(a)/h′(a). At an essential singularity no such shortcut exists and the Laurent series must be found directly — for example, the residue of e1/z at 0 is 1.

The residue theorem

Augustin-Louis Cauchy's residue theorem (1825) states that if f is holomorphic inside and on a positively oriented simple closed contour except at finitely many singularities ak, then ∮ f(z) dz = 2πi Σ Res(f, ak). Applications include the evaluation of difficult real definite integrals — trigonometric integrals, improper integrals of rational and Fourier type — by extending them into the complex plane, as well as counting zeros and poles (the argument principle) and inverting Laplace transforms via the Bromwich integral.

Residue at infinity

The residue at infinity is defined so that the sum of all residues of a rational function on the Riemann sphere, including infinity, is zero; it equals the negative of the residue of (1/z²)f(1/z) at z = 0.
