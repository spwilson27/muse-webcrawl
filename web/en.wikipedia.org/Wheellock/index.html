A wheellock, wheel-lock or wheel lock is a friction-wheel mechanism which was used to ignite a firearm, succeeding the matchlock and preceding the flintlock. It was the first self-igniting firearm mechanism: instead of a smouldering match, a spring-driven steel wheel spun against a piece of iron pyrite, showering sparks into the priming pan to fire the charge. The mechanism was developed in Europe around 1500 and remained in use alongside the simpler and cheaper matchlock and flintlock for roughly a century and a half.

Operation

The lock centres on a steel wheel with a serrated rim, mounted on an axle through the lock plate and driven by a powerful V-spring acting through a short chain. Before firing, the shooter wound the wheel against the spring with a separate spanner (key) placed on the squared axle. A piece of iron pyrite was held in the jaws of the cock, called the "dog", and lowered onto the wheel's rim. Pulling the trigger released the wheel, which spun rapidly; the friction of the pyrite against the spinning wheel produced white-hot metal particles — sparks — which fell into the pan, ignited the priming powder, and fired the main charge through the touch hole.

History

The earliest depiction of a wheellock appears in Leonardo da Vinci's Codex Atlanticus (drawn c. 1500), and a German origin in Nuremberg is also claimed; the first surviving examples date to about 1507–1510. Because the wheellock needed no burning match, it could be carried loaded and ready, making it ideal for pistols and for cavalry — the German Reiter pistol-armed horsemen of the mid-16th century were its characteristic users — and for hunting arms of the nobility, often lavishly decorated. In 1517–1518 the Emperor Maximilian I even banned the making of concealable wheellock pistols, among the earliest firearms regulations.

Decline

The mechanism was complex, expensive to make and prone to fouling and mechanical failure; it required precision-made parts that only skilled gunsmiths could supply. The cheaper, more robust snaphance and then the true flintlock superseded it in military service during the 17th century, though fine wheellock sporting guns continued to be made into the 18th century.
