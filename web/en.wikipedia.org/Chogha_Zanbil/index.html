Chogha Zanbil

Chogha Zanbil (Persian: چغازنبيل) is an ancient Elamite complex in the Khuzestan province of Iran. It is one of the few extant ziggurats outside Mesopotamia and contains the best-preserved and largest ziggurat in the world. It lies approximately 30 km (19 mi) southeast of Susa and 80 km (50 mi) north of Ahvaz.

The ziggurat at Chogha Zanbil, built around 1250 BCE

History

The complex was built around 1250 BCE by the Elamite king Untash-Napirisha, principally to honor the great god Inshushinak, the patron deity of Susa. Its original Elamite name was Dur Untash, "the town (or fortress) of Untash." The king planned a new religious capital with temples dedicated to deities of both the Elamite highlands and lowlands, but the city was never completed; construction ceased after Untash-Napirisha's death, though the site remained occupied until it was destroyed by the Assyrian king Ashurbanipal in 645 BCE.

The ziggurat

The ziggurat originally measured about 105 m (344 ft) square at the base and rose roughly 53 m (174 ft) in five levels; today about 25 m (82 ft) of height survives. Unlike Mesopotamian ziggurats built of stacked solid platforms, the Chogha Zanbil ziggurat was constructed as nested concentric walls, with a temple at the summit dedicated to Inshushinak. Inscribed glazed bricks name the king and the gods honored at the site.

Excavation and protection

The site was excavated between 1951 and 1962 by the French archaeologist Roman Ghirshman. In 1979, Chogha Zanbil became the first Iranian site inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List.
