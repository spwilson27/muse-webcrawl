Édouard Vuillard (11 November 1868 – 21 June 1940) was a French painter and printmaker associated with the Symbolist group Les Nabis. He is best known for his intimate interior scenes, in which figures dissolve into richly patterned wallpaper, fabrics and furnishings, and for decorative panels commissioned for private homes.

Career

Vuillard was born into a family that ran a corset and drapery business, an origin reflected in his lifelong fascination with textiles and pattern. In 1889 he joined the Académie Julian, where he met Pierre Bonnard, Paul Sérusier and other future Nabis, who called him "the Zouave" for his military moustache. His early work was dark and intimate; after 1900 his palette lightened and he painted portraits, landscapes and theatre decoration.

Style and legacy

Vuillard's paintings often flatten space so that figures merge with their surroundings, a technique of decorative camouflage that anticipates later abstraction. Famous works include Public Gardens (1894), a triptych of nine panels, and Interior, Mother and Sister of the Artist (1893). He remained in Paris through the interwar period, becoming a respected elder of French art, and died in 1940 after fleeing the German advance.
