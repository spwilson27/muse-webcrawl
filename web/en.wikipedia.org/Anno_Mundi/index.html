Anno Mundi

Anno Mundi (from Latin "in the year of the world"; Hebrew: l'vri'at ha'olam, "to the creation of the world"), abbreviated AM, is a calendar era based on the biblical accounts of the creation of the world and subsequent history. Two such calendar eras have seen notable use historically: the Byzantine era, used in the Eastern Roman (Byzantine) Empire and Orthodox countries, and the Hebrew calendar era, which remains in use in Judaism.

Hebrew calendar era

The Hebrew calendar counts years from the traditionally calculated date of creation, determined from the Masoretic Text of the Hebrew Bible. According to rabbinic reckoning in the Seder Olam Rabbah, creation occurred in a year corresponding to 3761 BC in the Julian calendar. The Hebrew year number therefore exceeds the Gregorian year by 3760 or 3761 (after the autumn new year, Rosh Hashanah). For example, most of the Gregorian year 2025 falls in the Hebrew year 5785 AM.

Byzantine era

The Byzantine Anno Mundi era, based on the Septuagint text, placed creation at 5509/5508 BC. It became the standard dating system of the Byzantine Empire from the late 10th century and remained in use in Russia until the calendar reforms of Peter the Great around 1700, and in some Orthodox contexts thereafter.

Other traditions

Western Christian chronographers produced their own creation eras: Bede's reckoning placed creation in 3952 BC, while the Ussher chronology of the 17th century famously arrived at 4004 BC. Freemasonry uses a related system, Anno Lucis, adding 4000 years to the Gregorian year.
