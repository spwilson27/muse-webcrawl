The Enderun School (Ottoman Turkish: اندرون مكتب, Enderûn Mektebi, "inner school") was the palace school of the Ottoman Empire, located in the inner (Enderun) court of Topkapı Palace in Istanbul. It educated boys — recruited largely through the devşirme levy of Christian children — for the highest offices of the imperial government, the palace service and the military, producing many of the empire's grand viziers, governors and artists.

Organisation

Students advanced through a hierarchy of chambers (oda) — from the Small and Large Chambers through the Falconer's, Expeditionary Force, Pantry, Treasury and Privy Chambers — combining training in Islamic law, Turkish, Arabic and Persian, mathematics, music, calligraphy and literature with martial arts, horsemanship and crafts. Advancement was strictly meritocratic under the supervision of the Kapı Ağası, the chief white eunuch, and graduates (in a ceremony called çıkma) were posted to senior provincial or palace positions.

Significance

Often compared to a university combined with a civil-service academy, the Enderun system underpinned the Ottoman state's famed ability to turn provincial and foreign-born recruits into loyal, highly trained administrators; a majority of grand viziers between the 15th and 17th centuries were its graduates. The school declined as the devşirme system lapsed in the 17th–18th centuries and was finally closed in 1909.
