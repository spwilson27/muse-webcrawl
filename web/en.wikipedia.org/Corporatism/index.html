Corporatism

Corporatism
Core ideaOrganisation of society into corporate interest groups recognised by the state
VariantsNeo-corporatism (societal), state corporatism (authoritarian)
Historical examplesFascist Italy; post-war Austria and Scandinavia (neo-corporatist)

Neo-corporatist bargaining brings government, employers, and unions into joint decision-making.

Corporatism is a political system of interest representation in which society is organised into recognised functional groups — "corporations" such as labour, business, agriculture, and the professions — that negotiate with each other and with the state. In its modern democratic form, called neo-corporatism, peak associations of employers and trade unions participate in making economic policy; in its authoritarian form, the state creates and controls the corporate bodies from above.

Origins

Corporatist ideas drew on medieval guilds, Catholic social teaching (notably the 1891 encyclical Rerum novarum), and syndicalism. The term derives from the Latin corpus, "body": society is conceived as an organism whose parts should cooperate rather than compete through class conflict or individualist markets.

Authoritarian corporatism

In the twentieth century corporatism was adopted by authoritarian regimes, most famously Mussolini's Italy, where the 1927 Charter of Labour and a system of state corporations were meant to replace parliamentary democracy and independent unions; the Chamber of Deputies was eventually replaced by the Chamber of Fasces and Corporations. Similar arrangements appeared in Salazar's Portugal, Franco's Spain, and Vargas's Brazil. In these systems corporatism functioned mainly as a means of state control.

Neo-corporatism

After 1945, democratic "societal" corporatism flourished in countries such as Austria, Sweden, Norway, and the Netherlands, where centralised wage bargaining between unions, employers, and government supported full employment and welfare states. Political scientists such as Philippe Schmitter distinguished this voluntary neo-corporatism sharply from the coercive state corporatism of the interwar dictatorships. Its influence has generally declined since the 1980s with decentralised bargaining and labour-market liberalisation.
