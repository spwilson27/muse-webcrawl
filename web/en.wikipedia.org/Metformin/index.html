Metformin is a medication used to treat type 2 diabetes, sold under brand names including Glucophage. It is the first-line drug for the condition in most guidelines, is notable for not causing weight gain or (when used alone) hypoglycaemia, and appears on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines. It belongs to the biguanide class and derives historically from compounds in the plant French lilac (Galega officinalis).

Medical uses

Metformin primarily lowers blood glucose by decreasing glucose production in the liver and reducing intestinal glucose absorption, while also improving insulin sensitivity. It is recommended as initial therapy for most people with type 2 diabetes, and may be combined with other agents or insulin. It is also used off-label for polycystic ovary syndrome and to counteract weight gain from antipsychotic medications. Research has explored possible anti-ageing and anti-cancer effects, though these are not established indications.

History

The biguanides were developed in the 1920s from guanidine compounds found in French lilac, a traditional remedy for the symptoms of diabetes. Metformin was synthesised in 1922 and studied clinically by the French physician Jean Sterne in the 1950s; it was introduced in France in 1957 and in the United States in 1995 — long delayed there because related biguanides (phenformin) had been withdrawn over lactic acidosis. The landmark UK Prospective Diabetes Study (1998) found cardiovascular benefits in overweight patients, cementing its first-line role.

Adverse effects

The most common side effects are gastrointestinal — nausea, diarrhoea, and abdominal discomfort — which extended-release preparations and slow dose titration help mitigate. Long-term use can cause vitamin B12 deficiency. The feared complication, lactic acidosis, is rare and occurs mainly in severe kidney or liver failure or acute illness; metformin is used cautiously or stopped when kidney function is severely reduced.
