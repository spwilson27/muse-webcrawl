Helmholtz's theorems

In fluid mechanics, Helmholtz's theorems, named for Hermann von Helmholtz, describe the three-dimensional motion of fluid in the vicinity of vortex lines. They were published in Helmholtz's 1858 paper "On Integrals of the Hydrodynamical Equations Which Express Vortex Motion". The theorems apply to inviscid flows and, in practice, to flows with low viscosity at points remote from boundaries.

Statements

The theorems are traditionally stated as three laws:

  1. First theorem: The strength of a vortex filament is constant along its length; a vortex line cannot end in the fluid — it must extend to the boundaries of the fluid, close on itself to form a vortex ring, or end at a boundary.
  2. Second theorem: A fluid element that is part of a vortex line at one time will always remain part of that vortex line; vortex lines move with the fluid (they are "frozen" into the flow, behaving as material lines).
  3. Third theorem: In the absence of rotational external forces, a fluid element that is initially irrotational remains irrotational; equivalently, the circulation around a material contour is conserved (closely related to Kelvin's circulation theorem).

Significance

Helmholtz's theorems are fundamental to the theory of lift and aerodynamics: they explain why a starting vortex is shed from a wing as a bound vortex forms around it, and they underpin the horseshoe vortex model of a finite wing. Because vortex lines cannot end in the fluid, the trailing vortices from wingtips extend back to the starting vortex, forming a closed system. The second theorem's requirement of inviscid flow explains why real vortices slowly diffuse and decay in viscous fluids. The ideas also have analogues in electromagnetism and modern topological fluid dynamics.
