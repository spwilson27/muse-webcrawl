The Tokubetsu Kōtō Keisatsu (特別高等警察, "Special Higher Police"), commonly abbreviated Tokkō, was the political police force of the Empire of Japan from 1911 to 1945. Established within the Home Ministry after the High Treason Incident of 1910, it was charged with investigating and suppressing political groups considered threats to the state — above all communists, anarchists, socialists and Korean independence activists. Because of its role in policing belief, it is often called the "thought police", and it is frequently compared with, though organisationally separate from, the military's Kempeitai.

History

The Tokkō was created in 1911 in the Tokyo Metropolitan Police Department and expanded nationwide after the Russian Revolution amid official fear of Bolshevism. Its powers grew with the Peace Preservation Law of 1925, which criminalised advocacy of altering the kokutai (national polity) or abolishing private property, and which the Tokkō enforced through surveillance, raids and arrests. Mass round-ups of communists followed in the March 15 Incident of 1928 and the April 16 Incident of 1929, effectively destroying the underground Japanese Communist Party by the mid-1930s.

Methods

Tokkō methods included informer networks, censorship support, interrogation and, increasingly, torture; the writer Takiji Kobayashi died in Tokkō custody in 1933, a case that became emblematic of its brutality. Rather than relying solely on punishment, the Tokkō pioneered the system of tenkō — ideological conversion — in which imprisoned leftists were pressured to renounce communism and were then released under supervision; the 1933 conversion statement of party leaders Sano Manabu and Nabeyama Sadachika triggered mass defections. During the 1930s and the Second World War the Tokkō's remit extended to religious groups, liberals, and critics of the war effort.

Abolition

The Tokkō was abolished in October 1945 by order of the Allied occupation authorities (SCAP), as part of the dismantling of Japan's mechanisms of thought control, along with the repeal of the Peace Preservation Law and the release of political prisoners. Its legacy continues to shape Japanese debates about state surveillance and civil liberties.
