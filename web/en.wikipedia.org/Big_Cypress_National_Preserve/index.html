Big Cypress National Preserve is a United States National Preserve located in South Florida, about 45 miles (72 km) west of Miami off the Tamiami Trail. The area lies immediately north of and adjacent to Everglades National Park. The preserve was established in 1974 to protect the Big Cypress Swamp watershed and is somewhat larger than the state of Rhode Island's land parcel in extent, encompassing roughly 720,567 acres (2,916 km²) of freshwater swamp, cypress forest, tropical hardwood hammocks, pinelands, prairies, and estuaries.

Unlike a national park, a national preserve permits certain uses that would otherwise be restricted, including hunting under state regulation, off-road vehicle use on designated trails, and the retention of private property and traditional uses by the Miccosukee and Seminole peoples. Big Cypress was the first unit of the National Park System designated as a national preserve.

Geography and hydrology

The preserve protects the western portion of the Big Cypress Swamp, a subtropical freshwater ecosystem fed by slow-moving sheet flow. Rainwater moves southward through sloughs, cypress strands, marl prairies, and mangrove estuaries toward the Ten Thousand Islands and the Gulf of Mexico. The watershed is essential to the health of the neighboring Everglades: water from Big Cypress helps sustain the estuaries of the Ten Thousand Islands and provides habitat recharge for the greater ecosystem.

Flora and fauna

Big Cypress is home to a rich diversity of wildlife, including the endangered Florida panther, American alligators, Florida black bears, white-tailed deer, bobcats, river otters, and a wide variety of wading birds such as wood storks, roseate spoonbills, and egrets. The preserve's cypress trees include dwarf pond cypress and towering bald cypress, along with royal palms, tropical hardwood hammocks, and wet prairies dotted with wildflowers and orchids, including the rare ghost orchid.

History

The region was inhabited for thousands of years by Indigenous peoples, most recently the Miccosukee and Seminole tribes, whose reserved rights were preserved in the enabling legislation. In the early 20th century, logging of old-growth cypress, oil exploration, and drainage for agriculture altered the landscape. In the late 1960s, plans for a massive jetport in the swamp galvanized the modern conservation movement; the airport project was halted after environmental studies showed it would devastate the Everglades, and the preserve was authorized in 1974 as part of the political settlement that followed. A single runway of the planned Everglades Jetport remains, used today for training flights.

Recreation

Visitors can explore the preserve via scenic drives such as the Loop Road, boardwalks at Kirby Storter Roadside Park, canoe trails, hiking routes including sections of the Florida National Scenic Trail, ranger-led programs, and designated off-road vehicle trails. Two visitor centers, the Oasis Visitor Center and the Nathaniel P. Reed Visitor Center, provide exhibits and information.
