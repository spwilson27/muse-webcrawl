Norman Rockwell

Norman Rockwell
Born: February 3, 1894, New York City, U.S.
Died: November 8, 1978, Stockbridge, Massachusetts, U.S.
Known for: Painting, illustration
Notable work: Saturday Evening Post covers; Four Freedoms series

Norman Percevel Rockwell (February 3, 1894 – November 8, 1978) was an American painter and illustrator whose work enjoyed broad popular appeal in the United States for its reflection of American culture. Rockwell is best known for the cover illustrations of everyday life he created for The Saturday Evening Post magazine over nearly five decades.

Early life

Rockwell was born in New York City, the second son of Jarvis Waring Rockwell and Anne Hill Rockwell. As a youth he studied at the Chase Art School, the National Academy of Design, and the Art Students League of New York, where he was taught by Thomas Fogarty and George Bridgman. At age 19 he became art editor of Boys' Life, the magazine of the Boy Scouts of America.

The Saturday Evening Post

In 1916 Rockwell submitted his first cover to The Saturday Evening Post; over the next 47 years he painted 323 covers for the magazine. His covers depicted small-town American life with warmth, humor, and meticulous detail, and became some of the most recognizable images in American visual culture. His association with the magazine ended in 1963, after which he worked for Look magazine, turning to themes of civil rights, poverty, and the space race.

Four Freedoms

In 1943, inspired by President Franklin D. Roosevelt's State of the Union address, Rockwell painted the Four Freedoms series: Freedom of Speech, Freedom of Worship, Freedom from Want, and Freedom from Fear. Published in The Saturday Evening Post, the paintings toured the country and helped raise more than $130 million in war bonds.

Later life and legacy

Rockwell spent his final years in Stockbridge, Massachusetts, where the Norman Rockwell Museum now preserves his studio and the largest collection of his work. In 1977 he received the Presidential Medal of Freedom. Although mid-century critics often dismissed his work as sentimental, his reputation has risen steadily, and he is now widely regarded as a defining chronicler of twentieth-century American life.
