Rule of 72

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In finance, the rule of 72 is a method for estimating an investment's doubling time. The rule states that the number of periods required to double an amount is approximately 72 divided by the interest rate per period (expressed as a percentage). For example, money invested at 6% annual interest doubles in about 72 ÷ 6 = 12 years.

Accuracy and derivation

The exact doubling time for a rate r is ln 2 / ln(1 + r). The number 72 is chosen because it approximates 100 × ln 2 ≈ 69.3 while adjusting upward for the compounding effect at typical interest rates, and because 72 has many divisors (1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9, 12…), making mental arithmetic easy. The rule is most accurate for rates between about 6% and 10%; a related rule of 69 (or 69.3) suits continuously compounded growth, and the rule of 70 is a common compromise.

History

An early reference appears in Luca Pacioli's Summa de arithmetica (1494), which states the rule without derivation. The rule is sometimes erroneously attributed to Albert Einstein, for whom it supposedly demonstrated the power of compound interest.

Uses

Besides doubling times for investments, the rule estimates how inflation erodes purchasing power (money halves in 72 ÷ inflation rate years) and how long population or GDP growth takes to double. Variants such as the rule of 114 and rule of 144 estimate tripling and quadrupling times.
