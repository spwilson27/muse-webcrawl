Vernacular photography refers to photographs, usually made by amateur photographers, that document everyday life and common subjects. The genre takes its name from vernacular architecture and language: just as vernacular buildings are made by local builders without formal design training, vernacular photographs are produced outside the traditions of fine-art photography and professional photojournalism, typically for personal, familial, or functional purposes.

History

Photography was practiced by amateurs from its earliest decades, but the genre expanded enormously with George Eastman's Kodak system (1888) and the inexpensive Brownie camera (1900), marketed with the slogan "You press the button, we do the rest." Photo booths, instant cameras (Polaroid, from 1948), disposable cameras, and finally digital cameras and smartphones successively broadened participation until billions of personal images were being made each year.

Characteristics and study

Vernacular photographs include snapshots, school and identity portraits, holiday pictures, real-photo postcards, and occupational or scientific records made for practical ends. Although long dismissed by art institutions, since the late 20th century vernacular photography has been collected by museums, studied by historians of everyday life, and mined by contemporary artists who appropriate found photographs. Scholars note recurring conventions — birthdays, weddings, new cars, posed groups — that make the images a rich archive of social customs.

Distinction from related terms

The term overlaps with "snapshot aesthetic" in art photography, where professionals such as Nan Goldin and the Düsseldorf school adopt casual styles, but vernacular photography proper denotes the pictures themselves rather than an artistic style derived from them.
