Astronomy is a natural science that studies celestial objects and phenomena. It uses mathematics, physics and chemistry to explain their origin and evolution. Objects of interest include planets, moons, stars, nebulae, galaxies and comets; relevant phenomena include supernova explosions, gamma ray bursts, quasars, blazars, pulsars and cosmic microwave background radiation. More generally, astronomy studies everything that originates beyond Earth's atmosphere.

History

Astronomy is among the oldest of the natural sciences. Babylonian, Egyptian, Greek, Chinese, Indian and Maya astronomers made systematic observations for calendars, navigation and ritual. Ptolemy's geocentric model dominated for over a millennium before Copernicus proposed heliocentrism in 1543; Galileo's telescopic observations from 1609, Kepler's laws, and Newton's gravitation transformed the field. In the 20th century, spectroscopy, photography, radio astronomy and space telescopes — above all Hubble — revealed an expanding universe of galaxies, dated by Edwin Hubble's measurements and the discovery of the cosmic microwave background.

Branches and methods

Observational astronomy gathers data across the electromagnetic spectrum: radio, infrared, optical, ultraviolet, X-ray and gamma-ray, plus gravitational waves and neutrinos. Theoretical astronomy models systems analytically and computationally. Major branches include solar astronomy, planetary science, stellar astronomy, galactic astronomy, extragalactic astronomy and physical cosmology. Amateur astronomers continue to contribute discoveries of comets, asteroids and variable stars.
