The Pancho Villa Expedition — now known officially in the United States as the Mexican Expedition, and sometimes called the Punitive Expedition — was a military operation conducted by the United States Army against the paramilitary forces of the Mexican revolutionary Francisco "Pancho" Villa during the Mexican Revolution. The expedition was launched in retaliation for Villa's raid on the town of Columbus, New Mexico, on 9 March 1916, in which 18 Americans were killed.

Course of the expedition

President Woodrow Wilson ordered Brigadier General John J. Pershing to pursue Villa into the Mexican state of Chihuahua. Beginning in mid-March 1916, American columns advanced deep into Mexico, eventually penetrating some 350 miles (560 km) south of the border. The campaign was notable for the early use of motor trucks and aeroplanes by the US Army: the 1st Aero Squadron provided reconnaissance, and truck companies supported the supply of the columns.

United States troops clashed with Villista forces at Guerrero and elsewhere, dispersing Villa's command, but Villa himself evaded capture. Tensions with the Mexican government of Venustiano Carranza, which had protested the incursion, led to armed clashes between American and Mexican federal troops at Parral in April 1916 and at Carrizal in June 1916, bringing the two countries close to war. A joint commission eventually negotiated an American withdrawal.

Outcome

The expedition failed in its immediate objective of capturing Villa, but it disrupted his forces and prevented further cross-border raids. It served as a valuable training and mobilisation experience for the US Army — and for Pershing and many officers who would serve in the First World War — shortly before American entry into that conflict. The last American troops withdrew from Mexico on 7 February 1917.
