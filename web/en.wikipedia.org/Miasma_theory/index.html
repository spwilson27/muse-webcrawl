Miasma theory

The miasma theory (from Greek miasma, "pollution") was the prevailing medical theory from antiquity until the late 19th century that diseases such as cholera, chlamydia and the Black Death were caused by a miasma — a noxious form of "bad air", also known as night air, emanating from rotting organic matter, stagnant water, and filth.

History

Ideas of disease-bearing air date to ancient Greece and Rome: Hippocratic writings linked illness to local airs and waters, and Galen developed the notion of miasmatic vapours. The word "malaria" itself derives from the Italian mal'aria ("bad air"), blamed on swamp fumes. In China and India, similar concepts of pestilential vapours existed independently.

The theory reached its greatest influence during the sanitary reforms of the 19th century. Edwin Chadwick and other reformers, believing disease arose from the stench of decay, championed sewage systems, clean water and ventilation — measures which, even though the underlying theory was wrong, greatly reduced waterborne disease and are credited with major public health gains.

Decline

Miasma theory competed with the contagionist idea that diseases spread from person to person. John Snow's 1854 investigation of the Broad Street cholera outbreak in London demonstrated a waterborne route of transmission, and the subsequent development of germ theory by Louis Pasteur, Robert Koch and others during the 1870s and 1880s displaced miasma theory in scientific medicine. Florence Nightingale, a committed miasmatist, nonetheless promoted hygiene practices that reduced mortality.
