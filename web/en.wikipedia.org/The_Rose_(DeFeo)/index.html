The Rose (DeFeo)

ArtistJay DeFeo
Year1958–1966
MediumOil with wood and mica on canvas
Dimensions328.6 cm × 234.3 cm × 27.9 cm; approx. 2,300 lb (1,040 kg)
LocationWhitney Museum of American Art, New York

The Rose is a monumental painting by American artist Jay DeFeo, created between 1958 and 1966 in her San Francisco studio. Nearly eleven feet tall and weighing over a ton, it is built up from paint layers as much as eleven inches thick, radiating from a central starburst form — one of the most famous and physically extreme paintings of the postwar period.

Creation

DeFeo, a central figure of the San Francisco Beat-era art scene, worked on the canvas for almost eight years, adding, carving and scraping oil paint mixed with wood and mica until the surface resembled a sculptural relief. She regarded the work as having "a center" toward which everything converged, and at various times titled it Deathrose and The White Rose before settling on The Rose.

Removal and disappearance

In 1965 DeFeo was evicted from her Fillmore Street studio; the painting, too large for the door, was removed by forklift through a cut in the wall — an event documented in Bruce Conner's short film The White Rose (1967). Exhibited at the Pasadena Art Museum in 1969, it was acquired by the San Francisco Art Institute, which, unable to conserve or display it, walled it behind a conference room partition in 1974, where it remained hidden for more than two decades.

Rediscovery and conservation

Rediscovered in 1994, the deteriorating work was acquired by the Whitney Museum of American Art and underwent a multi-year conservation campaign that stabilised its cracking, ton-weight structure. It was unveiled at the Whitney in 2003 and is now displayed on specially engineered supports as a cornerstone of the museum's postwar collection, and as the defining achievement of DeFeo's career.
