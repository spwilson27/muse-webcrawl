Ex post facto law

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An ex post facto law (from Latin, "after the fact") is a law that retroactively changes the legal consequences (or status) of actions that were committed, or relationships that existed, before the enactment of the law. In criminal law, it may criminalize actions that were legal when committed; it may aggravate a crime by increasing its punishment above what it was when committed; or it may change the rules of evidence to make conviction easier.

United States

The Constitution of the United States forbids Congress and the states from passing ex post facto laws. Article I, Section 9, Clause 3 provides that "No Bill of Attainder or ex post facto Law shall be passed" by Congress, and Article I, Section 10 applies the same prohibition to the states. The Supreme Court addressed the scope of the clause in Calder v. Bull (1798), in which Justice Samuel Chase listed four categories of laws that would be ex post facto: laws that make an act a crime that was innocent when done, laws that aggravate a crime beyond what it was when committed, laws that inflict a greater punishment than the law annexed to the crime when it was committed, and laws that alter the legal rules of evidence to permit conviction on less proof than was required at the time of the offence.

The prohibition applies only to criminal or punitive laws, not to civil legislation, although the Court has occasionally scrutinized civil laws that are so punitive in purpose or effect as to be criminal in nature. Retroactive tax laws, for example, have generally been upheld.

Other jurisdictions

Many legal systems restrict retroactive criminal laws. Article 7 of the European Convention on Human Rights prohibits punishment for an act that was not an offence at the time it was committed, with an exception for acts that were criminal according to the general principles of law recognised by civilised nations — a provision used in some prosecutions arising from the Second World War. Article 15 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights contains a similar rule. Canada, which has its own Charter prohibition under section 11(g), likewise restricts retroactive offences.

In some countries with civil law traditions, ex post facto civil laws are more widely accepted, though they may still be limited by constitutional principles of legal certainty and protection of legitimate expectations.
