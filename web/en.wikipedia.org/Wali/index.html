Wali

The tomb-shrine of a wali, a focus of devotion in many Muslim communities.

A wali (Arabic: وَلِيّ, walīy; plural أَوْلِيَاء, ʾawliyāʾ) is an Arabic word meaning "guardian", "protector", or "friend". In Islamic tradition — especially in Sufism — it most commonly denotes a Muslim saint, a person held to be a "friend of God" (walī Allāh) distinguished by exceptional piety and closeness to God.

Meanings

The Quran uses the term in several senses: God is described as the wali (protector) of the believers, and the phrase awliya Allah ("friends of God") appears in Quran 10:62 — "Verily, the friends of God shall have no fear, nor shall they grieve." In Islamic law, a wali also means a legal guardian, notably the male guardian whose consent is traditionally required for a woman's marriage contract.

Saints in Sufism

In Sufi thought, the awliya form a spiritual hierarchy through which divine grace is mediated to the world, headed by the qutb ("pole" or "axis"). Saints are believed to perform miracles (karamat), distinct from the miracles of prophets, and their tombs become pilgrimage sites where worshippers seek intercession and blessing (barakah). Veneration of saints and their shrines became central to popular Islam from Morocco to Indonesia, with festivals (urs, mawlid) marking saints' death anniversaries.

Criticism

Reformist movements such as Wahhabism and many Salafi currents condemn saint veneration and shrine pilgrimage as idolatry (shirk), and shrines have been demolished in areas under their control. Mainstream Sufi orders and many traditional scholars defend the practice as a legitimate expression of love for God's righteous servants.
