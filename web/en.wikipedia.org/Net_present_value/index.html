Net present value

The net present value (NPV) of a series of future cash flows is the difference between the present value of cash inflows and the present value of cash outflows over a period of time, where each cash flow is discounted at a rate reflecting the time value of money and the risk of the flows. NPV is a central tool of capital budgeting and investment appraisal in corporate finance.

NPV = Σt=0n Ct / (1 + r)t

where Ct is the net cash flow at time t and r is the discount rate, usually the project's cost of capital.

Decision rule

The standard decision rule is: accept a project if its NPV is positive (it is expected to add value after covering all costs including the required return on capital), reject it if NPV is negative, and be indifferent if NPV is zero. When mutually exclusive projects compete, the one with the higher NPV is preferred, because NPV measures value added directly in currency terms.

Interpretation and limitations

NPV rests on estimates of future cash flows and the choice of discount rate, both of which can be highly uncertain; small changes in the discount rate can materially change the result, especially for long-lived projects. Unlike the internal rate of return, NPV assumes reinvestment at the discount rate rather than at the project's own return, which is generally considered more realistic. NPV also ignores the value of managerial flexibility (real options) unless these are explicitly modelled, and it scales with project size, so percentage-return measures are often reported alongside it.
