The National Portrait Gallery (NPG) is an art gallery in London housing a collection of portraits of historically important or famous British people. When it opened in 1856, it was arguably the first national public gallery dedicated to portraits in the world. The gallery moved in 1896 to its current site at St Martin's Place, off Trafalgar Square, adjoining the National Gallery.

Collection

Uniquely, the gallery's primary criterion is the historical importance of the sitter rather than the artistic quality of the portrait, though it holds masterworks by artists including Hans Holbein the Younger, Anthony van Dyck, Joshua Reynolds, and Lucian Freud. Highlights include the Chandos portrait of William Shakespeare, portraits of the Tudor monarchs, and a large photographic archive. Only about a fraction of the more than 220,000 works can be displayed at any time.

History

The gallery was championed by Philip Henry Stanhope, 5th Earl Stanhope, with support from Thomas Babington Macaulay and Thomas Carlyle, whose vision was of a gallery of national worthies. It first opened at 29 Great George Street before moving to South Kensington and then to its permanent home.

Redevelopment

The gallery closed from 2020 to 2023 for the "Inspiring People" redevelopment, its largest building project, which added new galleries, restored the Victorian structure, and redisplayed the entire collection chronologically and thematically.
