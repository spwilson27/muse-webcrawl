A sphinx (plural sphinxes or sphinges) is a mythical creature with the head of a human, the body of a lion, and the wings of an eagle. In ancient Egyptian art, sphinxes were typically male and associated with royalty; in Greek tradition, the sphinx was female, treacherous and merciless, famous for the riddle she posed to travellers.

Egypt

The most famous sphinx is the Great Sphinx of Giza, a colossal limestone statue on the west bank of the Nile, generally attributed to the reign of Pharaoh Khafre (c. 2558–2532 BCE). It is 73 metres (240 ft) long and 20 metres (66 ft) high — the largest monolith statue in the world. Egyptian sphinxes, including the many androsphinxes (human-headed) and criosphinxes (ram-headed) that lined processional ways such as the avenue at Karnak, embodied royal strength and wisdom.

Greece

In Greek myth, the Sphinx of Thebes was a monster sent against the city; she asked all passers-by a riddle — "Which creature has one voice and yet becomes four-footed and two-footed and three-footed?" — and devoured those who could not answer. Oedipus solved it ("Man, who crawls on all fours as a baby, walks on two legs as an adult, and leans on a staff in old age"), whereupon the Sphinx destroyed herself. The story is central to Sophocles' Oedipus Rex.

Asia

Sphinx-like figures also appear in South and Southeast Asian art: the Indian purushamriga ("man-beast") appears in temple sculpture, and lion-human guardians (such as Burma's manussiha) protect pagodas.

Later art

Sphinxes were revived in Renaissance and Neoclassical art and architecture, and again in the Symbolist art of the 19th century (Gustave Moreau's Oedipus and the Sphinx, 1864). They remain popular garden ornaments and heraldic devices.
