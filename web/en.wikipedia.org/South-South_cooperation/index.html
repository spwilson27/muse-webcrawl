South–South cooperation is a term used by policymakers in the international development community for the exchange of resources, technology, and knowledge between developing countries, collectively known as the Global South. It is presented as a complement to traditional North–South aid, emphasising horizontal partnership, mutual benefit, and non-conditionality rather than donor–recipient hierarchy.

History

Its political roots lie in the 1955 Bandung Conference of Asian and African states and in the Non-Aligned Movement and the Group of 77 at the United Nations. The 1978 Buenos Aires Plan of Action gave the concept an institutional framework for "technical cooperation among developing countries". From the 2000s, rapid growth of emerging economies greatly expanded South–South trade, investment, and development finance—most prominently China's Belt and Road Initiative, India's development partnerships and lines of credit, Brazil's technical cooperation, and Cuba's overseas medical brigades.

Forms and debate

Cooperation spans infrastructure finance, agriculture and health programmes, training and scholarships, peacekeeping, and joint standard-setting through forums such as BRICS and the Forum on China–Africa Cooperation. Supporters argue it shares recent, context-appropriate development experience; critics question the transparency of flows, debt sustainability, and whether large emerging-market programmes replicate the tied-aid practices they claim to avoid. Triangular cooperation, in which a traditional donor supports a South–South exchange, has grown as a hybrid model.
