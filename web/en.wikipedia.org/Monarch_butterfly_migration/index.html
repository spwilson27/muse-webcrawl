Monarch butterfly migration


Species: Monarch butterfly (Danaus plexippus)
Distance: up to about 4,800 km (3,000 mi)
Overwintering sites: Central Mexico; coastal California

Monarch butterfly migration is the phenomenon in which the eastern North American population of the monarch butterfly (Danaus plexippus plexippus) travels each autumn from breeding grounds in the northern United States and southern Canada to overwintering sites in the oyamel fir forests of central Mexico — a journey of up to several thousand kilometres — and returns north in spring over several generations.

The cycle

Unlike birds, no individual monarch completes the round trip. Butterflies born in late summer and autumn enter reproductive diapause and live six to nine months, flying south to cluster in dense colonies on fir trees in the mountains of Michoacán and the State of Mexico. In spring these butterflies mate and move north, laying eggs on milkweed; their short-lived descendants continue the recolonization in successive generations, typically reaching Canada by the third or fourth generation. A western population migrates shorter distances to overwintering groves along the California coast.

Navigation

Monarchs navigate using a time-compensated sun compass, with circadian clocks located in their antennae, and can also sense Earth's magnetic field, which may serve as a backup compass on overcast days.

Conservation

Overwintering numbers have declined sharply since the 1990s, owing to loss of oyamel forest, declines in milkweed caused by herbicide use and habitat loss, extreme weather, and climate change. In 2022 the International Union for Conservation of Nature listed the migratory monarch as endangered. Mexico's Monarch Butterfly Biosphere Reserve, a UNESCO World Heritage Site, protects the main overwintering colonies.
