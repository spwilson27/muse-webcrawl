New Netherland (Dutch: Nieuw Nederland) was a 17th-century colonial province of the Dutch Republic located on the east coast of North America, encompassing territory in the present-day U.S. states of New York, New Jersey, Delaware, and parts of Connecticut and Pennsylvania. Claimed after Henry Hudson's 1609 voyage for the Dutch East India Company and settled from 1624 under the Dutch West India Company, its capital, New Amsterdam, stood at the southern tip of Manhattan Island.

History

Dutch traders established fur-trading posts along the Hudson River (Noortrivier), including Fort Orange near present-day Albany (1624). In 1626 Director Peter Minuit famously arranged the purchase of Manhattan from Native American inhabitants for trade goods legendarily valued at 60 guilders. The colony grew slowly around the fur trade and the patroonship system, notably the vast manor of Rensselaerswijck.

Relations with Native peoples deteriorated into Kieft's War (1643–45) under Director Willem Kieft. Peter Stuyvesant, director-general from 1647, imposed order, clashed with the neighboring English colonies and New Sweden (conquered in 1655), and surrendered New Amsterdam to an English fleet in 1664. Briefly retaken by the Dutch in 1673–74 during the Third Anglo-Dutch War, the colony was permanently ceded to England by the Treaty of Westminster (1674) and renamed New York.

Legacy

Despite its short life, New Netherland left enduring marks: Dutch place names (Brooklyn, Harlem, Staten Island, the Bronx), legal and religious tolerance traditions, architectural forms, and a multi-ethnic commercial character that foreshadowed New York City's later development.
