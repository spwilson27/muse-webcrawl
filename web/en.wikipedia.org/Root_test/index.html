Root test

In mathematics, the root test is a criterion for the convergence of an infinite series. It is named after Augustin-Louis Cauchy, who first published it, and is therefore also known as the Cauchy root test. The test depends on the behaviour of the nth root of the absolute value of the terms of the series as n tends to infinity.

Statement

Let an be the terms of a series, and define

L = lim sup n→∞ |an|1/n.

Then:

• If L < 1, the series converges absolutely.
• If L > 1, the series diverges.
• If L = 1, the test is inconclusive, and the series may converge absolutely, converge conditionally, or diverge.

Explanation

The root test works by comparing the series with a geometric series. If the nth root of |an| eventually stays below some number r < 1, then |an| is eventually bounded by rn, and since the geometric series with ratio r converges, the original series converges absolutely by the comparison test. Conversely, if the root eventually exceeds 1, the terms do not tend to zero, so the series must diverge.

The root test is stronger than the ratio test in the sense that whenever the ratio test determines convergence or divergence (using limits), the root test gives the same answer, but there are series for which the root test succeeds where the ratio test's limit does not exist. This is because the limit superior of the nth roots is always defined, and the geometric mean smoothing in the root makes it at least as powerful.

Examples

For the series Σ (1/2)n, the nth root of the general term is 1/2, so L = 1/2 < 1 and the series converges. For Σ nn/n!, the root test gives L = e > 1 in a related form and the series can be shown to diverge. For the harmonic series Σ 1/n, the nth root of 1/n tends to 1, so the test is inconclusive even though the series diverges—illustrating the limit of the criterion.
