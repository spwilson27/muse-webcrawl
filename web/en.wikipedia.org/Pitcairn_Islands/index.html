The Pitcairn Islands are a group of four volcanic islands in the southern Pacific Ocean, forming the last British Overseas Territory in the Pacific. Only the second-largest, Pitcairn Island itself (3.6 km long), is inhabited — by the descendants of the mutineers of HMS Bounty and their Tahitian companions. With roughly fifty residents, it is the smallest political jurisdiction in the world by population, accessible only by sea, most regularly via supply ship from Mangareva in French Polynesia.

History

Pitcairn was first sighted by Europeans in 1767 and named for midshipman Robert Pitcairn. In January 1790, nine mutineers led by Fletcher Christian landed there with eleven Tahitian women and six Tahitian men, burned the Bounty in what is now Bounty Bay, and founded a settlement. Violence consumed most of the men; by 1800 John Adams was the sole surviving mutineer, leading a community of women and children. Rediscovered by an American sealing ship in 1808, the islanders became celebrated in Britain. The population outgrew the island, and the whole community was resettled on Norfolk Island in 1856, though some families returned. Pitcairn formally became a British colony in 1838 — and notably granted women the vote that same year, among the earliest jurisdictions to do so.

Geography and the other islands

The group comprises Pitcairn, Henderson Island (a raised coral atoll and UNESCO World Heritage Site prized for its endemic birds), and the atolls of Ducie and Oeno. The surrounding exclusive economic zone, some 836,000 km², was designated a marine protected area in 2016, one of the world's largest.

Economy and life

Islanders support themselves through subsistence agriculture, handicrafts and curios sold to passing ships and online, honey production, postage stamps and collectible coins, and government employment subsidised from the United Kingdom. There is no airport; supplies arrive quarterly. The community speaks English and Pitkern, a creole blending 18th-century English and Tahitian.
