Nonconformist (Protestantism)

Nonconformity
RegionHistorically England and Wales; related movements elsewhere
OriginEnglish Reformation; crystallised by the Act of Uniformity 1662
TraditionsBaptists, Congregationalists, Methodists, Presbyterians, Quakers, Unitarians
Key figuresJohn Bunyan, John Wesley, Richard Baxter

Nonconformists are Protestant Christians who do not "conform" to the governance and usages of the established church — historically, in England and Wales, the Church of England. The term emerged after the Restoration of the monarchy in 1660, and was consolidated by the Act of Uniformity 1662, which required all ministers to assent to the Book of Common Prayer and episcopal ordination. Around 2,000 clergy who refused were ejected from their livings in the "Great Ejection" of 1662, forming the core of organised Dissent.

History

Origins

English Dissent grew out of the Puritan movement within the Elizabethan and Stuart church, which sought further reformation of worship and church government. Under Charles II, the Clarendon Code — including the Corporation Act (1661), the Act of Uniformity (1662), the Conventicle Act (1664) and the Five Mile Act (1665) — penalised Nonconformist worship and excluded Dissenters from municipal office and the universities.

Toleration and growth

The Toleration Act 1689, passed after the Glorious Revolution, granted freedom of worship to Trinitarian Protestant dissenters who registered their meeting houses, though civil disabilities largely remained until the repeal of the Test and Corporation Acts in 1828 and Catholic emancipation in 1829. The eighteenth and nineteenth centuries saw Nonconformity flourish, particularly through the Methodist revival led by John Wesley and George Whitefield, and through Baptist and Congregational chapel-building in the industrial towns of the North and in Wales, where Nonconformity became the dominant religious culture.

Beliefs and practice

Nonconformist traditions vary widely but typically emphasise the authority of Scripture, congregational independence or connexional governance rather than episcopacy, believer's baptism (among Baptists) or infant baptism, plain worship without prescribed liturgy, and a "gathered church" of professing believers. The term is generally not applied to Roman Catholics (historically called "recusants") or, in Scotland, to those outside the Church of Scotland, where the roles of establishment and dissent were differently arranged.

Political influence

The "Nonconformist conscience" became a major force in Victorian and Edwardian politics, closely associated with the Liberal Party: campaigns for disestablishment of the Church in Wales (achieved 1920), temperance, education reform and against the privileges of the Anglican establishment drew heavily on chapel culture. Figures such as the Welsh Liberal leader David Lloyd George emerged from this milieu.

Decline and legacy

Twentieth-century secularisation and ecumenism reduced the distinctiveness of Nonconformity; in 1972 many English Congregationalists and Presbyterians united to form the United Reformed Church. Many historic chapels remain landmarks of British towns, and the term "Nonconformist" survives in law and in the description of "free churches."
