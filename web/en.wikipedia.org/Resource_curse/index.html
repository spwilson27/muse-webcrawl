Resource curse

The resource curse, also known as the paradox of plenty, is the phenomenon whereby countries with an abundance of natural resources (such as fossil fuels and certain minerals) have less economic growth, less democracy, or worse development outcomes than countries with fewer natural resources. The term was coined by Richard Auty in 1993, although the underlying ideas had been studied earlier.

Mechanisms

Several mechanisms have been proposed. Dutch disease describes how a resource boom appreciates the currency and crowds out manufacturing and agriculture, leaving the economy exposed when prices fall. Commodity price volatility destabilises government revenue. Resource rents can also weaken institutions: governments that fund themselves from resource sales rather than taxation may be less accountable, and rents can fuel corruption, rent-seeking, and conflict over control of the resource.

Evidence

Influential studies by Sachs and Warner in the 1990s found a negative statistical association between resource abundance and growth. The strength and interpretation of the effect remain debated: later research suggests the curse is conditional on institutional quality, and some resource-rich countries, such as Norway, Botswana, Canada, and Australia, have prospered.

Policy responses

Proposed remedies include sovereign wealth funds to save windfalls and smooth spending, transparency initiatives such as the Extractive Industries Transparency Initiative, economic diversification, and fiscal rules limiting spending of resource revenue.
