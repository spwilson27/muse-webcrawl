Leveraged buyout

A leveraged buyout (LBO) is a type of financial transaction in which a company is acquired primarily with borrowed money. The assets of the company being acquired are often used as collateral for the loans, along with the assets of the acquiring company. The use of debt, which normally has a lower cost of capital than equity, allows the acquirer to raise returns on its invested equity and to carry out large acquisitions without committing much of its own capital. LBOs are typically executed by private equity firms, which seek to improve the target's performance and sell it, or take it public again, within several years.

How an LBO works

In a typical structure, the sponsor forms an acquisition vehicle that raises a stack of financing: senior secured bank loans, subordinated or mezzanine debt, and sometimes high-yield bonds, alongside a minority contribution of sponsor equity (historically as little as 10–30% of the price). The target's own cash flows service and repay the debt, so buyers favour companies with stable, predictable cash generation, low existing leverage and saleable assets. Returns come from debt paydown, operational improvement and multiple expansion at exit — via a sale, recapitalisation or initial public offering.

History

Early examples include the McLean Industries purchase of Pan-Atlantic Steamship Company in 1955. The technique flowered in the 1980s with firms such as Kohlberg Kravis Roberts, financed by junk bonds arranged by Drexel Burnham Lambert, culminating in the $31 billion buyout of RJR Nabisco in 1988–89 — chronicled in Barbarians at the Gate. After a lull in the early 1990s and 2000s retrenchment, cheap credit fuelled a second boom in 2006–07 (TXU, Hilton Hotels, First Data), followed by a sharp contraction in the financial crisis. LBOs remain a central activity of the private equity industry.

Criticism

LBOs are criticised for loading companies with debt, cutting jobs and research, extracting fees and dividends, and benefiting from tax-deductible interest, while failures under heavy debt can destroy the business. Defenders argue that leverage imposes discipline on managers, aligns incentives, and transfers assets to more efficient owners.
