Francis Bacon

Francis Bacon (1561–1626), philosopher, statesman, and essayist

Francis Bacon, 1st Viscount St Alban (22 January 1561 – 9 April 1626), was an English philosopher and statesman who served as Attorney General and Lord Chancellor of England. He is known as the father of empiricism: his works argued for the possibility of scientific knowledge based only upon inductive reasoning and careful observation of events in nature, and he is credited with developing the Baconian method, an early form of the scientific method.

Life and career

The son of Sir Nicholas Bacon, Lord Keeper of the Great Seal, Bacon was educated at Trinity College, Cambridge, and Gray's Inn. He sat in Parliament from 1584 and rose under James I, becoming Lord Chancellor in 1618 and Viscount St Alban in 1621. That same year he was charged with accepting bribes, fined, imprisoned briefly, and barred from office — a fall he attributed to political faction rather than genuine corruption. He spent his retirement writing at Gorhambury, and died in 1626, reportedly after catching a chill while conducting an experiment on preserving meat with snow.

Works and philosophy

In Novum Organum (1620) Bacon set out a new logic of induction, critiquing the "Idols" — prejudices of the mind — that obstruct inquiry. His Essays are classics of English prose, and the utopian New Atlantis envisioned a research institute, Salomon's House, prefiguring the Royal Society. His famous aphorism "knowledge is power" (ipsa scientia potestas est) encapsulates his vision of science in service of human welfare.
