William Harvey (1 April 1578 – 3 June 1657) was an English physician who made influential contributions to anatomy and physiology. He was the first known physician to describe completely, and in detail, the systemic circulation and the properties of blood being pumped to the brain and the rest of the body by the heart.

Education and career

Harvey was educated at King's School, Canterbury, and Gonville and Caius College, Cambridge, before studying medicine at the University of Padua, where he graduated in 1602 under the anatomist Hieronymus Fabricius. Returning to London, he became a fellow of the Royal College of Physicians, physician to St Bartholomew's Hospital, and Lumleian lecturer. He was appointed physician extraordinary to James I and later physician in ordinary to Charles I, whom he accompanied during the English Civil War.

Circulation of the blood

In 1628 Harvey published Exercitatio Anatomica de Motu Cordis et Sanguinis in Animalibus (De Motu Cordis), arguing from experiment, dissection and quantitative reasoning that blood is pumped by the heart around the body in a closed circuit, passing from arteries to veins. His calculation that the heart moves more blood in an hour than the body contains undermined the Galenic system that had dominated medicine for over a millennium. The work was initially controversial but was widely accepted within his lifetime; the missing capillary links were observed by Marcello Malpighi shortly after Harvey's death.

Later work

Harvey's De Generatione Animalium (1651) studied the development of the chick and deer embryo and popularised the doctrine ex ovo omnia ("everything from an egg"). He died in 1657 and was buried at Hempstead, Essex.
