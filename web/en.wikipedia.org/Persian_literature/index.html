Persian literature

Persian literature comprises the oral compositions and written texts in the Persian language, one of the world's oldest and most influential literary traditions. It spans more than two millennia, from the inscriptions and hymns of ancient Persia through the classical flowering of the 9th to 15th centuries to the modern literature of Iran, Afghanistan and Tajikistan, where Persian is known respectively as Farsi, Dari and Tajik.

Classical literature

New Persian literature emerged after the Arab conquest, written in Arabic script. Ferdowsi's epic Shahnameh ("Book of Kings", completed c. 1010), some 50,000 couplets recounting the mythical and historical past of Persia, is regarded as the national epic of Iran. Other monuments include:

  • The Rubaiyat of Omar Khayyam, quatrains on fate and the pleasures of life;
  • The Masnavi of Rumi, a vast Sufi poem of spiritual instruction;
  • The Divan of Hafez, lyric ghazals beloved throughout the Persian-speaking world;
  • Saadi's Gulistan ("The Rose Garden") and Bustan, classics of prose and verse wisdom;
  • Attar's Conference of the Birds and Nizami's romantic epics, including Layla and Majnun.

Forms and influence

Classical Persian poetry is built on quantitative metre and favours forms such as the ghazal, qasida, masnavi and ruba'i. Persian literature profoundly shaped the literatures of the Ottoman Empire, Mughal India and Central Asia, and Persian was for centuries the lingua franca of culture from Anatolia to Bengal.

Modern literature

The 20th century brought free verse, pioneered by Nima Yooshij, the fiction of Sadegh Hedayat — whose novel The Blind Owl (1937) is a landmark of modernism — and the poetry of Forough Farrokhzad and Ahmad Shamlou. Contemporary Iranian writing, including the works of Shahrnush Parsipur, continues to win international attention despite censorship.
