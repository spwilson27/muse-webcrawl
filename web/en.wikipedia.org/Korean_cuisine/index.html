Korean cuisine is the set of cooking traditions and practices of Korea, developed over centuries of social and political change. A Korean meal is built around steamed rice (bap), accompanied by a soup or stew and a variety of shared side dishes called banchan, of which the most emblematic is kimchi — vegetables, most famously napa cabbage, salted and fermented with chilli pepper flakes (gochugaru), garlic, ginger and seafood seasonings.

Characteristics

Fermentation is central to the cuisine: the three great jang pastes — doenjang (soybean paste), ganjang (soy sauce) and gochujang (chilli paste) — mature in earthenware crocks (onggi) and provide the savoury depth of everyday cooking. Korean food is noted for bold flavours: pungent garlic, sesame oil, ginger and chilli heat, balanced against mild rice and broths. Traditionally, meals were also guided by the philosophy of the five colours (obangsaek) and by medicinal ideas about food.

Principal dishes

  • Bibimbap — rice mixed with seasoned vegetables, meat, a fried egg and gochujang.
  • Bulgogi — thinly sliced beef marinated in soy sauce, sugar and sesame, then grilled.
  • Korean barbecue — meats such as samgyeopsal (pork belly) and galbi (short ribs) grilled at the table and wrapped in lettuce with ssamjang.
  • Kimchi jjigae and doenjang jjigae — everyday stews made with kimchi or soybean paste.
  • Japchae — stir-fried glass noodles made from sweet-potato starch with vegetables.
  • Tteokbokki — chewy rice cakes simmered in a sweet-spicy chilli sauce, a popular street food.
  • Samgye-tang — ginseng chicken soup, traditionally eaten on the hottest days of summer.

History

Korean cooking developed from the peninsula's agricultural rhythms — rice farming in the south, dry-field grains in the north — and was shaped by Buddhism's emphasis on vegetable dishes, Confucian court cuisine under the Joseon dynasty, and later exchanges: chilli peppers arrived from the Americas via Japan in the 16th–17th centuries and transformed kimchi into its now-familiar red form. Royal court cuisine (gungjung eumsik) codified elaborate multi-course presentations, while temple food preserved a refined vegetarian tradition. The division of Korea after 1945 and the Korean War introduced new ingredients and dishes, and from the late 20th century Korean food spread worldwide with the Korean diaspora and the global popularity of Korean popular culture.

Drinks

Traditional alcoholic drinks include soju (a distilled spirit), makgeolli (a milky rice wine) and cheongju (clear rice wine). Meals are often accompanied by barley tea or water, and desserts tend toward rice cakes (tteok), the shaved-ice dish bingsu, and sweet drinks such as sikhye.
