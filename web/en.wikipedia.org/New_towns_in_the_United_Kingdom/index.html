New towns in the United Kingdom

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

New towns in the United Kingdom are towns that were built after the Second World War under the New Towns Act 1946 (and later acts) to relocate populations away from large cities whose housing had been damaged in the war or was overcrowded and insanitary. Development was carried out by publicly appointed development corporations, which had powers to acquire land and build housing, industry, schools, and infrastructure according to master plans.

Waves of designation

The first wave, designated from 1946, consisted largely of towns around London — including Stevenage (the first, designated in 1946), Crawley, Hemel Hempstead, Harlow, and Basildon — intended to relieve pressure on the capital and implement the decentralisation vision of the Greater London Plan. A second wave in the 1960s included larger and more ambitious projects such as Milton Keynes, designated in 1967 as a new city for some 250,000 people, as well as Telford, Runcorn, Warrington, and Washington. New towns were also designated in Scotland (such as Cumbernauld, East Kilbride, Glenrothes, Irvine, and Livingston) and in Wales (Cwmbran and Newtown).

Design and legacy

British new towns typically featured neighbourhood units separated by landscaped green space, segregated pedestrian and vehicle circulation, purpose-built town centres, and a deliberate balance of housing and employment. They housed millions of people and are widely regarded as one of the most successful urban planning programmes in British history, though some have been criticised for car-dependence, monotonous architecture, and town centres that aged poorly. The development corporations were wound up in the late 20th century and their assets transferred to successor bodies. In the 21st century, the British government has again promoted new towns and large planned settlements as a response to the national housing shortage.
