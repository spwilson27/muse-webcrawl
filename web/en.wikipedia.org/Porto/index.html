Porto is the second-largest city in Portugal, after Lisbon, and the historical capital of the country's north. It stands on the northern bank of the Douro River near its mouth on the Atlantic. Porto gave its name both to Portugal itself (from the Roman Portus Cale) and to port wine, aged in the lodges of Vila Nova de Gaia across the river.

History

The settlement of Portus Cale dates to Roman times. Porto played a central role in Portugal's maritime expansion, and its citizens earned the nickname tripeiros ("tripe-eaters") for giving their meat to Henry the Navigator's fleet. The city resisted during the Peninsular War and the 1832–33 Siege of Porto in the Liberal Wars. Its medieval Ribeira quarter, the Clérigos Tower, São Bento station's azulejo panels and the iron Dom Luís I Bridge (1886) define its skyline.

Economy and culture

Greater Porto is a centre of textiles, footwear, wine and increasingly technology and tourism. Cultural landmarks include the Serralves Museum of contemporary art and Rem Koolhaas's Casa da Música (2005). The city's football club, FC Porto, is one of Portugal's "Big Three".
