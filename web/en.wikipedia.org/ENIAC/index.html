ENIAC

ENIAC (Electronic Numerical Integrator and Computer) was the first programmable, electronic, general-purpose digital computer, completed in 1945 at the University of Pennsylvania's Moore School of Electrical Engineering. It was designed by John Mauchly and J. Presper Eckert and funded by the United States Army to calculate artillery firing tables.

Design and operation

ENIAC contained roughly 17,468 vacuum tubes, 70,000 resistors, 10,000 capacitors and 1,500 relays; it weighed about 30 tons, occupied around 1,800 square feet and consumed some 150 kW of power. It operated in decimal rather than binary and could perform about 5,000 additions per second — orders of magnitude faster than electromechanical machines. Programming was initially done by physically rewiring plugboards and setting switches, a task carried out by a team of six women programmers — Kay McNulty, Betty Jennings, Betty Snyder, Marlyn Wescoff, Fran Bilas and Ruth Lichterman — whose contributions were recognised only decades later.

History

Publicly unveiled in February 1946, ENIAC was moved to the Ballistic Research Laboratory at Aberdeen Proving Ground in 1947. Its first production problem was a calculation related to the hydrogen bomb. In 1948 it was modified to run stored programs read from its function tables, in a form devised with input from John von Neumann. ENIAC ran until 2 October 1955, when it was shut down. Panels of the machine are preserved at the Smithsonian Institution, the University of Pennsylvania and other museums. In 1997 the IEEE designated it an IEEE Milestone.

Legacy

Eckert and Mauchly went on to found the Eckert–Mauchly Computer Corporation and build the UNIVAC I, the first commercial American computer. A long-running patent dispute culminated in the 1973 court decision Honeywell v. Sperry Rand, which invalidated the ENIAC patent and credited John Atanasoff's earlier work.
