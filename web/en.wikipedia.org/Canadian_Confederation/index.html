Canadian Confederation

Canadian Confederation (French: Confédération canadienne) was the process by which three British North American colonies — the Province of Canada, Nova Scotia, and New Brunswick — were united into one federation, the Dominion of Canada, on July 1, 1867. The term is also used to describe the subsequent expansion of the federation to include other provinces and territories.

Background

By the 1860s the Province of Canada (present-day Ontario and Quebec) was plagued by political deadlock between Canada East and Canada West. Maritime colonies faced economic pressures, and the American Civil War and Fenian raids raised fears that the United States might annex British North America. Britain favoured consolidation of its colonies into a stronger, self-governing federation.

The conferences

Delegates met at the Charlottetown Conference in September 1864, originally called to discuss Maritime union, and then at the Quebec Conference in October 1864, which produced the Seventy-Two Resolutions setting out the framework of the union. A final conference in London in 1866 finalized the terms, and the British Parliament passed the British North America Act, 1867.

The Dominion

The new Dominion consisted of four provinces: Ontario, Quebec, Nova Scotia, and New Brunswick. John A. Macdonald, a leading advocate of union, became Canada's first prime minister. The constitution divided powers between a federal Parliament and provincial legislatures and established a parliamentary system modelled on Britain's.

Expansion

Manitoba and the Northwest Territories joined in 1870, British Columbia in 1871, Prince Edward Island in 1873, Saskatchewan and Alberta in 1905, and Newfoundland — the last province — in 1949. The Arctic territories of Nunavut were created in 1999, completing the modern map of Canada.

Legacy

Confederation Day, July 1, is celebrated as Canada Day. The Fathers of Confederation are commemorated across the country, while historians continue to debate the exclusion of Indigenous peoples and other groups from the original negotiations.
