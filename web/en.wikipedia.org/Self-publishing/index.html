Self-publishing

Self-publishing
DefinitionPublication of media by its author without a traditional publisher
FormatsBooks, ebooks, music, magazines, comics, games, video
Key enablersPrint on demand, ebook platforms, desktop publishing
ExamplesAmazon Kindle Direct Publishing, IngramSpark, Smashwords

Self-publishing is the publication of media by its author without the involvement of an established third-party publisher. The author bears the cost and controls the process — editing, design, pricing, and marketing — and typically retains full rights in the work. The term most often refers to books, but applies equally to music, art, magazines, video, and games.

History

Authors have financed their own works for centuries: John Milton, William Blake, and later Virginia Woolf (through the Hogarth Press) all self-published, and "vanity presses" long offered paid publication to aspiring writers. The digital revolution transformed the economics. Desktop publishing in the 1980s, print on demand in the 1990s, and above all the ebook — Amazon launched the Kindle and its Direct Publishing platform in 2007 — removed the need for print runs, warehousing, and distribution deals.

Modern practice

Self-published authors commonly hire freelance editors and cover designers, purchase their own ISBNs, and distribute through retailers such as Amazon, Apple Books, and Kobo, or print services such as IngramSpark that also supply bookstores. Royalty rates on ebooks can reach 70 percent, far above traditional publishing contracts, but sales are heavily skewed: a small number of self-published successes — such as Andy Weir's The Martian and E. L. James's Fifty Shades of Grey, both later picked up by major publishers — stand against a vast output of titles that sell few copies.

Advantages and disadvantages

Advantages include creative control, speed, higher per-copy royalties, and retained rights. Disadvantages include the author bearing all costs and labour, difficulty obtaining physical bookstore distribution and mainstream review coverage, and a lingering stigma in some quarters. Self-publishing differs from vanity publishing, in which a company charges the author while posing as a selective traditional publisher. See also print on demand and vanity press.
