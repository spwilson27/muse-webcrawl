Julius Evola

Giulio Cesare Andrea Evola (Italian: [ˈɛːvola]; 19 May 1898 – 11 June 1974), generally known as Julius Evola, was an Italian philosopher, esotericist, and painter. He is known as a leading exponent of the Traditionalist School and for his writings on spiritualism, politics, and race.

Life and thought

Born in Rome to a Sicilian family, Evola fought as an artillery officer in the First World War and was briefly involved with the Dada art movement. He developed a philosophy he called "magical idealism" and later embraced Traditionalism, influenced by René Guénon. His most famous work, Revolt Against the Modern World (1934), contrasted an idealised traditional world of caste, hierarchy, and transcendence with a decadent modernity; Men Among the Ruins (1953) and Ride the Tiger (1961) extended his political and existential thought.

Controversy

Evola's anti-egalitarian, anti-democratic, and racial theories made him an influential and controversial figure: he attempted to influence Fascist Italy's racial policy, was regarded with suspicion by parts of both the Fascist and Nazi regimes, and after the war was tried and acquitted of charges of promoting fascism. His books have remained influential in far-right and neo-fascist circles, a legacy that continues to be debated by scholars.
