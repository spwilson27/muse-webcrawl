Mother sauces

[Image: The five mother sauces of French cuisine]

The mother sauces (French: sauces mères) are a group of basic sauces in French cuisine from which a large family of derivative or "daughter" sauces is made. The classification is credited to Antonin Carême in the early nineteenth century and was codified by Auguste Escoffier in Le Guide Culinaire (1903). Escoffier's canonical five are:

The five sauces

  • Béchamel - milk thickened with a white roux.
  • Velouté - a light stock (veal, chicken or fish) thickened with a blond roux.
  • Espagnole - a brown stock thickened with a brown roux, with tomato and mirepoix.
  • Hollandaise - an emulsion of butter and lemon juice (or vinegar) with egg yolks.
  • Tomate - a tomato-based sauce, classically with pork and aromatics.

Carême's original list had four (velouté, béchamel, espagnole and allemande); Escoffier replaced allemande with hollandaise and added tomate. Derivative sauces include demi-glace (from espagnole), sauce Mornay (béchamel with cheese), sauce Suprême (from velouté), and béarnaise (from hollandaise).
