Moral realism

Moral realism is the position in metaethics that moral statements express propositions about objective features of the world, and that some of these propositions are true because they accurately report moral facts that are independent of anyone's beliefs or attitudes. It holds, for instance, that the wrongness of torturing the innocent does not depend on anyone thinking it wrong.

Variants

Moral realism is often divided into moral naturalism, which identifies moral facts with natural facts (about wellbeing, flourishing, or the fulfilment of desires) accessible to science, and moral non-naturalism, associated with G. E. Moore and modern defenders such as Derek Parfit and Erik Wielenberg, which treats moral facts as real but irreducible to natural ones. Both variants oppose error theory (which accepts that moral statements aim at facts but denies any are true), expressivism, and subjectivism.

Arguments

Supporters point to the apparent objectivity of moral discourse, the possibility of genuine moral disagreement and progress, and "companions in guilt" arguments noting similarities between moral and other accepted facts, such as mathematical or epistemic norms. Critics raise J. L. Mackie's "argument from queerness" against metaphysically strange objective values, and evolutionary debunking arguments that trace moral beliefs to fitness rather than truth-tracking.
