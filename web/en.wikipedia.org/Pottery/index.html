Pottery is the process and the products of forming vessels and other objects with clay and other ceramic materials, which are fired at high temperatures to give them a hard and durable form. Major types include earthenware, stoneware and porcelain. The place where such wares are made by a potter is also called a pottery (plural "potteries").

Pottery is one of the oldest human inventions, originating before the Neolithic period, with ceramic objects such as the Gravettian figurine Venus of Dolní Věstonice discovered in the Czech Republic dating back to 29,000–25,000 BCE. Pottery vessels discovered in Jiangxi, China, date back to 20,000 BP. Early Neolithic and pre-Neolithic pottery was used by the Jōmon people of Japan from about 16,500 BP.

Production

Pottery production involves preparing clay, shaping it by hand-building (coiling, slab-building, pinching) or throwing on a potter's wheel, drying, decoration, glazing, and firing in a kiln. Firing temperatures range from around 1,000 °C for earthenware to 1,400 °C for porcelain. Glazes add color and make vessels waterproof and easier to clean.

History

Pottery developed independently in several regions. In East Asia, early vessels served cooking and storage. In the Mediterranean, Greek painted pottery and Roman terra sigillata became widely traded goods. Chinese porcelain, developed over centuries and exported along the Silk Road and by sea, was highly prized in Europe. Industrial production from the 18th century, centered in places such as Stoke-on-Trent in England, transformed pottery into a mass-produced commodity, while studio pottery movements in the 20th century revived artisanal production.

Archaeological significance

Because fired clay is nearly indestructible, pottery sherds are among the most common artifacts found at archaeological sites. Changes in style, fabric, and decoration allow archaeologists to date sites and trace cultural exchange, making ceramic analysis a cornerstone of the discipline.
