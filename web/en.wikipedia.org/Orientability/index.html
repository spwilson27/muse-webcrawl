Orientability

In mathematics, orientability is a property of manifolds and surfaces that measures whether it is possible to consistently choose a "handedness" or orientation throughout the space. A space is orientable if such a consistent choice exists, and non-orientable otherwise.

Orientable surfaces

For surfaces embedded in three-dimensional Euclidean space, a surface is orientable if it does not contain a Möbius strip — equivalently, if a figure drawn on the surface cannot be moved around a closed loop and returned as its own mirror image. The sphere, torus, and all surfaces formed by adding handles to a sphere are orientable and have two distinct sides. The Möbius strip and the Klein bottle are non-orientable: they are one-sided.

Orientations of manifolds

For a smooth n-dimensional manifold, an orientation can be defined as a continuous choice of orientation (ordered basis) of each tangent space, or equivalently a nowhere-vanishing n-form (volume form). Algebraically, a connected manifold is orientable if its top homology group Hn(M; Z) is isomorphic to Z. Every manifold has an orientable double cover. A non-orientable manifold admits no consistent volume form, so ordinary integration over it requires densities rather than differential forms.

Applications

Orientability matters in physics: for instance, the possibility of defining spinor fields (spin structures) and consistent notions of left and right in space-time models relates to orientability of the underlying manifold.
