Titus Oates (15 September 1649 – 12 or 13 August 1705) was an English perjurer who fabricated the "Popish Plot", a supposed Catholic conspiracy to assassinate King Charles II of England. Oates's accusations in 1678 triggered a wave of anti-Catholic hysteria in England and Scotland in which at least 22 men were executed on perjured evidence. When the plot was eventually exposed as a fabrication, Oates was convicted of perjury in 1685 and sentenced to life imprisonment, though he was released after the Glorious Revolution of 1688.

Early life

Oates was born at Oakham in Rutland, the son of Samuel Oates, a Baptist preacher and chaplain in the Parliamentarian army. He was educated at Merchant Taylors' School and at Cambridge, but left without a degree amid allegations of misconduct. He took Anglican orders and held minor clerical posts, losing them through scandals including a fabricated charge of sodomy against a schoolmaster — an accusation that nearly saw Oates himself convicted of perjury. He briefly served as a naval chaplain before being dismissed.

The Popish Plot

In 1677 Oates attached himself to the household of the Duke of Norfolk and began moving in Catholic circles; he was received into the Catholic Church and spent time at the Jesuit colleges of Valladolid and St Omer, from which he was expelled. Returning to London, he joined up with the anti-Catholic agitator Israel Tonge. In September 1678 Oates swore before the magistrate Sir Edmund Berry Godfrey that he had uncovered a vast Jesuit conspiracy to murder Charles II, massacre Protestants and install the king's Catholic brother James, Duke of York, on the throne.

Weeks later Godfrey was found dead on Primrose Hill — almost certainly murdered, though by whom remains unknown — and the discovery transformed Oates's story into a national panic. Oates named dozens of supposed plotters; among those tried and executed on his evidence were Edward Coleman, secretary to the Duchess of York, the Jesuit Thomas Whitbread and his companions, and the Archbishop of Armagh, Oliver Plunkett, executed in 1681 and later canonised. Parliament rewarded Oates with a pension and apartments at Whitehall.

Downfall

As executions mounted and Oates's testimony grew ever more extravagant and contradictory, scepticism increased; Charles II himself privately disbelieved the plot. By 1681 prosecutions were collapsing. When James II came to the throne in 1685, Oates was tried for perjury, convicted, and sentenced to be pilloried five times a year, whipped through London, and imprisoned for life. After the Glorious Revolution he was released and granted a small pension, ending his days in obscurity and dying in London in 1705.

Legacy

Oates remains a byword for the perjured informer, and the Popish Plot a classic case of moral panic, in which fabricated testimony, religious prejudice and political opportunism — the plot was exploited by the Earl of Shaftesbury in the campaign to exclude James from the succession — combined to send innocent men to the scaffold.
