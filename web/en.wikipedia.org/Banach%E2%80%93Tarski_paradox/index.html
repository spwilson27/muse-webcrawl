The Banach–Tarski paradox is a theorem in set-theoretic geometry which states that a solid ball in three-dimensional space can be split into a finite number of disjoint subsets, which can then be reassembled, using only rotations and translations, into two solid balls identical to the original. Equivalently, a ball can be cut into pieces and reassembled into a ball of any different size.

Explanation

The proof relies on the axiom of choice to select one point from each of infinitely many orbits of a free group of rotations acting on the sphere. The pieces are non-measurable sets — they have no well-defined volume — so the "paradox" does not contradict the conservation of volume for measurable pieces; it shows instead that volume cannot be consistently assigned to all subsets of space. Popular summaries ("a pea can be cut up and reassembled into the Sun") dramatise this volume-doubling.

History and reception

Published by Stefan Banach and Alfred Tarski in 1924, building on earlier work by Hausdorff and von Neumann, the result sharpened debate over the axiom of choice: the decomposition cannot be carried out constructively, and without the axiom the paradox may fail. It remains the most famous example of the counterintuitive consequences of non-measurable sets.
