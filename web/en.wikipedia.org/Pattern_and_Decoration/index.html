Pattern and Decoration

Pattern and Decoration (also known as P&D or the New Decorativeness) was an art movement in the United States during the mid-1970s. It was organized by a diverse group of artists—including Miriam Schapiro, Joyce Kozloff, Robert Kushner, and Kim MacConnel—who explored pattern, decoration, and handicrafts. Often supported by feminist art, the movement worked to subvert the traditions of the modernist establishment that demanded "serious" art be "masculine" and unadorned.

Origins

The movement drew on non-Western decorative traditions—Islamic tilework, Persian miniatures, Indian textiles, Turkish embroidery—as well as on sources devalued in the Western canon, such as quilting, wallpaper, and fabric design. Key exhibitions included "Ten Approaches to the Decorative" (1976) and shows organised by dealer Holly Solomon, whose gallery became a centre of the movement.

Reception and legacy

Critics initially dismissed Pattern and Decoration as kitsch, but the movement significantly influenced later art, anticipating postmodernism's embrace of ornament and craft. Major retrospectives, including "With Pleasure: Pattern and Decoration in American Art 1972–1985" at the Museum of Contemporary Art, Los Angeles (2019), prompted a reassessment of its importance.
