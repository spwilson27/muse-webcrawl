Income inequality

Income inequality is the extent to which income is distributed unevenly among a population. It is commonly measured with the Gini coefficient, which ranges from 0 (perfect equality) to 1 (maximal inequality), and by income shares accruing to particular groups, such as the share of total income earned by the top 1 percent. Income inequality differs from wealth inequality, which concerns the distribution of assets and is typically far more unequal.

Measurement

The Lorenz curve plots the cumulative share of income against the cumulative share of the population; the Gini coefficient is derived from the area between this curve and the line of perfect equality. Other measures include the Theil index, the Palma ratio (the share of the top 10 percent divided by that of the bottom 40 percent), and percentile ratios such as the 90/10 ratio.

Trends and causes

Within many advanced economies, inequality declined during the mid-20th century — the "Great Compression" — and rose again from around 1980, a pattern documented by Thomas Piketty, Emmanuel Saez, and others. Proposed causes include skill-biased technological change, globalization, declining unionization, changes in tax policy, and the growth of finance and executive pay. Between countries, global inequality has fallen in recent decades as incomes rose rapidly in China and India, even as within-country inequality often increased.

Effects and policy

Economists debate the effects of inequality on growth, health, social mobility, and political stability. Policy responses include progressive taxation, minimum wages, education investment, and transfer programs such as earned-income tax credits and social insurance.
