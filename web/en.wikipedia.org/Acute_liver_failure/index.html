Acute liver failure

Acute liver failure is the rapid onset of severe liver dysfunction in a person without pre-existing chronic liver disease, characterised by hepatic encephalopathy and impaired synthetic function (measured by a prolonged prothrombin time / elevated INR). It is a rare but frequently fatal medical emergency. Historically termed fulminant hepatic failure, it usually develops within days to weeks of the initial insult.

Causes

The commonest cause in many Western countries is paracetamol (acetaminophen) overdose, whether intentional or through repeated supratherapeutic dosing. Other causes include acute viral hepatitis (especially hepatitis A, B and E), other drugs and herbal products, autoimmune hepatitis, Wilson's disease, acute fatty liver of pregnancy, Budd–Chiari syndrome, ischaemic hepatitis ("shock liver") and malignant infiltration. In a proportion of cases no cause is found.

Clinical features

Patients present with jaundice, coagulopathy and encephalopathy ranging from confusion to deep coma. Complications include cerebral oedema and raised intracranial pressure (the leading cause of death in early series), infection, hypoglycaemia, renal failure and multi-organ failure.

Management

Management takes place in an intensive care unit, ideally at a liver transplant centre. Specific treatments include N-acetylcysteine for paracetamol poisoning (and with possible benefit in other causes), antivirals for hepatitis B, and delivery in pregnancy-related disease. Prognostic systems such as the King's College Criteria help identify patients unlikely to survive without emergency liver transplantation, which is the definitive treatment for those meeting poor-prognosis criteria.
