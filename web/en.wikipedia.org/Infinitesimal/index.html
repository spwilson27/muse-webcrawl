Infinitesimal

In mathematics, an infinitesimal number is a quantity that is closer to zero than any standard real number, but is not zero. Infinitesimals do not exist in the standard real number system, because the real numbers satisfy the Archimedean property: for any positive real number there is a natural number whose reciprocal is smaller. The concept was nonetheless used informally for centuries in the development of calculus.

History

The idea of infinitely small quantities appears in the work of Archimedes, whose method of exhaustion foreshadowed integral calculus. In the seventeenth century, Isaac Newton spoke of "fluxions" and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz built his calculus on differentials — quantities treated as smaller than any assignable magnitude yet not nothing. These foundations were criticised, most famously by George Berkeley in The Analyst (1734), who called infinitesimals "the ghosts of departed quantities".

In the nineteenth century, Augustin-Louis Cauchy, Karl Weierstrass and others rebuilt analysis on the rigorous epsilon–delta definition of limit, and infinitesimals were banished from standard mathematics for a century.

Nonstandard analysis

In 1960 Abraham Robinson showed that infinitesimals could be made fully rigorous. His nonstandard analysis extends the real numbers to the hyperreal numbers, a field that contains both infinitesimal and infinite elements and obeys a transfer principle: every first-order statement true of the reals is true of the hyperreals. In this framework a positive infinitesimal ε satisfies 0 < ε < r for every positive real r. Derivatives and integrals can then be defined in a way close to Leibniz's original practice.

Other approaches

Smooth infinitesimal analysis, developed within category theory and intuitionistic logic, uses nilpotent infinitesimals: quantities ε with ε² = 0 but ε ≠ 0, which cannot exist in classical fields but are consistent in the topos models of synthetic differential geometry. The surreal numbers also contain infinitesimal elements.

Properties

Every finite hyperreal number is infinitely close to a unique real number, its standard part. The sum of two infinitesimals is infinitesimal, and the product of an infinitesimal with a finite number is infinitesimal, while the reciprocal of a nonzero infinitesimal is an infinite hyperreal number.
