Hanbury Brown and Twiss effect

FieldQuantum optics, astronomy
Named forRobert Hanbury Brown, Richard Q. Twiss
Demonstrated1956

The Hanbury Brown and Twiss effect (HBT effect) is the correlation in intensity fluctuations observed at two detectors receiving light from the same source. Demonstrated by Robert Hanbury Brown and Richard Q. Twiss in 1956, the effect shows that photons from a thermal light source tend to arrive in pairs ("photon bunching"), a prediction of quantum optics that was initially controversial.

Intensity interferometry

Hanbury Brown and Twiss developed the effect into the technique of intensity interferometry, correlating intensity fluctuations rather than wave amplitudes. Using the Narrabri Stellar Intensity Interferometer in Australia, they measured the angular diameters of stars with high precision, insensitive to atmospheric phase distortion.

Physics

The effect is explained by the bosonic nature of photons and the statistical properties of thermal (chaotic) light: the second-order correlation function g²(0) equals 2 for thermal light, 1 for coherent light, and can fall below 1 (antibunching) for nonclassical light. Versions of the effect with electrons (fermions) show antibunching, reflecting the Pauli exclusion principle. HBT correlations are also used in particle physics to measure the size of the emission region in heavy-ion collisions.
