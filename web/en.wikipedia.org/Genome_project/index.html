Genome project

A genome project is a scientific endeavour to determine the complete DNA sequence of an organism's genome and to annotate it — identifying its genes and other functional elements. The results are deposited in public sequence databases such as GenBank and made available to researchers worldwide. Since the mid-1990s, genome projects have been completed for thousands of organisms, from bacteria and model organisms to humans, crops, and endangered species.

Approach

A genome project typically involves extracting DNA, sequencing it — historically by Sanger sequencing of cloned fragments assembled by overlap (shotgun sequencing), and increasingly by high-throughput short- and long-read technologies — assembling the reads into a continuous sequence, and annotating genes and regulatory regions computationally and by comparison with related species. Because raw assemblies contain gaps and errors, projects report quality levels from rough draft to "finished" reference quality.

The Human Genome Project

The most famous genome project is the Human Genome Project (HGP), an international effort launched in 1990 and declared substantially complete in 2003, which produced a reference sequence covering about 92% of the human genome at a cost of roughly US$3 billion. A privately led competitor, Celera Genomics, published its own draft simultaneously in 2001. The remaining gaps, mostly in repetitive regions, were closed by the Telomere-to-Telomere consortium, which published the first truly complete human genome sequence in 2022. Reference projects continue with efforts such as the human pangenome, which aims to represent human genetic diversity rather than a single sequence.

Later projects

Falling sequencing costs — from dollars per base to complete genomes for a few hundred dollars — have enabled large-scale efforts including the 1000 Genomes Project, the Earth BioGenome Project (which aims to sequence all known eukaryotic species), and genome projects for major crops, livestock, and pathogens, whose sequences underpin medicine, agriculture, and conservation biology.
