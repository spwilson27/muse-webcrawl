Mainline Protestant

The term mainline Protestant refers to a group of historically established Protestant Christian denominations in the United States, distinguished from evangelical, fundamentalist and charismatic movements. Mainline churches are often associated with moderate-to-liberal theology, ecumenism, and engagement with social issues.

Denominations

Groups commonly included are the United Methodist Church, the Evangelical Lutheran Church in America, the Presbyterian Church (USA), the Episcopal Church, the American Baptist Churches USA, the United Church of Christ, and the Christian Church (Disciples of Christ) — sometimes called the "Seven Sisters of American Protestantism."

History

Mainline denominations trace their origins to the Reformation-era churches of Europe and were culturally dominant in much of American religious life through the mid-20th century. Membership in most mainline bodies has declined since the 1960s, even as their institutions — seminaries, colleges, hospitals and social-service agencies — remain influential.

Characteristics

These churches generally accept historical-critical study of the Bible, ordain women (and in many cases LGBTQ clergy), participate in ecumenical councils such as the National Council of Churches, and emphasize social justice alongside personal faith.
