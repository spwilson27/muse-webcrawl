Gut-associated lymphoid tissue

A Peyer's patch, the best-known organised component of GALT.

Gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT) is a component of the mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT) which works in the immune system to protect the body from invasion in the gut. Because the gastrointestinal tract's surface is exposed to food antigens and an enormous load of microbes, GALT is one of the largest immune compartments in the body, containing a large fraction of its antibody-producing plasma cells.

Structure

Organised GALT includes the Peyer's patches of the ileum, isolated lymphoid follicles, the appendix and the tonsils at the gut's entrance. Specialised M cells in the follicle-associated epithelium sample antigens from the lumen and deliver them to underlying dendritic cells and lymphocytes. Diffuse effector tissue in the lamina propria is rich in IgA-secreting plasma cells; secretory IgA is transported into the lumen, where it neutralises pathogens and toxins without provoking inflammation.

Function

GALT must discriminate between dangerous pathogens and harmless food proteins and commensal bacteria — a balance called oral tolerance, whose failure is implicated in coeliac disease, inflammatory bowel disease and food allergy.
