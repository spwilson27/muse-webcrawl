Emphysema

SpecialtyPulmonology
SymptomsShortness of breath, chronic cough, wheezing
CausesCigarette smoking, air pollution, alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency
ClassificationA form of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)

Emphysema is a long-term lung disease in which the alveoli — the tiny air sacs of the lungs where gas exchange occurs — are damaged and their walls destroyed. The loss of elastic recoil and surface area causes air to become trapped in the lungs, producing progressive breathlessness. Emphysema, together with chronic bronchitis, is one of the conditions grouped under chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD).

Causes

The principal cause is long-term exposure to airborne irritants, above all cigarette smoke. Occupational dusts, indoor air pollution from biomass fuels, and outdoor pollution also contribute. A minority of cases arise from alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency, an inherited condition that leaves the lungs unprotected against the enzyme neutrophil elastase, allowing it to digest lung tissue; this can cause emphysema in young non-smokers.

Pathophysiology

Inflammation in the small airways and lung parenchyma destroys alveolar septa. The resulting enlarged air spaces (bullae when large) reduce the surface available for oxygen uptake, and the loss of elastic tissue lets airways collapse on exhalation, trapping air and hyperinflating the chest — producing the classic "barrel chest" appearance. Patterns include centriacinar emphysema (typical of smokers, upper lobes), panacinar emphysema (associated with alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency, lower lobes), and paraseptal emphysema.

Signs and symptoms

The main symptom is exertional dyspnoea that progresses to breathlessness at rest, often with a chronic cough, wheeze, fatigue, and weight loss. Patients may breathe through pursed lips to keep airways open. Diagnosis is confirmed with spirometry showing obstructive airflow limitation, imaging showing hyperinflation and bullae, and measurement of gas transfer (DLCO), which is characteristically reduced.

Treatment

Damage to the alveoli is irreversible, so treatment aims to stop progression and relieve symptoms. Smoking cessation is the single most effective measure. Bronchodilators, inhaled corticosteroids, pulmonary rehabilitation, and supplemental oxygen improve quality of life; in selected severe cases, lung volume reduction surgery, endobronchial valves, or lung transplantation may be considered. Vaccination against influenza and pneumococcus is recommended to prevent exacerbations.
