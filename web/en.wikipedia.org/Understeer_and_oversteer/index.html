Understeer and oversteer

Understeer and oversteer are vehicle dynamics terms used to describe the sensitivity of a vehicle to steering input. Understeer occurs when the front tyres lose grip before the rear, so the car turns less than the driver intends and runs wide of the corner. Oversteer occurs when the rear tyres lose grip first, causing the rear of the car to swing outward and the car to turn more sharply than intended, potentially into a spin.

Definitions

In engineering terms, a vehicle is said to understeer when the slip angle of the front axle exceeds that of the rear axle; it oversteers when the reverse is true, and is neutral when they are equal. The static margin and the distribution of weight, suspension geometry, tyre characteristics, and aerodynamic downforce all influence the balance.

Causes and handling

Most manufacturers tune road cars to understeer mildly at the limit, because running wide is considered more predictable and recoverable for ordinary drivers than a sudden rotation. Oversteer can be provoked by abrupt throttle application in rear-wheel-drive cars, by lifting off the throttle mid-corner in front-wheel-drive cars, or by weight transfer under braking. Racing drivers may exploit controlled oversteer — drifting or "loose" setups — to rotate the car quickly, but excessive oversteer costs time and stability.

Electronic aids

Electronic stability control systems detect incipient understeer or oversteer by comparing steering input with the vehicle's actual rotation (yaw), then brake individual wheels and reduce engine torque to restore the intended path. Such systems have been mandatory on new cars in the United States and the European Union since the early 2010s.
