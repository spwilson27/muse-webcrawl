Thor Heyerdahl (6 October 1914 – 18 April 2002) was a Norwegian adventurer and ethnographer known for the Kon-Tiki expedition of 1947, in which he sailed 8,000 km (5,000 mi) across the Pacific Ocean in a hand-built balsawood raft from South America to the Tuamotu Islands.

Kon-Tiki

Heyerdahl argued that Polynesia could have been settled in part from South America, contrary to the scholarly consensus favouring Asian origins. To demonstrate the feasibility of the voyage, he and five companions built the raft Kon-Tiki from balsa logs using only materials and technology available to pre-Columbian peoples, and drifted with the Humboldt Current to the Raroia atoll in 101 days. His book about the expedition sold tens of millions of copies, and the documentary film won an Academy Award. Modern genetics has largely shown Polynesians to be of Asian origin, though with evidence of some pre-Columbian South American contact.

Other expeditions

He led archaeological work on Easter Island (1955–56), and crossed the Atlantic in the papyrus reed boats Ra (1969, unsuccessful) and Ra II (1970, successful) from Morocco to Barbados, arguing for possible ancient contacts between Egypt and the Americas. His 1977–78 Tigris reed-boat voyage in Mesopotamia ended with the vessel burned in protest at regional wars. Later projects took him to the Maldives, Tenerife and Azerbaijan.
