Jōdo-shū (Japanese: 浄土宗, "The Pure Land School") is the largest school of Pure Land Buddhism in Japan. Founded by the monk Hōnen in 1175, it teaches that in the present degenerate age (mappō) liberation through one's own efforts is effectively impossible, and that birth in the Pure Land of the Buddha Amitābha (Amida) is attained through entrusting faith expressed in the recitation of the nembutsu: Namu Amida Butsu ("I take refuge in Amida Buddha").

History

Hōnen, a Tendai monk, turned to the Pure Land teachings of the Chinese master Shandao and in 1175 began teaching exclusive reliance on the nembutsu. His treatise Senchakushū (1198) argued that the nembutsu was the practice selected by Amida's Primal Vow for the salvation of all beings. The movement spread rapidly among commoners and was persecuted by the established schools; Hōnen was exiled in 1207. After his death his disciples, including Benchō and Shōkō, organized the tradition, and the Chinzei branch descending from Benchō became the mainstream of the school.

Doctrine

The school is based on the three Pure Land sutras and on the doctrine of the Primal Vow, in which Amida pledged to save all who sincerely call upon his name. Jōdo-shū emphasizes "other-power" (tariki) over "self-power" (jiriki): salvation is Amida's gift, not the practitioner's achievement. Reciting the nembutsu is nonetheless encouraged repeatedly and throughout life, as the natural expression of gratitude and faith; at death, devotees expect Amida to descend with a host of bodhisattvas to welcome them (raigō) to the Pure Land.

Organization

The head temple is Chion-in in Kyoto, where Hōnen died; the school's other major temple is Zōjō-ji in Tokyo, the Tokugawa family temple. Jōdo-shū counts some 7,000 temples and millions of adherents in Japan, making it one of the country's largest Buddhist denominations. Its offshoot, Jōdo Shinshū, founded by Hōnen's disciple Shinran, is of comparable size.
