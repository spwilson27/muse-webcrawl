Ordnance Survey (OS) is the national mapping agency for Great Britain. Founded in 1791, it produces and maintains the definitive geographic data and maps of England, Scotland and Wales, from large-scale digital databases used by government and business to the familiar paper leisure maps used by walkers. Its name reflects its military origins: it began under the Board of Ordnance, the department responsible for supplying the army.

History

After the Jacobite rising of 1745 exposed the poor state of maps of the Scottish Highlands, a military survey of Scotland was begun in 1747. In 1791 the Board of Ordnance purchased a new theodolite and began the triangulation of southern England, driven by fears of invasion during the wars with France. The first one-inch-to-the-mile map, of Kent, was published in 1801.

Products

The OS MasterMap database records hundreds of millions of individual features. Paper and digital leisure products include the pink-covered Landranger series at 1:50,000 and the orange Explorer series at 1:25,000, the latter showing rights of way. The National Grid, introduced in 1936, provides the reference system for British maps.

Retriangulation and modern era

The retriangulation of Great Britain (1936–1962) built the network of concrete trig pillars still seen on hilltops. Since the 1970s surveying has been progressively digitised, and today OS data underpins emergency services, utilities, insurance and satellite navigation.
