Edvard Munch

Edvard Munch (12 December 1863 – 23 January 1944) was a Norwegian painter whose intensely evocative treatment of psychological themes built upon late-19th-century Symbolism and greatly influenced German Expressionism. His most famous work, The Scream (1893), has become an enduring icon of modern art and of anxiety itself.

The Scream (1893), Munch's most famous work

Life

Munch grew up in Christiania (now Oslo) amid illness and bereavement: his mother died of tuberculosis when he was five, and his favourite sister Sophie died at fifteen — losses that haunted his art. He studied engineering briefly before joining the city's bohemian circle and devoting himself to painting. A scandal-provoking exhibition in Berlin in 1892 made him famous across Germany; he lived and worked there and in France before a breakdown in 1908 led him to return to Norway. He spent his later years in relative seclusion at Ekely, near Oslo.

Work

Munch's recurring subjects include love, jealousy, melancholy, sickness, and death, explored in series such as The Frieze of Life. Key works include The Sick Child, Madonna, The Sun, Melancholy, Anxiety, and Death in the Sickroom. He painted The Scream in several versions, writing that he "sensed a great, unending scream piercing through nature." His radical simplification of form, feverish colour, and swirling line made him a forerunner of Expressionism.

Legacy

On his death Munch bequeathed some 1,100 paintings, 18,000 prints, and thousands of drawings and writings to the city of Oslo. They form the core of the MUNCH museum, which opened in a new waterfront building in 2021. In 2012 a pastel version of The Scream sold at auction for nearly US$120 million, then a record for a work of art.
