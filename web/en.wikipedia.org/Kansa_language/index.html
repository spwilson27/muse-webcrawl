Kansa language

The Kansa language (Kansa: Kką:ze íe) is a Siouan language of the Dhegiha branch, historically spoken by the Kaw people (also called the Kansa or Kanza) of northeastern Kansas and now Oklahoma. It is closely related to Osage, Quapaw, Omaha-Ponca and Kansa's fellow Dhegiha languages, and the Kansas state name derives from the people and their language.

History

Kansa was documented in the 19th century by missionaries and by the ethnologist James Owen Dorsey, who recorded extensive texts. As the Kaw were removed to Indian Territory (Oklahoma) in 1873 and children were sent to boarding schools, English replaced Kansa; the last fully fluent first-language speakers died in the early 1980s, among them Walter Kekahbah, Ralph Pepper and Maud McCauley Rowe.

Revitalisation

The Kaw Nation, headquartered in Kaw City, Oklahoma, runs a language programme drawing on Dorsey's manuscripts and later recordings. Kansa has been taught in community classes and through online materials, and a dictionary and grammar (notably Robert Rankin's work) support revival. The language has no remaining native-speaking community, but second-language learners and ceremonial use continue.

Features

Like other Siouan languages, Kansa is verb-final with postpositions, uses positional classifiers for shape and posture, and contrasts aspirated, glottalised and plain stops. Its phonology lacks the fricative richness of Osage, from which it diverged several centuries ago.
