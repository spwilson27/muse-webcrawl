P-38 can opener

The P-38, a small folding can opener issued with United States military field rations.

The P-38 is a small folding can opener that was developed in 1942 and issued in the canned field rations of the United States Armed Forces from World War II through the 1980s. Originally designed for and distributed in the K-ration, it was later included in the C-ration. The larger P-51 was a companion design for opening larger institutional cans.

Design and use

The P-38 consists of a short metal blade that serves as a handle, with a small, hinged metal tooth that folds out to pierce the can lid. A notch just under the hinge point holds the opener around the rim of the can as the device is "walked" around the rim to cut the lid. The device is only 1.5 inches (38 mm) long — the dimension from which its name is popularly, though not certainly, derived. Other explanations hold that the name refers to the 38 punctures supposedly needed to open a C-ration can, or to the P-38 Lightning fighter aircraft.

History

The opener was developed in a few weeks in the summer of 1942 by the Subsistence Research Laboratory in Chicago, under the direction of Major (later Colonel) Ralph D. Sutherland. It was first issued with K-rations in 1942 and later packed in every case of C-rations until the C-ration was replaced by the Meal, Ready-to-Eat (MRE) in the 1980s. The MRE used retort pouches and no longer required a can opener, ending the P-38's long service.

Nicknames

American soldiers gave the P-38 a number of nicknames, including "John Wayne" — reputedly because the actor demonstrated its use in a training film — as well as "the Army's best invention" and, among some units, a small "key to the rations". Australian soldiers knew a similar opener on their ration packs as the FRED ("Field Ration Eating Device").

Successors

A similar but larger device, the P-51, was produced for opening larger cans such as those used in kitchens and the B-ration. The P-38 design remains in production for civilian campers and survival kits, and original military examples are common collectibles.
