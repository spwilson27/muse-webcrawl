HIV/AIDS

HIV/AIDS is a spectrum of conditions caused by infection with the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), a retrovirus that attacks the immune system, particularly CD4+ T cells. Following initial infection, a person may experience a brief influenza-like illness; the infection then typically proceeds through a prolonged period with few symptoms. Without treatment, HIV progressively weakens immune defenses, eventually leading to acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS), in which opportunistic infections and cancers that a healthy immune system would suppress become life-threatening.

Transmission

HIV is transmitted through specific body fluids — blood, semen, vaginal and rectal fluids, and breast milk — most commonly through unprotected sexual contact, sharing of contaminated needles, transfusion of contaminated blood products, and from mother to child during pregnancy, childbirth, or breastfeeding. It is not spread by casual contact, saliva, tears, or insect bites.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is by blood or saliva tests detecting antibodies, antigens, or viral RNA. There is no cure or vaccine, but antiretroviral therapy (ART) — combinations of drugs that suppress viral replication — can reduce the viral load to undetectable levels, restoring near-normal life expectancy and eliminating the risk of sexual transmission (undetectable = untransmittable, or U=U). Pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) and post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) can prevent infection in people at risk or after exposure.

Epidemiology

Since the epidemic was first recognized in 1981, roughly 40 million people have died of AIDS-related illnesses, and about 39 million people were living with HIV as of the early 2020s. Sub-Saharan Africa remains the most affected region. The red ribbon is the international symbol of awareness and support for people living with HIV.
