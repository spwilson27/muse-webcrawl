The Trans-Canada Highway (French: Route Transcanadienne) is a transcontinental federal–provincial highway system that travels through all ten provinces of Canada, from the Pacific Ocean on the west coast to the Atlantic Ocean on the east coast. The main route spans 7,476 km (4,645 mi), making it one of the longest national highways in the world.

The highway system is recognizable by its distinctive white-on-green maple leaf route markers. Although the system is mostly a two-lane highway through rural areas, it is upgraded to freeway standard through major urban centres.

History

The Trans-Canada Highway Act was passed in 1949, providing for a cost-sharing agreement between the federal government and the provinces. Construction proceeded through the 1950s; the most difficult section was through Rogers Pass in British Columbia, where the highway opened in 1962. Prime Minister John Diefenbaker officially opened the highway on 3 September 1962 at Rogers Pass. The final sections, including links in Newfoundland, were completed by 1971.

Route

The main route begins (Kilometre Zero) in Victoria, British Columbia, crosses Vancouver Island, then passes through the Fraser Canyon and the Rocky Mountains via Rogers Pass, through Calgary, Regina, Winnipeg, and the long northern Ontario section along Lake Superior. It continues through Ottawa and Montreal, across New Brunswick and Nova Scotia, and—via ferry—through Newfoundland to St. John's. A northern route through Ontario and Quebec (Highway 11/117 corridor) and other branches form part of the wider system.

Significance

The highway is a powerful symbol of Canadian unity, binding the country coast to coast. Points of interest along the route include the "Mile Zero" markers at both termini, the Terry Fox Memorial near Thunder Bay—where Fox's Marathon of Hope ended—and some of Canada's most celebrated mountain scenery.
