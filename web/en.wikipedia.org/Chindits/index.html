Chindits


Chindits in Burma, 1944

The Chindits, officially known as the Long Range Penetration Groups, were special operations units of the British and Indian armies that saw action in Burma during the Second World War. The Chindits were formed under Brigadier Orde Wingate to conduct long-range penetration operations deep behind Japanese lines, disrupting communications and supply routes in support of conventional offensives. Their name derived from the Burmese word chinthe, the mythical lion-like creatures guarding Burmese temples, which also provided their formation sign.

Operations

The first Chindit operation, Operation Longcloth (February–April 1943), sent some 3,000 men of the 77th Indian Infantry Brigade marching into Burma to cut railway lines. Although its material results were limited and roughly a third of the force was lost, the expedition was widely publicised and raised Allied morale at a low point of the Burma campaign. Wingate was promoted and given an expanded force, the Indian 3rd Infantry Division or "Special Force".

The second and much larger operation, Operation Thursday, began in March 1944, when Chindit brigades were flown by glider and transport aircraft into clearings deep in Burma, where they established fortified strongholds such as "Broadway" and "White City". They supported General Joseph Stilwell's advance on Myitkyina and inflicted significant disruption on Japanese forces. Wingate was killed in an air crash on 24 March 1944 and was succeeded by Brigadier Walter Lentaigne. Exhausted by disease, malnutrition and heavy fighting, the Chindits were withdrawn in August 1944 and the force was disbanded early in 1945.

Legacy

Historians debate the military value of the Chindit operations relative to their cost, but their experience influenced post-war special forces thinking, air supply techniques and long-range jungle operations.
