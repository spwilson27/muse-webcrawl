Alport syndrome

Alport syndrome is a genetic disorder affecting around 1 in 5,000–10,000 children, characterized by glomerulonephritis, end-stage kidney disease, and hearing loss. It can also affect the eyes, causing lenticonus and retinopathy. The syndrome was named after the British physician A. Cecil Alport, who identified it in a family in 1927.

Cause

Alport syndrome is caused by mutations in the COL4A3, COL4A4, or COL4A5 genes, which encode chains of type IV collagen, a major component of basement membranes in the kidney, inner ear, and eye. About 80% of cases are X-linked (COL4A5), while most of the remainder are autosomal recessive or dominant (COL4A3/COL4A4). Defective collagen networks weaken the glomerular basement membrane, which splits and scars over time.

Signs and diagnosis

The earliest sign is usually persistent microscopic haematuria (blood in the urine), followed by proteinuria and progressive loss of kidney function. Sensorineural hearing loss commonly develops in late childhood or adolescence. Diagnosis rests on family history, kidney biopsy findings under electron microscopy, skin biopsy, and genetic testing.

Treatment

There is no cure, but angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors and angiotensin receptor blockers slow progression by reducing proteinuria. Kidney transplantation is usually successful when end-stage disease develops, though a small minority of recipients develop anti-GBM disease in the graft.
