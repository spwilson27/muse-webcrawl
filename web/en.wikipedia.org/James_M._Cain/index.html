James Mallahan Cain (July 1, 1892 – October 27, 1977) was an American writer and journalist, one of the founders of the hardboiled school of crime fiction. His novels The Postman Always Rings Twice (1934), Double Indemnity (1936) and Mildred Pierce (1941) became classic films noir, and his spare, unsentimental style influenced generations of writers and filmmakers.

Early life and journalism

Cain was born in Annapolis, Maryland, the son of a college president and a former opera singer. After graduating from Washington College, where his father was president, he served in France in the First World War as editor of an army newspaper. He then worked as a reporter and editorial writer for the Baltimore American, the Baltimore Sun and the New York World, and wrote columns and dialogues for The American Mercury under H. L. Mencken. In 1931 he moved to Hollywood as a screenwriter, though his screenplays were rarely successful.

Novels

Cain's first novel, The Postman Always Rings Twice (1934), the story of a drifter and a roadside café owner's wife who murder her husband, was a sensation and a bestseller, banned in some places for its violence and eroticism. Albert Camus cited it as a model for The Stranger. Double Indemnity (serialized 1936), in which an insurance salesman and a femme fatale plot a murder disguised as a train accident, and Mildred Pierce (1941), a portrait of a mother's ambition and betrayal, consolidated his reputation. Billy Wilder's film of Double Indemnity (1944), with a screenplay by Raymond Chandler, is a landmark of film noir.

Style and legacy

Cain disliked the label "hardboiled" but wrote in a flat, affectless first-person style in which lovers plot murders for money and desire, then destroy themselves. He often said he wrote "love stories". Late novels such as The Butterfly (1947) never matched his early success, and some works appeared only posthumously, including The Cocktail Waitress (2012). He died in Maryland in 1977.
