CMOS

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia (recreated from memory)

Complementary metal–oxide–semiconductor (CMOS) is a technology for constructing integrated circuits, used in microprocessors, microcontrollers, memory chips, digital logic circuits, and image sensors. CMOS technology uses complementary pairs of p-type and n-type MOSFETs arranged so that the circuit draws significant power only when switching states, giving it very low static power consumption.

History

CMOS was patented in 1963 by Frank Wanlass of Fairchild Semiconductor (U.S. patent granted 1967), and early work was also done by Chih-Tang Sah. Although slower than TTL logic at first, CMOS's low power made it dominant as device densities grew; by the 1980s–1990s it had become the near-universal fabrication technology for VLSI chips. CMOS image sensors ("active pixel sensors") later displaced CCDs in cameras and phones.

Working principle

The basic building block is the CMOS inverter: a PMOS transistor on top connected to the supply and an NMOS transistor below connected to ground, with gates tied to the input. For a logic-high input the NMOS conducts and pulls the output low; for a logic-low input the PMOS conducts and pulls it high. In either steady state one transistor is off, so almost no current flows except briefly during transitions. Combining transistors in series and parallel implements NAND, NOR, and more complex gates.

Characteristics and scaling

CMOS offers high noise immunity and dense layout but is vulnerable to latch-up, a parasitic short-circuit condition. Decades of lithographic scaling (following Moore's law and Dennard scaling) shrank CMOS devices to nanometer dimensions, though power density limits ended classical Dennard scaling around 2005, driving multicore designs.
