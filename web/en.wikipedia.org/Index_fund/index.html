Index fund

An index fund (also index tracker) is a mutual fund or exchange-traded fund (ETF) designed to follow a prescribed rule set so that its portfolio mirrors the components of a financial market index, such as the S&P 500 or the FTSE 100. Index funds pursue passive management: rather than attempting to select securities that will outperform, they aim to replicate the index's return, less small tracking error and fees.

History

The first index fund for institutional investors was created in 1971 by Wells Fargo's Management Sciences division. John C. Bogle founded The Vanguard Group and launched the first index fund available to individual investors, the First Index Investment Trust (now Vanguard 500 Index Fund), in 1976; it was initially derided as "Bogle's folly." Assets in index products grew enormously from the 1990s onward, aided by the rise of ETFs beginning with the SPDR S&P 500 fund in 1993, and passive funds surpassed actively managed funds in U.S. equity assets in 2019.

Advantages and criticism

Index funds charge much lower fees than actively managed funds because they require little research and trade infrequently; they also offer broad diversification and, in ETF form, tax efficiency. Studies by S&P Dow Jones Indices (SPIVA) consistently find most active managers underperform their benchmarks over long horizons. Critics raise concerns about corporate governance concentration, the effect of index flows on prices, and the absence of price discovery.
