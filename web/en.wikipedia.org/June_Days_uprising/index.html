June Days uprising

Date: June 23–26, 1848
Location: Paris, France
Combatants: Parisian workers and insurgents vs. the French Second Republic (General Cavaignac)
Result: Uprising crushed; thousands killed; mass arrests and deportations

The June Days uprising (23–26 June 1848) was a bloody insurrection by Parisian workers against the French Second Republic, provoked by the closure of the National Workshops. Its suppression by General Louis-Eugène Cavaignac, in which several thousand people were killed, ended the alliance between workers and the republican bourgeoisie that had made the February Revolution and opened the way to the rise of Louis-Napoléon Bonaparte.

Background

The February Revolution of 1848 had overthrown King Louis-Philippe and proclaimed a republic with universal male suffrage. To relieve mass unemployment, the provisional government created the National Workshops, which by June employed over 100,000 men on public works. The conservative majority elected to the Constituent Assembly in April regarded the workshops as a costly hotbed of socialism. On 21 June the government decreed their dissolution: younger men were to enlist in the army and the rest sent to provincial labour projects. For the workers, this meant the loss of their livelihood and of the revolution's promise of the "right to work."

The fighting

On 23 June barricades rose across the working-class districts of eastern Paris under the slogan "Bread or lead!" The Assembly granted Cavaignac, the minister of war, full powers; regular troops, the Mobile Guard — many of them themselves young workers — and National Guards from the provinces methodically stormed the barricades over four days. The Archbishop of Paris, Denis Auguste Affre, was killed while attempting to mediate. When the last strongholds in the Faubourg Saint-Antoine fell on 26 June, roughly 1,500 people had been killed in the fighting, with many more insurgents shot after capture; estimates of total deaths range upward of 3,000–5,000. Some 11,000–15,000 prisoners were arrested, and about 4,500 were deported to Algeria.

Consequences

The June Days shattered the "illusion of February," in Karl Marx's analysis — he treated the rising in The Class Struggles in France as the first great battle of the modern class war. Politically, the repression swung the republic rightward: the press was curbed, political clubs restricted, and Cavaignac's prestige could not prevent the election of Louis-Napoléon Bonaparte as president in December 1848, leading within three years to his coup and the Second Empire.
