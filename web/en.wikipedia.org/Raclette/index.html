Raclette

Melting the cut surface of a half-wheel of raclette and scraping the soft cheese onto potatoes.

Raclette (/rəˈklɛt/, French: [ʁaklɛt]) is a Swiss dish, also popular in the French Alps, based on heating cheese and scraping off the melted part, then served with boiled potatoes, gherkins, pickled onions and often cured meats. The term is also the name of the cheese itself, a semi-hard cow's milk cheese produced in the canton of Valais and in French-speaking alpine regions.

The word derives from the French verb racler, "to scrape", a reference to the way the melted cheese is scraped from the wheel onto the plate.

History

Melting cheese before an open fire is described in medieval writings from Swiss monasteries; the dish was traditionally eaten by peasants and herders in the Valais, who carried cheese with them and softened it by the fire in the evening. Raclette in the modern form — a half wheel mounted before a heat source — spread through the Valais in the early 20th century and was popularised nationally after its promotion at the Swiss national exhibition of 1964. In France, the cheese is made in Savoie, Franche-Comté, Auvergne and Brittany, and French raclette is protected by an IGP designation.

Preparation

Traditionally, half a wheel of raclette cheese is held in front of a fire or an electric heating element until the exposed face melts; the molten layer is then scraped with a knife onto a warm plate of small boiled potatoes in their skins. Since the 1950s, table-top raclette grills have become common for home use: diners melt individual slices in small pans (coupelles) under a grill, often cooking vegetables or meat on the hot surface above. Typical accompaniments are cornichons, pickled onions, black pepper, paprika and dry white wine or hot tea.

The cheese

Raclette cheese is made from raw or pasteurised cow's milk, formed into wheels of about 6 kg and aged for three to six months. It has a firm, smooth texture, a brownish-orange rind, and melts evenly without separating — a quality that, together with its full, slightly acidic flavour, made it the standard cheese for the dish. Valais raclette carries a Swiss AOP designation.
