Golgi tendon organ

The Golgi tendon organ (GTO) is a proprioceptive sensory receptor organ that senses changes in muscle tension. It lies at the origin and insertion of skeletal muscle fibers into tendons and is named after the Italian physician Camillo Golgi, who described it in 1880.

Structure

Each Golgi tendon organ consists of a capsule of connective tissue enclosing braided collagen fibers connected in series with a small bundle of muscle fibers (typically 10–20). It is innervated by a single type Ib sensory nerve fiber, whose branches wind among the collagen bundles. When the muscle contracts, the collagen fibers are pulled taut and compress the nerve endings, increasing their firing rate in proportion to the force generated.

Function

Because it sits in series with the muscle, the Golgi tendon organ — unlike the muscle spindle, which senses length — measures the tension the muscle produces. Its signals travel to the spinal cord, where Ib interneurons inhibit the motor neurons of the same muscle (autogenic inhibition, historically called the inverse myotatic reflex) and excite antagonists. This feedback contributes to smooth control of force, fine motor coordination, and transitions between posture and movement, and helps protect muscle and tendon from damaging overload.

Clinical significance

Altered tendon-organ feedback is implicated in the rigidity and clasp-knife response seen after upper motor neuron lesions. The classic "clasp-knife phenomenon" — initial resistance to passive stretch that suddenly gives way — was long attributed to Golgi tendon organ reflexes, though modern accounts emphasise more complex spinal circuitry.
