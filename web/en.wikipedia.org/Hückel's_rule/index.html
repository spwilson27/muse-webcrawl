Hückel's rule

In organic chemistry, Hückel's rule predicts that a planar ring molecule will have aromatic properties if it has 4n + 2 π electrons, where n = 0, 1, 2, .... The rule was proposed by German physicist Erich Hückel in 1931 on the basis of quantum mechanical (Hückel molecular orbital) calculations.

Statement

A cyclic, planar, fully conjugated molecule or ion is aromatic — and hence unusually stable — when its π-electron count equals 4n + 2 for some non-negative integer n. Classic examples include:

  • Benzene (6 π electrons, n = 1), the archetypal aromatic compound.
  • The cyclopentadienyl anion (6 π electrons) and tropylium cation (6 π electrons).
  • Naphthalene and other polycyclic aromatics (10 π electrons, n = 2, counting the periphery).

Conversely, planar conjugated rings with 4n π electrons are antiaromatic and destabilised; cyclobutadiene (4 π electrons) is the standard example.

Criteria and refinements

For the rule to apply, the ring must be (nearly) planar so that p orbitals can overlap continuously, and every atom in the ring must contribute a p orbital to the conjugated system. Large rings may escape antiaromaticity by puckering out of planarity — cyclooctatetraene, with 8 π electrons, adopts a tub shape and behaves as an ordinary alkene. Möbius aromaticity, in which the p-orbital system has a half-twist, reverses the rule (4n electrons become aromatic).
