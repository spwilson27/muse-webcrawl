Paris Declaration Respecting Maritime Law

The Paris Declaration Respecting Maritime Law of 16 April 1856 was an international agreement concluded at the Congress of Paris at the end of the Crimean War. It was the first multilateral treaty to codify rules of naval warfare and maritime neutrality.

Provisions

The Declaration set out four principles: (1) privateering is abolished; (2) the neutral flag covers enemy goods, except contraband of war; (3) neutral goods, except contraband, are not liable to capture under an enemy flag; and (4) blockades, to be binding, must be effective — maintained by a force sufficient to actually prevent access to the enemy coast.

Signatories and legacy

Most major maritime powers acceded to the Declaration. The United States declined to sign because its proposed amendment exempting all private property at sea from capture was rejected, although it generally observed the rules in subsequent conflicts. The Declaration's principles were later supplemented by the Hague Conventions of 1907 and remain a foundation of the modern law of naval warfare.
