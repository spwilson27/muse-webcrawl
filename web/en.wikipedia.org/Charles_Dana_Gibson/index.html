Charles Dana Gibson (1867–1944) was an American illustrator best known for creating the Gibson Girl, the pen-and-ink ideal of female beauty and independence that defined turn-of-the-century American fashion and manners. His work appeared above all in Life magazine, which he later owned and edited.

Career

Gibson sold his first drawing to Life in 1886 and soon became its star contributor, producing weekly illustrations of high society — debutantes, golf and tennis parties, courtship, and married life. His tall, elegant Gibson Girl, modelled in part by his wife Irene Langhorne and her sisters, influenced hairstyles, dress, and ideals for two decades. Gibson also illustrated books and, during the First World War, headed the Division of Pictorial Publicity, producing famous recruiting posters such as the image of Columbia summoning artists to enlist.

Later life

As photography and new illustration styles displaced pen-and-ink society drawing, Gibson's popularity waned. He took control of Life in 1918 and remained its editor and owner until retiring in 1932. He spent his later years painting in oils at his home in Maine.
