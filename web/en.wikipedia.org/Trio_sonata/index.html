Trio sonata


A Baroque chamber ensemble

The trio sonata is a genre of chamber music that flourished in the Baroque era. Despite its name, it is written for two melodic instruments plus basso continuo—a combination of three written parts (hence "trio") but typically played by at least four musicians, since the continuo part is realized by a bass instrument such as cello or bassoon together with a chordal instrument such as harpsichord or organ.

Forms

Two principal types existed: the sonata da chiesa (church sonata), usually in four movements in slow–fast–slow–fast order, and the sonata da camera (chamber sonata), essentially a suite of dances. The melodic parts, most often two violins, engage in imitative counterpoint of equal importance.

Composers

Arcangelo Corelli's sets of trio sonatas (opp. 1–4, published 1681–1694) established the model imitated throughout Europe. Other notable composers include Henry Purcell, Georg Philipp Telemann, George Frideric Handel, and Johann Sebastian Bach, whose six trio sonatas for organ (BWV 525–530) transfer the texture to a single instrument. The form declined in the mid-18th century as the continuo fell out of use and the string quartet rose.
