Gladius is a Latin word meaning "sword," and in modern usage it refers specifically to the short sword carried by Roman legionaries, which became so emblematic of the Roman army that gladius was used as a general word for a sword in Latin. Adopted from the weapons of Celtiberian mercenaries during the Punic Wars — hence its early name, gladius hispaniensis ("Spanish sword") — the gladius was used from roughly the 3rd century BC until it was gradually replaced by the longer spatha in the 2nd and 3rd centuries AD.

Design

The gladius was a double-edged sword with a short, stiff blade and a long tapering point, typically 45–68 cm long and about 5 cm wide, with a total weight of roughly 0.7–1 kg. It had a grip of wood or ivory, often with finger grooves, a rounded pommel and a guard, and was carried in a scabbard on the right hip (on the left by officers). Archaeologists distinguish successive patterns: the long, waisted hispaniensis; the Mainz type with its distinctive long point; the similar Fulham type; and the shorter, parallel-edged Pompeii type, which became standard in the later 1st century AD.

Use in battle

The gladius was designed primarily for thrusting in close combat. Roman tactics paired it with the large curved shield (scutum): legionaries advanced behind their shields and struck short, punching thrusts at the enemy's abdomen and thighs, conserving energy and staying protected, though the blade could also cut effectively. Vegetius emphasized training recruits to thrust rather than slash. Combined with the pilum javelin, discipline and formation drill, the gladius-and-scutum system underpinned Roman battlefield success for centuries.

Legacy

The word survives in the names of the gladiator (a swordsman of the arena, many of whom fought with the gladius), the gladiolus flower (named for its sword-shaped leaves), and in the anatomical "gladius" — the internal shell or "pen" of squid. Surviving examples are held in museums across Europe, and the sword remains an enduring symbol of ancient Rome.
