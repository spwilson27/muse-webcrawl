Sleep

Sleep is a universal behavior in animals

Sleep is a state of reduced mental and physical activity in which consciousness is altered and sensory activity is inhibited to a certain extent. During sleep, there is a decrease in muscle activity and in interactions with the environment. Sleep differs from wakefulness in the ability to react to stimuli, but it is more reactive than a coma or disorders of consciousness: sleep displays distinct, active brain patterns.

Sleep occurs in repeating periods, in which the body alternates between two distinct modes: rapid eye movement (REM) sleep and non-REM sleep. During REM sleep, brain activity resembles wakefulness, the eyes move rapidly, muscles are paralyzed, and most dreaming occurs. Non-REM sleep comprises three stages of progressively deeper sleep (N1, N2, N3); deep slow-wave sleep (N3) predominates early in the night, while REM periods lengthen toward morning.

Regulation

Sleep timing and structure are controlled by the circadian clock — centered on the suprachiasmatic nucleus and entrained by light — and by homeostatic sleep pressure that builds during wakefulness, partly through the accumulation of adenosine, and dissipates during sleep. The hormone melatonin, released in darkness, helps regulate the sleep–wake cycle.

Functions

Sleep is believed to serve many functions: memory consolidation, emotional regulation, metabolic and immune maintenance, brain development, and clearance of metabolic waste via the glymphatic system. Sleep deprivation impairs cognition, mood, reaction time, and long-term health, and is associated with obesity, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and neurodegeneration.

Disorders and culture

Common sleep disorders include insomnia, obstructive sleep apnea, narcolepsy, restless legs syndrome, and parasomnias such as sleepwalking. Sleep needs vary by age, with newborns sleeping 14–17 hours and most adults needing 7–9. Cultural practices such as the siesta, biphasic sleep, and co-sleeping vary widely around the world.
