Collateralized debt obligation

A collateralized debt obligation (CDO) is a type of structured asset-backed security. Essentially, a CDO is a security backed by a pool of assets — typically bonds, loans, or mortgage-backed securities — whose cash flows are divided into slices, or tranches, with different levels of risk and return. CDOs were first created in the late 1980s and became notorious for their role in the subprime mortgage crisis and the 2007–2008 financial crisis.

Structure

A special purpose vehicle acquires a portfolio of debt assets and issues tranched notes to investors. Payments flow in a "waterfall": senior tranches are paid first and carry the highest credit ratings and lowest yields; mezzanine tranches absorb losses next; the equity tranche receives the residual cash flow, earns the most in good times, and takes the first losses. Collateralized loan obligations (CLOs), backed by leveraged corporate loans, are a closely related and now larger market.

Role in the financial crisis

In the 2000s, banks issued vast numbers of CDOs backed by residential mortgage-backed securities, including subprime mortgages; "CDO-squared" instruments repackaged tranches of other CDOs, and synthetic CDOs used credit default swaps to reference mortgage risk without owning the bonds. Rating agencies assigned AAA ratings to senior tranches based on models that underestimated correlated housing defaults. When US house prices fell, CDO values collapsed, inflicting huge losses on banks and investors worldwide and freezing credit markets.

Aftermath

Issuance of mortgage CDOs collapsed after 2008 and regulators imposed risk-retention and disclosure rules. CLOs, backed by diversified corporate loans with stronger structures, survived and grew into a trillion-dollar market, though regulators continue to monitor their risks.
