Non-interventionism

Non-interventionism or non-intervention is a political philosophy or national foreign policy doctrine that opposes interference in the domestic politics and affairs of other countries but, in contrast to isolationism, is not necessarily opposed to international commitments in general. It holds that states should refrain from military intervention in the internal affairs of sovereign nations, whether by invasion, covert action, or support of armed factions.

Principles and history

The norm of non-intervention is embedded in the Charter of the United Nations and in the Westphalian idea of sovereignty. Prominent historical advocates include George Washington, whose Farewell Address warned against permanent alliances, and the United States' early-20th-century non-interventionist movement. During the Spanish Civil War, the Non-Intervention Committee of Britain and France attempted — largely ineffectively — to keep foreign powers out of the conflict.

Debates

Critics argue that strict non-intervention can enable atrocities, prompting doctrines of humanitarian intervention and the responsibility to protect. Supporters reply that intervention usually worsens conflicts and violates self-determination. The term is also used in economics for opposition to government interference in markets.
