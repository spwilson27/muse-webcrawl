Turret clock

A turret clock face on a church tower

A turret clock or tower clock is a large clock mounted in a tower, typically driving visible dials on the outside of the building and often striking bells. Turret clocks are found on churches, town halls, railway stations and other public buildings, and were historically among the principal public timekeepers of a community.

History

Mechanical turret clocks appeared in Europe in the late 13th and early 14th centuries; early examples such as those at Milan (1335) and Norwich struck the hours on bells before dials became common. Early movements used verge-and-foliot escapements and were notoriously inaccurate, driven by falling weights. The introduction of the pendulum in the 17th century (and the anchor escapement) greatly improved accuracy. Iron frame movements — flat-bed, birdcage and chair-frame types — became standard.

Design

A traditional turret clock movement has separate trains for going (timekeeping) and striking, each powered by heavy weights wound by hand or, in later examples, automatically. A pendulum regulates the going train, and motion work drives hands on one or more dials through leading-off rods. Many turret clocks incorporate gravity escapements, notably the double three-legged gravity escapement designed by Edmund Beckett (later Lord Grimthorpe) for the Great Clock at Westminster — "Big Ben" — whose accuracy set a standard for turret clocks worldwide.

Modern clocks

Many historic movements have been fitted with automatic winding and electric motors, while new tower clocks are usually electric. Famous turret clocks include those at Westminster, Prague (the astronomical clock), and the Zytglogge in Bern.
