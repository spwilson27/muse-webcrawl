Homology

Homology (from Greek ὁμός homos, "same") is a general way of associating a sequence of algebraic objects, such as abelian groups or modules, with other mathematical objects such as topological spaces. Homology groups were originally introduced in algebraic topology by Henri Poincaré in the late 19th century, building on ideas of Bernhard Riemann. Intuitively, homology detects "holes" of different dimensions in a space: the zeroth homology counts connected components, the first counts one-dimensional loops or tunnels, the second counts enclosed voids, and so on.

Construction

In a typical construction for a topological space, one forms a chain complex of groups connected by boundary operators whose composite is zero; the homology groups are the quotient of the cycles (chains with zero boundary) by the boundaries (chains that are themselves boundaries). Different constructions — singular, simplicial, cellular, Čech — give the same homology for well-behaved spaces. The Euler characteristic can be recovered as the alternating sum of the ranks (Betti numbers) of the homology groups.

Applications

Beyond topology, homological methods pervade algebra through homological algebra — derived functors such as Ext and Tor, group cohomology, and sheaf cohomology — and appear in data analysis as persistent homology.

The word "homology" is also used in biology for organs or genes sharing common ancestry, and in chemistry for series of related compounds.
