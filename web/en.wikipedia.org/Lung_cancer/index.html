Lung cancer

Lung cancer, also known as lung carcinoma, is a malignant tumour characterised by uncontrolled cell growth in the tissues of the lung. It is the most commonly diagnosed cancer worldwide and the leading cause of cancer death in both men and women. The great majority of cases are caused by long-term tobacco smoking.

Types

Lung cancers are broadly divided into non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC, about 85% of cases), which includes adenocarcinoma, squamous-cell carcinoma and large-cell carcinoma, and small-cell lung cancer (SCLC, about 15%), which grows and spreads faster and is almost exclusively associated with smoking. Treatment and prognosis differ substantially between the two categories.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms may include a persistent cough, coughing up blood (haemoptysis), breathlessness, chest pain, weight loss and fatigue. Many early lung cancers cause no symptoms and are found incidentally on imaging; by the time of diagnosis the disease has often spread (metastasised) to other organs such as the bones, brain, liver and adrenal glands.

Causes

Tobacco smoke causes roughly 85% or more of lung cancers; risk rises with the number of cigarettes smoked and the duration of smoking, and falls after quitting. Other causes include second-hand smoke, radon gas, asbestos, air pollution, and inherited genetic susceptibility. Lung cancer in people who have never smoked is itself a significant cause of cancer death and is often driven by specific mutations, for example in the EGFR gene.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is usually by imaging (chest X-ray and CT) followed by biopsy to confirm the type. Low-dose CT screening of high-risk people, such as heavy smokers, reduces lung-cancer mortality and is recommended in several countries. Treatment depends on type, stage and molecular profile, and includes surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, targeted drugs against mutations in genes such as EGFR, ALK and KRAS, and immunotherapy with checkpoint inhibitors. Despite improvements, overall five-year survival remains low — under roughly 25% in most countries — largely because most cases are diagnosed at an advanced stage.

Prevention

Smoking prevention and cessation are by far the most effective preventive measures; tobacco control policies are credited with large declines in lung-cancer rates in many high-income countries. Testing homes for radon and reducing occupational exposure to carcinogens provide additional benefit.
