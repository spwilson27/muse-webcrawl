A hepatocyte is a cell of the main parenchymal tissue of the liver. Hepatocytes make up about 80 percent of the liver's mass and perform most of the organ's metabolic, endocrine and detoxifying functions.

Structure

Hepatocytes are large polyhedral epithelial cells, usually 20–30 micrometres across, with abundant cytoplasm rich in organelles: extensive rough and smooth endoplasmic reticulum, numerous mitochondria, peroxisomes and Golgi complexes. Many hepatocytes are polyploid, and some are binucleate. In the liver lobule they are arranged in cords or plates one to two cells thick, separated by vascular sinusoids lined by fenestrated endothelium. Each hepatocyte has a basolateral surface facing the space of Disse and blood plasma, and an apical surface contributing to bile canaliculi, which drain bile toward the canals of Hering and the bile ducts.

Functions

Hepatocytes synthesize plasma proteins including albumin, clotting factors, complement components and transport proteins such as transferrin. They produce and secrete bile, metabolize carbohydrates (storing glucose as glycogen), synthesize cholesterol and bile salts, process lipids and lipoproteins, and detoxify drugs and other xenobiotics through phase I (cytochrome P450) and phase II reactions. They also convert ammonia to urea, store vitamins A, D and B12 and iron, and break down hormones and haemoglobin, producing bilirubin for excretion.

Regeneration and clinical significance

The liver has a remarkable regenerative capacity driven mainly by hepatocyte proliferation; after partial hepatectomy, quiescent hepatocytes re-enter the cell cycle and restore liver mass. Damage to hepatocytes is the central event in hepatitis, fatty liver disease, cirrhosis and drug-induced liver injury, and hepatocellular carcinoma arises from hepatocytes. Isolated hepatocytes and hepatocyte-like cells derived from stem cells are widely used in toxicology and drug metabolism studies.
