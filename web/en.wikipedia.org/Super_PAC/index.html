Super PAC

Super PACs, officially known as "independent expenditure-only political action committees", are a type of political action committee (PAC) in the United States that may raise unlimited sums of money from corporations, unions, associations and individuals, and then spend unlimited sums to advocate for or against political candidates. Unlike conventional PACs, super PACs may not contribute money directly to a candidate's campaign or coordinate their spending with a campaign.

Legal origins

Super PACs emerged in 2010 after two court decisions. In Citizens United v. Federal Election Commission, the US Supreme Court held that the government may not restrict independent political expenditures by corporations and unions. In SpeechNow.org v. FEC, the D.C. Circuit held that contribution limits could not be applied to groups making only independent expenditures. Combined with an FEC advisory process, these rulings created the super PAC.

Operation

Super PACs must register with the FEC and disclose their donors, though they may accept funds from nonprofit 501(c)(4) organisations that do not themselves disclose donors — a channel sometimes described as "dark money". Because they cannot coordinate with candidates, super PACs often run television advertising, including negative ads, in support of or opposition to federal candidates. Many are run by former aides of the candidates they support.

Criticism and debate

Critics argue super PACs allow wealthy donors outsized influence and undermine formal non-coordination rules in practice; supporters argue they are a form of protected political speech and association. Super PAC spending has grown into the billions of dollars per election cycle, making them a central feature of modern American campaigns.
