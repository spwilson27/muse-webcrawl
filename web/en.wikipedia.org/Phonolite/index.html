Phonolite

TypeExtrusive igneous (volcanic) rock
CompositionNepheline and alkali feldspar; silica-undersaturated
Intrusive equivalentNepheline syenite
Notable occurrencesDevils Tower (Wyoming); Eifel (Germany); Cripple Creek (Colorado)

Phonolite is an uncommon extrusive igneous rock, the volcanic equivalent of nepheline syenite. It is a fine-grained, silica-undersaturated rock composed predominantly of alkali feldspar and the feldspathoid mineral nepheline, with smaller amounts of pyroxene, amphibole, sodalite-group minerals and other feldspathoids. Its name, from Greek phōnē ("sound") and lithos ("stone"), refers to the ringing sound made when an unfractured slab of the rock is struck — a property that led to its use for lithophones and roofing tiles.

Formation

Phonolite magmas form by advanced fractional crystallisation of alkali basaltic magmas, often with crustal contamination, in continental rift and intraplate settings. Because the magma is silica-undersaturated, feldspathoids crystallise instead of quartz. Phonolite lavas are highly viscous and typically build steep-sided lava domes and coulées, or freeze as plugs and dikes in volcanic necks; erosional remnants can form striking landmarks, such as Devils Tower in Wyoming, a phonolite porphyry intrusion.

Occurrences and uses

Well-known phonolite provinces include the Eifel district of Germany, the Kaiserstuhl, the Chaîne des Puys in France, the Canary Islands, and the East African Rift, where phonolitic lavas accompany rift volcanism. Phonolite has been quarried as a dimension and roofing stone in parts of Europe, and phonolitic rocks host some alkaline ore deposits, notably the gold ores of Cripple Creek, Colorado.
