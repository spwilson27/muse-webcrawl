The monarchy of the United Kingdom, commonly referred to as the British monarchy, is the form of government used by the United Kingdom by which a hereditary monarch reigns as the head of state, with their powers regulated by the British constitution. The current system is a constitutional monarchy. The reigning monarch is Charles III, who has reigned since the death of his mother, Queen Elizabeth II, on 8 September 2022.

The monarch and their immediate family undertake various official, ceremonial, diplomatic and representational duties. The monarch's constitutional powers, known collectively as the royal prerogative, include summoning and dissolving Parliament, appointing the prime minister, granting royal assent to legislation, and serving as commander-in-chief of the British Armed Forces. By convention, however, these powers are exercised largely on the advice of ministers or according to established precedent.

History

The British monarchy traces its origins to the small kingdoms of Anglo-Saxon England and early medieval Scotland, which consolidated into the kingdoms of England and Scotland by the 10th century. The Norman Conquest of 1066 reshaped the English monarchy, and over the following centuries royal power was progressively limited: Magna Carta (1215) constrained the king's authority, the English Civil War and the execution of Charles I in 1649 led to a brief republic, and the Glorious Revolution of 1688 established parliamentary supremacy, confirmed by the Bill of Rights 1689.

The Acts of Union 1707 united the crowns of England and Scotland as Great Britain, and the Act of Union 1800 added Ireland, creating the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland. During the reign of Queen Victoria (1837–1901), the monarchy acquired much of its modern ceremonial character. Elizabeth II, who reigned from 1952 to 2022, was the longest-reigning British monarch.

Constitutional role

The monarch is the head of state but not the head of government; executive power is exercised by the prime minister and cabinet in the monarch's name through His Majesty's Government. In the uncodified British constitution, the monarch retains important functions: opening Parliament and delivering the King's Speech (written by the government), holding weekly audiences with the prime minister, accrediting ambassadors, and conferring honours. The monarch is also Supreme Governor of the Church of England and, separately, monarch of the other Commonwealth realms.

Succession

Succession is governed by the Bill of Rights 1689, the Act of Settlement 1701, and the Succession to the Crown Act 2013, which introduced absolute primogeniture for those born after 28 October 2011 and ended the disqualification of those married to Roman Catholics. The monarch must be a Protestant in communion with the Church of England.

Finances and residences

The monarchy is funded principally through the Sovereign Grant, a percentage of the profits of the Crown Estate paid by the Treasury. Official residences include Buckingham Palace, Windsor Castle, the Palace of Holyroodhouse, and Hillsborough Castle, while Sandringham House and Balmoral Castle are private estates.
