Snow

Snow
Chemical compositionH2O (ice Ih)
Crystal systemHexagonal
Density (fresh)30–300 kg/m³
Density (wet)up to ~500 kg/m³
Albedo0.80–0.90 (fresh snow)

Snow comprises individual ice crystals that grow while suspended in the atmosphere—usually within clouds—and then fall, accumulating on the ground where they undergo further changes. It consists of frozen crystalline water throughout its life cycle, starting when, under suitable conditions, the ice crystals form in the atmosphere and, having grown to millimeter size, precipitate and accumulate on surfaces.

Snowfall varies by location and season. Most of the permanently snow-covered areas are near the poles or at high altitudes, where snow accumulates year after year in glaciers and ice caps. Seasonal snow is climatically important because it reflects solar radiation (high albedo), insulates the ground, and stores water that is released during spring melt.

Formation

Snow crystals form around microscopic particles in the atmosphere, such as dust or pollen, when water vapor deposits directly as ice in a process called deposition. As crystals grow in the hexagonal lattice of ice Ih, they develop into six-armed dendrites, plates, columns, or needles depending on temperature and humidity. The intricate shapes of snowflakes were studied by Wilson Bentley, who photographed thousands of crystals beginning in 1885, and by Ukichiro Nakaya, who produced the first artificial snow crystals in the 1930s.

Types and classification

The International Classification for Seasonal Snow on the Ground distinguishes forms including new snow, faceted crystals, depth hoar, melt forms, and ice formations. Falling snow is described by visibility and intensity: light, moderate, and heavy. A blizzard is a severe snowstorm characterized by strong winds and low visibility lasting several hours.

On the ground

Once on the ground, snow forms a snowpack whose properties change through metamorphism, melting, and refreezing. Snowpack is a crucial water resource in mountainous regions such as the western United States, where spring runoff supplies rivers and reservoirs. Layers within the snowpack can become unstable, producing avalanches on steep slopes.

Ecology and human impact

Many animals rely on the subnivean zone—the insulated space beneath snow—for winter survival. For humans, snow enables winter sports such as skiing and snowboarding, but also disrupts transport, damages roofs under heavy loads, and requires removal by plowing and salting. Snowmelt can cause flooding when it occurs rapidly.
