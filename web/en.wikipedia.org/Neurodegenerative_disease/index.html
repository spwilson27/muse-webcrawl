Neurodegenerative disease

Neurodegeneration involves progressive loss of neurons and brain volume.

A neurodegenerative disease is caused by the progressive loss of structure or function of neurons, which may ultimately involve neuronal death. Neurodegenerative diseases represent a major and growing cause of mortality and disability worldwide, particularly in ageing populations. Many similarities exist between these diseases at a sub-cellular level, including atypical protein assemblies (proteinopathies) and induced cell death.

Major diseases

The most common neurodegenerative diseases include Alzheimer's disease and other dementias, Parkinson's disease, Huntington's disease, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), multiple system atrophy, and the prion diseases such as Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease. Each is characterized by the misfolding and aggregation of particular proteins: amyloid-beta and tau in Alzheimer's disease, alpha-synuclein in Parkinson's disease, huntingtin in Huntington's disease, and TDP-43 in many cases of ALS and frontotemporal dementia.

Mechanisms

Shared mechanisms include protein misfolding and prion-like spread, mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, impaired protein clearance via the ubiquitin–proteasome system and autophagy, excitotoxicity, and chronic neuroinflammation involving microglia. Genetic risk factors — such as the APOE ε4 allele in Alzheimer's disease — interact with age and environment.

Treatment

Most neurodegenerative diseases lack cures; treatment is symptomatic. Levodopa remains the mainstay for Parkinson's motor symptoms, cholinesterase inhibitors are used in Alzheimer's disease, and monoclonal antibodies targeting amyloid (such as lecanemab and donanemab) have received approval for early Alzheimer's disease in some jurisdictions, with modest effects on progression.
