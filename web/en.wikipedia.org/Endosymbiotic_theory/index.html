Endosymbiotic theory

The endosymbiotic theory (or symbiogenesis) states that certain organelles of eukaryotic cells — principally mitochondria and chloroplasts — originated as free-living bacteria that were engulfed by a host cell and evolved into permanent, integrated symbionts. It is now the mainstream explanation for the origin of these organelles and a landmark example of evolution by symbiosis rather than by gradual mutation alone.

History

The idea was first proposed by the Russian botanist Konstantin Mereschkowski in 1905 for chloroplasts, and extended by Ivan Wallin in the 1920s to mitochondria, but it was largely ignored until Lynn Margulis revived and championed it in her 1967 paper "On the Origin of Mitosing Cells" and her 1970 book Origin of Eukaryotic Cells. Initially controversial, the theory gained decisive support from molecular evidence in the 1970s and 1980s.

Evidence

  • Mitochondria and chloroplasts have their own small, circular DNA, similar to bacterial genomes.
  • They are surrounded by double membranes, consistent with engulfing.
  • They reproduce by binary fission, independently of the cell cycle.
  • Their ribosomes resemble bacterial (70S) ribosomes rather than eukaryotic (80S) ones.
  • Phylogenetic analyses place mitochondrial genes within the alphaproteobacteria and chloroplast genes within the cyanobacteria.

Details

The ancestral mitochondrion is thought to derive from an alphaproteobacterium taken up by an archaeal host related to the Asgard archaea. Chloroplasts descend from a cyanobacterium engulfed by an early eukaryote; secondary and tertiary endosymbioses — eukaryotes engulfing other eukaryotes with plastids — produced the complex plastids of many algae. Over time, most endosymbiont genes were transferred to the host nucleus (endosymbiotic gene transfer), leaving organelles dependent on the cell.
