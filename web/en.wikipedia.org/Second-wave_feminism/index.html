Second-wave feminism

Second-wave feminism was a period of feminist activity that began in the early 1960s in the United States and spread throughout the Western world and beyond, lasting into the 1980s. Whereas the first wave centred on legal rights such as suffrage, the second wave broadened the debate to a wide range of issues: sexuality, family, the workplace, reproductive rights, domestic violence, marital rape, divorce law, and de facto inequalities beyond the statute book.

Origins

Simone de Beauvoir's The Second Sex (1949) laid philosophical groundwork, famously declaring that one is not born but becomes a woman. In the United States, Betty Friedan's The Feminine Mystique (1963) named "the problem that has no name" — the dissatisfaction of educated suburban housewives — and Friedan co-founded the National Organization for Women (NOW) in 1966. In France, the Mouvement de libération des femmes emerged around 1970; the Manifesto of the 343 (1971) pressed for abortion rights.

Campaigns and achievements

Second-wave activists won major legal changes: equal-pay and anti-discrimination laws, wider access to contraception and abortion (Roe v. Wade in the US, 1973; the Veil law in France, 1975), no-fault divorce, and the opening of professions and higher education. The slogan "the personal is political" framed housework, sexuality, and violence as public issues. In the US, the Equal Rights Amendment passed Congress in 1972 but ultimately failed ratification. The wave was internally diverse — liberal, radical, socialist, and cultural feminisms — and was criticised, notably by Black feminists such as bell hooks, for centring white middle-class experience, critiques that shaped the third wave.

Legacy

The second wave transformed women's education, employment, and legal status across the West, and its debates over pornography, surrogacy, and the family remain live in later feminisms.
