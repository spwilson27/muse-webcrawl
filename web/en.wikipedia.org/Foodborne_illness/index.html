Foodborne illness

Other namesFood poisoning, foodborne disease
SpecialtyInfectious disease, toxicology
SymptomsVomiting, diarrhoea, abdominal pain, fever
CausesBacteria, viruses, parasites, toxins, chemicals

Foodborne illness (also foodborne disease and food poisoning) is any illness resulting from eating food contaminated with pathogens — bacteria, viruses, or parasites — or their toxins. Symptoms most commonly include vomiting, diarrhoea, abdominal cramps, and fever, and onset ranges from hours to weeks depending on the agent. The World Health Organization estimates that contaminated food causes about 600 million illnesses and 420,000 deaths each year worldwide.

Causes

The most common causes in surveillance data are norovirus, Salmonella, Clostridium perfringens, Campylobacter, and Staphylococcus aureus. More dangerous agents include Shiga-toxin-producing Escherichia coli, which can cause haemolytic–uremic syndrome, and Listeria monocytogenes, a particular risk in pregnancy. Toxin-mediated illnesses include botulism, caused by Clostridium botulinum, and natural toxins such as aflatoxin and marine toxins in shellfish.

Transmission and prevention

Contamination can occur at any stage of production, processing, distribution, or preparation. Improper cooking, inadequate refrigeration, cross-contamination between raw and cooked foods, and infected food handlers are leading contributors. Prevention rests on food safety practices: hygiene, separation of raw and cooked foods, thorough cooking, and safe storage temperatures.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is usually clinical; stool cultures or molecular tests can identify the agent in outbreaks. Treatment is mainly supportive, with oral rehydration for fluid losses; antibiotics are reserved for specific infections. Public-health authorities investigate clusters of illness to trace contaminated products and order recalls.
