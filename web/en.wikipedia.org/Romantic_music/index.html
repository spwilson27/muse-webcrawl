Romantic music is a stylistic movement in Western classical music associated with the period from about 1800 or the 1820s to around 1910 — the musical expression of the wider Romantic movement in literature and art. It prized emotional expression, individuality, and the evocation of nature, literature and the supernatural over the balance and restraint of the Classical style. (Despite the similar name, it is unrelated to the Romance languages.)

Characteristics

Romantic composers expanded harmony with rich chromaticism, stretched forms to unprecedented lengths, enlarged the orchestra and demanded extreme ranges of dynamics and tempo flexibility (rubato). New genres flourished: the character piece and nocturne for solo piano, the art song (Lied), the symphonic poem, and music drama. Nationalism became a powerful theme, with composers from Chopin to Smetana drawing on the folk music and history of their homelands.

Early and high Romanticism

The movement's early figures include Schubert, whose songs and "Unfinished" Symphony redefined expressive depth; Weber and Mendelssohn; and the virtuoso pianist-composers Chopin and Liszt alongside Robert and Clara Schumann. By mid-century the era reached heroic scale in Berlioz's Symphonie fantastique, the operas of Verdi and Wagner — whose Tristan und Isolde (1865) pushed tonality to its limits — and the symphonies of Brahms, Bruckner and Tchaikovsky.

Late Romanticism and legacy

In the decades around 1900, Mahler, Richard Strauss, Rachmaninoff and Elgar carried Romantic idioms to enormous orchestral forces even as modernists such as Debussy, Stravinsky and Schoenberg broke with them. Romantic music remains the core of the modern concert and opera repertoire, and its language profoundly shaped film scoring in the 20th century.
