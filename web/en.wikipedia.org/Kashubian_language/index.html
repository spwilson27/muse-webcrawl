Kashubian (Kashubian: kaszëbsczi jãzëk) is a West Slavic language spoken by the Kashubians in the historical region of Pomerania in north-central Poland. It is the closest living relative of Polish within the Lechitic group and the only surviving descendant of the Pomeranian dialects; its status — language or dialect of Polish — was long debated, but Polish law has recognised it since 2005 as a regional language, the only one so designated in the country.

History

Kashubian developed from the Slavic dialects of the Pomeranian coast. The oldest written records date to the 15th–16th centuries, mainly religious texts. Florian Ceynowa (1817–1881), often called the father of Kashubian literature, created an early literary standard and grammar. Under Prussian and later German rule, and again under communist Poland, the language faced Germanisation and Polonisation pressures; nevertheless a press, theatre, and literature developed, with writers such as Aleksander Majkowski, author of the novel Żëcé i przigòdë Remùsa ("The Life and Adventures of Remus").

Features

Kashubian retains features lost in Polish, notably the vowel ë (schwa), nasal vowels distinct from Polish ones, and movable initial stress in northern dialects versus penultimate stress influenced by Polish in the south. Its alphabet uses letters including ã, é, ë, ò, ô, ù, ż. Vocabulary shares the Lechitic core with Polish but includes archaic Slavic words and many German loanwords reflecting centuries of contact.

Modern status

The Act of 6 January 2005 on national and ethnic minorities and on the regional language made Kashubian Poland's only recognised regional language. It may be used as an auxiliary language in commune offices, appears on bilingual signs, is taught in hundreds of schools, and is offered as a subject in the Polish matura exam (since 2005). Media include the journal Pomerania, radio broadcasts, and a Kashubian Wikipedia. Census figures suggest some 87,000–110,000 home speakers, with several hundred thousand declaring Kashubian identity; intergenerational transmission is nonetheless weakening, and UNESCO classifies the language as endangered.
