Decision-making


A decision-making flowchart

In psychology, decision-making is the cognitive process resulting in the selection of a belief or a course of action among several possible alternatives. It is studied in disciplines including psychology, economics, management science, and political science, and can be rational, heuristic, or emotional.

Overview

Decision-making involves identifying a problem or opportunity, gathering information, generating alternatives, weighing evidence, choosing among alternatives, taking action, and reviewing the outcome. Classical decision theory assumed fully rational agents; Herbert Simon instead proposed bounded rationality, in which decision-makers "satisfice" with choices that are good enough given limited information and cognitive capacity.

Biases and heuristics

Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky's research program showed that human judgment under uncertainty relies on heuristics such as availability, representativeness, and anchoring, which can produce systematic cognitive biases. Prospect theory, developed by Kahneman and Tversky in 1979, describes how people evaluate gains and losses asymmetrically.

Group decision-making

Groups can pool information but are vulnerable to failures such as groupthink and polarization. Structured techniques including consensus decision-making, voting, and the Delphi method aim to improve group choices.
