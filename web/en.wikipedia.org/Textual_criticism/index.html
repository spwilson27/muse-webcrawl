Textual criticism is a branch of philology concerned with identifying and removing errors in texts — especially manuscripts copied by hand before printing — and with producing a reliable edition that approximates the author's original as closely as the surviving evidence allows.

Methods

The classical method, systematised in the 19th century and associated with Karl Lachmann, proceeds by recensio: collecting all witnesses, collating their variant readings, and grouping them into a family tree (stemma codicum) based on shared errors, so that the reading of their common ancestor can be reconstructed. This is followed by examinatio (judging which readings are original using principles such as preferring the more difficult reading, lectio difficilior potior) and emendatio (conjectural correction where all witnesses err). Many modern editors instead use an eclectic method, choosing readings case by case, or edit a single "best text".

Applications

Textual criticism is fundamental to the study of the Bible — where thousands of Greek New Testament manuscripts, plus versions and quotations, must be sifted to establish the text printed in editions such as Nestle–Aland — and to classical literature, medieval works, and authors like Shakespeare, whose plays survive in differing quarto and folio versions. The discipline has been transformed by digital tools that allow vast collations and phylogenetic analysis of manuscript traditions.
