Traboule

A traboule is a type of covered passageway found principally in the French city of Lyon, and also in Villefranche-sur-Saône, Mâcon, and Saint-Étienne. Traboules pass through buildings and their courtyards, linking one street to another, often on different levels. Lyon has several hundred of them, concentrated in the Vieux Lyon, Croix-Rousse, and Presqu'île districts.

History

The earliest traboules date from the Renaissance, when they gave residents direct access to water from wells in courtyards and to the River Saône. In the nineteenth century, the traboules of Croix-Rousse became vital to the silk industry: the canuts (silk workers) used them to move bolts of silk between workshops quickly while keeping the fabric dry. During the canut revolts of 1831 and 1834 the passages helped workers move through the quarter, and in the Second World War they served the French Resistance in evading the Gestapo.

Architecture

Traboules typically combine vaulted passage segments with open courtyards ringed by galleries and Italianate staircases. The most famous, the Tour Rose traboule in Vieux Lyon, is named for its pink-painted circular stair tower. Some traboules are open to the public under agreements between the city and residents; doors are often marked with small plaques or lion-head markers.
