Digital audio

Digital audio is a representation of sound recorded in, or converted into, digital form. In a digital audio system, sound is converted into a stream of discrete numbers representing changes in air pressure over time, typically by pulse-code modulation (PCM), which can then be stored, processed, transmitted, and reproduced.

A sampled waveform: analogue sound converted to discrete samples.

Sampling and quantisation

An analog-to-digital converter samples the waveform at regular intervals (the sample rate) and assigns each sample a value at a fixed resolution (the bit depth). The Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem states that the sample rate must be at least twice the highest frequency to be captured. Compact Disc audio uses 44,100 samples per second at 16 bits per sample; professional systems commonly use 48 kHz or higher at 24 bits.

History

PCM was patented by Alec Reeves in 1937 for telephony. Digital recording developed in the 1970s, with commercial digital recorders from Denon and others; the Compact Disc, launched in 1982 by Philips and Sony, brought digital audio to consumers. Later formats and technologies include DAT, MiniDisc, MP3 and other perceptual codecs, digital audio workstations, and streaming services.

Advantages

Digital copies can be made without generational loss, editing is non-destructive and precise, and error correction can recover data from damaged media. Compression codecs trade file size against perceived quality by discarding information the ear is unlikely to notice.
