Edict of Toleration

An edict of toleration is a declaration, made by a government or ruler, implying that specific religious groups will not be persecuted for engaging in their religious practices and traditions. Such edicts typically stop short of full legal equality but end active state repression of a faith. Famous examples span from ancient Persia and Ashoka's India to early modern Europe.

TypeGovernment declaration
SubjectReligious toleration
Notable examplesEdict of Milan (313), Edict of Nantes (1598), Patent of Toleration (1781)

Edicts of toleration granted limited freedom of worship by decree.

Notable edicts

  • Edict of Milan (313) — Constantine and Licinius granted Christianity legal status and restored confiscated church property throughout the Roman Empire.
  • Edict of Nantes (1598) — Henry IV of France granted the Huguenots substantial rights; it was revoked by Louis XIV's Edict of Fontainebleau in 1685.
  • Warsaw Confederation (1573) — the Polish–Lithuanian nobility guaranteed religious peace among dissenting faiths.
  • Patent of Toleration (1781) — Joseph II of Austria extended civil rights to Lutherans, Calvinists, and Orthodox Christians, followed by a Toleration Edict for Jews in 1782.
  • Galerius's edict (311) — the Edict of Serdica ended the Diocletianic Persecution of Christians in the East.

Significance

Edicts of toleration were pragmatic instruments: rulers issued them to secure peace, revenue, or loyalty, and they could be withdrawn by a successor. They nevertheless mark key steps in the long development of freedom of religion as a legal right rather than a revocable privilege.
