Line printer

A line printer prints one entire line of text before advancing to another line. Most early line printers were impact printers that used a chain of type characters (a print chain or train), a rapidly rotating drum of type slugs, or a band of characters, struck against an inked ribbon and paper by a bank of hammers. The entire line was printed in a single hammering cycle, giving speeds far beyond character-at-a-time devices.

History

Line printers were the standard output device for mainframe and minicomputer installations from the 1950s through the 1980s. Notable models include the IBM 1403, introduced in 1959 with the IBM 1401 computer, which could print 600 lines per minute and was famed for its print quality; later models such as the IBM 3211 reached 2,000 lines per minute. Output was typically on continuous fanfold ("green bar") paper, 14⅞ inches wide with 132 columns.

Other mechanisms included drum printers, in which a complete set of characters for each column position was embossed around a rotating drum, and band printers using a steel band of type. Non-impact line printers using thermal or electrostatic technology appeared later but were less common.

Decline

Line printers declined as interactive terminals, cheaper dot matrix printers, and high-speed laser printers took over. The term survives in the Unix lp command family (lpr, lpq, lprm) and in "line printer daemon" (LPD) network printing protocols.
