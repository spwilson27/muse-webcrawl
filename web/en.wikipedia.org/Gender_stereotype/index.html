Gender stereotype

A gender stereotype is a generalised view or preconception about attributes, characteristics, or roles that ought to be possessed by, or performed by, women and men. A gender stereotype is harmful when it limits women's and men's capacity to develop their personal abilities, pursue their professional careers and/or make choices about their lives.

Content of stereotypes

Psychological research, summarised in the stereotype content model and Alice Eagly's social role theory, finds two recurring dimensions: women are stereotyped as high in communion or warmth (nurturing, kind, emotionally expressive) but lower in agency or competence, while men are stereotyped as agentic (assertive, independent, competent) but less warm. These beliefs arise in part from observing the sexes in different social roles — women as caregivers, men as breadwinners — and then inferring corresponding inner traits.

Effects

Gender stereotypes influence children's toy and subject choices, teachers' expectations, hiring and promotion decisions, and self-perceptions. Stereotype threat — the anxiety of confirming a negative stereotype about one's group — has been shown to depress performance, for example of girls in mathematics tests under some conditions. "Benevolent" stereotypes (such as the idealisation of women as pure and needing protection) can also sustain inequality by justifying restrictive arrangements, a pattern termed benevolent sexism.

Change and policy

Stereotypes have weakened as women's labour-force participation and education have risen, but they persist in media representations and occupational sorting. International instruments including CEDAW (Article 5) call on states to eliminate stereotyped concepts of the roles of men and women in education and public life.
