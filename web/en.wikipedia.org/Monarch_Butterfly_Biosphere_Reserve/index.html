Monarch Butterfly Biosphere Reserve

Overwintering monarchs blanket the fir forests of the reserve

The Monarch Butterfly Biosphere Reserve (Spanish: Reserva de la Biosfera de la Mariposa Monarca) is a protected area in the Trans-Mexican Volcanic Belt pine–oak forests of eastern Michoacán and western Mexico State, about 100 km northwest of Mexico City. Covering some 56,000 hectares, it protects the oyamel fir forests where the great majority of the eastern North American population of monarch butterflies (Danaus plexippus) spends the winter. It was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2008.

The overwintering colonies

Each autumn, monarchs migrate up to 4,500 km from breeding grounds in the United States and Canada to a handful of mountain sites at 3,000 m altitude, where cool, moist fir forest lets them survive in semi-dormancy. Tens of millions cluster on tree trunks and branches — sometimes bending them under their weight — in colonies occupying a few hectares. The location of the sites was a famous scientific mystery until their documentation in 1975 by Ken Brugger and Catalina Trail, working with Fred and Norah Urquhart. In March the butterflies mate, stream north, and die, leaving descendants to complete the multi-generational return.

Threats and conservation

Illegal logging long threatened the fir forest; enforcement and compensation schemes reduced it sharply, though small-scale logging persists. The population itself has fallen steeply since the 1990s — measured by the area of forest occupied, down over 80% from its peak — driven by loss of milkweed to herbicide use in North American farmland, climate extremes, and storms that can kill hundreds of millions of butterflies at once. Mexico, the US, and Canada coordinate conservation, and local communities run tourism and reforestation programmes.
