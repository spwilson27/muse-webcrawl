The Serious Fraud Office (SFO) is a non-ministerial government department of the Government of the United Kingdom that investigates and prosecutes serious or complex fraud and corruption in England, Wales and Northern Ireland. It was established by the Criminal Justice Act 1987 following the recommendations of the Roskill Report (1986), which concluded that the existing system was inadequate for complex fraud trials. The SFO began operating in April 1988.

Powers and role

Uniquely, the SFO both investigates and prosecutes its cases, combining lawyers, investigators, forensic accountants and digital-forensics specialists in case teams. Under section 2 of the Criminal Justice Act 1987, it can compel individuals and companies to answer questions and produce documents during an investigation. Cases are accepted only where the suspected fraud is serious or complex, typically involving sums over £1 million, significant international dimensions, or cases likely to give rise to widespread public concern. The Bribery Act 2010 gave the SFO a leading role in prosecuting overseas corruption, and since 2014 it has been able to agree deferred prosecution agreements (DPAs) with companies.

Notable cases

Landmark investigations include the Guinness share-support scandal, the collapse of Barlow Clowes, the Blue Arrow trial, the BAE Systems arms-deal inquiries, Rolls-Royce (a £497 million DPA in 2017), Barclays, Glencore (which pleaded guilty and was fined in 2022) and the LIBOR manipulation cases, including the conviction of trader Tom Hayes in 2015. The agency has also faced criticism and legal defeats, notably the collapse of the Tesco trial and judicial criticism in the Unaoil and ENRC-related litigation.
