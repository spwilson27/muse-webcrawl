Gene-centered view of evolution

The gene-centered view of evolution (also called the gene's eye view) holds that evolution is best understood by focusing on the gene as the fundamental unit of natural selection. Since genes (replicators) persist across generations while individual organisms are temporary "vehicles" or "survival machines", adaptive traits evolve because they help the genes that produce them to replicate, even when this appears costly to the individual organism.

Origins

The view grew out of the population genetics of R. A. Fisher, J. B. S. Haldane and Sewall Wright, and was developed in George C. Williams's Adaptation and Natural Selection (1966), which sharply criticised group selection. W. D. Hamilton's theory of inclusive fitness (1964) showed mathematically how a gene for altruism toward relatives can spread. The perspective was famously popularised by Richard Dawkins in The Selfish Gene (1976) and extended in The Extended Phenotype (1982).

Main ideas

Under the gene's eye view, apparently altruistic behaviours — sterile worker castes in social insects, alarm calls, parental care — are explained by genes promoting copies of themselves in relatives. The framework also illuminates intragenomic conflict, such as meiotic drive and segregation distorters, where genes subvert fair inheritance to their own advantage. Dawkins coined the term meme for cultural replicators by analogy with genes.

Criticism

Critics argue that selection also acts meaningfully at other levels (organisms, groups, species), that genes are not truly independent agents, and that development and environment complicate gene-level accounts — positions associated with multilevel selection theory and the extended evolutionary synthesis. Proponents reply that gene-level bookkeeping remains mathematically equivalent to, and often clearer than, other formulations.
