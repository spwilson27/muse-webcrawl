Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), also known as epidermoid carcinoma, is a type of cancer that begins in squamous cells, the thin, flat cells that form the surface of the skin and line many organs, including the mouth, throat, esophagus, lungs, cervix, and anus. Squamous cell carcinoma is one of the most common forms of cancer worldwide.

Signs and symptoms

Cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma typically presents as a firm, pink or flesh-colored nodule, a scaly red patch, or an ulcer that fails to heal. It most often arises on sun-exposed areas such as the face, ears, neck, forearms, and backs of the hands. Unlike the more common basal cell carcinoma, SCC has a greater tendency to grow rapidly and to metastasize, particularly in immunosuppressed patients.

Causes

The principal cause of cutaneous SCC is cumulative exposure to ultraviolet radiation from sunlight or tanning beds, which damages DNA in keratinocytes, including mutations in the TP53 tumor suppressor gene. Other risk factors include infection with human papillomavirus (HPV), chronic inflammation and non-healing wounds (Marjolin ulcer), exposure to arsenic and certain industrial chemicals, ionizing radiation, and immunosuppression following organ transplantation.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is confirmed by skin biopsy. Histologically, SCC is characterized by nests of atypical squamous epithelial cells invading the dermis, often with keratin pearl formation. Tumors are graded by differentiation, and staging considers tumor size, depth of invasion, perineural involvement, and lymph node spread.

Treatment

Surgical excision is the mainstay of treatment; Mohs micrographic surgery is preferred for high-risk or facial lesions. Radiation therapy may be used when surgery is not feasible. Advanced or metastatic disease may be treated with immunotherapy, including the PD-1 inhibitors cemiplimab and pembrolizumab.

Sites

Squamous cell carcinoma occurs at many anatomical sites: skin, head and neck (including oral cavity and larynx), esophagus, lung (where it is strongly linked to smoking), cervix, and penis. At each site it shares a squamous morphology but has distinct causes, staging, and treatment.
