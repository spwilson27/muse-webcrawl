The Forth Bridge is a cantilever railway bridge across the Firth of Forth in Scotland, connecting South Queensferry near Edinburgh with North Queensferry in Fife. Opened in 1890, it was the longest single cantilever bridge span in the world at the time and remains one of the most recognisable structures in Scotland. Its two main spans of 521 metres (1,710 ft) were the longest cantilever spans ever built until the Quebec Bridge surpassed them in 1917.

Background and construction

An earlier design by Thomas Bouch for a suspension bridge at the site was abandoned after Bouch's Tay Bridge collapsed in a storm in 1879, killing all aboard a passing train. Engineers John Fowler and Benjamin Baker therefore produced a massive, demonstrably rigid cantilever design in steel—a relatively new material for bridges of this scale. Construction began in 1882 under contractor William Arrol & Co. and employed some 4,600 workers at its peak; at least 57 men died during the works. The bridge uses about 53,000 tonnes of steel and 6.5 million rivets.

Design

The bridge consists of three great double cantilevers resting on granite piers, with two suspended spans between them. Its distinctive red-oxide paint and continuous maintenance gave rise to the expression "like painting the Forth Bridge" for a never-ending task. In 2011 a ten-year repainting programme using modern glass-flake epoxy coatings was completed, ending the era of continuous painting.

Heritage

The Forth Bridge is a Category A listed building and was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2015, recognised as an extraordinary and imaginative milestone in bridge design. It stands alongside the Forth Road Bridge (1964) and the Queensferry Crossing (2017), three centuries-spanning crossings of the Forth at Queensferry.
