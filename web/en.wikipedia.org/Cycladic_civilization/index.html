Cycladic civilization

Cycladic civilization (also known as Cycladic culture or the Cycladic period) is an Early Bronze Age culture of the Cyclades islands in the Aegean Sea, flourishing roughly between 3200 and 2000 BC. It is best known for its distinctive marble figurines and for the seafaring trade networks that linked the Aegean world.

History

Scholars divide the period into Early Cycladic I, II, and III phases, based on pottery and burial styles. Early Cycladic II (the Keros-Syros culture) marks the height of the culture, with prosperous settlements such as Chalandriani on Syros and craft production in marble, obsidian, and metal. The island of Keros held a major ritual centre where broken marble figurines were deposited. In the later phases, Cycladic settlements came increasingly under the influence of Minoan Crete and then the Mycenaean mainland.

Art

The most famous Cycladic art is the marble figurines, mostly female figures with folded arms, lyre-shaped heads, and minimal features once enhanced with paint. Their austere abstraction influenced twentieth-century artists such as Modigliani and Brancusi. Other artefacts include marble vessels, "frying pan" vessels of uncertain use, and pottery decorated with spirals and images of longboats, showing the importance of the sea.

Society

Cycladic communities farmed barley, sheep, and goats, fished for tuna, and traded obsidian from Melos and metals across the Aegean. Cemeteries of small cist graves outside settlements provide most of the evidence for the culture, since few large buildings survive.
