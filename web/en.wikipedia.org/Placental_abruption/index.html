Placental abruption

Placental abruption (also known as abruptio placentae) is a complication of pregnancy in which the placenta partially or completely separates from the inner wall of the uterus before delivery. It occurs in roughly 1 in 100 pregnancies, most often in the third trimester, and can deprive the baby of oxygen and nutrients while causing heavy bleeding in the mother. It is a medical emergency.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms include vaginal bleeding (though bleeding may be concealed behind the placenta), abdominal or back pain, uterine tenderness and frequent contractions, and signs of fetal distress. Severity ranges from mild, partial separations to complete abruption, which can be rapidly fatal to the baby and dangerous to the mother through haemorrhage and disseminated intravascular coagulation.

Causes and risk factors

The exact cause is often unknown. Risk factors include high blood pressure and pre-eclampsia, previous abruption, abdominal trauma, smoking, cocaine use, premature rupture of membranes, and advanced maternal age. The underlying event is bleeding into the decidua, which splits the placenta away from the uterus.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is primarily clinical, based on symptoms and fetal monitoring; ultrasound can help but may miss many cases. Management depends on severity and gestational age: mild cases may be monitored in hospital, while significant abruption usually requires prompt delivery, often by caesarean section, along with blood transfusion and resuscitation for the mother.
