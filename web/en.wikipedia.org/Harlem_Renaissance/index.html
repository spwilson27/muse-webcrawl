Harlem Renaissance

The Harlem Renaissance was an intellectual and cultural revival of African American music, dance, art, fashion, literature, theatre, politics and scholarship centred in Harlem, Manhattan, New York City, spanning the 1920s and 1930s. At the time, it was known as the "New Negro Movement," named after The New Negro (1925), an anthology edited by Alain Locke.

Background

The movement grew out of the Great Migration, in which hundreds of thousands of African Americans moved from the rural South to Northern cities in search of work and escape from Jim Crow laws. Harlem became a cultural capital where Black artists, writers, and musicians could find publishers, patrons, and audiences—though often filtered through white-owned publishing houses and clubs such as the Cotton Club.

Literature and arts

Major writers included Langston Hughes, Zora Neale Hurston, Claude McKay, Countee Cullen, and Jean Toomer, whose work explored Black identity, folk culture, and modern urban life. Visual artists such as Aaron Douglas developed a distinctive modernist style, while photographers like James Van Der Zee documented Harlem society.

Music

Jazz and blues defined the era's sound. Duke Ellington's orchestra at the Cotton Club, Louis Armstrong, Bessie Smith, Fats Waller, and Billie Holiday brought Black musical forms to national and international audiences, transforming American popular music.

Decline and legacy

The Great Depression dried up patronage and publishing, and the Harlem riot of 1935 is often taken as the movement's end. Its legacy, however, was lasting: it reshaped American culture, asserted Black artistic independence, and laid groundwork for the Civil Rights Movement.
