Bicycle lane

TypesConventional, buffered, protected (cycle track), contraflow, advisory
Typical width1.5–2.0 m (one-way)
MarkingsBicycle symbol, directional arrows, solid or broken lines; green surfacing in some jurisdictions

Bicycle lanes (or bike lanes) are portions of a roadway designated for use by bicyclists, typically delineated by painted lines, symbols, and signage. They are a core element of cycling infrastructure, intended to separate cyclists from motor traffic in space (if not always physically) and to organize predictable movements by all road users.

Types

A conventional bike lane is a painted strip adjacent to motor traffic. Buffered lanes add a hatched buffer zone; protected bike lanes (cycle tracks) place a physical barrier — curbs, bollards, parked cars, or planters — between cyclists and moving traffic. Contraflow lanes allow two-way cycling on one-way streets, while advisory lanes, common in the Netherlands and rural areas, are marked with broken lines that motorists may enter when no cyclist is present.

Design and debate

Design guidance such as the NACTO Urban Bikeway Design Guide and Dutch CROW manual specifies widths, intersection treatments, and door-zone avoidance — lanes should not pass within reach of opening parked-car doors. Research generally finds that protected lanes reduce cyclist injuries substantially and increase ridership, while painted lanes show more mixed safety effects; critics of all on-road lanes include vehicular-cycling advocates, though separated infrastructure is now the dominant policy approach in cities promoting cycling.

History

Dedicated cycle paths date to the 1890s bicycle boom (and earlier stone-surfaced sidepaths in the United States). The modern painted bike lane spread from the 1970s — Davis, California is credited with pioneering marked lanes in the U.S. — and the protected-lane networks of Copenhagen and Amsterdam became global models in the 21st century.
