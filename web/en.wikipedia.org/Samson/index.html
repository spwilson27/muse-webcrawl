Samson

Samson (Hebrew: שִׁמְשׁוֹן, Shimshon, "man of the sun") was the last of the major judges of ancient Israel mentioned in the Hebrew Bible, in chapters 13 to 16 of the Book of Judges. He is renowned for the supernatural strength granted by God, which resided in his uncut hair as a sign of his Nazirite vow, and for his tragic entanglement with Delilah, who discovered the secret of his strength and betrayed him to the Philistines.

Birth and Nazirite vow

According to Judges, an angel announced Samson's birth to the barren wife of Manoah of the tribe of Dan, instructing that the boy be a Nazirite from the womb: he must never cut his hair, drink wine, or eat anything unclean. His birth thus paralleled other biblical annunciations of special children.

Feats of strength

The narrative recounts Samson killing a lion with his bare hands, and later finding honey in its carcass — the source of his famous riddle, "Out of the eater came something to eat; out of the strong came something sweet." When his Philistine wife was given to another man, he caught three hundred foxes, tied torches to their tails, and burned the Philistines' crops. With the jawbone of a donkey he slew a thousand Philistines, and he once carried off the city gates of Gaza to a hilltop near Hebron.

Delilah and capture

In the Valley of Sorek, Samson loved Delilah, whom the lords of the Philistines bribed to learn the secret of his strength. After three false answers, he told her the truth: his strength lay in his uncut hair. While he slept, his hair was shaved; his strength left him, and the Philistines seized him, gouged out his eyes, and set him to grinding grain in prison in Gaza.

Death

As his hair grew back, Samson was brought out to entertain the crowd at a great festival in the temple of Dagon. Placed between the two central pillars, he prayed for strength one last time and pulled the temple down, killing himself and — the text says — more Philistines in his death than in his life.

Interpretation and legacy

Samson's story has been retold for millennia: John Milton's verse drama Samson Agonistes (1671) and Handel's oratorio Samson (1743) are landmarks, and Cecil B. DeMille's film Samson and Delilah (1949) fixed his image in popular culture. Scholars note folktale and solar-myth motifs behind the narrative; his name derives from shemesh, "sun", and his home area near Beth-Shemesh ("house of the sun"). In Jewish tradition Samson is a judge of Israel; in the New Testament he is listed among the heroes of faith (Hebrews 11).
