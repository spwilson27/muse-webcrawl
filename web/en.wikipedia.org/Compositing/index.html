Compositing

Compositing is the process or technique of combining of visual elements from separate sources into single images, often to create the illusion that all those elements are parts of the same scene. Live-action shots that involve compositing are often called "chroma key", "blue screen", or "green screen" shots, after the keying technique used to isolate foreground subjects.

Basic procedure

In the digital method of compositing, software commands designate a narrowly defined colour range in the foreground image to be replaced (the "matte"), so that separately filmed or rendered background elements can be inserted. The mathematical foundation is the alpha channel and the "over" operator formalized by Porter and Duff in 1984: each pixel carries a coverage (alpha) value, and images are combined according to their premultiplied colours. Node-based compositing packages such as Nuke represent the process as a graph of image operations, allowing complex shots with hundreds of layers — live action, CGI, matte paintings, and particle effects — to be integrated with consistent lighting, grain, and colour.

History

Photographic compositing dates to the 19th century; filmmakers such as Georges Méliès used multiple exposures and mattes, and optical printers later allowed travelling mattes for films such as Star Wars. Digital compositing displaced optical methods in the 1990s and is now the final image-assembly stage of nearly all visual-effects production, as well as a core operation in desktop window systems (compositing window managers).
