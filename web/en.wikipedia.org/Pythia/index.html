The Pythia (/ˈpɪθiə/; Greek: Πυθία) was the name of the high priestess of the Temple of Apollo at Delphi. She specifically served as its oracle and was known as the Oracle of Delphi. Her title was derived from Pytho, the place name given to Delphi in honour of the god Apollo's slaying of the serpent Python. The Pythia was established in the 8th century BC and was widely credited for her prophecies, which she delivered in a frenzied state induced — according to tradition — by vapours rising from a chasm in the rock, speaking in tongues that priests interpreted into ambiguous, often hexametric, utterances.

The office

The Pythia was chosen from among the women of Delphi; in the oracle's heyday up to three Pythias served in rotation. She prepared for a consultation by ritual purification in the Castalian Spring, drinking from the Kassotis spring, chewing laurel leaves and taking her seat on a tripod over the chasm in the temple's inner sanctuary (the adyton). Consultations originally occurred once a year, on Apollo's birthday, and later monthly. Inquirers — statesmen, lawgivers, generals and private citizens from across the Greek world and beyond — paid a fee and sacrificed a goat before putting their questions.

Famous pronouncements

Delphic responses were proverbially ambiguous: Croesus of Lydia was told that if he attacked Persia he would "destroy a great empire" — his own. Athens was advised to trust in "wooden walls" before Salamis, interpreted by Themistocles as the fleet. Socrates' companion Chaerephon was told no man was wiser than Socrates. The geographer Strabo and the essayist Plutarch — himself a priest at Delphi — describe the oracle's workings; Plutarch also records its decline. The oracle was suppressed under the Christian emperor Theodosius I in AD 393.

The vapours debate

Plutarch reported a sweet-smelling pneuma (breath or vapour) in the adyton. Modern geological research has proposed that the temple sat above intersecting faults emitting light hydrocarbon gases such as ethylene, which can induce trance-like states, though the hypothesis remains debated among classicists and geologists.
