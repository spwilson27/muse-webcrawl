Planetary migration

Planetary migration

Planetary migration is the alteration of the orbit of a planet or other body in orbit around a star through interactions with a gas disk, a disk of planetesimals, or other bodies. Migration changes the semi-major axis, eccentricity, and inclination of a planet's orbit. The phenomenon explains why many exoplanetary systems—such as hot Jupiters orbiting very close to their stars—do not match the architecture expected from in-situ formation, and it is a central element of modern models of the early Solar System.

Disk-driven migration

A planet embedded in a protoplanetary disk excites spiral density waves at Lindblad resonances, which exert torques on the planet. Low-mass planets that do not open a gap undergo Type I migration, typically inward and rapid. A massive planet that clears a gap in the disk undergoes Type II migration, coupled to the viscous evolution of the disk. Under some conditions, rapid runaway (Type III) migration can occur. Stopping mechanisms such as disk edges, dead-zone boundaries, and magnetospheric cavities may halt migration and preserve close-in planets.

Planetesimal-driven migration

Scattering of planetesimals can also drive migration: a planet that ejects planetesimals loses angular momentum, while one that scatters them inward gains it. In the Solar System, Neptune's outward migration through the primordial Kuiper belt is inferred from the resonant populations of Kuiper belt objects, including the Plutinos in 3:2 resonance with Neptune.

Solar System models

The Nice model proposes that the giant planets formed in a compact configuration and later migrated after a dynamical instability, triggering the Late Heavy Bombardment and sculpting the Kuiper belt and asteroid belt. The Grand Tack hypothesis proposes that Jupiter migrated inward to about 1.5 AU before reversing course ("tacking") when Saturn locked into resonance with it, truncating the inner disk and explaining the small mass of Mars.

Other mechanisms

Additional mechanisms include planet–planet scattering (which can produce eccentric orbits), Kozai–Lidov oscillations combined with tidal friction (a route to hot Jupiters), and tidal migration of close-in planets over billions of years.
