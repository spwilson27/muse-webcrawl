Kaolin

Kaolin, also called china clay, is a soft, white clay composed chiefly of the mineral kaolinite. It is formed by the chemical weathering of aluminium silicate minerals such as feldspar. Kaolin has long been used in the manufacture of porcelain and fine china, and today it is also a major industrial material in paper coating, paints, rubber, plastics, cosmetics and pharmaceuticals.

Name and history

The name kaolin derives from Gaoling (高嶺, "high ridge"), a village near Jingdezhen in Jiangxi province, China, where the clay was mined for centuries for the imperial porcelain kilns. Samples were described to Europe in the early 18th century by the French Jesuit François Xavier d'Entrecolles, and the material gave its name to the mineral kaolinite.

Formation and deposits

Kaolin forms where feldspar-rich rocks such as granite are altered by hydrothermal fluids or by prolonged weathering under warm, wet conditions, a process called kaolinisation. Important deposits occur in Cornwall in England, in Georgia and South Carolina in the United States, in the Amazon basin of Brazil, and in Ukraine and China.

Production

Mined kaolin is refined by washing, settling, centrifuging and sometimes magnetic separation and chemical bleaching to remove coloured impurities such as iron oxides. The product is graded by particle size and brightness according to its intended use. World production amounts to tens of millions of tonnes per year.

Uses

Kaolin's largest traditional use is in ceramics: mixed with feldspar and quartz it forms porcelain, and it is also used in tableware, sanitary ware and electrical insulators. The paper industry uses fine kaolin as a coating pigment that gives paper a smooth, glossy, printable surface and as a filler. Other uses include paint extender, rubber and plastic filler, toothpaste abrasive, face masks and cosmetics, and — as kaolinite — a traditional anti-diarrhoeal medicine that adsorbs toxins in the gut.
