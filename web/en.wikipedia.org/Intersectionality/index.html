Intersectionality

Overlapping social identities in intersectional analysis
Term coined1989, by Kimberlé Crenshaw
FieldSociology, feminist theory, critical legal studies
Key texts"Demarginalizing the Intersection of Race and Sex" (1989); "Mapping the Margins" (1991)

Intersectionality is a sociological and feminist analytical framework for understanding how a person's various social and political identities — such as race, gender, class, sexuality, ability and age — combine to create unique modes of discrimination and privilege. The term was coined in 1989 by American civil-rights advocate and legal scholar Kimberlé Crenshaw, building on earlier work in Black feminist thought.

Origins

Crenshaw introduced the concept in her 1989 article "Demarginalizing the Intersection of Race and Sex", criticising anti-discrimination law and feminist and anti-racist politics for operating on a "single-axis framework". She illustrated the problem with cases such as DeGraffenreid v. General Motors (1976), in which Black women plaintiffs could not sue as a distinct class: discrimination claims had to be framed as sex discrimination (measured against white women) or race discrimination (measured against Black men), leaving their combined experience legally invisible. She used the metaphor of a traffic intersection: discrimination, like traffic, may flow in several directions at once. Precursors include Sojourner Truth's 1851 "Ain't I a Woman?" speech, Anna Julia Cooper, and the Combahee River Collective's 1977 statement on interlocking systems of oppression.

Development and applications

In "Mapping the Margins" (1991) Crenshaw extended the framework to structural, political and representational intersectionality. Sociologist Patricia Hill Collins developed the related concept of a "matrix of domination". The framework has been applied in public health, education, law, organisational diversity work and policy analysis, and is a core concept in gender studies; the UN and other bodies have used intersectional approaches to inequality. Scholars of intersectionality emphasise that the categories are not simply additive but mutually constitutive.

Criticism and debate

Intersectionality has been debated within and outside academia: some critics argue it encourages identity-based fragmentation or lacks methodological precision; others, including Crenshaw, respond to criticisms that it is primarily concerned with identity rather than with structures of power. Conservative commentators have criticised its influence in education and policy, while some scholars caution against reducing it to a checklist of identities.
