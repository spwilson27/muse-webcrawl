The Five Classics (Chinese: 五經; pinyin: Wǔjīng) are five pre-Qin dynasty Chinese texts traditionally attributed to, or edited by, Confucius and forming the core canon of Confucianism. Together with the Four Books they constituted the standard curriculum of Chinese education and the basis of the imperial examination system for over two thousand years.

The five texts

  • Classic of Poetry (Shijing): an anthology of 305 poems dating from the 11th to 7th centuries BCE, including folk songs, court odes, and dynastic hymns.
  • Book of Documents (Shujing or Shangshu): a collection of speeches, proclamations, and documents attributed to rulers and ministers of early China.
  • Book of Rites (Liji): a compilation describing the rituals, court ceremonies, and social norms of the Zhou dynasty.
  • I Ching (Yijing, "Book of Changes"): a divination manual built around 64 hexagrams, with layers of philosophical commentary (the "Ten Wings") traditionally attributed to Confucius.
  • Spring and Autumn Annals (Chunqiu): a terse chronicle of the state of Lu covering 722–481 BCE, traditionally held to encode Confucius's moral judgements in its precise word choices.

History

The texts survived — partly in reconstructed form — the Qin dynasty's burning of books in 213 BCE. Under Emperor Wu of Han, Confucianism became state ideology and chairs (boshi) were established for the study of each classic in 136 BCE, fixing the canonical five. Commentarial traditions accumulated for two millennia; the versions carved on the Xiping Stone Classics (175–183 CE) and printed in standard editions anchored scholarship, and mastery of the classics was the path to office through the imperial examinations until their abolition in 1905.

Influence

The Five Classics shaped Chinese political thought, historiography, ethics, and literary style, and were equally central to traditional education in Korea, Japan, and Vietnam. Modern scholarship has subjected their authorship and dating to critical scrutiny, particularly the "Old Text" chapters of the Book of Documents, shown in the Qing dynasty to be later forgeries.
