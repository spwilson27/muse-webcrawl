The Roman Inquisition, formally the Supreme Sacred Congregation of the Roman and Universal Inquisition — commonly called the Holy Office — was a system of tribunals established by the Catholic Church in 1542 to combat the spread of Protestantism and to police doctrine in Italy. Unlike the Spanish Inquisition, which answered to the Spanish Crown, the Roman Inquisition was directed from Rome by a congregation of cardinals. It operated in the Papal States, most Italian states, and Malta until the 18th–19th centuries, and its institutional successor survives today as the Dicastery for the Doctrine of the Faith.

Foundation and organisation

Pope Paul III created the Congregation of the Holy Office with the bull Licet ab initio on 21 July 1542, appointing six cardinals as inquisitors general — among them Gian Pietro Carafa, the future Pope Paul IV, a driving force for severity. Local tribunals staffed by inquisitors (often Dominicans) operated under Roman supervision, with standardised procedures and mandatory appeals to Rome, which made the Roman Inquisition comparatively bureaucratic and, by early-modern standards, procedurally restrained: death sentences were relatively rare, and torture was regulated by formal rules.

Activities

The tribunal's early targets were Italian evangelical and reform-minded circles; leading figures such as Bernardino Ochino and Peter Martyr Vermigli fled into exile. It policed books through the Index Librorum Prohibitorum (first issued 1559 under Paul IV; the Tridentine Index followed in 1564) and prosecuted offences including heresy, apostasy, blasphemy, witchcraft and "superstition," solicitation in the confessional, and prohibited reading.

Famous cases

  • Giordano Bruno — philosopher arrested in Venice in 1592, tried in Rome for heretical doctrines, and burned at the stake in Campo de' Fiori on 17 February 1600.
  • Galileo Galilei — summoned to Rome in 1632–33 after publishing the Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems; he abjured heliocentrism, was found "vehemently suspect of heresy," and spent the rest of his life under house arrest. Copernicus's De revolutionibus had been placed on the Index in 1616 pending correction.
  • The miller Menocchio (Domenico Scandella) — a Friulian miller with an unorthodox personal cosmology, tried twice and executed in 1599; his case, reconstructed by Carlo Ginzburg in The Cheese and the Worms, is a classic of microhistory.

Decline and legacy

Enlightenment-era rulers progressively restricted or abolished the tribunals (Tuscany abolished its inquisition in 1782; Napoleon suppressed several tribunals), and by the mid-19th century the Roman Inquisition's judicial activity had largely ceased, though the Holy Office continued as a doctrinal congregation. In 1908 it was renamed the Congregation of the Holy Office; in 1965 Paul VI reformed it as the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith, ending the Index. The Vatican opened the Inquisition archives to scholars in 1998, enabling modern assessments that distinguish its bureaucratic reality from both its apologetic and its "Black Legend" depictions.
