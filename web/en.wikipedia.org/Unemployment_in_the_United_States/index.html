Unemployment in the United States

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Unemployment in the United States is measured by the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS), which reports the unemployment rate monthly as part of the Current Population Survey. A person is counted as unemployed if they are jobless, available for work, and have actively looked for work in the previous four weeks. The headline rate (known as U-3) has averaged around 5 to 6 percent in recent decades, reaching a postwar high of 10.0 percent in October 2009 after the Great Recession, and a peak of 14.7 percent in April 2020 during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Definitions and measures

The BLS publishes six alternative measures of labour underutilisation, U-1 through U-6. U-6, the broadest, includes discouraged workers, marginally attached workers, and people employed part-time for economic reasons ("involuntary part-time" workers), and is consequently several points higher than the headline rate. People who have not searched for work in the preceding four weeks are not counted in the labour force at all.

History

National unemployment data collection began in earnest in the 1940s. The highest rate of the 20th century occurred during the Great Depression, when unemployment is estimated to have reached roughly 25 percent in 1933. Postwar unemployment was comparatively mild until the 1970s, when stagflation pushed rates above 9 percent in 1975 and again to 10.8 percent in 1982. The long expansions of the 1990s and 2010s brought rates down to 3.5 percent by 2019, a 50-year low, before the pandemic shock of 2020 produced the sharpest — and shortest — spike on record.

Unemployment insurance

Unemployment insurance is a joint federal–state programme, established by the Social Security Act of 1935, that provides temporary partial wage replacement to workers who lose their jobs through no fault of their own. Regular benefits are generally available for up to 26 weeks; in major downturns, Congress has repeatedly authorised extended federal benefits, most recently during the Great Recession and the COVID-19 pandemic.

Variation by group and region

Unemployment rates differ markedly by race, age, and education: rates for teenagers are typically two to three times the adult rate, and rates for Black and Hispanic workers are persistently higher than for white workers. Rates also vary by state and industry, rising most sharply in goods-producing sectors such as construction and manufacturing during recessions.
