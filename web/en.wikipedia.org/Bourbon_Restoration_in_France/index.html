The Bourbon Restoration was the period of French history from the fall of Napoleon Bonaparte in 1814 (briefly interrupted by the Hundred Days in 1815) to the July Revolution of 1830, during which the House of Bourbon again ruled France under Louis XVIII and Charles X. The restored monarchy was a constitutional one under the Charter of 1814, but it was marked by tension between liberal concessions and the reactionary ambitions of the ultra-royalists.

The first restoration and the Hundred Days

After Napoleon's abdication in April 1814, the Senate recalled Louis XVIII, brother of the executed Louis XVI. The Charter of 1814 preserved many revolutionary gains — legal equality, the Napoleonic Code, and the property of purchasers of national lands — while restoring the monarchy and a bicameral legislature. Napoleon's return from Elba in March 1815 forced Louis to flee to Ghent; after Waterloo, the king was restored a second time ("in the baggage train of the allies", as critics said), followed by the White Terror against Bonapartists and former revolutionaries in the south.

Louis XVIII and Charles X

Louis XVIII steered a moderate course, though the assassination of the duc de Berry in 1820 empowered the ultras led by his brother, the comte d'Artois. Succeeding as Charles X in 1824, the new king pursued reactionary policies: indemnifying émigrés for confiscated lands, passing an anti-sacrilege law, and dissolving the National Guard. His intervention in Spain (1823) and the capture of Algiers (1830) were foreign successes that could not offset domestic unpopularity.

Fall

In July 1830 Charles issued the July Ordinances, suspending press freedom and dissolving the newly elected chamber. Paris rose in the Trois Glorieuses (27–29 July); Charles abdicated, and the crown passed to his cousin Louis Philippe of the Orléans branch, inaugurating the July Monarchy. Talleyrand's gibe that the Bourbons had "learned nothing and forgotten nothing" became the era's epitaph.
