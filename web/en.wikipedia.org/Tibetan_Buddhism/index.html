Tibetan Buddhism is the form of Buddhism practised in Tibet, Mongolia, Bhutan and neighbouring Himalayan regions. It combines the essence of Mahayana Buddhism with Tantric and Shamanic influences, and preserves the Vajrayana teachings of eighth-century India. It is characterised by the institution of reincarnate lamas (tulkus), elaborate ritual, and a vast scholastic literature preserved in the Tibetan language.

History

Buddhism was first introduced to Tibet during the Tibetan Empire in the 7th century CE under King Songtsen Gampo, and was firmly established in the 8th century when the Indian masters Śāntarakṣita and Padmasambhava were invited to Tibet and the first monastery, Samye, was founded. After a period of suppression under Langdarma in the 9th century, a "later diffusion" from the 10th century onward revived monastic Buddhism, notably through the Bengali master Atiśa, whose arrival in 1042 reshaped Tibetan religious life.

Four major schools eventually emerged: the Nyingma ("ancient") school tracing itself to Padmasambhava; the Kagyu, founded by the translator Marpa and his disciple Milarepa; the Sakya, named for its monastery founded in 1073; and the Gelug, founded by Tsongkhapa in the early 15th century, whose head, the Dalai Lama, became Tibet's political as well as spiritual leader from the 17th century.

Teachings and practices

Tibetan Buddhism upholds the Mahayana goal of Buddhahood for the benefit of all beings, centred on the bodhisattva ideal and the philosophy of emptiness (śūnyatā). Distinctive practices include deity yoga, guru yoga, the recitation of mantras such as Om mani padme hum, the use of mandalas and thangka paintings, pilgrimage, prostrations, and prayer wheels. The Bardo Thodol ("Tibetan Book of the Dead") guides consciousness through the intermediate state after death.

Schools

  • Nyingma — the oldest school, emphasising the Dzogchen teachings.
  • Kagyu — the "oral lineage", known for Mahamudra meditation and the yogin Milarepa.
  • Sakya — noted for scholarship and the Lamdre teaching.
  • Gelug — the school of the Dalai Lamas, stressing monastic discipline and scholastic debate.
  • Jonang and Bon — smaller traditions, Bon predating Buddhism in Tibet.

Diaspora

Following the Chinese annexation of Tibet and the failed uprising of 1959, the 14th Dalai Lama fled to India, where a large exile community re-established the great monasteries in Karnataka and Himachal Pradesh. Tibetan Buddhism has since spread widely in the West.
