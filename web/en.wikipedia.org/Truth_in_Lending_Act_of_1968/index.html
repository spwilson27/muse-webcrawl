Truth in Lending Act of 1968

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Truth in Lending Act (TILA) of 1968 is a United States federal law designed to promote the informed use of consumer credit by requiring standardised disclosures about its terms and cost. It is Title I of the Consumer Credit Protection Act and is codified at 15 U.S.C. § 1601 et seq. The law does not regulate interest rates themselves; instead, it ensures that borrowers can compare credit offers on a common basis.

Key provisions

TILA requires creditors to disclose the annual percentage rate (APR), the finance charge, the total of payments, and the payment schedule before a consumer becomes obligated on a credit transaction. It grants borrowers a right of rescission — generally three business days — on loans secured by their principal dwelling, regulates credit card billing practices and advertising, and limits liability for unauthorised card use. Implementation is through Regulation Z, issued first by the Federal Reserve and, since 2011, by the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau.

Amendments

The Act has been amended many times, notably by the Fair Credit Billing Act (1974), the Truth in Lending Simplification and Reform Act (1980), the Home Ownership and Equity Protection Act (1994) against predatory mortgage lending, the Credit CARD Act (2009), and the Dodd–Frank Act (2010), which combined TILA and real-estate settlement disclosures into integrated mortgage forms.
