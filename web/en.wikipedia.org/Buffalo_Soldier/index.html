Buffalo Soldier

Buffalo Soldiers were United States Army regiments composed exclusively of African American soldiers, formed during the 19th century to serve on the American frontier. The nickname is said to have been given by Native American tribes, possibly comparing the soldiers' hair to the fur of the American bison, or honouring their ferocity in battle.

Origins

On 28 July 1866, Congress authorised six all-Black regiments, consolidated in 1869 into four: the 9th and 10th Cavalry and the 24th and 25th Infantry. Many recruits were veterans of the U.S. Colored Troops of the Civil War or men who had been enslaved. The regiments were led mostly by white officers, with notable exceptions such as Henry O. Flipper, the first Black graduate of West Point.

Service

Buffalo Soldiers fought in the Indian Wars, guarded settlers, stagecoaches and railroad crews, built forts and roads, and served as some of the first national park rangers in Yosemite and Sequoia. They fought in the Spanish-American War — the 10th Cavalry charged at San Juan Hill alongside the Rough Riders — and in the Philippine-American War, the Mexican Border campaign and both World Wars. Twenty-three Buffalo Soldiers received the Medal of Honor.

Legacy

The regiments were disbanded as the Army desegregated after President Truman's Executive Order 9981 (1948) and the Korean War. Their story is commemorated at Fort Leavenworth's Buffalo Soldier Monument, dedicated in 1992, in museums, and in popular culture — including Bob Marley's 1983 song "Buffalo Soldier".
