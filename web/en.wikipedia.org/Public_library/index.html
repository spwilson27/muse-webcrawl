Public library

A public library is a library that is accessible by the general public and is generally funded from public sources, such as taxes. It is operated by librarians and library paraprofessionals, who are also civil servants. Public libraries provide free access to books, periodicals, and increasingly to digital media, internet access, and community programming.

History

Precursors include subscription and circulating libraries of the 18th century and social libraries such as Benjamin Franklin's Library Company of Philadelphia (1731). The modern tax-supported public library emerged in the 19th century: the United Kingdom's Public Libraries Act 1850 enabled municipal libraries, and in the United States the Boston Public Library (opened 1854) became a landmark. Philanthropist Andrew Carnegie funded more than 2,500 "Carnegie libraries" worldwide in the late 19th and early 20th centuries.

Services

Core services include lending of books and media, reference assistance, children's services and storytimes, public computers and Wi-Fi, study space, local history collections, and programs from language classes to job-search help. Many libraries have added makerspaces, seed libraries, and lending of tools and instruments. Digital services include e-book lending and streaming media.

Role and debates

Public libraries are often described as pillars of democracy and equal opportunity, providing free information regardless of income. Contemporary debates include funding pressures, challenges to book collections and intellectual freedom, the digital divide, and the library's evolving role as a community hub and safe public space.
