Juan Manuel Fangio

Born: 24 June 1911, Balcarce, Argentina
Died: 17 July 1995 (aged 84), Buenos Aires, Argentina
Nationality: Argentine
Formula One: 5 World Championships (1951, 1954, 1955, 1956, 1957); 24 wins from 52 entries
Teams: Alfa Romeo, Maserati, Mercedes-Benz, Ferrari

Juan Manuel Fangio (24 June 1911 – 17 July 1995) was an Argentine racing driver who dominated the first decade of Formula One, winning the World Drivers' Championship five times. Nicknamed El Chueco ("the bowlegged one") and El Maestro ("The Master"), he holds records for the highest winning percentage in Formula One (46%) and remains the only driver to have won the championship with four different teams.

Early career

Fangio was born in Balcarce to Italian immigrants and left school to work as a mechanic, building his own cars and racing in brutal long-distance South American road races such as the Gran Premio del Norte in a modified Chevrolet. Backed after the war by the government of Juan Perón, he moved to Europe in 1948, quickly winning races in France and Italy and joining Alfa Romeo for the inaugural Formula One World Championship in 1950, finishing runner-up to teammate Giuseppe Farina.

Five championships

Fangio won his first title in 1951 with Alfa Romeo. A neck injury at Monza in 1952 sidelined him for a season, but he returned to win consecutive championships in 1954 (with Maserati and then Mercedes-Benz), 1955 (Mercedes), 1956 (Ferrari) and 1957 (Maserati). His victory at the 1957 German Grand Prix at the Nürburgring — recovering a huge deficit after a bungled pit stop to pass the Ferraris on the penultimate lap — is often called the greatest drive in Formula One history. He also won the 12 Hours of Sebring twice and endurance races across Europe.

Retirement and legacy

Fangio retired in 1958 after the French Grand Prix, having been kidnapped briefly by Fidel Castro's rebels before that year's Cuban Grand Prix (and released unharmed). He remained a revered elder statesman of motor racing, president of Mercedes-Benz Argentina, until his death in 1995. His record of five titles stood until Michael Schumacher surpassed it in 2003, and his combination of speed, mechanical sympathy and sportsmanship defines the heroic early era of Grand Prix racing.
