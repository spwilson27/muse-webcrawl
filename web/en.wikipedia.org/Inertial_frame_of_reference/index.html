Inertial frame of reference

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In classical physics and special relativity, an inertial frame of reference (also called an inertial reference frame, inertial frame, or Galilean reference frame) is a frame of reference in which Newton's first law of motion — the law of inertia — holds: a body with no net force acting on it moves with constant velocity (which may be zero) in a straight line. In such a frame, all of Newton's laws of motion take their simple standard form.

Definition

All inertial frames are in a state of constant, rectilinear motion with respect to one another; an accelerometer moving with any of them would detect zero proper acceleration. Any frame that moves with constant velocity relative to an inertial frame is itself inertial; any frame that accelerates or rotates relative to one is a non-inertial frame, in which fictitious forces (such as the centrifugal force and Coriolis force) must be introduced to apply Newton's laws.

Whether a given frame is truly inertial is ultimately an empirical question. A frame fixed to the surface of the Earth is only approximately inertial, because the Earth rotates and orbits the Sun; for most everyday purposes the approximation is excellent, though the rotation is detectable over time by instruments such as the Foucault pendulum. Frames fixed relative to the distant stars, or to the cosmic microwave background, provide better realisations.

Newtonian mechanics

In Newtonian mechanics, inertial frames are related by Galilean transformations, and there is no privileged inertial frame: the laws of mechanics are the same in all of them (Galilean relativity). Newton himself posited absolute space as the ultimate inertial frame, an idea criticised by Leibniz and later by Ernst Mach, who argued that inertia should arise from the distribution of matter in the universe ("Mach's principle").

Special relativity

Einstein's special theory of relativity (1905) rests on two postulates: the laws of physics are the same in all inertial frames, and the speed of light in vacuum is the same for all inertial observers. Inertial frames are then related by Lorentz transformations rather than Galilean ones, with consequences including time dilation, length contraction and the relativity of simultaneity. In special relativity there is no universal "now" and no absolute rest frame, but inertial frames remain fundamental as the frames in which the metric of flat spacetime takes the Minkowski form.

General relativity

In general relativity, gravity is described as spacetime curvature, and global inertial frames generally do not exist in curved spacetime. However, by the equivalence principle, a freely falling frame in a sufficiently small region of spacetime behaves as an inertial frame — locally, gravity is indistinguishable from its absence. Inertial frames are thus local approximations, precise in the limit of small regions and short times.
