Heparin

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Heparin, also known as unfractionated heparin (UFH), is a medication and naturally occurring glycosaminoglycan. As a drug it is a widely used anticoagulant ("blood thinner") that prevents the formation and extension of blood clots. It is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines. Unlike many anticoagulants, heparin must be given by injection or infusion because it is not absorbed from the gut.

Medical uses

Heparin is used to treat and prevent deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, and arterial thromboembolism; in acute coronary syndromes such as unstable angina and myocardial infarction; during cardiopulmonary bypass and haemodialysis; and to keep intravenous catheters patent. Because it does not cross the placenta, it is a preferred anticoagulant in pregnancy. Its effect is monitored with the activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) and can be rapidly reversed by protamine sulfate.

Mechanism of action

Heparin works by binding to and greatly accelerating the activity of antithrombin III, a natural inhibitor of clotting enzymes. The heparin–antithrombin complex inactivates thrombin (factor IIa) and factor Xa, among other proteases, halting the coagulation cascade. A specific pentasaccharide sequence within the heparin chain is responsible for antithrombin binding. Low-molecular-weight heparins (such as enoxaparin), produced by depolymerising UFH, act predominantly on factor Xa, have more predictable effects, and can often be given without laboratory monitoring; the synthetic pentasaccharide fondaparinux is a further refinement of the same idea.

Adverse effects

The chief risk is bleeding. A distinctive complication is heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT), an immune reaction in which antibodies against platelet factor 4–heparin complexes activate platelets, paradoxically causing thrombosis while platelet counts fall; affected patients must stop all heparin and receive alternative anticoagulants. Long-term use can contribute to osteoporosis and hyperkalaemia.

History and production

Heparin was discovered in 1916 by Jay McLean, a medical student working under William Henry Howell at Johns Hopkins, and named from the Greek hēpar (liver), the tissue from which it was first isolated in quantity. Clinical use began in the 1930s following purification work in Toronto by Charles Best and colleagues. Pharmaceutical heparin is extracted from porcine intestinal mucosa (and formerly bovine lung); a contamination crisis in 2008, traced to oversulfated chondroitin sulfate in the supply chain, caused deaths and led to tighter testing.
