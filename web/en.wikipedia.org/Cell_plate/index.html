Cell plate

The cell plate is a structure that forms in plant cells during cell division (cytokinesis), specifically during telophase of mitosis. It is the precursor to the new cell wall that will separate the two daughter cells. The formation of the cell plate is a distinguishing feature of plant cell division, contrasting with the cleavage furrow mechanism seen in animal cells.

Formation

During telophase, remnants of the mitotic spindle form a structure called the phragmoplast, which serves as a scaffold for cell plate assembly. Golgi-derived vesicles carrying cell wall materials (such as pectin, hemicellulose and glycoproteins) are transported along phragmoplast microtubules to the cell equator, where they fuse to form a disc-like structure. The cell plate grows outward from the center (centrifugally) until it fuses with the parent cell wall at predetermined attachment sites.

Maturation

As the plate matures, the vesicle membranes become the plasma membranes of the two daughter cells, and callose is deposited and later replaced partially by cellulose, converting the young plate into a primary cell wall with a middle lamella. Plasmodesmata may form across the plate, maintaining cytoplasmic continuity between the daughters.
