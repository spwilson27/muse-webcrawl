Cinema of Quebec

The cinema of Quebec is the film industry of the Canadian province of Quebec. It is distinct within Canadian cinema for its predominantly French-language production, its close relationship to Quebec's national identity, and its unusually strong domestic box-office performance within the province. Major centres of production are in Montreal, home to the National Film Board of Canada.

History

Film production in Quebec began in the early twentieth century with newsreels and the pioneering work of figures such as Léo-Ernest Ouimet. Through the first half of the century the industry was constrained by the Catholic Church's influence over exhibition and by competition from Hollywood. The founding of the National Film Board in 1939, and especially its French Unit in the 1960s, nurtured the cinéma direct movement of directors like Michel Brault, Gilles Groulx, and Pierre Perrault.

The Quiet Revolution and after

The Quiet Revolution of the 1960s gave Quebec cinema a new cultural confidence. Films such as Mon oncle Antoine (1971) by Claude Jutra are regarded as classics. Denys Arcand's The Decline of the American Empire (1986) and The Barbarian Invasions (2003) — the latter winning the Academy Award for Best Foreign Language Film — brought international acclaim.

Contemporary cinema

Twenty-first-century Quebec directors including Denis Villeneuve (Incendies, 2010), Jean-Marc Vallée ( C.R.A.Z.Y., 2005), Xavier Dolan (Mommy, 2014), and Philippe Falardeau (Monsieur Lazhar, 2011) achieved major festival success. Quebec films regularly capture a far larger share of their home market than English-Canadian films do in theirs, supported by provincial funding agencies such as SODEC and the Prix Iris awards.
