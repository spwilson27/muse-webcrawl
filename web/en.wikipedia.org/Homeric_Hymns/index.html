Homeric Hymns

The Homeric Hymns (Ancient Greek: Ὁμηρικοὶ ὕμνοι, Homērikoi hymnoi) are a collection of thirty-three ancient Greek hymns and four epigrams celebrating the Olympian gods and other divine figures. The hymns are "Homeric" in the sense that they employ the same epic dialect and dactylic hexameter meter as the Iliad and Odyssey; in antiquity they were often attributed to Homer himself, though scholars now date them to various authors between roughly the 7th century BC and later periods.

Contents

The longest and most famous hymns are the Hymn to Demeter, which recounts the abduction of Persephone and the founding of the Eleusinian Mysteries; the Hymn to Apollo, describing the god's birth on Delos and his establishment of the oracle at Delphi; the Hymn to Hermes, a playful account of the infant god stealing Apollo's cattle and inventing the lyre; the Hymn to Aphrodite; and the Hymn to Dionysus. Many of the shorter hymns are only a few lines long and probably served as preludes (prooimia) with which rhapsodes introduced epic recitations.

Transmission

The hymns survive in medieval manuscripts alongside other works attributed to Homer. The ancient historian Thucydides quoted the Hymn to Apollo as a work of Homer, an attribution that long lent the collection authority. Modern critical editions and translations have made the hymns an important source for Greek religion and myth.
