Girard Desargues (21 February 1591 – September 1661) was a French mathematician and engineer, regarded as one of the founders of projective geometry. His name is attached to Desargues's theorem, the Desargues graph, and the lunar crater Desargues. He also invented the term "involution" in geometry, devised an improved design for water pumps, and wrote on practical applications of perspective for stonecutters, carpenters, and architects.

Life

Desargues was born in Lyon into a family in royal service. He worked in Paris as an engineer and architect, may have served as a military engineer at the siege of La Rochelle, and moved in the circle of Marin Mersenne alongside Descartes, Pascal, and Fermat. He later returned to Lyon, where he died.

Work

Desargues's principal mathematical work, the Rough draft of an essay on the results of taking plane sections of a cone (1639), developed a unified treatment of conic sections using projective methods, including points at infinity for parallel lines, the pole and polar relationship, and involution. His style was notoriously idiosyncratic — he coined botanical terms for geometric objects — and the book, printed in only a small number of copies, was almost entirely lost until a copy was rediscovered in the nineteenth century.

Desargues's theorem states that if two triangles are in perspective from a point, then they are in perspective from a line, and vice versa. It is a foundational result of projective geometry, though Desargues's own presentation of it is embedded in his work on conics, and his immediate influence ran largely through his pupil Blaise Pascal and the engraver Abraham Bosse, whose treatise on Desargues's perspective methods provoked controversy with the Paris Academy.
