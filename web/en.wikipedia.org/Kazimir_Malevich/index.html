Kazimir Severinovich Malevich (23 February 1879 – 15 May 1935) was a Russian avant-garde artist and art theorist whose pioneering work and writing influenced the development of abstract art in the 20th century. Born in Kiev to parents of Polish descent, he founded Suprematism in 1915, one of the earliest and most radical movements of purely abstract art, centred on basic geometric forms — above all the square — painted in a limited range of colours.

Early career

Malevich studied drawing in Kiev and later in Moscow, working through Impressionist, Symbolist, Fauvist, and Cubist styles. By 1912–13 he was a leading figure of the Russian avant-garde, exhibiting with the Donkey's Tail and Union of Youth groups and designing the sets and costumes for the Futurist opera Victory over the Sun (1913), in which a black square first appeared as a stage element.

Suprematism

At the "Last Futurist Exhibition 0,10" in Petrograd in December 1915, Malevich hung 39 abstract works, placing the Black Square across the corner of the room — the position reserved in Russian homes for religious icons. His manifesto From Cubism and Futurism to Suprematism proclaimed "the supremacy of pure feeling" over depiction of the visible world. Works such as Black Cross, Black Circle, and White on White (1918) pushed painting toward what he called the "zero of form". After the Revolution he taught in Vitebsk, where Marc Chagall was displaced by his UNOVIS group, and in Petrograd/Leningrad, extending Suprematism into architecture, design, and pedagogy.

Later years and legacy

Under Stalin, abstract art fell from favour in favour of Socialist Realism. Malevich travelled to Germany in 1927, leaving works with the Bauhaus circle; on his return he was interrogated and increasingly marginalised, and he returned to figurative painting in his final years. He died of cancer in Leningrad in 1935, his coffin decorated with Suprematist motifs. Rediscovered in the West after the Second World War, his work became foundational for minimalism and conceptual art; his paintings now command among the highest prices for Russian art — Suprematist Composition sold for $85.8 million in 2018.
