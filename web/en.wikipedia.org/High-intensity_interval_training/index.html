High-intensity interval training

High-intensity interval training (HIIT) is a training protocol alternating short periods of intense or explosive anaerobic exercise with brief recovery periods until the point of exhaustion. HIIT involves exercises performed in repeated quick bursts at maximum or near-maximum effort with periods of rest or low activity between bouts. Sessions typically last under 30 minutes, although durations vary, and the format is used in both aerobic (cardio) and resistance training.

Procedure

HIIT sessions generally consist of a warm-up period followed by repetitions of high-intensity work separated by medium-intensity recovery. A well-known research protocol is the Tabata method, developed by Japanese scientist Izumi Tabata: eight rounds of 20 seconds of ultra-intense exercise at about 170% of VO2max separated by 10 seconds of rest. Other formats include the Gibala regimen and sprint interval training (SIT).

Effects

Research indicates HIIT can produce improvements in VO2max, insulin sensitivity, and fat oxidation comparable or superior to moderate-intensity continuous training, in less time. An "afterburn" effect (excess post-exercise oxygen consumption, EPOC) elevates metabolic rate after the session. Because of its intensity, HIIT is demanding and may carry a higher risk of injury or overtraining for beginners, and medical caution is advised for people with cardiovascular disease.
