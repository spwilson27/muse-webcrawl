Stability theory

Stability theory addresses the stability of solutions of differential equations and of trajectories of dynamical systems under small perturbations of initial conditions. The heat equation, for example, is a stable partial differential equation because small perturbations of initial data lead to small variations in temperature at a later time as a result of the maximum principle. In partial differential equations one may measure the distances between functions using Lp norms or the sup norm, giving different notions of stability.

In dynamical systems, an orbit is said to be Lyapunov stable if the forward orbit of any point is in a small enough neighborhood of the given orbit. Various criteria have been developed to prove stability or instability of an orbit. In important cases, the problem may be reduced to a well-studied question in linear algebra.

Overview

Many parts of the qualitative theory of differential equations and dynamical systems deal with asymptotic properties of solutions and the trajectories: what happens to the system after a long period of time. The simplest kind of behavior is an equilibrium point, or fixed point, and then periodic orbits. If a particular orbit is well understood, it is natural to ask whether a nearby orbit changes in a similar manner. Stability theory asks whether a nearby solution will stay close to the given solution and even converge to it.

Stability of fixed points

The simplest kind of an orbit is a fixed point, or an equilibrium point. If a mechanical system is in a stable equilibrium state then a small push will result in a localized motion, for example small oscillations as in the case of a pendulum. In a damped system, an equilibrium state is furthermore asymptotically stable. On the other hand, for an unstable equilibrium, such as a ball resting on top of a hill, certain small pushes will result in a state of large amplitude motion that may or may not converge to the original state.

There are useful tests of stability for linear and nonlinear systems. Linear stability analysis applies to a linearization about the equilibrium. Let A be the Jacobian matrix of a nonlinear differential system at an equilibrium point. Then the equilibrium is asymptotically stable if all eigenvalues of A have negative real parts; it is unstable if at least one eigenvalue has a positive real part; and if eigenvalues lie on the imaginary axis, other tests must be applied.

Lyapunov stability

The notion of Lyapunov stability, developed by the Russian mathematician Aleksandr Lyapunov in the 1890s, considers the behavior of solutions near an equilibrium without requiring knowledge of the exact solutions. A solution is Lyapunov stable if solutions that start close to it remain close for all future time. Lyapunov's second method, based on the construction of a Lyapunov function whose value decreases along trajectories, gives a powerful criterion for proving stability, and is a cornerstone of modern control theory. Stability theory is closely connected with control theory and the theory of dynamical systems.
