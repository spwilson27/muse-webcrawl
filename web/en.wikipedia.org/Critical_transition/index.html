Critical transition

Critical transitions are abrupt shifts in the state of a complex system — such as an ecosystem, the climate, a financial market, or a physiological system — that occur when conditions cross a threshold or tipping point. Unlike gradual responses to gradual change, a critical transition can be large, sudden, and difficult or impossible to reverse, because the system reorganises around a different stable state.

Mechanism

Many critical transitions are explained by bifurcation theory: as a control parameter changes, the system's current stable state loses stability and the system jumps to an alternative state. Feedback loops can then hold the system in its new state even if conditions partly recover, a phenomenon known as hysteresis. Examples include the eutrophication of shallow lakes, the collapse of fisheries, desertification, epileptic seizures, and shifts in ocean circulation.

Early warning signals

Research led by Marten Scheffer and others has shown that systems approaching a critical transition often recover more slowly from small disturbances — "critical slowing down" — producing measurable statistical signals such as increased variance and autocorrelation in time-series data. Detecting such early warning signals is an active area of research aimed at anticipating collapses in ecosystems, climate subsystems, and other complex systems.
