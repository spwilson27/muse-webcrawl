The imperial cult of ancient Rome identified emperors and some members of their families with the divinely sanctioned authority of the Roman State. Emerging under Augustus, it offered a common focus of loyalty and religious observance across a vast, culturally diverse empire, blending ruler worship, traditional Roman religion and expressions of civic allegiance.

Origins

Ruler cults were familiar in the Hellenistic kingdoms and in Rome's own traditions of honouring the genius (guardian spirit) of the head of a household and the deified founders of cities. After the assassination of Julius Caesar, a comet seen during games in his honour was interpreted as his soul ascending to heaven, and the Senate formally deified him as Divus Julius in 42 BC — giving his heir Octavian the potent title divi filius, "son of the god".

Under Augustus

Augustus carefully calibrated the cult. In Rome itself he refused worship of his person while alive, but permitted temples to "Rome and Augustus" in the provinces, and encouraged cult of his genius and of the Lares Augusti in neighbourhood shrines. Deceased emperors of good reputation could be voted divine honours (apotheosis) by the Senate; emperors judged tyrants instead suffered damnatio memoriae, the erasure of their memory.

Function and practice

The cult was administered locally by priests — notably the seviri Augustales, often wealthy freedmen for whom the office was a route to status — and by provincial assemblies that maintained great temples and festivals. Participation was a public demonstration of loyalty to Rome. This made refusal conspicuous: Jews obtained exemption, but Christians, who would sacrifice to no god but their own, periodically faced persecution when authorities demanded the test of offering wine and incense before the emperor's image.

Decline

The cult faded during the 4th century as Christianity became first tolerated, then favoured, then the state religion. The emperor's religious role was transformed rather than abolished: Christian emperors ruled as God's vicegerents, but were no longer themselves worshipped as gods.
