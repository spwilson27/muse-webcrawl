Three-act structure

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The three-act structure is a model used in narrative fiction and screenwriting that divides a story into three parts, or acts: the setup, the confrontation, and the resolution. The model was popularised for screenwriters by Syd Field in his 1979 book Screenplay: The Foundations of Screenwriting, and it has roots in classical dramatic theory going back to Aristotle's Poetics, which held that a whole story has a beginning, a middle, and an end.

The acts

Act One (Setup) establishes the main characters, their world, and the story's central conflict. It typically ends with a "plot point" — an inciting incident that locks the protagonist into the action. In a feature film this act usually runs about the first quarter of the running time.

Act Two (Confrontation) is the longest section. The protagonist attempts to solve the problem posed by the inciting incident but meets escalating obstacles; circumstances grow worse, and the character must develop new skills or self-knowledge. A midpoint reversal and a "dark night" low point are common features. It ends with a second plot point that brings the story to crisis.

Act Three (Resolution) contains the climax — the most intense point of the story, in which the central question is answered — followed by a falling action and conclusion that resolve the remaining subplots.

Criticism and alternatives

Many writers and theorists caution that the model is descriptive rather than prescriptive, and that strict adherence can produce formulaic stories. Alternative frameworks include the hero's journey, Kishōtenketsu (a four-part East Asian structure), five-act structure in classical drama, and sequence approaches used in television writing.
