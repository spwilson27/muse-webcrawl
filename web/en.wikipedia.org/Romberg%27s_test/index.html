In Romberg's test the patient stands with feet together, first with eyes open, then closed.

Romberg's test, also called the Romberg sign, is a bedside neurological examination used to assess a patient's sense of balance, specifically the integrity of proprioception and the dorsal columns of the spinal cord. It was described by the German neurologist Moritz Heinrich Romberg in the 1840s–1850s in connection with tabes dorsalis.

Procedure

The patient is asked to stand with feet together and arms at the sides or folded across the chest, first with the eyes open and then with the eyes closed, for up to about a minute. The examiner stands close by, ready to catch the patient if they fall. A normal person sways slightly but remains upright in both conditions.

Interpretation

The test is positive if the patient is steady with the eyes open but loses balance when the eyes are closed. This indicates that balance is being maintained by vision because proprioceptive input — from the dorsal columns, which carry vibration and joint-position sense — is impaired. Causes include vitamin B12 deficiency (subacute combined degeneration), tabes dorsalis (neurosyphilis), diabetic neuropathy and other sensory neuropathies. By contrast, a patient with cerebellar ataxia is unsteady even with the eyes open, so eye closure does not markedly worsen performance — a "negative" Romberg in someone who is nevertheless ataxic.

Variants

The sharpened or tandem Romberg test, in which the patient stands heel-to-toe, is more sensitive and is used in assessing vestibular problems, chronic alcohol misuse and, in a modified form, in field sobriety testing by police.
