Dow Jones Industrial Average

The Dow Jones Industrial Average, 1896–present (log scale)

The Dow Jones Industrial Average (DJIA), commonly known as the Dow, is a stock market index of 30 prominent companies listed on stock exchanges in the United States. It is one of the oldest and most widely followed equity indices, created by Charles Dow, editor of The Wall Street Journal, and his business associate Edward Jones; it was first published on May 26, 1896.

Calculation

The DJIA is a price-weighted index: its value is the sum of the prices of the component stocks divided by the Dow divisor, a figure adjusted for stock splits, spin-offs, and changes in composition. Because of price weighting, higher-priced stocks exert more influence on the index regardless of company size.

Components

The 30 components are large “blue-chip” companies spanning most industrial sectors, including technology, finance, healthcare, and consumer goods. Composition is chosen by a committee and changes infrequently; long-serving members have included firms such as Boeing, Coca-Cola, IBM, and Microsoft.

Criticism

Financial analysts note that with only 30 stocks and price weighting, the Dow is a narrower and less representative measure of the overall market than broader market-capitalization-weighted indices such as the S&P 500, though it remains a popular barometer in news media.
