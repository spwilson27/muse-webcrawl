Mount Elbrus is the highest mountain in Russia and in Europe, a dormant stratovolcano in the western Caucasus near the border with Georgia. Its western summit rises to 5,642 metres (18,510 ft), making Elbrus the tenth-most prominent peak in the world and the highest of the Seven Summits in Europe. The eastern summit is slightly lower, at 5,621 metres, and the two are joined by a saddle carrying one of the highest mountain refuges in Europe.

Geography and climate

Elbrus stands about 10 kilometres north of the main Caucasus watershed, in the republics of Kabardino-Balkaria and Karachay-Cherkessia. The peak is heavily glaciated: some twenty glaciers feed the valleys below, and the standard climbing route from the south follows cable cars and chairlifts from the Baksan valley to refuges above 4,000 metres. The weather is notoriously changeable, and the summit is frequently in cloud even when the lower mountain is clear.

Climbing history

The lower eastern summit was first climbed in 1829 by Killar Khashirov, a Karachay guide with a Russian military expedition; the higher western summit was reached in 1874 by a party led by Florence Crauford Grove with guide Ahiya Sottaiev. During the Second World War, German mountain troops occupied the area in 1942 and planted flags on the summit. Elbrus is now climbed by thousands of people each year, though altitude, weather and crevasses still cause regular fatalities.

In culture

In Greek mythology the Caucasus was the place of Prometheus's punishment, and Elbrus is sometimes named as the mountain to which he was chained. The peak also gives its name to the Russian Elbrus family of computers and processors.
