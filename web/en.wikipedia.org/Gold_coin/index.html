Gold coin

A gold coin is a coin made mostly or entirely of gold. Most gold coins minted since 1800 are 90–92% gold (22 karat), while most of today's gold bullion coins are pure gold, such as the Britannia, Canadian Maple Leaf, and American Buffalo. Alloyed gold coins, like the American Gold Eagle and South African Krugerrand, are typically 91.7% gold by weight, with the remainder being silver and copper.

History

Gold was first used for coinage in the kingdom of Lydia in the 7th century BC, initially in the form of electrum, a natural alloy of gold and silver. Pure gold coins were issued under King Croesus. Gold coinage spread through the Greek and Roman worlds — the Roman aureus and later the solidus of Constantinople, which remained a standard of international trade for centuries.

Through the medieval and early modern periods, coins such as the Venetian ducat, the Spanish doubloon and the British sovereign circulated widely. In the 19th and early 20th centuries, under the classical gold standard, gold coins formed the everyday currency of many nations. Most countries abandoned circulating gold coinage during the upheavals of the First World War and the Great Depression; the United States ended domestic gold coin circulation in 1933.

Bullion coins

Modern gold coins are produced mainly as bullion for investors and as commemorative issues for collectors. Their market value is closely tied to the spot price of gold, plus a minting premium. Legal-tender status is usually nominal relative to their metal value.

Collecting

Rare and historically significant gold coins can command prices far above their bullion value. One of the most famous is the 1933 American double eagle, an example of which sold at auction in 2021 for $18.9 million, making it the most valuable coin ever sold at the time.
