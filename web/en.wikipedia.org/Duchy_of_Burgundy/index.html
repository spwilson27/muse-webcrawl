Duchy of Burgundy

The Burgundian lands under Charles the Bold, 1477 (placeholder image)

The Duchy of Burgundy (French: Duché de Bourgogne) was a medieval kingdom-turned-duchy in eastern France, centred on Dijon. From 1003 it was held by a cadet branch of the French Capetian dynasty, and from 1363 by the Valois dukes, under whom the duchy became the nucleus of the powerful Burgundian State stretching into the Low Countries.

Early history

The duchy emerged from the former Kingdom of the Burgundians and the Frankish kingdom of Burgundy. In 1002–1032 the duchy was contested and then granted to Robert, son of King Robert II of France, founding the Capetian line of dukes, the House of Burgundy. The dukes were among the great peers of France, and the duchy prospered on agriculture and the wine trade.

Valois Burgundy

When the Capetian line died out in 1361, King John II granted the duchy in 1363 to his son Philip the Bold. Through marriage and acquisition, Philip and his successors — John the Fearless, Philip the Good and Charles the Bold — built a great territorial bloc including Flanders, Brabant, Holland and Luxembourg. The Burgundian court at Dijon and Bruges became a centre of art, chivalry and early Renaissance culture, rivalling France itself.

End of the duchy

Charles the Bold's ambitions collapsed with his death at the Battle of Nancy in 1477. Louis XI of France seized the duchy proper, while Charles's daughter Mary retained the Burgundian Netherlands, which passed to the Habsburgs through her marriage to Maximilian of Austria. The duchy thereafter remained a French royal province until the Revolution.
