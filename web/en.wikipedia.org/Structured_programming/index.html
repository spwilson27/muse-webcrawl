Structured programming

Structured programming is a programming paradigm aimed at improving the clarity, quality, and development time of a computer program by making extensive use of the structured control flow constructs of selection (if/then/else) and repetition (while and for), block structures, and subroutines, in contrast to using simple tests and jumps such as the go to statement, which can lead to "spaghetti code" that is difficult to follow and maintain.

The structured program theorem of Corrado Böhm and Giuseppe Jacopini (1966) proved that sequence, selection, and iteration suffice to express any computable function. Edsger W. Dijkstra's 1968 letter "Go To Statement Considered Harmful" popularized the discipline, and languages such as ALGOL, Pascal, and C embodied structured constructs; methodologies for top-down design and stepwise refinement by Niklaus Wirth and others followed.

Elements

Control structures combine statements into blocks with a single entry and single exit. Emergent results include clearer program proofs, easier debugging, and modular decomposition into procedures and, later, objects. Exceptions, early returns, and multiple exits are pragmatic deviations debated since the classic literature; constructs such as break and continue remain common accommodations.

Influence

Structured programming laid groundwork for later paradigms — procedural, modular, and object-oriented programming — and remains taught as the foundation of control flow in essentially all high-level languages.
