Uluru-Kata Tjuta National Park

Uluṟu-Kata Tjuṯa National Park is a protected area in the Northern Territory of Australia, around 1,431 kilometres south of Darwin and 440 kilometres south-west of Alice Springs. It covers 1,326 square kilometres and contains two of Australia's most famous natural landmarks, Uluṟu (Ayers Rock) and Kata Tjuṯa (the Olgas), enormous rock formations of deep significance to the Aṉangu people, the Aboriginal traditional owners of the land.

History

The park was first declared a national park in 1958 as Ayers Rock–Mount Olga National Park. In 1985 the title to the land was handed back to the Aṉangu, who lease it back to Parks Australia; the park is jointly managed by a board with an Aṉangu majority. The park was listed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1987 for its natural values, and its listing was extended in 1994 to recognise its cultural landscape values as well.

Features

Uluṟu is a massive sandstone monolith rising 348 metres above the surrounding plain, sacred to Aṉangu culture. Kata Tjuṯa, about 25 kilometres to the west, consists of 36 domed rock formations. The climb of Uluṟu was permanently closed on 26 October 2019 at the request of the traditional owners. The park supports distinctive desert flora and fauna adapted to the arid central Australian environment.
