Liberal arts education

A liberal arts education is a philosophy of learning that emphasises broad general knowledge and intellectual skills — critical thinking, communication and problem-solving — over narrow vocational or technical training. The term comes from the Latin artes liberales, the arts considered worthy of a free person (liber) in classical antiquity.

Historical roots

In the medieval university the liberal arts comprised seven subjects divided into the trivium — grammar, rhetoric and logic — and the quadrivium — arithmetic, geometry, music and astronomy. They formed the foundation on which students proceeded to philosophy, law, medicine and theology. The tradition descends from the Greek idea of paideia and the Roman humanitas, and was revived in Renaissance humanism.

Modern practice

Today the liberal arts encompass the humanities, social sciences, natural sciences and formal sciences studied in breadth. In the United States the model is embodied by liberal arts colleges — small, residential, undergraduate institutions such as Williams, Amherst and Swarthmore — and by general-education requirements at larger universities. Similar ideals appear in the German concept of Bildung and, more recently, in new liberal arts universities in Europe and Asia.

Defenders argue that a liberal education prepares citizens for democratic life and workers for a changing economy; critics question its cost and job-market relevance, and enrolments in humanities subjects have declined in many countries in favour of business and STEM fields.
