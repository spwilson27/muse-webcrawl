Sycophancy

Sycophancy
Meaning: Insincere, excessive flattery
Greek origin: sykophantēs
Synonyms: Toadyism, obsequiousness

Sycophancy is insincere flattery given to gain advantage, typically directed at someone powerful or wealthy. A sycophant — colloquially a "toady," "brown-noser," or "yes-man" — praises and obeys not from genuine admiration but from self-interest.

Etymology and history

The word derives from the ancient Greek sykophantēs, which in classical Athens denoted an informer or vexatious prosecutor who brought accusations for profit. In later Greek and then in Latin and English, the meaning shifted toward the fawning flatterer of the powerful, the sense dominant today. Sycophants are recurring figures in literature and political satire, from Roman comedy to Shakespeare's courtiers.

Psychology

Research in social psychology links sycophancy to power asymmetries: subordinates flatter superiors because those superiors control valued outcomes. Flattery often works even when recognized as strategic, since recipients enjoy praise and discount the flatterer's motives. In modern discussions, the term is also applied to artificial intelligence systems that agree with users' views rather than answering accurately.
