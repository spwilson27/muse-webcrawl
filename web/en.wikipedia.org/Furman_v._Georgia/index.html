Furman v. Georgia

Furman v. Georgia, 408 U.S. 238 (1972), was a landmark criminal case in which the United States Supreme Court invalidated all then-existing legal constructions for the death penalty in the United States. In a 5–4 per curiam decision, the Court held that the discretionary capital sentencing statutes under review violated the Eighth and Fourteenth Amendments' prohibition of cruel and unusual punishment because they produced arbitrary and racially discriminatory results.

Background

William Henry Furman had been convicted of felony murder in Georgia after a homeowner was shot during a burglary. His case was consolidated with two others, Jackson v. Georgia and Branch v. Texas. Each justice wrote a separate opinion — the longest set of opinions in the Court's history to that point. Justices Brennan and Marshall argued that capital punishment was unconstitutional in all circumstances; Justices Douglas, Stewart, and White rested on the arbitrary, capricious, and discriminatory manner in which death sentences were imposed under statutes giving juries unguided discretion.

Effect

The decision effectively suspended the death penalty nationwide: the sentences of more than 600 people on death row were vacated, and executions halted until states rewrote their statutes. Many states responded with mandatory death penalty laws or "guided discretion" statutes providing bifurcated trials and enumerated aggravating circumstances.

Aftermath

In Gregg v. Georgia (1976), the Court upheld Georgia's new guided-discretion statute, holding that the death penalty is not inherently cruel and unusual, and executions resumed in 1977. Furman thus produced a four-year national moratorium and reshaped American capital punishment law, while debates over arbitrariness and racial disparity that animated the case continue in later litigation such as McCleskey v. Kemp (1987).
