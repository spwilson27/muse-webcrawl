Orogeny

Folded and uplifted strata record the compression of an orogenic belt.

Orogeny is the process of mountain building, in which the Earth's lithosphere is shortened, thickened, and deformed by tectonic forces, producing a mountain range or orogenic belt. The word derives from the Greek óros (mountain) and génesis (creation). Orogeny is one of the principal ways in which continental crust is created and reworked.

Processes

Most orogenies occur at convergent plate boundaries. Where oceanic lithosphere subducts beneath a continent, as in the Andes, compression, magmatism, and accretion of terranes build a cordillera. Where two continents collide — the Himalayas, raised by the collision of India with Asia, are the archetype — subduction ceases and the crust crumples into folds and thrust faults, thickening to 70 kilometres or more. Rocks caught in mountain building are folded, faulted, metamorphosed, and intruded by granite, then exposed by erosion.

Major orogenies

Geologists name individual orogenic episodes: the Caledonian and Variscan (Hercynian) orogenies built mountains across Europe and North America in the Palaeozoic; the Alpine orogeny, still active, raised the Alps, Zagros, and Himalayas; and the Laramide orogeny shaped the Rocky Mountains. Ancient orogenic belts, long since eroded to their roots, record the assembly of past supercontinents.

Orogeny operates over tens to hundreds of millions of years, and erosion works against it throughout, so mountain height reflects a balance between tectonic uplift and denudation.
