Torsion bar suspension

Diagram of a torsion bar suspension

A torsion bar suspension, also known as a torsion-spring suspension, is a vehicle suspension system that uses a torsion bar as its main weight-bearing spring. One end of a long metal bar is fixed to the vehicle chassis while the other end is attached to a lever arm connected to the wheel; vertical motion of the wheel twists the bar about its axis, and the bar's resistance to twisting provides the spring action.

Principles

The effective spring rate depends on the bar's length, diameter, geometry and the elastic modulus of its material, usually steel. Because the bars can be mounted longitudinally or transversely and tucked along the chassis, the system allows a compact layout and easy adjustment of ride height — turning a bolt preloads the bar to raise or lower the vehicle.

Applications

Torsion bars were widely used in military vehicles of the Second World War era, including many German and Soviet tanks, because they gave large wheel travel and good cross-country performance. In automobiles they featured prominently in the Volkswagen Beetle, the Porsche 911, the Chrysler products of the 1950s–1980s, and many pickup trucks and SUVs, where front torsion bars remained common into the 21st century.

Advantages and disadvantages

Advantages include durability, compact packaging and adjustable ride height. Disadvantages include a sometimes harsh ride, limited progressiveness of the spring rate, and difficulty of use with certain independent suspension layouts compared with coil springs.
