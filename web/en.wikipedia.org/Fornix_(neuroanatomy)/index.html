Fornix (neuroanatomy)

The fornix, the major output tract of the hippocampus.

The fornix (from Latin fornix, "arch") is a C-shaped bundle of nerve fibres (axons) in the brain that acts as the major output tract of the hippocampus. It carries signals from the hippocampus to the mammillary bodies and septal nuclei, and forms a central component of the limbic system and of the extended hippocampal memory circuit.

Structure

The fibres begin in the hippocampus on each side of the brain as the alveus, collect into the fimbria, and continue as the paired crura of the fornix. The crura arch forward and meet beneath the corpus callosum to form the body of the fornix; at the front, near the anterior commissure, the bundle divides into columns. Fibres passing in front of the anterior commissure (precommissural fornix) project mainly to the septal nuclei and nucleus accumbens, while those passing behind it (postcommissural fornix) descend to the mammillary bodies of the hypothalamus.

Function

Through its projection to the mammillary bodies, the fornix links the hippocampus to the anterior thalamic nuclei and cingulate cortex — the circuit described by James Papez in 1937. This circuitry is essential for episodic memory and spatial navigation.

Clinical significance

Damage to the fornix, from tumours such as colloid cysts of the third ventricle, surgery, trauma or degeneration, can produce severe anterograde amnesia resembling that of medial temporal lobe damage. Degeneration of the fornix is among the earliest detectable changes in Alzheimer's disease, and diffusion tensor imaging of the fornix is used as a biomarker in research. Deep brain stimulation of the fornix has been trialled as a treatment for Alzheimer's disease.
