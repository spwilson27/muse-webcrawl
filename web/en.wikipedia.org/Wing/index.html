Wing

A wing is a type of fin that produces lift while moving through air or some other fluid. Accordingly, wings have streamlined cross-sections that are subject to aerodynamic forces and act as airfoils. A wing's aerodynamic efficiency is expressed as its lift-to-drag ratio. The lift a wing generates at a given speed and angle of attack can be one to two orders of magnitude greater than the total drag on the wing.

Wings may be found in nature, on birds, bats, insects, and extinct pterosaurs, and in human technology, most prominently on aircraft, where they provide the lift that keeps the vehicle airborne, as well as on hydrofoils, sails, and racing cars, where downward force or sideways force is desired.

Aerodynamics

As a wing moves through a fluid, air flows faster over its upper surface and slower beneath it, and the wing deflects air downward; together these effects produce an upward force, lift. Lift depends on the wing's shape (its airfoil section and planform), its angle of attack, the speed of the flow, and the density of the fluid. Too steep an angle of attack causes the flow to separate and the wing to stall, with a sudden loss of lift.

Wing configurations

Aircraft wings vary by position (high-wing, mid-wing, low-wing), by shape (straight, swept, delta, elliptical), and by number (monoplane, biplane, triplane). Control surfaces such as ailerons, flaps, slats, and spoilers are typically mounted on the wing to control roll and to adjust lift and drag during takeoff and landing.

Wings in nature

Powered flight evolved independently in insects, pterosaurs, birds, and bats, and in each case the wing is a modified forelimb or, in insects, an outgrowth of the exoskeleton. Bird wings bear feathers, bat wings are membranes of skin stretched between elongated fingers, and insect wings are thin cuticular membranes driven by flight muscles.
