Four-acceleration

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In the theory of relativity, the four-acceleration is a four-vector that generalises the classical acceleration to four-dimensional spacetime. It is defined as the derivative of the four-velocity with respect to the object's proper time, or covariantly as the covariant derivative of the four-velocity along the worldline:

Aμ = DUμ/dτ = dUμ/dτ + Γμνλ Uν dxλ/dτ

where U is the four-velocity, τ is proper time, and Γ denotes the Christoffel symbols.

Properties

The four-acceleration is always orthogonal to the four-velocity: since the four-velocity has constant magnitude (−c² in the −+++ signature), their inner product vanishes, A · U = 0. In the instantaneous rest frame of the object, the four-acceleration reduces to (0, a), where a is the ordinary three-acceleration (the proper acceleration measured by an accelerometer). The magnitude of the four-acceleration equals the magnitude of the proper acceleration, |A| = α.

Relation to four-force

Four-acceleration is related to the four-force by the relativistic generalisation of Newton's second law, Fμ = m Aμ, for a particle of constant rest mass. For an object moving in a straight line with proper acceleration α, the four-acceleration in a laboratory frame takes components involving the Lorentz factor γ, and repeated application leads to hyperbolic motion — the trajectory of constant proper acceleration central to discussions of relativistic rockets and the Rindler horizon.

Geodesics

For a free-falling particle acted on only by gravity, the four-acceleration vanishes: in general relativity gravity is not a force, and unaccelerated (geodesic) motion has Aμ = 0. Nonzero four-acceleration therefore measures deviation from geodesic motion and corresponds exactly to what an accelerometer reads.
