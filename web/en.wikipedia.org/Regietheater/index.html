Regietheater

Regietheater (German for "director's theatre") is a term describing the modern practice in opera and theatre production of allowing — indeed expecting — a stage director to impose a strong interpretive concept on a work, often departing radically from the composer's or playwright's stated setting, period, and staging directions. In the English-speaking opera world the related term Eurotrash is sometimes used pejoratively for such productions.

A Regietheater staging may relocate a classic opera to a modern setting.

Origins

The approach has roots in the revolutionary productions of the early 20th century and in Walter Felsenstein's Komische Oper in East Berlin, but it flourished especially in post-war Germany, where heavily subsidised opera houses each employed an Intendant and house directors expected to produce fresh interpretations of the standard repertoire. Landmarks include Wieland Wagner's stripped-down post-war Bayreuth productions and Patrice Chéreau's centenary Ring cycle at Bayreuth in 1976, initially booed and later acclaimed.

Characteristics

Typical features include updating the setting to the present day or another era, political or psychoanalytic readings of the characters, symbolic sets, and stagings that contradict the libretto's literal action. Directors associated with the practice include Peter Konwitschny, Calixto Bieito, Claus Guth, and Dmitri Tcherniakov.

Reception

Supporters argue that Regietheater keeps canonical works alive and reveals meanings latent in text and score; detractors accuse directors of self-indulgence, incoherence, and disrespect toward composers. The debate intensifies when productions premiere to booing — a familiar ritual at German and Austrian houses — and polarises press and public.
