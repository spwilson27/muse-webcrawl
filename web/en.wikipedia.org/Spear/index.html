Spear

A spear is a pole weapon consisting of a shaft, usually of wood, with a pointed head. The head may be simply the sharpened end of the shaft itself, as is the case with fire-hardened spears, or made of a more durable material fastened to the shaft, such as bone, flint, obsidian or metal. Spears have been used since the Palaeolithic as hunting tools and weapons, and they were the principal weapon of infantry and cavalry for most of recorded history until the widespread adoption of firearms.

Prehistory

Stone points that may have tipped spears date back as much as 500,000 years. The Schöningen spears, wooden throwing spears found in Germany and dated to roughly 300,000–400,000 years ago, are among the oldest complete weapons known, showing that early humans hunted large game cooperatively. The spear-thrower (atlatl) extended the weapon's range and power in the Upper Palaeolithic, and the bow gradually replaced it for some hunting, but spears remained universal.

Military use

Ancient armies were built around the spear: the Greek hoplite's doru, used with the phalanx formation, and the Macedonian sarissa pike, up to 6 metres long, dominated Mediterranean warfare. Roman legionaries threw the heavy pilum javelin before closing with swords. In the Middle Ages spears evolved into pikes, lances, halberds and other polearms; massed pike formations of Swiss and German infantry transformed fifteenth- and sixteenth-century warfare. Cavalry used the lance in charges until the twentieth century, while the bayonet — a blade that converts a musket or rifle into a spear — kept the spear's role alive in modern infantry.

Hunting, fishing and sport

Spears remain in use for fishing (including the multi-pronged leister and the Hawaiian sling), in traditional hunting, and in sport as the javelin throw, an Olympic event descended from military and hunting throws.
