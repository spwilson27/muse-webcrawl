Forward contract

In finance, a forward contract (often simply a forward) is a non-standardised, over-the-counter derivative contract between two parties to buy or sell an asset at a specified price (the forward price) on a specified future date. The buyer takes a long position and the seller a short position. Unlike exchange-traded futures contracts, forwards are customised, are not marked to market daily, and carry counterparty credit risk; settlement typically occurs once, at maturity.

Payoff and pricing

At maturity, the payoff to the long position is the spot price of the asset minus the delivery price fixed in the contract; the short's payoff is the reverse, making the contract zero-sum. Under the no-arbitrage principle, the forward price for an asset with no income or carrying costs is the spot price grown at the risk-free rate: F = S·erT. Adjustments apply for dividends, storage costs, and convenience yield. In foreign exchange markets — the largest forward market — the forward rate is determined by the interest-rate differential between the two currencies, a relationship known as covered interest rate parity; a currency trading at a higher interest rate trades at a forward discount.

Uses

Forwards are used both to hedge and to speculate. Importers and exporters use FX forwards to lock in exchange rates; farmers and commodity producers lock in selling prices; airlines hedge fuel costs. Because terms are negotiated privately, forwards can be tailored to exact quantities, dates, and grades, but this flexibility comes with illiquidity and default risk, which after the 2008 financial crisis prompted regulators to push more over-the-counter derivatives into central clearing and margining regimes.

History

Forward agreements are among the oldest financial contracts: rice forwards were traded at the Dōjima exchange in 18th-century Japan, and commodity forwards were common in European trade centuries earlier. The modern over-the-counter forward market expanded rapidly from the 1970s with floating exchange rates and financial deregulation.
