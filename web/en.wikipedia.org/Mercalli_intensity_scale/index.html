Modified Mercalli intensity scale

The Modified Mercalli intensity scale (MM, MMI, or MCS) measures the effects of an earthquake at a given location. This is in contrast with the seismic magnitude usually reported for an earthquake, which is a measure of the energy released. The scale quantifies the effects of shaking on the Earth's surface, humans, objects of nature, and man-made structures on a scale from I (not felt) to XII (total destruction).

History

The scale descends from the Rossi–Forel scale and the ten-degree scale of the Italian volcanologist Giuseppe Mercalli (1897, revised 1902). German geophysicist August Heinrich Sieberg expanded Mercalli's descriptions, and the American seismologists Harry O. Wood and Frank Neumann published a "modified" version in 1931, which became the standard in the United States.

The scale

Lower degrees describe the manner in which the earthquake is felt by people; higher degrees are based on observed structural damage. Level I is not felt except by very few; VI is felt by all, with slight damage; VIII causes considerable damage in ordinary buildings; X destroys most masonry and frame structures; XII is total damage, with objects thrown into the air.

Correlation with magnitude

Intensity is not solely determined by magnitude: a shallow earthquake causes stronger shaking at the surface than a deep one of the same magnitude, and local geology and building quality strongly affect the observed intensity. The United States Geological Survey produces instrumental "ShakeMaps" estimating intensity from recorded ground motion.
