Polynesians

Polynesians are an ethnolinguistic group of closely related peoples native to Polynesia, the vast triangular region of the Pacific Ocean bounded by Hawaii, Easter Island (Rapa Nui), and New Zealand (Aotearoa). They speak Polynesian languages, a subgroup of the Oceanic branch of the Austronesian language family, and share cultural traditions rooted in a common ancestral homeland known in tradition as Hawaiki.

Origins and voyaging

Polynesians descend from Austronesian-speaking seafarers whose ancestors moved from Taiwan through Island Southeast Asia and into the western Pacific, developing the Lapita culture in the Bismarck Archipelago around 1500 BCE. From Samoa and Tonga, voyagers settled eastern Polynesia in one of the great feats of pre-modern navigation, reaching the Cook Islands, the Marquesas, Tahiti, Hawaii, Easter Island, and finally New Zealand — probably by around 1250–1300 CE. Settlement was accomplished with double-hulled voyaging canoes and sophisticated non-instrument navigation using stars, swell patterns, and bird flight.

Culture

Traditional Polynesian societies were organized into chiefdoms governed by hereditary chiefs (aliʻi, ariki), with social life structured by concepts such as mana (spiritual authority) and tapu (sacred restriction, the source of the English word "taboo"). Tattooing, carving, and kapa (barkcloth) manufacture were highly developed arts. Subsistence combined agriculture — taro, sweet potato, breadfruit, banana — with sophisticated fishing.

Modern population

The largest Polynesian peoples today include the Māori of New Zealand, Native Hawaiians, Samoans, Tahitians, Tongans, and Cook Islanders. Significant diaspora communities live in New Zealand, Australia, Hawaii, and the western United States; Auckland is sometimes called the world's largest Polynesian city.
