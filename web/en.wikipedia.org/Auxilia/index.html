The auxilia were introduced as non-citizen troops attached to the citizen legions by the emperor Augustus after his reorganisation of the imperial Roman army from 27 BC. By the second century AD they numbered roughly as many men as the legionaries and provided most of the army's cavalry and specialist troops.

Organisation

Auxiliaries served in their own units: infantry cohorts (cohortes), cavalry wings (alae), and mixed units (cohortes equitatae), typically of about 500 (quingenary) or 1,000 (milliary) men. Units were often named for the people from whom they were first raised — Gauls, Thracians, Syrians, Batavians — although local recruitment gradually diluted their ethnic character. Specialist units included Syrian archers, Balearic slingers and Numidian light horse.

Service and citizenship

Auxiliaries enlisted for 25 years and were paid less than legionaries. On honourable discharge they received a bronze diploma granting Roman citizenship to themselves and their descendants — one of the principal engines of Romanisation in the provinces. Their sons often went on to serve in the legions.

Role

Auxiliary units garrisoned the frontier provinces, patrolled the limes, and supplied the cavalry screens and missile troops that the heavy legionary infantry lacked. In Britain, most of the garrison of Hadrian's Wall and the northern forts consisted of auxiliary regiments.
