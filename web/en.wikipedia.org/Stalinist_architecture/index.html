Stalinist architecture, also known as Stalinist Empire style or Socialist Classicism, is the architecture of the Soviet Union under the leadership of Joseph Stalin, between 1933 (when Boris Iofan's draft for the Palace of the Soviets was officially approved) and 1955 (when Nikita Khrushchev condemned "excesses" of the past decades and dissolved the USSR Academy of Architecture).

Characteristics

The style is characterised by monumental scale, strict symmetry, lavish ornamentation drawn from classical, Baroque and Russian national traditions, and the integration of sculpture and mosaics glorifying the state and its ideology. It was the architectural expression of socialist realism, rejecting the avant-garde constructivism of the 1920s in favour of historicist forms intended to be national in form and socialist in content.

Notable buildings

The most famous examples are the seven tiered skyscrapers in Moscow known as the Seven Sisters (1947–1953), including the main building of Moscow State University and the Kotelnicheskaya Embankment Building. The style was exported across the Eastern Bloc: the Palace of Culture and Science in Warsaw, the Karl-Marx-Allee in Berlin, and the Largo ensemble in Sofia. The Moscow Metro's ornate stations are a celebrated underground expression of the style.

Decline

After Stalin's death, Khrushchev's 1955 decree "On the elimination of excesses in design and construction" ended the style in favour of cheap, standardised mass housing such as the prefabricated khrushchyovka apartment blocks.
