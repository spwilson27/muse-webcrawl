Treaty of Tientsin

SignedJune 1858
LocationTientsin (Tianjin), Qing China
PartiesQing Empire; United Kingdom; France; United States; Russia

The Treaties of Tientsin (now spelled Tianjin) were a series of agreements signed in June 1858 between the Qing dynasty of China and the United Kingdom, France, the United States, and Russia, during the Second Opium War. They are numbered among the "unequal treaties" imposed on China in the 19th century.

Terms

The treaties opened eleven additional ports to foreign trade, including Niuzhuang, Tamsui, Hankou, and Nanjing; permitted foreign ships, including warships, on the Yangtze River; allowed foreign envoys to reside in Beijing; tolerated Christian missionary activity and travel in the interior; and imposed indemnities on China. The opium trade was effectively legalised through a subsequent tariff agreement. The American and Russian treaties were negotiated separately on similar lines.

Ratification and consequences

When the Western envoys attempted to enter Beijing in 1859 to exchange ratifications, Qing forces repulsed them at the Taku Forts, prolonging the war. After the Anglo-French expedition captured Beijing in 1860, the treaties were ratified under the Convention of Peking, which added further concessions, including the cession of Kowloon to Britain.
