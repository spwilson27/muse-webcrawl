A referendum on the Treaty establishing a Constitution for Europe was held in France on 29 May 2005. Voters were asked whether France should ratify the proposed European Constitution, signed in Rome in October 2004. The treaty was rejected by 54.67 percent of voters on a turnout of about 69 percent — a result that, together with the Dutch rejection three days later, effectively killed the Constitutional Treaty and precipitated a crisis in the European Union.

Background

President Jacques Chirac chose the referendum route rather than ratification by parliament, expecting a comfortable "yes". The "yes" campaign was backed by the governing UMP, most of the Socialist Party leadership, and the main employers' and trade-union confederations. The "no" camp was unusually broad: it united the far-right National Front, communists, Trotskyist parties, sovereigntists of the right, and a dissident wing of the Socialists led by Laurent Fabius, who argued that the treaty entrenched an excessively liberal, "Anglo-Saxon" economic model. Worries about the relocation of jobs, the proposed services directive (symbolised by the mythical "Polish plumber"), and possible Turkish accession to the EU also drove the "no" vote.

Result and consequences

Every region of mainland France voted "no". Chirac replaced Prime Minister Jean-Pierre Raffarin with Dominique de Villepin, but his authority was badly weakened. The Netherlands rejected the treaty on 1 June 2005 by 61 percent, and the ratification process was suspended. Most of the treaty's institutional provisions were later repackaged in the Treaty of Lisbon (2007), which France ratified through parliament without a second referendum — a decision that remained controversial in French politics.
