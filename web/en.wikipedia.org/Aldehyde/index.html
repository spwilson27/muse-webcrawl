Aldehyde

An aldehyde is an organic compound containing a functional group with the structure R−CH=O. The group itself — a carbonyl carbon bonded to a hydrogen atom and to an R group — is called the aldehyde group or formyl group. Aldehydes are common and play important roles in technology and biology.

Structure of the aldehyde functional group.

Structure and bonding

The carbonyl carbon is sp²-hybridised and planar, bonded to hydrogen, to the R group, and by a double bond to oxygen. Unlike ketones, in which the carbonyl carbon carries two carbon groups, an aldehyde always has at least one hydrogen attached to the carbonyl carbon.

Nomenclature

Aldehydes are named by replacing the -e of the parent alkane with -al: methanal (formaldehyde), ethanal (acetaldehyde), propanal, and so on. The common name formaldehyde for methanal is retained in general use.

Reactions

Aldehydes are versatile intermediates. They are easily oxidised to carboxylic acids, reduced to primary alcohols, and undergo nucleophilic addition reactions at the carbonyl carbon. Tollens' reagent and Fehling's solution oxidise aldehydes but not most ketones, providing a classical chemical test distinguishing the two classes.

Occurrence and uses

Formaldehyde is produced industrially on a huge scale for resins and disinfectants. Benzaldehyde gives the flavour of almonds; cinnamaldehyde gives cinnamon its scent; vanillin is the principal flavouring of vanilla. Retinal, an aldehyde derivative of vitamin A, is essential to vision.
