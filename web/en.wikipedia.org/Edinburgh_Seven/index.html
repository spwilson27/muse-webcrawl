Edinburgh Seven

The Edinburgh Seven were the first group of matriculated undergraduate female students at any British university. Led by Sophia Jex-Blake, they began studying medicine at the University of Edinburgh in 1869, and although they were ultimately prevented from graduating, their campaign drew national attention and contributed directly to legislation ensuring that women could study medicine at British universities.

The campaign to study

In 1869 the university court agreed to admit women to study medicine in separate classes. The seven women were:

  • Sophia Jex-Blake
  • Isabel Thorne
  • Edith Pechey
  • Matilda Chaplin
  • Helen Evans
  • Mary Anderson
  • Emily Bovell

The women performed exceptionally well — Pechey came top of the chemistry examination — but met mounting hostility. Fees for their separate classes were raised, lecturers withdrew support, and male students campaigned against them.

The Surgeons' Hall riot

On 18 November 1870, as the women arrived to sit an anatomy examination at Surgeons' Hall, a large crowd gathered; the gates were slammed in their faces and they were pelted with mud and rubbish. The incident, known as the Surgeons' Hall riot, won the women considerable public sympathy.

Outcome and legacy

In 1873 the Court of Session ruled that the university had acted beyond its powers in admitting the women, and they were denied their degrees. Jex-Blake and others continued their training elsewhere; several qualified abroad, in Bern and Paris. In 1876 the Russell Gurney Act removed the legal barrier to examining bodies licensing women, and in 1877 the first women qualified as doctors in Britain through the Irish licensing body.

In 2019, 150 years after they matriculated, the University of Edinburgh awarded the Edinburgh Seven posthumous honorary MBChB degrees, collected by seven female medical students on their behalf.
