Apple

An apple is a round, edible fruit produced by the apple tree (Malus domestica), a deciduous tree in the rose family (Rosaceae). Apples are among the most widely cultivated tree fruits in the world; thousands of cultivars are grown for eating fresh, cooking, and cider production.

Origin and history

The domesticated apple descends from Malus sieversii, a wild apple still found in the mountains of Central Asia, in southern Kazakhstan. From there apples spread along trade routes to Europe and, later, the Americas. Because apples grown from seed rarely resemble the parent, desirable varieties are propagated by grafting. Figures such as Johnny Appleseed helped spread orchards across North America.

Cultivation

Apples grow best in temperate climates with a period of winter chill. World production is led by China, followed by the United States and Poland. Popular cultivars include Gala, Fuji, Granny Smith, Honeycrisp and Golden Delicious. Orchards must manage pests such as codling moth and diseases such as apple scab and fire blight.

Uses and nutrition

Apples are eaten raw, cooked in pies and sauces, dried, and pressed for juice and cider. They provide dietary fibre, vitamin C and potassium, and contain the soluble fibre pectin. Apple seeds contain trace amounts of amygdalin, but in quantities harmless in normal consumption.

In culture

Apples feature prominently in mythology and folklore — the golden apples of the Hesperides, the apple of discord, the fruit in the Garden of Eden in Western tradition, and the apple said to have inspired Isaac Newton's insight into gravity.
