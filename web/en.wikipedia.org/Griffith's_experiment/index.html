Griffith's experiment

ResearcherFrederick Griffith
Published1928, Journal of Hygiene
OrganismStreptococcus pneumoniae
DemonstratedBacterial transformation

Griffith's experiment, published in 1928 by the British bacteriologist Frederick Griffith, was the first experiment to demonstrate bacterial transformation — that bacteria can transfer genetic information and thereby change their form and function. It provided the first strong evidence for a transferable hereditary "transforming principle", later identified as DNA.

The experiment

Griffith worked with two strains of the pneumonia-causing bacterium Streptococcus pneumoniae: the virulent smooth (S) strain, which bears a protective polysaccharide capsule and kills mice, and the harmless rough (R) strain, which lacks the capsule. He found that:

  • Living S bacteria injected into mice killed them.
  • Living R bacteria left mice healthy.
  • Heat-killed S bacteria left mice healthy.
  • A mixture of living R bacteria and heat-killed S bacteria killed the mice — and living S bacteria could be recovered from their blood.

Griffith concluded that some "transforming principle" released from the dead S cells had converted living R cells into the virulent S form, and that the change was inherited by their descendants.

Significance

Griffith did not identify the transforming substance. Building directly on his result, Oswald Avery, Colin MacLeod and Maclyn McCarty at the Rockefeller Institute showed in 1944 that the transforming principle was DNA — a landmark that met initial scepticism because proteins were widely assumed to carry hereditary information. Together with the Hershey–Chase experiment of 1952, this work established DNA as the genetic material and launched molecular biology.
