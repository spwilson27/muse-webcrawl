The Russian Constitution of 1906, formally the revised Fundamental Laws of the Russian Empire, was promulgated by Emperor Nicholas II on 23 April 1906, on the eve of the opening of the first State Duma. Together with the October Manifesto of 1905, it marked the Russian Empire's first, limited move from absolute autocracy toward a constitutional order, though historians debate whether it amounts to a true constitution.

Background

The Revolution of 1905 — the general strike, the Bloody Sunday massacre and mutinies including that on the battleship Potemkin — forced Nicholas II to promise civil liberties and an elected legislature in the October Manifesto. The Fundamental Laws were drafted under Sergei Witte's influence but issued unilaterally by the Tsar, who retained the title of autocrat and control over ministers, foreign policy and the armed forces.

Provisions

The Laws created a bicameral legislature: the elected State Duma and the reformed State Council, half appointed by the Emperor. No law could take effect without the approval of both chambers and the Emperor, and the Emperor could dissolve the Duma and rule by emergency decree under Article 87. Citizens were guaranteed certain rights — inviolability of person and property, freedom of conscience, speech, assembly and association — but these were qualified by law.

Operation and end

Four Dumas sat between 1906 and 1917. The electoral law of 3 June 1907, issued in breach of the Laws' own amendment procedure, weighted representation towards landowners and produced the more compliant Third Duma. The constitutional experiment ended with the February Revolution of 1917 and the abdication of Nicholas II; the Provisional Government governed without a constitution until the October Revolution.
