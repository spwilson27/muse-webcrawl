Don Juan (Byron)

Don Juan (pronounced in the English fashion, "Don Joo-an") is a satirical and epic poem by Lord Byron, based on the legend of Don Juan, which Byron reverses, portraying Juan not as a womaniser but as someone easily seduced by women. It is a variation on the epic form. Byron himself called it an "Epic Satire". He began writing it in 1818 and continued until his death in 1824; by then he had completed sixteen cantos, leaving an unfinished seventeenth. The poem was published in instalments between 1819 and 1824.

Structure and style

The poem is written in ottava rima, an eight-line stanza rhyming ABABABCC, borrowed from Italian comic poets such as Pulci and Ariosto and used in English by Byron's contemporary John Hookham Frere. The stanza's closing couplet lends itself to punchlines and anticlimax, which Byron exploits for comic and satirical effect. The narrator frequently digresses, addressing the reader directly and commenting on contemporary politics, literature, and society.

Synopsis

Juan is born in Seville to Don José and Donna Inez. As a youth he has an affair with Donna Julia, the young wife of the elderly Don Alfonso, and is sent abroad to escape the scandal. His ship is wrecked; after drifting in an open boat (in a section notorious for its depiction of cannibalism among the starving survivors), he washes up on a Greek island, where he is rescued and loved by Haidée, the daughter of the pirate Lambro. Lambro returns, Juan is wounded and sold into slavery in Constantinople, where he is smuggled, disguised as a girl, into the seraglio of the Sultana Gulbeyaz.

Escaping, Juan joins the Russian army at the siege of Ismail, rescues a young Muslim girl, Leila, and becomes a hero at the court of Catherine the Great in St Petersburg. He is sent on a diplomatic mission to England, where the final cantos satirise English society through a series of episodes at a country house, including the appearance of a ghostly friar. The poem breaks off unfinished at Byron's death.

Publication and reception

The first two cantos were published anonymously in 1819 by John Murray. The poem was widely condemned as immoral by contemporary critics, yet it sold well and its popularity grew; later cantos were issued by the radical publisher John Hunt. Byron defended the work as a satire on the hypocrisies of the age.

Legacy

Don Juan is generally considered Byron's masterpiece and one of the greatest long poems in English of the Romantic period. Its mixture of comedy, narrative pace, verbal brilliance, and serious reflection influenced later comic and satirical verse, and its hero gave rise to the notion of the "Byronic" Juan — more sinned against than sinning.
