Outdoor education

Focus: Experiential learning in natural settings

Related fields: Environmental education, adventure education

Outdoor education is organised learning that takes place in the outdoors, typically involving experiential activities in natural or semi-natural settings. Programmes commonly aim to develop personal and social skills, physical fitness, self-confidence, teamwork, leadership and environmental awareness through activities such as hiking, camping, canoeing, climbing, ropes courses and expeditions.

Outdoor education draws on the philosophy of experiential education — learning through direct experience and reflection — and is practised in schools, youth organisations, outdoor centres and therapeutic programmes in many countries, from Scandinavian friluftsliv ("open-air life") traditions to forest schools for young children.

History

The roots of outdoor education lie in movements such as camping in the late 19th century, the Boy Scouts (founded 1908) and Girl Guides, and progressive education. A key figure was the German educator Kurt Hahn, who founded Gordonstoun School in Scotland and, during the Second World War, the first Outward Bound school at Aberdyfi in Wales (1941), designed to build resilience and self-reliance in young seamen. Outward Bound subsequently spread internationally, and residential outdoor centres became a fixture of school education in Britain, North America, Australia and elsewhere.

Forms and activities

Outdoor education spans a spectrum from school grounds-based environmental studies and forest school sessions for pre-school children, through adventure activities such as rock climbing, kayaking and mountain walking, to multi-week wilderness expeditions. It overlaps with environmental education (focused on ecological knowledge and stewardship), adventure education (focused on challenge and personal growth) and place-based education. Wilderness therapy programmes use extended outdoor experiences as a therapeutic intervention for at-risk youth.

Benefits and evidence

Research reviews generally report small-to-moderate positive effects of outdoor and adventure programmes on self-concept, locus of control, interpersonal skills and school engagement, with some evidence of benefits for physical activity levels and mental wellbeing. Proponents argue that contact with nature counters sedentary, screen-based childhoods — a concern popularised as "nature deficit disorder". Critics note variable programme quality, cost and access barriers, and the need for attention to safety: the field has developed professional standards for instructor qualifications and risk management.
