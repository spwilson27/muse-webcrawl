Chautauqua was an adult education and social movement in the United States, highly popular in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. Named for Chautauqua Lake in western New York, where the first assembly met in 1874, Chautauquas spread across rural North America, bringing lectures, concerts, drama, and preaching to communities far from universities and theatres. Theodore Roosevelt is said to have called it "the most American thing in America."

Origins

The movement began as the Chautauqua Lake Sunday School Assembly, founded in 1874 by Methodist minister John Heyl Vincent and businessman Lewis Miller to train Sunday-school teachers. Held in a lakeside camp-meeting grove, it broadened within a few years into a summer programme of lectures on science, history, literature, and current affairs, alongside music and worship. The permanent community that grew there became the Chautauqua Institution, which still operates a nine-week summer season.

Home study and the CLSC

In 1878 Vincent launched the Chautauqua Literary and Scientific Circle (CLSC), a four-year course of guided home reading — in effect one of America's first correspondence-study programmes — whose local "circles" enrolled tens of thousands, many of them women and rural readers with little access to formal education. The movement also underpinned a Chautauqua University (chartered 1883) and the popular magazine The Chautauquan.

Circuit Chautauquas

From the early 1900s, independent and then commercial "circuit" or "tent" Chautauquas — organised by bureaus such as the Redpath Lyceum Bureau — travelled from town to town each summer, setting up a big brown tent for a week of programming: orators like William Jennings Bryan, evangelists, bands, singers, lecturers on science and travel, and dramatic readers. At their peak in the mid-1920s, circuits reached thousands of towns and audiences numbering in the tens of millions. Competition from radio, cinema, and the automobile, worsened by the Great Depression, ended the circuits by the mid-1930s.

Legacy

Chautauqua blurred education and entertainment ("edutainment"), professionalised the lecture platform, and spread middle-class cultural aspirations across rural America. Surviving independent Chautauquas — notably the Chautauqua Institution itself and assemblies at Lakeside, Ohio, and elsewhere — continue the format today.
