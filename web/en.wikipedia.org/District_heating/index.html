District heating

District heating

Type: Centralised heating system

Heat sources: Combined heat and power, waste heat, geothermal, biomass

Distribution medium: Hot water or steam

Widespread in: Northern and Eastern Europe, Russia, China

District heating (also known as heat networks or teleheating) is a system for distributing heat generated in a centralized location through a system of insulated pipes for residential and commercial heating requirements such as space heating and water heating. The heat is often obtained from a cogeneration plant burning fossil fuels or biomass, but heat-only boiler stations, geothermal heating, heat pumps and central solar heating are also used, as is excess heat from industrial processes and nuclear power.

How it works

A central plant heats water (or produces steam) which is pumped through a network of supply pipes to buildings, where heat exchangers transfer the heat to the buildings' own heating systems. The cooled water returns to the plant through a second pipe to be reheated. Modern systems typically use water at temperatures below 100 °C, improving efficiency and allowing low-grade heat sources to be used.

History

Hot-water district heating dates to the Roman era hypocaust-like systems and to 14th-century Chaudes-Aigues in France, which used geothermal water. Commercial systems began with Birdsill Holly's steam system in Lockport, New York, in 1877. The technology spread widely in the Soviet Union and Scandinavia during the 20th century; Denmark now heats the majority of its homes this way.

Advantages and disadvantages

District heating can achieve higher efficiency and better pollution control than individual boilers, especially when coupled with combined heat and power generation, and it can exploit waste heat that would otherwise be discarded. Its drawbacks include high capital cost for the pipe network, heat losses over long distances, and the need for sufficient heat density in the area served.

Generations of technology

Engineers describe successive "generations" of district heating: first-generation steam systems, second-generation pressurized hot water in concrete ducts, third-generation prefabricated insulated pipes, and fourth-generation low-temperature networks designed to integrate renewable energy and reduce losses.
