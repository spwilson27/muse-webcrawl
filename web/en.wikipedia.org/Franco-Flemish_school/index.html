Franco-Flemish school

The Franco-Flemish school, also called the Netherlandish school, was the dominant style of European polyphonic vocal music from roughly 1420 to the mid-16th century, produced by composers originating in the Low Countries and northern France who worked at courts and cathedrals throughout Europe, especially in Italy.

Generations

Music historians commonly divide the school into generations. The first includes Guillaume Du Fay and Gilles Binchois, associated with the Burgundian court. The second includes Johannes Ockeghem and Antoine Busnois. The third is dominated by Josquin des Prez, along with Pierre de la Rue, Jacob Obrecht, and Heinrich Isaac. Later generations include Nicolas Gombert, Jacob Clemens non Papa, and Adrian Willaert, who carried the style to Venice, and it culminates in the international late-Renaissance idiom of Orlande de Lassus.

Style

Franco-Flemish composers perfected imitative counterpoint, in which a melodic idea passes systematically through all voices, and made extensive use of canon and cantus firmus technique in masses and motets. Their music spread widely thanks to the new technology of music printing after 1501.

Influence

Because these composers held leading posts at the papal chapel, the imperial court, and Italian cathedrals, their contrapuntal language became the foundation of European art music pedagogy for centuries.
