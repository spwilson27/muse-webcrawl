Ash'ari


A manuscript of a work of Ash'ari theology (kalam).

Ash'arism (Arabic: الأشعرية, al-Ash'ariyya) is a school of Sunni Islamic theology (kalam) founded by the Arab scholar Abu al-Hasan al-Ash'ari (873–935/6 CE) in Basra and Baghdad. Together with the Maturidi school, it became one of the two dominant orthodox theological traditions of Sunni Islam, shaping the doctrine of the Shafi'i and Maliki schools of law in particular. Adherents are called Ash'aris or Ash'arites.

Al-Ash'ari began his career as a follower of the rationalist Mu'tazila school, studying under his stepfather Abu Ali al-Jubba'i. Around the age of forty he publicly repudiated Mu'tazilism and set out to defend traditional Sunni doctrines — the uncreatedness of the Qur'an, the reality of the divine attributes, and the beatific vision — using the rational methods of kalam. His best-known works include the Maqalat al-Islamiyyin, a heresiographical survey, and al-Luma' ("The Gleams"), a concise statement of his theology.

Doctrine

Ash'ari theology occupies a middle position between literalist traditionalism and Mu'tazilite rationalism. Ash'aris affirm that God has real attributes — knowledge, power, will, life, hearing, sight and speech — that are neither identical to His essence nor other than it, and they interpret anthropomorphic Qur'anic expressions either by consigning their modality to God ("without asking how", bila kayf) or, in later generations, by figurative interpretation.

The school's most distinctive doctrine is kasb ("acquisition"): God creates all human acts, but human beings "acquire" their acts and are therefore responsible for them, a solution to the tension between divine omnipotence and moral accountability. Ash'arism also developed an atomistic physics in which God continually recreates the world at each instant — a form of occasionalism most famously articulated by al-Ghazali, under which created things have no necessary causal power of their own, and fire does not burn cotton by necessity but by God's habit ('ada).

Development and influence

After al-Ash'ari's death, followers such as al-Baqillani systematised the school, proving doctrines through atomism and the temporal origination of the world. Later luminaries include al-Juwayni, his student al-Ghazali (1058–1111), whose Incoherence of the Philosophers attacked the metaphysics of Ibn Sina and al-Farabi, and Fakhr al-Din al-Razi, in whose hands Ash'ari kalam absorbed much philosophical method. The school spread through the madrasa networks patronised by the Seljuk vizier Nizam al-Mulk and became the standard theology taught from North Africa to Southeast Asia. In the modern period Ash'arism remains influential at institutions such as al-Azhar in Cairo, though it has been criticised and opposed by Salafi and Hanbali traditionalist movements, who regard kalam itself as an innovation.
