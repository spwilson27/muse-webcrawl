Mu'tazila

The Mu'tazila (Arabic: المعتزلة, al-muʿtazila, "those who withdraw") was a school of Islamic speculative theology (kalam) that flourished in Basra and Baghdad from the 8th to the 10th centuries CE and remained influential for centuries afterward, especially within Zaydi and Twelver Shi'a theology. Its adherents were famous for championing human reason and free will.

Origins

According to the traditional account, the school took its name from Wasil ibn Ata (d. 748), a student of al-Hasan al-Basri, who "withdrew" from his teacher's circle after maintaining that the Muslim who commits a grave sin occupies an "intermediate position" (al-manzila bayna al-manzilatayn) — neither believer nor unbeliever. His associate Amr ibn Ubayd helped propagate the doctrine in Basra. Later Mu'tazilites organised their teaching around five principles (al-uṣūl al-khamsa): the unity of God (tawḥīd), divine justice (ʿadl), the promise and the threat (of reward and punishment), the intermediate position, and commanding right and forbidding wrong.

Doctrine

From divine unity the Mu'tazila argued that God's attributes are identical with His essence and that the Qur'an, being distinct from God, must be created — a doctrine the Abbasid caliph al-Ma'mun tried to enforce from 833 through the mihna (inquisition), during which the traditionalist Ahmad ibn Hanbal was persecuted for refusing to confess the Qur'an's createdness. From divine justice they argued that humans create their own acts and are therefore justly rewarded or punished; that God cannot do evil or impose the impossible; and that reason can discern good and evil prior to revelation. Leading systematicians included Abu al-Hudhayl, al-Nazzam, al-Jahiz, the Jubba'i father and son, and the Qadi 'Abd al-Jabbar (d. 1025), whose Sharḥ al-uṣūl al-khamsa and al-Mughnī are the school's masterworks.

Decline and legacy

The caliph al-Mutawakkil ended the mihna around 848 and turned against the Mu'tazila; in the following centuries Ash'ari and Maturidi theologians displaced them in Sunni Islam, though Mu'tazilite ideas survived in Zaydi Yemen and within Twelver Shi'a kalam, and were revived in modern times by reformers such as Muhammad Abduh.
