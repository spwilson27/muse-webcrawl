Kalam

Kalam (Arabic: عِلْم الكَلام, ʿilm al-kalām, "science of discourse"), usually foreshortened to kalam and sometimes called Islamic scholastic theology, is the discipline of seeking theological principles through dialectic and rational argument. A scholar of kalam is called a mutakallim (plural mutakallimūn). Kalam arose in the early centuries of Islam as Muslim thinkers debated questions of doctrine — the nature of God and His attributes, free will and predestination, the createdness of the Qur'an, and the status of the grave sinner — and defended Islamic beliefs against other religions and against internal dissent.

History

The earliest debates grew out of political disputes over succession to the caliphate and the status of Muslims who committed grave sins, questions first taken up by the Kharijites, the Murji'ites and the early partisans of free will known as the Qadariyya. In the 8th and 9th centuries the Mu'tazila developed kalam into a systematic rational theology organised around "five principles", including divine unity and justice. Under the Abbasid caliph al-Ma'mun, Mu'tazilite doctrine was briefly enforced as state orthodoxy through the mihna (inquisition), which persecuted traditionalists such as Ahmad ibn Hanbal.

In reaction, Abu al-Hasan al-Ash'ari (d. c. 936), a former Mu'tazilite, founded the Ash'ari school, which used rational methods to defend doctrines closer to traditional Sunni belief, while Abu Mansur al-Maturidi (d. 944) founded a parallel school in Samarkand that became dominant among Hanafis. Later Ash'ari thinkers such as al-Baqillani, al-Juwayni, al-Ghazali and Fakhr al-Din al-Razi increasingly incorporated logic and elements of philosophy, producing the elaborate "later kalam" (kalām al-mutaʾakhkhirīn). Shi'a Islam developed its own kalam traditions, and kalam remains a subject taught in madrasas and debated by modern Muslim reformers.

Main topics

Classic kalam treatises open with epistemology and proofs for the existence of God — most famously the argument from the temporal origination (ḥudūth) of the world, grounded in an atomistic picture of matter and accidents — and proceed to divine attributes, prophecy, free will and human acts, the imamate or caliphate, and eschatology. Ash'ari kalam is associated with occasionalism and the doctrine of the "acquisition" (kasb) of human acts; Maturidi kalam grants somewhat greater scope to human reason and free will.
