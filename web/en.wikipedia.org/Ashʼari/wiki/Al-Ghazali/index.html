Abū Ḥāmid Muḥammad ibn Muḥammad al-Ṭūsī al-Ghazālī (c. 1058 – 19 December 1111), known in Persian as Abū Ḥāmed Mohammad-e Ghazzālī and in Latinised form as Algazel, was a Persian polymath — theologian, philosopher, jurist, logician and mystic — and one of the most prominent and influential scholars in the history of Islam. He was given the honorific title Ḥujjat al-Islām, "Proof of Islam".

Life

Al-Ghazali was born in Tus in Khorasan and educated there and at Nishapur, where he studied under the great Ash'ari theologian al-Juwayni. In 1091 the vizier Nizam al-Mulk appointed him professor of law at the Nizamiyya madrasa in Baghdad, the most prestigious academic post in the Seljuk world. Four years later he suffered a spiritual crisis: he lost the ability to lecture, abandoned his post, gave up his wealth, and spent some eleven years as a wandering Sufi — in Damascus, Jerusalem, Hebron, Mecca and Medina — before returning to Tus, where he founded a small madrasa and Sufi hospice and wrote his masterworks. He briefly resumed teaching at Nishapur in 1106 before retiring again to Tus, where he died in 1111.

Works and thought

His magnum opus, Iḥyāʾ ʿulūm al-dīn ("The Revival of the Religious Sciences"), integrates jurisprudence, theology and Sufism into a comprehensive programme of spiritual discipline and is among the most widely read books in the Muslim world. In Tahāfut al-falāsifa ("The Incoherence of the Philosophers") he attacked the metaphysical doctrines of the falasifa (notably Ibn Sina and al-Farabi) on twenty points, most famously arguing for occasionalism — that God is the direct cause of every event — using the example of fire and cotton: the burning of cotton is created by God, not necessitated by the nature of fire. His autobiography al-Munqidh min al-Ḍalāl ("Deliverance from Error") recounts his intellectual and spiritual quest. He also wrote the ethical mirror for princes, works on logic (in which field he helped normalise Aristotelian logic within Islamic theology), and the famous letter of counsel Ayyuhā al-walad.

Influence

Al-Ghazali's synthesis brought Sufism into the heart of Sunni orthodoxy and reshaped Ash'ari theology. His writings were translated into Hebrew and Latin in medieval Europe, influencing scholastic thinkers, and his critique of causality has often been compared with that of David Hume. The Andalusian philosopher Ibn Rushd (Averroes) wrote a famous rebuttal, Tahāfut al-Tahāfut ("The Incoherence of the Incoherence").
