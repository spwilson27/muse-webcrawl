Fin de siècle

Fin de siècle (French for "end of the century") is a term typically applied to the closing years of the 19th century, especially the 1890s, and to the cultural mood associated with them: a mixture of decadence, aesthetic refinement, boredom with convention, anxiety about decline, and anticipation of a new age.

Cultural character

The fin de siècle mood was expressed across Europe in the Decadent movement in literature, Symbolism in poetry and painting, and Art Nouveau in the decorative arts. Writers such as Joris-Karl Huysmans, whose novel À rebours (1884) became a touchstone of decadence, and Oscar Wilde in Britain, embodied an aesthetic of artifice, sensation, and revolt against bourgeois respectability. In Vienna, Paris, and London alike, the period saw fascination with the occult, with mysticism, and with new psychological theories.

Degeneration and anxiety

Alongside artistic experiment ran a current of pessimism. Max Nordau's bestselling Degeneration (1892) attacked modern art and culture as symptoms of social and biological decay. Fears of national decline, urban overcrowding, and moral decay coexisted with excitement about technology and progress, giving the era its characteristic tension.

Legacy

The term is sometimes extended to the end of any century, and the mood it names — end-of-an-era exhaustion mixed with anticipation — has been applied retrospectively to other transitional periods.
