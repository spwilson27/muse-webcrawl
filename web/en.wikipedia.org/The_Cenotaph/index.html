The Cenotaph

The Cenotaph is a war memorial on Whitehall in London, England. Designed by Sir Edwin Lutyens, it was unveiled in 1920 as the United Kingdom's national memorial to the British and Commonwealth dead of the First World War; it was rededicated in 1946 to include those of the Second World War, and now represents all British and Commonwealth service personnel killed in all later conflicts. The word cenotaph means "empty tomb".

Design

The memorial is a spare pylon of Portland stone, subtly curved so that its vertical lines, if extended, would meet about 300 metres above the ground, and its horizontal surfaces form parts of a sphere centred below ground — an application of the classical device of entasis. It bears no religious imagery, to be inclusive of all faiths and none; the only words are "The Glorious Dead", chosen by Rudyard Kipling. Sculpted laurel wreaths appear on each end, and the flags of the armed services flank the monument. A temporary wood-and-plaster version erected for the 1919 victory parade proved so popular that a permanent version in stone was commissioned and unveiled by King George V on Armistice Day, 11 November 1920, at a ceremony combined with the burial of the Unknown Warrior in Westminster Abbey.

Remembrance

The Cenotaph is the focus of the National Service of Remembrance, held each year on Remembrance Sunday, the closest Sunday to 11 November. The sovereign, politicians, Commonwealth representatives and the armed forces lay wreaths of poppies, followed by a march-past of thousands of veterans. Lutyens's design spawned many similar cenotaphs throughout Britain and the Commonwealth.
