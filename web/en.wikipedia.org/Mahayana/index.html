Mahayana (Sanskrit: महायान, romanized: Mahāyāna, "Great Vehicle") is a term for a broad group of Buddhist traditions, texts, philosophies, and practices developed in ancient India (c. 1st century BC onwards) and considered one of the three main existing branches of Buddhism, the others being Theravada and Vajrayana. Mahayana is the largest branch, followed mainly in East Asia (China, Japan, Korea, Vietnam) and the Himalayan region, and accepts the main scriptures and teachings of early Buddhism while also recognising various additional sutras and doctrines.

Doctrines

Central to Mahayana is the bodhisattva ideal: the practitioner aspires to full Buddhahood for the sake of all sentient beings, rather than seeking only personal liberation as an arhat — the goal that Mahayana texts polemically label the "lesser vehicle" (Hinayana). Its philosophy includes the doctrine of emptiness (śūnyatā), systematised by Nagarjuna's Madhyamaka school, which holds that all phenomena lack inherent existence; the Yogacara school's "mind-only" analysis; the teaching of Buddha-nature (tathāgatagarbha), an innate potential for awakening in all beings; and the trikaya doctrine of the Buddha's three bodies. Devotion to celestial Buddhas and bodhisattvas — Amitabha, Avalokiteshvara, Manjushri — underlies Pure Land practice and much East Asian Buddhism.

History and spread

Mahayana sutras such as the Perfection of Wisdom texts and the Lotus Sutra began to appear from about the 1st century BC. Carried along Silk Road trade routes, the tradition reached China by the 2nd century AD, where schools such as Tiantai, Huayan, Chan (Zen), and Pure Land developed, and from there spread to Korea, Japan, and Vietnam. A later, tantrically influenced form, Vajrayana, came to dominate in Tibet and Mongolia. Mahayana monasteries and universities such as Nalanda were major centres of learning for Asia.
