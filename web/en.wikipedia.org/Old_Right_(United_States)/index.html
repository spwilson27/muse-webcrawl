Old Right (United States)

The Old Right is an informal designation for a group of American conservatives prominent from the 1910s through the mid-1950s. Formed in opposition to both the New Deal at home and American entry into the Second World War abroad, the Old Right defended limited government, free markets and a non-interventionist foreign policy, and resisted the growth of the federal state.

Figures and ideas

Prominent figures associated with the Old Right include Senator Robert A. Taft of Ohio, the journalist H. L. Mencken, the writer Albert Jay Nock, former president Herbert Hoover, and the novelist Rose Wilder Lane. Its intellectuals combined classical liberal economics with hostility to militarism and conscription; many had supported the isolationist America First Committee before Pearl Harbor.

Unlike the post-war conservative movement that coalesced around William F. Buckley Jr.'s National Review, the Old Right generally rejected Cold War anti-communist interventionism and military alliances. The historian Murray Rothbard, who popularised the label, regarded the movement as the last significant American political tendency opposed to both domestic statism and foreign war.

Decline and legacy

The Old Right waned after Taft's death in 1953 and the rise of the "New Right" fusionism of the 1950s and 1960s, which embraced an aggressive international anti-communism the older movement had opposed. Elements of its thought survived in later libertarian and paleoconservative currents.
