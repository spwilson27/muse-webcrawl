Scholarly publishing

Academic journals are the principal vehicle of scholarly publishing.

Scholarly publishing is the system by which academic research is produced, evaluated by peer review, disseminated, and preserved. Its main channels are academic journals, scholarly books (monographs), and conference proceedings. The process typically involves submission of a manuscript by an author, evaluation by editors and independent referees, revision, and publication in print and electronic form.

History

The first scholarly journals, the Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society and the French Journal des sçavans, both appeared in 1665. The modern system took shape in the twentieth century as peer review became standard and commercial publishers entered the field, leading to rapid growth in the number of journals and in subscription prices—the so-called "serials crisis".

Peer review

Peer review is intended to assess the validity, significance, and originality of submitted work. It is usually conducted anonymously (single- or double-blind) by researchers in the same field, and most manuscripts are revised or rejected before acceptance.

Business models and open access

Traditional journals are funded by subscriptions, often bundled in "big deal" packages. Open access publishing makes articles freely available, frequently funded by article processing charges paid by authors or their institutions. The economics of the industry, with high profit margins for major commercial publishers, has been widely criticised, and initiatives such as Plan S have pushed for immediate open access to publicly funded research.
