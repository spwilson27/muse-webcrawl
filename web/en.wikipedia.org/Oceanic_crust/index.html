Oceanic crust

Oceanic crust is the uppermost layer of the oceanic portion of the tectonic plates, forming the bedrock beneath the world's oceans. It is composed mostly of mafic rocks — basalt and gabbro, rich in iron and magnesium — and is much thinner, denser and younger than the continental crust beneath the continents.

Structure

A complete oceanic crustal section, as seen in ophiolite complexes thrust onto land, consists (from top to bottom) of: a thin layer of deep-sea sediments; pillow basalts erupted underwater; a sheeted dyke complex of parallel basaltic dykes through which magma rose; and coarse-grained gabbro crystallised in a magma chamber. Total thickness is typically only 5–10 km, compared with 30–50 km or more for continental crust, and its density is about 3.0 g/cm³ versus roughly 2.7 g/cm³ for continents.

Formation and lifecycle

Oceanic crust is continuously created at mid-ocean ridges, where tectonic plates diverge and mantle material wells up, melts and solidifies; the composition of this new crust is close to that of mid-ocean ridge basalt (MORB). As the plates move apart, the crust cools, thickens and subsides, accumulating sediment. At convergent margins the old, dense crust sinks back into the mantle in subduction zones. Because of this recycling, no oceanic crust is older than about 340 million years (and most is under 200 million years), in stark contrast to continental rocks up to 4 billion years old. The age of the crust increases symmetrically away from ridges, and the pattern of magnetic stripes recorded in it provided key evidence for seafloor spreading and plate tectonics.
