Stress concentration

A stress concentration (often called a stress raiser or stress riser) is a location in an object where the stress is significantly greater than the surrounding region. Stress concentrations occur when there are irregularities in the geometry or material of a structural component that cause an interruption to the flow of stress, such as holes, grooves, notches, sharp corners, and threads.

Stress concentration factor

The severity is quantified by the stress concentration factor Kt, the ratio of the highest stress at the discontinuity to the nominal stress of the gross cross-section. For a small circular hole in a wide plate under uniaxial tension, Kt = 3, so the stress at the hole's edge is three times the average stress. Charts of Kt for common geometries are standard references in machine design; for fatigue loading an analogous fatigue stress concentration factor Kf accounts for notch sensitivity of the material.

Causes and mitigation

Stress concentrations are a major cause of crack initiation and fatigue failure. Designers mitigate them by using generous fillet radii at internal corners, avoiding abrupt changes in cross-section, adding relief holes at the ends of slots or cracks, polishing surfaces to remove scratches, and using reinforcement around openings. In fracture mechanics, a sharp crack is the limiting case of a notch, producing a theoretically singular stress field characterised by the stress intensity factor.
