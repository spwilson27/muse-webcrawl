Shingon Buddhism

Shingon Buddhism (Japanese: 真言宗, Shingon-shū, "True Word school") is one of the major schools of Japanese Buddhism and one of the few surviving Vajrayana (esoteric Buddhist) lineages in East Asia. It was founded in the early 9th century by the monk Kūkai (774–835, posthumously Kōbō Daishi), who brought esoteric teachings and texts from Tang-dynasty China, where the corresponding tradition is called Tangmi. "Shingon" is the Japanese reading of the Chinese zhēnyán, "true word" — a translation of Sanskrit mantra.

Doctrine

Shingon teaches that the cosmic Buddha Mahāvairocana (Dainichi) is the embodiment of ultimate reality, and that the entire universe is his self-expression — the "Dharma-body preaching the Dharma". Enlightenment is attainable in this very body (sokushin jōbutsu) through the Three Mysteries: forming mudras (ritual hand gestures) with the body, reciting mantras with the voice, and visualising mandalas and deities with the mind, thereby aligning the practitioner's body, speech and mind with those of the Buddha. The two great mandalas — the Womb Realm (Garbhadhātu) and Diamond Realm (Vajradhātu) mandalas — map this cosmos and structure initiation (kanjō, abhiseka), in which the adept enters the mandala and receives the deity's empowerment.

History

Kūkai studied esoteric Buddhism in Chang'an under the master Huiguo in 805 and returned to Japan in 806. Granted Mount Kōya as a monastic centre in 816 and the Tō-ji temple in Kyoto in 823, he systematised the teachings in works such as the Ten Stages of the Development of Mind. Shingon became deeply woven into Japanese religious life: its rituals for the protection of the state, rain-making and healing were patronised by the court, and its rich art — mandala paintings, ritual implements, sculpture — profoundly influenced Japanese culture. Kūkai himself is venerated across Japan and is credited in legend with inventing the kana syllabary and countless works of engineering and charity; at Mount Kōya he is believed to remain in eternal meditation in his mausoleum at Okunoin, Japan's largest cemetery.

Shingon today

The school is organised in numerous branches (the largest being the Kōyasan, Chisan-ha and Buzan-ha lines) with several thousand temples and millions of adherents. Mount Kōya, with its temple lodgings (shukubō) and Okunoin cemetery, is a UNESCO World Heritage Site and a major pilgrimage destination, including for those walking the Shikoku Pilgrimage of 88 temples associated with Kūkai.
