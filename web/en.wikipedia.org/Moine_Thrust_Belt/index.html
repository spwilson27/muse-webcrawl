Moine Thrust Belt

The Moine Thrust Belt is a major geological structure in the Northwest Highlands of Scotland, forming the western margin of the Caledonian mountain belt. The belt runs for about 190 kilometres (120 miles), from the Sleat peninsula on the Isle of Skye in the southwest to Loch Eriboll on the north coast. It is the most intensely studied thrust belt in the world and was historically crucial in demonstrating that large-scale thrust faulting could transport rock sheets over long distances.

Geology

The thrust belt formed during the Caledonian orogeny, roughly 430–420 million years ago, when the continents of Laurentia and Baltica collided. During compression, older rocks — principally metamorphic Moine schists and ancient Lewisian gneiss — were pushed westward and upward over younger Cambrian–Ordovician sedimentary rocks along a series of low-angle faults called thrusts. The basal fault, the Moine Thrust itself, is overlain by a complex stack of thrust slices (imbricates) containing repetitions of the Cambrian–Ordovician succession of the Durness Group.

Scientific importance

In the nineteenth century the structure of the Northwest Highlands was the subject of the famous "Highlands Controversy" between Roderick Murchison, who argued the Moine schists lay conformably above the Cambrian limestones, and James Nicol, who recognised a fault. Detailed mapping by Ben Peach and John Horne of the Geological Survey in the 1880s proved the thrust interpretation and produced the classic 1907 memoir The Geological Structure of the North-West Highlands of Scotland, a landmark in structural geology. Features of the belt include the mylonites of the Moine Thrust zone and the classic exposures at Knockan Crag, now a visitor site in the North West Highlands Geopark.
