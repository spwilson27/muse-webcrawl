United States home front during World War II

The United States home front during World War II supported the war effort in many ways, including a wide range of volunteer efforts and submitting to government-managed rationing and price controls. There was a general feeling of agreement that the sacrifices were for the good of the nation during the war.

Mobilization

After the attack on Pearl Harbor on 7 December 1941, the United States rapidly converted its peacetime economy to war production. Automobile factories began producing tanks and aircraft; by 1944 the United States was producing nearly half of the world's armaments. The War Production Board coordinated industrial output, while the draft and voluntary enlistment brought some 16 million Americans into military service.

Rationing and controls

The Office of Price Administration administered rationing of gasoline, rubber, sugar, coffee, meat, butter, and other goods. Citizens received ration books with stamps for controlled items. Scrap drives collected metal, rubber, and cooking fat for recycling into war materiel, and "victory gardens" supplemented the food supply — by 1944 an estimated 20 million gardens produced roughly 40 percent of the nation's vegetables.

Women and work

With millions of men overseas, women entered the industrial workforce in unprecedented numbers; the female labor force grew by more than six million. The cultural icon "Rosie the Riveter" celebrated women working in defense plants. African Americans, Mexican Americans, and other groups also found new industrial opportunities, though discrimination persisted and helped spark the civil rights movement.

Civil liberties

The home front also saw serious violations of civil liberties, most notably the internment of some 120,000 Japanese Americans — most of them U.S. citizens — under Executive Order 9066. German and Italian Americans faced lesser restrictions. The Supreme Court upheld internment in Korematsu v. United States (1944), a decision widely condemned in later decades; Congress apologized and paid reparations in 1988.
