Chi-squared test

A chi-squared test (also chi-square or χ² test) is a statistical hypothesis test used to determine whether there is a significant association between categorical variables, or whether an observed distribution differs from a theoretical one. It is named after the chi-squared distribution, with which the test statistic is compared.

Common variants

Pearson's chi-squared test, introduced by Karl Pearson in 1900, is the best-known form. It computes the sum over cells of (observed − expected)² / expected and compares it with the chi-squared distribution with the appropriate degrees of freedom. It is used in two main settings: the test of independence, which asks whether two categorical variables in a contingency table are associated, and the goodness-of-fit test, which asks whether sample data match a specified distribution.

Assumptions

The test requires independent observations and adequately large expected cell counts (conventionally at least 5 in most cells). For 2×2 tables with small samples, Fisher's exact test is often preferred.
