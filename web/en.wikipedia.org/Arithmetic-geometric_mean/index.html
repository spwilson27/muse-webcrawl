Arithmetic–geometric mean

In mathematics, the arithmetic–geometric mean (AGM or agM) of two positive real numbers x and y is the mutual limit of two sequences: the arithmetic means and the geometric means of corresponding pairs of numbers.

Definition

To find the arithmetic–geometric mean of a0 = x and g0 = y, iterate as follows:

an+1 = (an + gn) / 2 (the arithmetic mean),

gn+1 = √(an gn) (the geometric mean),

until an and gn converge to the same limit, agM(x, y). Convergence is extremely fast (quadratic), so only a few iterations are generally needed to achieve high precision.

Example

To find the AGM of a0 = 24 and g0 = 6, iterate:

  • a1 = 15, g1 = 12
  • a2 = 13.5, g2 ≈ 13.4164
  • a3 ≈ 13.4582, g3 ≈ 13.4581

So agM(24, 6) ≈ 13.458.

History

The method was first studied by Lagrange and Legendre, and developed extensively by Gauss, who used it in connection with elliptic integrals. Gauss discovered that the AGM is related to the complete elliptic integral of the first kind: agM(a, b) = π/2 divided by an elliptic integral, giving an extremely rapid algorithm for computing elliptic integrals and hence π.

Applications

The arithmetic–geometric mean appears in fast algorithms for computing π (such as the Brent–Salamin algorithm), in the theory of elliptic functions and theta functions, and in geometry (for example the perimeter of an ellipse has no elementary closed form but can be expressed through the AGM).
