Minimum wage

A minimum wage is the lowest remuneration that employers can legally pay their employees — the price floor below which employees may not sell their labour. Most countries had introduced minimum wage legislation by the end of the 20th century; the first modern laws were enacted in New Zealand (1894) and Australia (1896). Supporters say it increases the standard of living of workers, reduces poverty and inequality, and stimulates demand; opponents argue it can increase unemployment, particularly among low-skilled and young workers, and raise prices.

History

Wage controls have older precedents — England's Statute of Labourers (1351) actually fixed maximum wages — but the modern statutory minimum began in Australasia. In the United States, a federal minimum wage was established by the Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938 at $0.25 per hour; the federal rate has been $7.25 per hour since 2009, with many states and cities setting higher floors. The United Kingdom introduced a National Minimum Wage in 1999 and a higher "National Living Wage" for older workers in 2016; Germany adopted a statutory minimum wage in 2015.

Economic debate

The neoclassical model predicts job losses when a wage floor exceeds the market-clearing wage, while monopsony models predict possible employment gains. Influential empirical work, such as Card and Krueger's 1994 fast-food study, found little or no negative employment effect from moderate increases; later research remains contested but widely finds small disemployment effects relative to wage gains for low-paid workers.
