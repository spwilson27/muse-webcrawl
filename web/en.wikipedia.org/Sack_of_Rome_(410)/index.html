Sack of Rome (410)

Date24 August 410 AD
LocationRome, Roman Empire
AttackersVisigoths under Alaric I
OutcomeThree days of plunder; Visigoths depart

The Sack of Rome on 24 August 410 AD was undertaken by the Visigoths led by their king, Alaric I. At that time Rome was no longer the administrative capital of the Western Roman Empire — that role had passed first to Mediolanum and then to Ravenna — but it remained the empire's largest city and its symbolic heart, styled the "Eternal City." The sack was a profound shock across the Roman world: Rome had not fallen to a foreign enemy for 800 years, since the Gauls under Brennus took the city in 390 BC.

Background

Alaric and his Goths had served as foederati (allied troops) of the empire and repeatedly sought a permanent settlement, land and official rank within it. After the execution of the Roman general Stilicho in 408 and the massacre of the families of Gothic soldiers in Roman service, Alaric marched on Italy. He besieged Rome twice in 408–409, extracting large ransoms and briefly setting up a rival emperor, Priscus Attalus, before negotiations with the court at Ravenna under Emperor Honorius finally collapsed.

The sack

On the night of 24 August 410 the Salarian Gate was opened to the Goths — later tradition held that sympathetic slaves or sympathisers let them in. The Visigoths plundered the city for three days. Many great palaces and public buildings were looted and some set on fire, including the Gardens of Sallust and the Basilica Aemilia, and the mausoleums of Augustus and Hadrian were ransacked, the imperial ashes scattered. Alaric, an Arian Christian, ordered that those who took refuge in the great basilicas of St Peter and St Paul be spared, and church treasures surrendered to his men were carried in procession to safety. Nevertheless there was killing, enslavement and widespread destruction; among the captives was the emperor's half-sister, Galla Placidia. After three days the Goths left the city laden with plunder and moved south toward Sicily and Africa; Alaric died later that year at Consentia.

Impact

News of the sack reverberated around the Mediterranean. In Bethlehem, St Jerome lamented: "The city which had taken the whole world was itself taken." The catastrophe provoked intense debate between pagans, who blamed the abandonment of the old gods, and Christians; it was in direct response that St Augustine wrote his great apologetic work The City of God. Militarily the damage to Rome was reparable, but the psychological blow to the prestige of the Western Empire was immense, and the event is commonly treated as a milestone in the decline and fall of the Western Roman Empire.
