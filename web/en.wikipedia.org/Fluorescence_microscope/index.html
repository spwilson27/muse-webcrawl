Fluorescence microscope

A fluorescence microscope is an optical microscope that uses fluorescence, instead of or in addition to scattering, reflection, and absorption, to study the properties of organic or inorganic substances. A fluorescence microscope relies on samples that fluoresce: they emit light of a longer wavelength after absorbing light of a shorter wavelength. Specimens are labelled with fluorescent dyes (fluorophores) or, in molecular biology, express fluorescent proteins such as GFP.

Principle

The specimen is illuminated with light of a specific excitation wavelength (usually ultraviolet or blue). Fluorophores absorb this light and emit light of longer wavelength (the Stokes shift). In the common epifluorescence design, a dichroic beamsplitter directs excitation light onto the specimen through the objective and passes the emitted fluorescence to the eyepiece or camera, while optical filters block the scattered excitation light, so fluorescent structures appear bright against a dark background.

Techniques

Major variants include confocal microscopy, which rejects out-of-focus light to produce optical sections; total internal reflection fluorescence (TIRF) microscopy, which images only a thin layer near the coverslip; FRET microscopy, which measures molecular proximity; and super-resolution methods such as STED and PALM/STORM, which surpass the diffraction limit. Fluorescence microscopy is fundamental to cell biology, immunofluorescence diagnostics, and live-cell imaging.
