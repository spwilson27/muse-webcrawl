Omertà

Omertà is a code of silence and honor originating in southern Italy, particularly associated with the Mafia organizations of Sicily (Cosa Nostra), Calabria ('Ndrangheta), and Campania (Camorra). It obliges silence about the activities of criminal organizations, forbids cooperation with authorities or outsiders, and requires that disputes be settled personally or within the group rather than through the law.

Origins

The word may derive from the Spanish hombredad or the Sicilian omu ("man"), reflecting an ideal of manliness: enduring hardship without complaint and never appealing to outside authority. The code developed in a society where state institutions were distrusted and often absent, and self-help justice was common. Some elements predate the Mafia itself, rooted in centuries of foreign domination of Sicily.

The code

Under omertà, it is considered shameful — even for an innocent person, or for the victim of a crime — to inform the police. A wounded man was traditionally expected to tell his assailant nothing if questioned, saying only that if he recovered he would take his own revenge. Breaking the code could be punished by death. The code has been a major obstacle to law enforcement in southern Italy.

Breaking the code

From the 1980s, a number of mafiosi — most famously Tommaso Buscetta, whose testimony underpinned the Maxi Trial of 1986–87 — became state witnesses (pentiti), breaking omertà in exchange for protection. Their evidence enabled major prosecutions, though the code remains a powerful cultural force in parts of Italy and in Italian organized crime abroad.
