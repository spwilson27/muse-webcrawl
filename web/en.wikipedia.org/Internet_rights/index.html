Internet rights

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Internet rights are the legal and human rights concerning access to, and use of, the Internet and related information and communication technologies. The concept overlaps with digital rights, a term used to describe the human rights that allow individuals to access, use, create and publish digital media or to access and use computers and other electronic devices or communications networks. Internet rights include freedom of expression online, privacy and data protection, the right of access to the Internet, net neutrality, and protection against arbitrary censorship and surveillance.

Background

The debate over Internet rights emerged alongside the mass adoption of the World Wide Web in the 1990s. Early declarations, such as the 1996 "Declaration of the Independence of Cyberspace" by John Perry Barlow, asserted that the online world should be free from government interference. As the Internet became central to commerce, education and civic life, governments and international bodies began framing Internet access and online freedoms in the language of established human rights law, particularly the Universal Declaration of Human Rights and the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights.

Access as a right

Several states and international organisations have treated Internet access as a right or as an essential enabler of other rights. In 2011, a report by the United Nations Special Rapporteur on freedom of opinion and expression, Frank La Rue, described access to the Internet as a fundamental human right linked to freedom of expression. Countries including Finland, Estonia, France, Greece and Costa Rica have recognised aspects of Internet access in law or constitutional jurisprudence. Finland in 2010 became the first country to make broadband access a legal right for every citizen.

Freedom of expression and censorship

Internet rights encompass protection from state and private censorship. Issues include website blocking, content takedown regimes, liability of intermediaries, and the moderation policies of large platforms. Critics of expansive takedown rules argue they chill lawful speech, while proponents contend that regulation is necessary to combat illegal content, harassment and disinformation. The "manila principles" and other civil-society frameworks propose due-process safeguards for content removal.

Privacy and surveillance

Privacy online is a core Internet right, covering data protection, the right to encryption, anonymity, and freedom from mass surveillance. Landmark disclosures in 2013 concerning signals-intelligence programmes intensified debate, leading to reforms and to instruments such as the European Union's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), which codified rights of access, rectification and erasure of personal data.

Net neutrality

Net neutrality — the principle that Internet service providers should treat all data equally — is often framed as an Internet right because it affects equal access to information and the ability of smaller speakers to reach audiences. Regulatory approaches differ: the European Union adopted Open Internet rules in 2015, while the United States has alternated between classification regimes.
