The Treaty of Versailles was a peace treaty signed on 28 June 1919, exactly five years after the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand. As the most important of the peace treaties that ended the First World War, it ended the state of war between Germany and most of the Allied Powers, and was signed in the Hall of Mirrors of the Palace of Versailles — the same room where the German Empire had been proclaimed in 1871.

Terms

Article 231, the "war guilt" clause, assigned responsibility for the war to Germany and its allies, providing the legal basis for reparations, later fixed at 132 billion gold marks. Germany lost about 13% of its territory and some 10% of its population: Alsace-Lorraine returned to France, Eupen-Malmédy went to Belgium, northern Schleswig to Denmark, and large eastern territories to the reborn Poland, with Danzig made a free city. All German colonies became League of Nations mandates. The Rhineland was demilitarized and occupied. The German army was limited to 100,000 men, conscription was abolished, and tanks, military aircraft, and submarines were prohibited.

The treaty also created the League of Nations and the International Labour Organization.

Reception and consequences

The German delegation signed under protest, and the treaty was widely resented in Germany as a Diktat; John Maynard Keynes attacked its economics in The Economic Consequences of the Peace (1919). The U.S. Senate refused to ratify it, and the United States made a separate peace in 1921. Historians debate the treaty's harshness and its role in the instability that led to the Second World War; the reparations and war-guilt provisions became central grievances exploited by the Nazi Party.
