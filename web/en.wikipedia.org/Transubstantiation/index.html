Transubstantiation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Transubstantiation (Latin: transsubstantiatio; Greek: metousiosis) is, according to the teaching of the Catholic Church and some others, "the change of the whole substance of bread into the substance of the Body of Christ and of the whole substance of wine into the substance of the Blood of Christ". This change is brought about in the eucharistic prayer through the efficacy of the word of Christ and by the action of the Holy Spirit. However, the outward characteristics (the accidents) of the bread and wine — that is, their taste, smell, colour and texture — remain unchanged.

History

The term gained currency in the 12th century, and the doctrine was formally defined at the Fourth Lateran Council in 1215. Scholastic theologians, above all Thomas Aquinas, articulated the doctrine using Aristotelian categories of substance and accident. The Council of Trent (1551) reaffirmed transubstantiation against Reformation-era denials.

Theological discussion

During the Reformation, most Protestant traditions rejected transubstantiation: Lutherans taught a real presence often termed sacramental union, Reformed churches taught a spiritual presence, and others held memorialist views. Eastern Orthodox theology affirms a real change (metabole) while generally declining to define its mechanism. Ecumenical dialogues have found substantial convergence on the reality of Christ's presence while differing on metaphysical explanation.
