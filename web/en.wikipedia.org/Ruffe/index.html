The ruffe (Gymnocephalus cernua), also known as the Eurasian ruffe or pope, is a freshwater fish found in temperate regions of Europe and northern Asia. It has been introduced into the Great Lakes of North America, where it is considered an invasive species. It is a small member of the perch family (Percidae), typically 12–25 cm (5–10 in) long, with a mottled olive-brown coloration and fused dorsal fins armed with spines.

Description and biology

The ruffe has a deep body, large eyes set high on the head, and a down-turned mouth adapted to bottom feeding. Its first (spiny) and second (soft-rayed) dorsal fins are joined, unlike in its relative the European perch, whose fins are separate. A highly developed lateral-line system and sensitive chemoreceptors allow it to feed in darkness on insect larvae, small crustaceans and fish eggs. Ruffe mature early, spawn repeatedly from April to June, and females can lay up to 200,000 eggs per year, giving the species a high reproductive rate.

Distribution and invasiveness

Native to the Caspian, Black, Aral, Baltic and Arctic Ocean basins, the ruffe spread through western Europe largely via canals and the bait trade. It was first recorded in North America in the St. Louis River estuary at Duluth, Minnesota, in 1986, probably introduced in ballast water. From there it spread through Lake Superior and into other Great Lakes. Because it competes with native species such as yellow perch and walleye for food and can dominate new habitats, it is monitored by fisheries agencies and its possession and transport are regulated in several jurisdictions.
