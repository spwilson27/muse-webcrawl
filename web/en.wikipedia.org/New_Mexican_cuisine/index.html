New Mexican cuisine is the regional cooking style of the U.S. state of New Mexico, a fusion of Pueblo Native American and Hispano Spanish colonial foodways with later Mexican influences. It is distinguished from neighbouring Tex-Mex and interior Mexican cuisines above all by its use of the New Mexico chile, in both red and green forms, as the defining sauce ingredient.

The official state question of New Mexico, adopted in 1999, is "Red or green?" — referring to the diner's choice of chile sauce. Ordering both is known as "Christmas".

Ingredients

The New Mexico chile is a cultivar group of Capsicum annuum developed in the state, famously grown in the Hatch Valley. Other staples include corn, beans, and squash (the Pueblo "three sisters"), blue cornmeal, piñon (pine nuts), and flour and corn tortillas. Sopaipillas — puffed fried dough served with honey — and biscochitos, anise-flavoured sugar cookies (the official state cookie), are signature breads and sweets.

Characteristic dishes

Signature dishes include stacked or rolled enchiladas (often with a fried egg on top), breakfast burritos (popularised in New Mexico in the 1970s), carne adovada (pork braised in red chile), posole, green chile stew, tamales, huevos rancheros, and the green chile cheeseburger, promoted along a state-designated Green Chile Cheeseburger Trail. Navajo tacos made with fry bread belong to the state's Indigenous food traditions.

History

The cuisine developed from Pueblo agricultural practices adopted by Spanish settlers after the founding of Santa Fe in 1610, evolving in relative isolation for centuries. Ingredients such as wheat, pork, and cattle arrived with the colonists, while techniques and crops of the Rio Grande Pueblos remained foundational.
