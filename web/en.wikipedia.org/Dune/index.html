Dune

A dune is a landform composed of wind- or water-driven sand, typically found in deserts, on beaches and in river channels. Dunes occur where loose sand is available, winds blow frequently enough to move it, and there is an obstacle or change in conditions that causes deposition. Individual dunes range from a few metres to several hundred metres in height; the tallest, such as those in the Badain Jaran Desert of China, exceed 400 metres.

Formation

Sand is transported by the wind mainly through saltation — grains bounce along the surface in short hops — and surface creep. When wind speed drops, grains are deposited; over time a mound grows, developing a gentle windward (stoss) slope and a steep lee slope at the angle of repose of dry sand, roughly 30–34 degrees, known as the slip face. Sand cascading down the slip face makes the whole dune migrate downwind, sometimes tens of metres per year.

Types of dunes

  • Barchan (crescentic) dunes — crescent-shaped, horns pointing downwind; form where wind blows from one direction and sand supply is limited.
  • Transverse dunes — long ridges perpendicular to the wind, formed with abundant sand.
  • Linear (longitudinal) dunes — long ridges parallel to the prevailing wind direction.
  • Star dunes — pyramidal dunes with arms radiating from a central peak, formed by winds from multiple directions; among the tallest dune types.
  • Parabolic dunes — U-shaped dunes with horns pointing upwind, commonly stabilised by vegetation on coasts.
  • Dome dunes — low circular or oval mounds without a slip face.

Coastal and subaqueous dunes

Coastal dune systems form where onshore winds blow dry beach sand inland; they provide natural flood defence and distinctive habitats, often stabilised by marram grass and other salt-tolerant plants. Under water, sand ripples and dunes form on river and sea beds by the same principles of flow and deposition, scaled by current strength.

Extraterrestrial dunes

Dunes are widespread on Mars, where fields of barchans and linear dunes occur in craters and polar regions, and on Saturn's moon Titan, where vast equatorial seas of linear dunes are composed of organic hydrocarbon particles rather than silicate sand.
