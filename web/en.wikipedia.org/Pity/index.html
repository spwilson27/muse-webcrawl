Pity

Pity is a sympathetic sorrow for one who suffers. It is a feeling of sympathy and compassion for the suffering of other people. Pity is connected with regret and with a sense of the suffering of another — that someone else is in distress — together with a willingness to relieve it. The word derives from the Latin pietas, via Old French pitié, and is closely related to the concept of piety.

Philosophical accounts

Aristotle, in the Rhetoric, defined pity (eleos) as a certain pain at an apparently destructive or painful evil befalling one who does not deserve it, which one might expect to befall oneself or a friend. For Aristotle, pity requires the judgement that the sufferer's misfortune is undeserved and that we ourselves are vulnerable to something similar.

David Hume analysed pity as a form of sympathy, and Adam Smith treated related sympathetic responses as the foundation of moral judgement in The Theory of Moral Sentiments. Jean-Jacques Rousseau regarded pity as a natural, pre-rational sentiment that tempers self-love and forms the basis of humanity's social virtues.

Criticism

Friedrich Nietzsche was famously critical of pity (Mitleid), arguing that it multiplies suffering, preserves what should perish, and disguises a subtle contempt for the sufferer. Others, such as Immanuel Kant, distinguished pity as an inclination from beneficence done from duty. In contemporary usage, "pity" often carries a condescending nuance — to be pitied can feel demeaning — and is distinguished from empathy and compassion, which imply a more equal and action-oriented stance toward the sufferer.

In religion and literature

Divine pity or mercy is central to many religions: the Hebrew Bible's rachamim, the Christian virtue of mercy, and the Islamic name of God ar-Rahman ("the Most Merciful") all emphasise compassionate feeling for the suffering. In literature, pity — together with fear — is the emotion that tragedy arouses according to Aristotle's Poetics, leading to catharsis.
