Cherokee Outlet

The Cherokee Outlet, often called the Cherokee Strip, was a tract of land in what is now northwestern Oklahoma. It consisted of about 60 miles (97 km) by 226 miles (364 km) of land — roughly 6 million acres — lying between the Cherokee Nation's main reservation lands and the Kansas border. The strip ran along the 37th parallel, bounded on the east by the 96th meridian and on the west by the 100th meridian.

History

The Outlet was created under the Treaty of New Echota in 1835, which guaranteed the Cherokee people a perpetual outlet to hunting grounds in the West after their removal from the southeastern United States. Following the American Civil War, the 1866 treaty required the Cherokee to allow other Native American tribes to settle in the Outlet and contemplated its eventual sale.

During the 1870s and 1880s, large cattle companies leased grazing land in the Outlet from the Cherokee Nation. The Cherokee Strip Live Stock Association organized these leases beginning in 1883. Pressure from settlers and the federal government mounted, and the Cherokee agreed to sell the Outlet to the United States in 1891 for about $8.6 million.

The Land Run of 1893

On September 16, 1893, the Outlet was opened to non-Native settlement in the largest of Oklahoma's land runs. An estimated 100,000 people raced to claim some 42,000 homesteads of 160 acres each. Towns such as Enid, Woodward, Alva, and Perry sprang up almost overnight.

Legacy

The region today forms much of northwestern Oklahoma, including the counties of the Oklahoma Panhandle's eastern neighbors. The name "Cherokee Strip" is still used for the area, and a regional museum in Perry preserves its history.
