Deism

Deism is a philosophical and religious position holding that a creator God can be known through reason and observation of the natural world, but rejecting revealed religion, scripture-based authority, miracles and divine intervention. Deists often compare God to a watchmaker who designs the universe, sets it running according to natural laws, and does not interfere thereafter.

History

Deism flourished in seventeenth- and eighteenth-century England and France during the Enlightenment. Writers such as John Toland, Matthew Tindal and Anthony Collins attacked priestcraft and defended "natural religion"; Voltaire promoted deism in France, and Jean-Jacques Rousseau's "Profession of Faith of the Savoyard Vicar" presented an emotional variant. In America, deist ideas influenced figures including Thomas Paine, whose The Age of Reason (1794) popularised the position, as well as Thomas Jefferson and Benjamin Franklin.

Beliefs and decline

Classical deists affirmed one God, the immortality of the soul and moral accountability, while denying the divinity of Jesus and the authority of the Bible. The movement waned in the nineteenth century as evangelical revivals, Romanticism and later scientific naturalism reshaped religious thought, though deist ideas persist in modern forms of natural theology and in some strands of Unitarianism.
