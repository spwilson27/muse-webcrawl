Treaty of Crépy

The Treaty of Crépy (also the Peace of Crépy) was concluded on 18 September 1544 at Crépy-en-Laonnois, Picardy, between King Francis I of France and Holy Roman Emperor Charles V, ending the fourth and last of the Habsburg–Valois Italian Wars fought between the two rulers. The settlement came after Charles's invasion of France had stalled before Paris and both monarchs faced financial exhaustion and the Ottoman threat in the Mediterranean.

Terms

Francis renounced his claims to Naples, Milan, Flanders, and Artois, while Charles abandoned his claim to the Duchy of Burgundy. France recovered territories in Piedmont temporarily and promised assistance against the Ottomans and support for a general council of the Church. A secret clause envisaged a marriage between Charles's daughter or niece and Francis's son Charles, Duke of Orléans, who would receive Milan or the Netherlands as a dowry — a scheme voided by the young duke's death in 1545. Hostilities resumed under Francis's successor Henry II in 1551, and the Franco-Habsburg rivalry was settled only by the Peace of Cateau-Cambrésis in 1559.
