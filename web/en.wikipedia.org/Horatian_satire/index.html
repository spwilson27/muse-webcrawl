Horatian satire

Horatian satire is a form of satire named after the Roman poet Horace (65–8 BCE), whose Satires (Sermones) established a gentle, tolerant, and amused mode of criticising human folly. In contrast to the harsher, indignant Juvenalian satire, the Horatian satirist presents himself as a genial observer who laughs at the failings of others — and often of himself — with the aim of reforming them through wit rather than attacking them with contempt.

Characteristics

Horatian satire is marked by a conversational tone, urbane humour, and a preference for depicting everyday characters and minor vices: greed, ambition, social climbing, and self-delusion. Its speaker is typically worldly and self-deprecating, inviting the reader to share his amused detachment rather than his anger. The mode aims to entertain while it instructs, following Horace's own dictum that poetry should both delight and teach.

Influence

The Horatian mode strongly influenced later writers including Alexander Pope in works such as his Imitations of Horace, and it remains the default register for much light verse, humorous essay writing, and comic commentary. Critics commonly contrast the Horatian and Juvenalian styles as the two poles of satire: the smiling reformer versus the outraged moralist.
