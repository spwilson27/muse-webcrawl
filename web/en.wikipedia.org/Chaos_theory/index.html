Chaos theory is an interdisciplinary area of mathematics and science focusing on the behaviour of dynamical systems that are highly sensitive to initial conditions — an effect popularly called the butterfly effect. Small differences in initial conditions yield widely diverging outcomes for chaotic systems, rendering long-term prediction impossible in general, even though the systems are fully deterministic: their future is completely determined by their initial state, with no randomness involved. Chaotic behaviour occurs in weather, fluid turbulence, population dynamics, economics, and many other domains.

History

Henri Poincaré's 1890s work on the three-body problem first revealed the possibility of non-periodic, extremely complicated deterministic motion. Modern chaos theory crystallized in 1963 when meteorologist Edward Lorenz, modelling atmospheric convection, found that rounding his computer's initial values changed the forecast completely; his simplified equations produced the famous butterfly-shaped Lorenz attractor. In the 1970s, work by Robert May on the logistic map, Mitchell Feigenbaum's discovery of universal constants in period-doubling bifurcations, and Mandelbrot's fractal geometry unified the field.

Characteristics

A system is typically called chaotic if it has: (1) sensitive dependence on initial conditions (measured by a positive Lyapunov exponent), (2) topological transitivity/mixing, and (3) dense periodic orbits. Chaotic trajectories often live on strange attractors — sets with fractal structure, like the Lorenz and Rössler attractors.

Applications

Chaos theory informs weather forecasting limits (about two weeks), cardiac and neural dynamics, population ecology, cryptography, robotics, and engineering control of chaos (small perturbations used to stabilize unstable periodic orbits, e.g., in lasers and spacecraft trajectories).
