Ibn al-Haytham

Born: c. 965, Basra
Died: c. 1040, Cairo
Known for: Book of Optics, experimental method, Alhazen's problem
Latin name: Alhazen

Ibn al-Haytham (Abū ʿAlī al-Ḥasan ibn al-Ḥasan ibn al-Haytham; Latinised as Alhazen; c. 965 – c. 1040) was an Arab mathematician, astronomer, and physicist of the Islamic Golden Age, often called "the father of optics".

Life

Born in Basra, he was invited to Egypt by the Fatimid caliph al-Hakim to regulate Nile flooding; unable to achieve this, he reportedly feigned madness and remained under house arrest in Cairo until the caliph's death, thereafter supporting himself by copying manuscripts while writing his scientific works.

Optics and method

His seven-volume Book of Optics (Kitāb al-Manāẓir, 1011–1021) overturned ancient emission theories of vision, arguing that light from objects enters the eye, and combined geometrical optics with controlled experiments on reflection, refraction, mirrors, lenses, and the camera obscura. "Alhazen's problem" — finding the reflection point on spherical mirrors — he solved geometrically. His insistence on mathematical proof tested by experiment is frequently cited as an early form of the scientific method, and the Optics, in Latin translation, influenced Bacon, Kepler, Snell, and Descartes.

Other work

He wrote on astronomy (critiquing Ptolemy's physical inconsistencies), number theory (including Wilson's theorem's first statement), and geometry, with some 200 works attributed to him.
