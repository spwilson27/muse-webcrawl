Robert Owen (14 May 1771 – 17 November 1858) was a Welsh textile manufacturer, philanthropist and social reformer, and a founder of utopian socialism and the co-operative movement. He believed that human character is formed by environment, and that better conditions, education, and surroundings would produce better people—a philosophy he put into practice in his mills.

New Lanark

In 1800 Owen became manager and part-owner of the cotton mills at New Lanark, Scotland, founded by David Dale, whose daughter Caroline he married. There he improved housing and sanitation, opened the company store selling quality goods at low prices, reduced working hours, ended the employment of very young children, and in 1816 opened the Institute for the Formation of Character, including what is often described as Britain's first infant school. New Lanark remained profitable and became internationally famous, receiving thousands of visitors including royalty.

Later ventures and influence

Owen advocateded factory legislation, helping secure the Cotton Mills and Factories Act 1819, and promoted his "villages of co-operation" as a remedy for poverty. In 1825 he purchased the community of New Harmony, Indiana, and invested most of his fortune in an experimental communal settlement, which dissolved within a few years. Back in Britain he became a leader of the early trade-union and co-operative movements, presiding over the Grand National Consolidated Trades Union (1834). His followers, the Owenites, influenced later socialist and cooperative thought; the term "socialism" entered common usage through Owenite publications in the 1830s.
