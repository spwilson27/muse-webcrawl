Wharf

A wharf is a man-made structure on the shore of a harbour or on the bank of a river or canal where ships may dock to load and unload cargo or passengers. It normally includes one or more berths and may include piers, warehouses, cranes, and railway or road access for the transfer of goods.

Structure and terminology

A wharf is generally built parallel to the shore, in contrast to a pier or jetty, which extends out into the water. Historically wharves were timber-piled structures; modern wharves are usually built of concrete and steel sheet piling, with bollards for mooring lines and fenders to protect both vessel and structure. The term is sometimes said to derive from the Old English hwearf, meaning a bank or shore.

History

Wharves have been built since antiquity; the ports of the Roman world featured masonry quays along the Tiber and at Ostia. In medieval and early modern London, the legal quays along the Thames handled taxed imports, and "wharfinger" was the occupation of the wharf's owner or keeper. The industrial age brought vast wharf complexes in ports such as Liverpool, Hamburg, and New York, many of which have since been redeveloped for housing, leisure, and tourism — London's Docklands and Canary Wharf being a prominent example.
