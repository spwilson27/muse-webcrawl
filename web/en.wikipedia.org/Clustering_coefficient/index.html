Clustering coefficient

In graph theory and network analysis, the clustering coefficient is a measure of the degree to which nodes in a graph tend to cluster together. It quantifies, roughly, how close a node's neighbors are to forming a complete subgraph (a clique). Real-world networks—social, biological, and technological—typically show far more clustering than random graphs of the same size.

Local clustering coefficient

The local clustering coefficient of a node is the proportion of possible connections among its neighbors that actually exist. If a node has k neighbors, there are at most k(k−1)/2 edges between them in an undirected graph; the local coefficient is the fraction of those possible edges that are present. A value of 1 means all the node's neighbors are connected to one another; a value of 0 means none of them are.

Global clustering coefficient

The global clustering coefficient, also called transitivity, is based on counting triangles in the network: it is the ratio of the number of closed triplets (triangles) to the total number of connected triplets of nodes. Averaging the local coefficients over all nodes gives a related but distinct measure. Together with short average path lengths, high clustering is a defining property of small-world networks, as formalized in the Watts–Strogatz model.

Applications

Clustering coefficients are used to study the structure of social networks, where they capture the tendency of friends of a person to know each other; in neuroscience, to describe connectivity in the brain; and in epidemiology, where clustering influences how diseases spread through a population.
