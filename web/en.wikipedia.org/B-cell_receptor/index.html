B-cell receptor

The B-cell receptor (BCR) is a transmembrane protein complex on the surface of B lymphocytes. It consists of a membrane-bound immunoglobulin (antibody) molecule — usually IgM or IgD — associated with the signal-transducing heterodimer Igα/Igβ (CD79a/CD79b). The BCR is the fundamental means by which B cells recognize specific antigens.

Structure

The membrane immunoglobulin has two heavy chains and two light chains joined by disulfide bonds, forming a Y shape whose two tips constitute the antigen-binding sites. Because the cytoplasmic tail of the membrane antibody is very short, signalling is carried out by the associated Igα/Igβ chains, which bear immunoreceptor tyrosine-based activation motifs (ITAMs) in their cytoplasmic domains.

Signalling and activation

When antigen cross-links BCRs on the cell surface, tyrosine kinases including Lyn, Syk, and Btk phosphorylate the ITAMs, triggering signalling cascades that lead to B-cell activation, proliferation, and differentiation into antibody-secreting plasma cells and memory B cells. Co-receptors such as CD19 and CD21 modulate the activation threshold.

Diversity

The enormous diversity of B-cell receptors is generated during B-cell development in the bone marrow by V(D)J recombination of immunoglobulin gene segments, together with junctional diversity. After antigen encounter, somatic hypermutation further refines antibody affinity in germinal centres.
