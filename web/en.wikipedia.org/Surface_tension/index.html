Surface tension

Common symbolsγ, σ
SI unitnewton per metre (N/m)
Other unitsdyn/cm, erg/cm²
In SI base unitskg·s−2

Surface tension is the tendency of liquid surfaces at rest to shrink into the minimum surface area possible. Surface tension is what allows objects with a higher density than water, such as razor blades or insects like water striders, to float on a water surface without becoming even partly submerged.

At liquid–air interfaces, surface tension results from the greater attraction of liquid molecules to each other (due to cohesion) than to the molecules in the air (due to adhesion). There are two primary mechanisms in play. One is an inward force on the surface molecules causing the liquid to contract. The second is a tangential force parallel to the surface of the liquid. The net effect is that the liquid behaves as if its surface were covered with a stretched elastic membrane.

Definition and units

Surface tension, represented by the symbol γ, is measured as the force exerted per unit length of a line drawn in the surface, or equivalently as the energy required to increase the surface area by a unit amount. Its SI unit is the newton per metre, equivalent to the joule per square metre. At 20 °C, the surface tension of pure water against air is about 0.0728 N/m; mercury has a much higher value, about 0.465 N/m, while soap solutions have much lower values, which is why they form stable bubbles.

Causes

Molecules inside a liquid are attracted in all directions by their neighbours, whereas molecules at the surface experience a net inward attraction because there are no liquid molecules above them. Creating additional surface therefore requires work, since more molecules must be brought to the energetically unfavourable surface. This energy cost is the microscopic origin of surface tension.

Effects

Surface tension is responsible for the spherical shape of small droplets and soap bubbles (via the Young–Laplace equation, which relates pressure differences across a curved interface to surface tension and curvature), for capillary action — the rise of liquids in narrow tubes — and for phenomena such as the beading of water on hydrophobic surfaces. Detergents and other surfactants reduce surface tension, enabling water to spread and wet surfaces more effectively. Temperature generally lowers surface tension, which vanishes at a liquid's critical point.

Measurement

Common methods of measurement include the Wilhelmy plate, the Du Noüy ring, the pendant drop method, capillary rise, and the spinning drop technique used for very low interfacial tensions.
