Aliquot sum

AreaNumber theory
Notations(n)
DefinitionSum of the proper divisors of n

In number theory, the aliquot sum s(n) of a positive integer n is the sum of all proper divisors of n, that is, all divisors of n other than n itself. It can be used to characterize the prime numbers, perfect numbers, defective numbers, and abundant numbers, and to define the aliquot sequence of a number.

For example, the proper divisors of 12 are 1, 2, 3, 4, and 6, so the aliquot sum of 12 is 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 6 = 16. The aliquot sum of a prime number p is 1, since its only proper divisor is 1.

Definitions

The aliquot sum is related to the divisor function σ(n), the sum of all divisors of n including n itself, by the formula s(n) = σ(n) − n. A number n is called:

  • perfect if s(n) = n (e.g., 6, 28, 496, 8128);
  • deficient (or defective) if s(n) < n;
  • abundant if s(n) > n (e.g., 12, the smallest abundant number).

Two distinct numbers are called amicable if each equals the aliquot sum of the other, as with 220 and 284. A cycle of the aliquot map of length greater than two gives a sociable number chain.

Aliquot sequences

Iterating the aliquot sum produces the aliquot sequence n, s(n), s(s(n)), … Such a sequence may terminate at 0 (after reaching a prime, whose aliquot sum is 1, then 0), enter a cycle (a perfect, amicable, or sociable loop), or grow without bound. Catalan's conjecture (also called the Catalan–Dickson conjecture) proposes that every aliquot sequence ends in one of these ways; whether sequences starting at numbers such as 276 ever terminate remains an open problem.

Values

The aliquot sums of the first few positive integers are: s(1) = 0, s(2) = 1, s(3) = 1, s(4) = 3, s(5) = 1, s(6) = 6, s(7) = 1, s(8) = 7, s(9) = 4, s(10) = 8, s(11) = 1, s(12) = 16. Numbers n for which s(n) = 0 are exactly 1; numbers for which s(n) = 1 are exactly the primes.
