Kirchhoff's circuit laws

Kirchhoff's laws
Formulated byGustav Kirchhoff
Year1845
LawsCurrent law (KCL); Voltage law (KVL)

Kirchhoff's laws relate currents at a junction and voltages around a loop.

Kirchhoff's circuit laws are two equalities that deal with the current and potential difference (voltage) in the lumped element model of electrical circuits. They were first described in 1845 by German physicist Gustav Kirchhoff and are widely used in electrical engineering for circuit analysis.

Kirchhoff's current law (KCL)

The current entering any junction is equal to the current leaving that junction; that is, the algebraic sum of currents in a network meeting at a point is zero:

Σ Iin = Σ Iout,  i.e.  Σk Ik = 0

KCL is a consequence of the conservation of electric charge: since charge does not accumulate at a node (in the steady, lumped model), what flows in must flow out.

Kirchhoff's voltage law (KVL)

The directed sum of the potential differences (voltages) around any closed loop in a circuit is zero:

Σk Vk = 0

KVL expresses conservation of energy: a charge carried around a closed loop returns to its starting point with unchanged potential energy, so the sum of voltage rises equals the sum of voltage drops.

Limitations

The laws follow from Maxwell's equations under the assumptions of the lumped-element model, in which the net charge on every component is zero and magnetic flux is confined within inductors. KCL can be violated in appearance when significant parasitic capacitance accumulates charge, and KVL fails when a changing magnetic flux links the measurement loop itself (the classic two-resistor paradox analysed by Walter Lewin illustrates the role of non-conservative fields). For high-frequency distributed systems, transmission-line theory replaces the lumped model.

Applications

Combined with Ohm's law and component constitutive relations, Kirchhoff's laws yield the systems of equations solved by nodal analysis and mesh analysis — the standard hand-calculation and simulation (SPICE) techniques for circuit analysis.
