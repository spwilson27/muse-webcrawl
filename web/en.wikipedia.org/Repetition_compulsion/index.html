Repetition compulsion

Repetition compulsion is a neurotic defence mechanism in psychology, first described by Sigmund Freud, in which a person repeats a traumatic event or its circumstances over and over again. This includes re-enacting the event, or putting oneself in situations that have a high probability of the event occurring again, even when the experiences are painful or destructive.

Repetition compulsion describes the puzzling tendency to re-enact painful experiences.

Freud's account

Freud introduced the concept prominently in Beyond the Pleasure Principle (1920). He noted that patients in psychoanalysis repeated repressed material as contemporary experiences rather than remembering it as the past, and that shell-shocked soldiers repeatedly revisited their trauma in dreams. He also cited the child's fort-da game, in which a toddler repeatedly threw away and retrieved a spool, as an attempt to master the distress of the mother's absence. Because such repetitions appear to contradict the pleasure principle, Freud used them as evidence for a conservative drive in the mind and ultimately for his controversial hypothesis of the death drive (Thanatos).

Later interpretations

Subsequent theorists have reframed repetition compulsion in terms of attempted mastery, attachment patterns, and the re-creation of early relational scenarios in later life — for example, repeatedly choosing partners resembling an abusive parent. In modern trauma psychology, related phenomena are discussed under re-enactment and traumatic re-experiencing, including the intrusive memories characteristic of post-traumatic stress disorder.
