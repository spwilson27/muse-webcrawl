Constitutional convention (politics)

Constitutional convention
TypeDeliberative assembly
PurposeDrafting or revising a constitution
Famous examplesPhiladelphia Convention (1787); German Parlamentarischer Rat (1948–49); South African negotiations (1994–96)

A constitutional convention is a gathering for the purpose of writing a new constitution or revising an existing one. Members of a constitutional convention are often, though not necessarily, elected by popular vote specifically for that task, distinguishing a convention from an ordinary legislature that happens to amend a constitution.

History

The best-known example is the Philadelphia Convention of 1787, which, called to revise the Articles of Confederation, instead drafted the Constitution of the United States. In the United States, state constitutional conventions have been used repeatedly to rewrite state constitutions, and Article V of the federal constitution provides for a convention to propose amendments, though one has never been called.

Conventions in other countries

Many countries emerging from war, colonial rule or authoritarian government have used conventions or constituent assemblies: the Parliamentary Council that drafted West Germany's Basic Law (1948–49), Iceland's Constitutional Council (2011), Ireland's Constitutional Convention (2012–14) and Citizens' Assembly, and Chile's elected Constitutional Convention (2021–22), whose draft was rejected in a referendum.

Conventions versus referendums

Drafts produced by conventions are commonly submitted to a referendum for ratification. Advocates argue that a dedicated, often partly sortition-based body can deliberate more freely than a legislature; critics question the democratic legitimacy of unelected delegates and the risk of "runaway" conventions exceeding their mandate.
