Louisiana Creoles (French: Créoles de Louisiane) are people descended from the inhabitants of colonial Louisiana during the periods of French (1699–1763, 1800–1803) and Spanish (1763–1800) rule. The term originally denoted those born in the colony — of French, Spanish, African or mixed heritage — as distinct from immigrants born abroad. Louisiana Creoles share a cultural heritage marked by the French and Creole languages, Catholicism, and distinctive foodways, music and architecture. During the 19th century, the free people of colour of New Orleans (gens de couleur libres), many of them Creoles of mixed European and African descent, formed a notable third class between whites and the enslaved in the city's caste system.

Etymology and definition

"Creole" derives from the Spanish criollo and Portuguese crioulo, ultimately from criar, "to raise" — meaning born and raised in the colony. In Louisiana the term's scope has been contested: it has been claimed by white descendants of French and Spanish colonists, by Creoles of colour, and by the broader mixed-heritage community. Historians generally use it for the whole population formed in colonial Louisiana before the Louisiana Purchase of 1803 and its subsequent acculturation.

History

French colonisation began in 1699. The colony's population grew through French and German settlers, enslaved West Africans (especially from Senegambia), and unions among Europeans, Africans and Native Americans. Under Spanish rule the manumission practice of coartación allowed enslaved people to purchase freedom, enlarging the free population of colour. After the Haitian Revolution, thousands of refugees from Saint-Domingue — white, free coloured and enslaved — doubled New Orleans's population in 1809–1810, reinforcing its French-speaking Creole culture.

Free Creoles of colour in antebellum New Orleans were often educated, property-owning artisans, musicians and merchants; some owned plantations and enslaved people themselves. The Creole community of the Cane River region, founded by Marie Thérèse Coincoin and her descendants, centred on Isle Brevelle and includes the Melrose Plantation historic site. The imposition of the American binary racial order after the Purchase, and especially after Reconstruction, progressively erased the intermediate status of Creoles of colour; the 1896 Plessy v. Ferguson case — itself brought by the Creole Comité des Citoyens of New Orleans, with Homer Plessy, a Creole of colour, as plaintiff — entrenched segregation.

Culture

Language

Louisiana Creole (Kouri-Vini) is a French-based creole language distinct from Louisiana (Cajun) French, historically spoken by Creoles of all races. Both languages declined sharply in the 20th century under English-only schooling policies, and revival efforts continue today.

Music and food

Creoles of colour were central to the birth of jazz in New Orleans — figures such as Jelly Roll Morton proudly asserted Creole identity — and to zydeco music in southwest Louisiana. Creole cuisine, distinct in its urban, tomato-using forms from Cajun cooking, gave the world gumbo, jambalaya and étouffée.

Architecture

Creole architecture includes the raised Creole cottage, French Quarter townhouses with courtyards, and the use of bousillage (mud and moss) construction, blending French, Spanish, African and Caribbean building traditions.
