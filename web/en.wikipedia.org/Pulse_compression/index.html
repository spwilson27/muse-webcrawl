Pulse compression

Pulse compression is a signal processing technique used in radar and sonar to obtain the range resolution of a very short pulse while retaining the energy—and hence detection range—of a long transmitted pulse. The transmitter sends a long pulse modulated in frequency or phase; on reception, a matched filter compresses the echo into a narrow spike whose width depends on the signal bandwidth rather than the pulse duration.

Principle

For a simple short pulse, fine range resolution requires high peak power to put enough energy on target. Pulse compression decouples these: energy is set by the long pulse length, while resolution is set by the bandwidth B of the modulation, giving an effective compressed width of about 1/B. The ratio of the transmitted pulse length to the compressed width is called the pulse compression ratio.

Modulation schemes

Linear frequency modulation (chirp) sweeps the carrier frequency across the pulse and is compressed with a dispersive delay line or digital matched filter; it is tolerant of Doppler shift, which merely shifts the compressed peak in range. Phase coding divides the pulse into chips with binary or polyphase codes such as Barker codes or Frank codes; phase-coded systems achieve low time sidelobes only with good Doppler tolerance trade-offs. Imperfect compression produces range sidelobes that can mask weak targets near strong ones, so weighting (windowing) is often applied at some cost in resolution and signal-to-noise ratio.

Applications

Pulse compression is standard in modern surveillance and weather radars, synthetic aperture radar imaging, sonar, and medical ultrasound. In weather radar it improves sensitivity to light precipitation while retaining the ability to resolve storm structure.
