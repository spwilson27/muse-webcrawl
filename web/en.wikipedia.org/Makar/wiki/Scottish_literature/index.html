Scottish literature is the literature written in Scotland or by Scottish writers, in the country's several languages: Scottish Gaelic, Scots, Scottish English, and historically Latin and the Brittonic and Norse tongues of the early middle ages. It stretches from early medieval heroic and religious poetry to the internationally influential novel tradition of Walter Scott and the modern Scottish Renaissance.

Early and medieval literature

Scotland's earliest poetry includes the Brittonic Gododdin attributed to Aneirin and Gaelic religious verse associated with Iona. In the fourteenth century John Barbour's Brus (1375), a verse epic of Robert the Bruce, inaugurated Scots literary tradition. The fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries were a golden age of the makars: Robert Henryson's Testament of Cresseid and Morall Fabillis, William Dunbar's court poetry, and Gavin Douglas's Eneados, the first complete Aeneid in a northern vernacular. Gaelic tradition flourished in parallel in the bardic schools and clan patronage.

Enlightenment to the nineteenth century

The Union of 1707 shifted Scotland's literary centre of gravity toward English-language print culture, but Scots vernacular poetry revived with Allan Ramsay and Robert Fergusson and culminated in Robert Burns (1759–1796), whose songs and poems in Scots became central to Scottish national identity. The Scottish Enlightenment produced philosophy and history of European stature—David Hume, Adam Smith, William Robertson—while Walter Scott's Waverley novels (from 1814) effectively created the historical novel and made Scotland a literary landscape for the world. Later nineteenth-century writing ranged from Margaret Oliphant and Robert Louis Stevenson to the sentimental "Kailyard" school and the beginnings of a new Gaelic poetry.

Twentieth century and after

The Scottish Renaissance of the 1920s–30s, led by Hugh MacDiarmid's poetry in synthetic Scots and works such as Lewis Grassic Gibbon's A Scots Quair (1932–34), sought to renew Scottish writing on modernist lines. Post-war and contemporary literature includes Muriel Spark, Alasdair Gray's Lanark (1981), James Kelman's Booker-winning How Late It Was, How Late (1994), Irvine Welsh's Trainspotting (1993), a strong Gaelic revival led by Sorley MacLean and Derick Thomson, and internationally successful genre fiction from Iain Banks and Ian Rankin.
