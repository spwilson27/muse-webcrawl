William Dunbar (c. 1459 or 1460 – c. 1520) was a Scottish poet and churchman who served as a court poet, or makar, at the court of King James IV of Scotland. He is widely regarded as one of the greatest of the Scottish Chaucerians and the most versatile poet of the Scottish Renaissance, celebrated for the range of his work, which extends from courtly allegory and religious meditation to comic flyting, satire, and bawdy verse.

Life

Little is known for certain of Dunbar's early life. He probably came from East Lothian and is thought to have studied at the University of St Andrews, graduating as a master of arts around 1479. He entered the Franciscan order and travelled abroad in his youth, later referring in his poetry to time spent in France and possibly England. By 1500 he had come to the attention of James IV, who granted him a pension, and Dunbar spent much of the following decade at the royal court in Edinburgh, writing poems for state occasions, festivals, and petitioning the king — often humorously and insistently — for a church benefice.

In 1501 Dunbar accompanied an embassy to England in connection with the marriage negotiations between James IV and Margaret Tudor, daughter of Henry VII. When the marriage took place in 1503, he celebrated it in his allegorical poem The Thrissil and the Rois ("The Thistle and the Rose"). He was ordained a priest in 1504 and continued to receive royal favour. References to Dunbar fade from the record after about 1513, the year of the disastrous Battle of Flodden, in which James IV was killed. Dunbar is generally believed to have died around 1520.

Works

Around eighty poems are attributed to Dunbar, many preserved in manuscript collections such as the Bannatyne Manuscript. His major works include:

  • The Thrissil and the Rois (1503) — an allegorical celebration of the marriage of James IV and Margaret Tudor, in which the Lion (Scotland), the Thistle, and the Rose symbolise the union of the crowns.
  • The Goldyn Targe — a dream allegory in which the poet, shielded by Reason's golden targe (shield), is assailed by the arrows of Venus and Beauty.
  • The Flyting of Dunbar and Kennedie — a virtuoso exchange of comic abuse with his fellow makar Walter Kennedy, in the Scots flyting tradition.
  • Lament for the Makaris ("I that in heill wes and gladnes") — a meditation on mortality whose refrain, Timor mortis conturbat me ("the fear of death disturbs me"), tolls through a list of dead poets of Scotland and England.
  • The Tretis of the Tua Mariit Wemen and the Wedo — a comic, and at times savage, portrait of three women discussing their marriages.
  • The Dance of the Sevin Deidly Synnis — a vision of hell in which the seven deadly sins dance before Mahound (the Devil).
  • Devotional poems, including Rorate celi desuper and the Marian lyric "Hale, sterne superne".
  • Petitionary and occasional poems, such as "Schir, ye have mony servitouris" and the poem on the Queen's reception at Aberdeen.

Style and reputation

Dunbar wrote in Middle Scots with an extraordinary command of register: he could move from aureate, Latinate diction in courtly allegory to vigorous demotic Scots in flyting and satire. Scholars have praised his metrical invention, his alliterative energy, and his ability to compress sharp observation into a few lines. Together with Robert Henryson, Gavin Douglas, and David Lyndsay, he stands at the summit of medieval Scottish literature. The critic and poet Edwin Morgan and others have ranked him among the finest poets ever to write in Scots.
