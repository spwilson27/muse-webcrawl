Scots is a West Germanic language variety spoken in Scotland and parts of Ireland and descended, like English, from the Northumbrian dialect of Old English. Whether it is classified as a distinct language or a group of English dialects is a longstanding sociolinguistic and political question; it is recognised by the Scottish and UK governments as a language in its own right under the European Charter for Regional or Minority Languages, and the 2011 Scottish census recorded around 1.5 million speakers.

History

Northumbrian Old English spread into south-eastern Scotland from the seventh century. From the fourteenth century, as the Scottish court and burghs developed, the variety evolved into Early and Middle Scots, the language of the makars—Henryson, Dunbar, and Douglas—and the prestige written language of the Scottish kingdom, with its own spelling conventions (quh- for wh-, sch- for sh-) and vocabulary from Gaelic, Norse, Dutch, and French. After the Union of the Crowns (1603) and the Union of Parliaments (1707), written Scots declined in favour of English, though spoken Scots remained the vernacular of most Lowlanders.

Varieties and features

Regional varieties include Doric (North-East), Glaswegian and Central Scots, Borders Scots, the conservative dialects of Shetland and Orkney (heavily Norse-influenced), and Ulster Scots. Characteristic features include the retention of the velar fricative in loch and nicht, vowel patterns governed by the Scottish Vowel Length Rule (Aitken's law), and lexis such as wee (small), bairn (child), ken (know), and outwith (outside). Most speakers move along a continuum between broad Scots and Scottish Standard English rather than speaking a sharply bounded code.

Status and revival

Scots suffered long-term educational stigmatisation but has seen renewed institutional support: a Scots language policy by the Scottish Government, Scots in the school curriculum, dictionaries (the Dictionary of the Scots Language), and a literary tradition running from Robert Burns through Hugh MacDiarmid's synthetic Scots to contemporary writers such as James Kelman and Matthew Fitt.
