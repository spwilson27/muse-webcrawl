Robert Henryson

Bornc. 1425
Diedc. 1500, Dunfermline (reputedly)
OccupationPoet, schoolmaster, notary
LanguageMiddle Scots
Notable worksThe Testament of Cresseid; Morall Fabillis; Orpheus and Eurydice

Robert Henryson (middle Scots: Robert Henrysoun; c. 1425 – c. 1500) was a Scottish poet, one of the great makars of the Northern Renaissance and, with William Dunbar and Gavin Douglas, a central figure of the golden age of Scots poetry. Little is known of his life with certainty: university records and legal documents place him at the University of Glasgow as a teacher of law and as a notary public, and tradition and internal evidence associate him with Dunfermline, where he may have been master of the grammar school attached to the abbey.

Works

Henryson's masterpiece is The Testament of Cresseid (c. 1490s), a tragic sequel to Chaucer's Troilus and Criseyde, in which the abandoned Cresseid, diseased and cast out, blasphemes against Cupid and Venus and is punished with leprosy, achieving recognition and repentance before her death; its stanza beginning "O brittle joy, O shaddow of delight" and Cresseid's complaint are among the most celebrated passages in Scots literature. His Morall Fabillis of Esope the Phrygian rework thirteen Aesopic fables into richly characterised narratives with explicit moralities, while Orpheus and Eurydice, the tale of Robene and Makyne—the finest early Scots pastoral—and poems such as The Garmont of Gud Ladeis complete a small but major corpus.

Reputation

William Dunbar, in his Lament for the Makaris, records "Maister Robert Henrisoun" among the dead poets taken by Death. Henryson's works circulated in print from the early sixteenth century (Chepman and Myllar, 1508) and were edited in the nineteenth century by David Laing. Seamus Heaney translated part of the Testament and the Morall Fabillis, praising Henryson's gravity and humanity.
