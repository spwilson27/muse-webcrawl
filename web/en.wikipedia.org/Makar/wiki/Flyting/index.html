Flyting

OriginOld Norse flýta; Old English flītan, "to contend"
TraditionsNorse, Anglo-Saxon, Scots
Famous exampleThe Flyting of Dunbar and Kennedie (c. 1500)
FormContest of ritual insults in verse

Flyting is a ritual, poetic exchange of insults practised mainly in the courts of northern Europe from the 5th to the 16th century. The poems, exchanges, or contests are usually involving two persons, and were a contest of insult performed in verse before an audience. Flyting has parallels in Norse senna, the insult-contests of heroic legend and skaldic tradition, and arguably in later traditions of ritual abuse such as Inuit song duels and the African-American "dozens."

In Norse and English tradition

In the Poetic Edda, the gods themselves engage in flyting: in Lokasenna, Loki insults each god in turn at Ægir's feast and is answered in kind, and in Hárbardsljóð Odin, disguised as a ferryman, exchanges taunts with Thor. In Old English poetry, the confrontation between Beowulf and Unferth in Beowulf has the form of a flyting, each man questioning the other's deeds and courage. Such exchanges could claim practical stakes: the loser in reputation might lose status, and in the Norse sagas verbal contests sometimes precede or substitute for violence.

Scottish flyting

The tradition flowered at the court of James IV of Scotland among the makars. The supreme surviving example is The Flyting of Dunbar and Kennedie (c. 1500–1513), in which William Dunbar and Walter Kennedy trade hundreds of lines of extravagantly learned abuse, attacking each other's poetry, ancestry, appearance, and morals in both Scots and (in Kennedy's case) partly Gaelic voices. A later exchange between Alexander Montgomerie and Patrick Hume of Polwarth continued the genre. Scholars read these poems as courtly entertainment showcasing virtuosity in rhetoric and invective, as well as a vehicle for genuine literary rivalry.

Legacy

The word survives in Scots and in scholarship; modern commentators have linked flyting to rap battles and other competitive improvised-insult traditions, though the comparison is analogical rather than genealogical.
