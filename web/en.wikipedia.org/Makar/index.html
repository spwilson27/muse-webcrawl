Makar

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A makar (Scots: [ˈmɑkər], plural makaris) is a term from Scottish literature for a poet or bard, often roughly equivalent to the English word "maker". The term is most commonly applied to the poets of the Scottish Renaissance of the 15th and 16th centuries — the so-called Makars — who wrote in Middle Scots at the courts of James III, James IV, and James V. Their work, influenced by Chaucer (and often called "Scottish Chaucerian"), represents the golden age of Scots poetry, encompassing courtly romance, moral allegory, flyting, and comic verse.

The word derives from Middle Scots makar, from mak — "to make" — cognate with English maker, reflecting the conception of the poet as a craftsman or maker of verse. In modern Scotland the title has been revived: since 2004, The Makar is Scotland's national poet laureate, appointed by the Scottish Parliament.

The Renaissance makars

The makars flourished under the Stewart court, when Scots was the language of a confident Renaissance kingdom. They absorbed English and continental influences — Chaucer, Lydgate, French courtly forms, Latin rhetoric — while writing in a rich literary Scots (sometimes called "aureate" Scots for its Latinate diction in high styles). Genres included the dream vision, the complaint, the romance, the moral fable, petitionary and occasional verse, and the flyting: a ritual exchange of extravagant invective between rival poets.

Major figures and works

  • Robert Henryson (c. 1425 – c. 1506): The Morall Fabillis of Esope (animal fables), the tragic love poem The Testament of Cresseid, and Orpheus and Eurydice.
  • William Dunbar (c. 1460 – c. 1513): the most versatile of the makars — The Tretis of the Tua Mariit Wemen and the Wedo, The Flyting of Dunbar and Kennedie, the allegory The Goldyn Targe, the dance-macabre Lament for the Makaris with its famous refrain Timor mortis conturbat me, and The Thrissil and the Rois, celebrating the marriage of James IV and Margaret Tudor (1503).
  • Gavin Douglas (c. 1474 – 1522): the allegorical Palice of Honour and the Eneados (1513), the first complete translation of Virgil's Aeneid into a vernacular of Britain, with original Scots prologues to each book.
  • David Lyndsay (c. 1486 – 1555): Ane Satyre of the Thrie Estaitis, a vast morality play attacking social and clerical corruption, and numerous court poems.
  • Others include John Barbour (an earlier figure, author of The Brus), Blind Harry (The Wallace), Walter Kennedy, Richard Holland (The Buke of the Howlat), James I himself (The Kingis Quair), Alexander Scott, and Alexander Montgomerie, the leading makar of James VI's court and the Castalian Band.

Decline

The makar tradition waned after the Union of the Crowns (1603) removed the Scottish court to London, as Scots lost prestige against English and print culture standardised around London norms. The poetry survived in manuscripts such as the Bannatyne Manuscript (1568), which preserved much of the corpus. Interest revived with 18th-century antiquarians (Allan Ramsay's The Ever Green, 1724) and the makars became central to the canon of Scottish literature studied today.

The modern Makar

In 2004 the Scottish Parliament created the post of national poet, titled The Makar (sometimes described in English as the Scots Makar), in deliberate homage to the Renaissance tradition. Holders have been Edwin Morgan (2004–2010), Liz Lochhead (2011–2016), Jackie Kay (2016–2021), and Kathleen Jamie (appointed 2021). Cities such as Edinburgh also appoint local makars. The Makar writes and performs for national occasions and promotes poetry throughout Scotland.
