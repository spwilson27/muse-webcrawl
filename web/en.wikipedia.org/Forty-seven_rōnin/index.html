The forty-seven rōnin (Japanese: 四十七士, Shijūshichishi), also known as the Akō incident or Akō vendetta, were a group of masterless samurai (rōnin) in early 18th-century Japan who avenged the death of their lord, Asano Naganori. Their story is one of the most famous in Japanese history and a national exemplar of the samurai virtues of loyalty, honour, and sacrifice embodied in bushidō.

The incident

In 1701, Asano Naganori, the young daimyō of Akō, was assigned to receive envoys of the Emperor in Edo and placed under the tutelage of the shogunate protocol official Kira Yoshinaka. According to the traditional account, Kira repeatedly insulted and humiliated Asano, who finally attacked and wounded him with a dagger inside Edo Castle—an offence gravely aggravated by drawing a weapon in the shōgun's palace. Asano was ordered to commit seppuku the same day, his lands were confiscated, and his retainers were made rōnin. Kira received no punishment.

The vendetta

Led by the chief retainer Ōishi Yoshio, forty-seven of Asano's former samurai plotted revenge while dispersing and feigning dissipation to lull Kira's spies. On a snowy night—traditionally dated to January 30, 1703—they stormed Kira's mansion in Edo, fought past his guards, and found Kira hiding in a storehouse. When he refused to take his own life, they beheaded him and laid his head on Asano's grave at the temple of Sengaku-ji.

Aftermath and legacy

The rōnin surrendered to the authorities. Although public opinion celebrated them, the shogunate ruled that they had broken the law; forty-six of them were permitted the honourable death of seppuku on March 20, 1703 (one younger member was pardoned). They were buried beside their lord at Sengaku-ji, where their graves remain a place of pilgrimage. The story was rapidly adapted for puppet theatre and kabuki as Chūshingura ("The Treasury of Loyal Retainers"), with names and details altered to evade censorship, and has inspired countless plays, novels, and films, including the 2013 Hollywood film 47 Ronin.
