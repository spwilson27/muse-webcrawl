Tier 2 capital

Tier 2 capital is a component of a bank's regulatory capital under the Basel Accords. It consists of supplementary, or "gone-concern", capital: instruments and reserves that can absorb losses if the bank is wound up, ranking behind depositors but ahead of shareholders. Together, Tier 1 capital (the highest-quality going-concern capital, mostly common equity) and Tier 2 capital make up a bank's total capital, which regulators compare with risk-weighted assets to assess solvency.

Components

Under Basel III, Tier 2 capital includes subordinated debt with an original maturity of at least five years, certain loan-loss reserves (general provisions, subject to limits), revaluation reserves on assets (in some jurisdictions), and other instruments that are subordinated, unsecured and fully loss-absorbing at the point of non-viability. Contingent convertible bonds ("CoCos") that do not qualify as Additional Tier 1 may be included in Tier 2. Instruments are progressively amortised — their recognised value reduced — during the last five years before maturity.

Role in regulation

The Basel framework requires banks to hold total capital of at least 8 percent of risk-weighted assets, of which Tier 2 can form at most 2 percentage points of the minimum (Tier 1 must be at least 6 percent, including at least 4.5 percent Common Equity Tier 1). Tier 2 is considered less reliable than Tier 1 because its instruments may have fixed maturities and their loss-absorption depends partly on the legal process of resolution; Basel III strengthened the criteria so that Tier 2 instruments must be written down or converted to equity when the regulator declares the bank non-viable.
