Civil service

Civil servants staff the permanent departments of government.

The civil service is the body of government officials who are employed in civil occupations that are neither political nor judicial. Civil servants are typically career administrators hired on professional merit, and they implement the policies of the government of the day while remaining politically impartial. The term usually excludes members of the armed forces, elected politicians, judges and employees of state-owned commercial enterprises.

History

The earliest large-scale professional bureaucracy developed in imperial China, where entry to the mandarinate was governed by the imperial examination system testing mastery of the Confucian classics — a system that lasted, in evolving form, for over a millennium. Modern merit-based services were shaped by reforms such as Britain's Northcote–Trevelyan Report of 1854, which recommended recruitment by competitive examination and promotion on merit, and the United States' Pendleton Civil Service Reform Act of 1883, enacted after the assassination of President Garfield by a disappointed office-seeker exposed the corruption of the "spoils system".

Principles

Classical civil service systems rest on four principles: recruitment on merit through fair and open competition; political impartiality, serving governments of any party; permanence, providing continuity and institutional memory across elections; and accountability to ministers or executives, who answer to the legislature for departmental actions. Civil servants are generally expected to uphold values of integrity, honesty, objectivity and impartiality.

Around the world

In the United Kingdom, His Majesty's Civil Service supports the UK Government and the devolved administrations, with permanent secretaries heading departments. The United States federal civil service employs roughly two million civilians under the Office of Personnel Management and the merit system principles. France recruits many senior officials through competitive concours and grandes écoles such as the École nationale d'administration (now the Institut national du service public). India's Indian Administrative Service continues the tradition of the colonial Indian Civil Service. Reforms under the banner of New Public Management have sought to introduce performance targets, fixed-term appointments and market-style incentives into many services.
