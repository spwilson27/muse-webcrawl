Digital printing

Digital printing is a method of printing from a digital-based image directly to a variety of media. It usually refers to professional printing where small-run jobs from desktop publishing and other digital sources are printed using large-format and/or high-volume laser or inkjet printers.

Digital printing has a higher cost per page than more traditional offset printing methods, but this price is usually offset by avoiding the cost of all the technical steps required to make printing plates. It also allows for on-demand printing, short turnaround time, and even modification of the image (variable data) used for each impression. The savings in labour and the ever-increasing capability of digital presses means that digital printing is reaching the point where it can match or supersede offset printing technology's ability to produce larger print runs of several thousand sheets at a low price.

Process

The biggest difference between digital printing and traditional methods such as lithography, flexography, gravure or letterpress is that there is no need to replace printing plates in digital printing, whereas in analog printing the plates are repeatedly replaced. This results in quicker turnaround time and lower cost when using digital printing, but typically a loss of some fine-image detail by most commercial digital printing processes. The most popular methods include inkjet and laser printers that deposit pigment or toner onto a wide variety of substrates including paper, canvas, glass, metal, marble and other substances.

The digital file — typically a PDF or desktop-publishing file — is processed by a raster image processor (RIP), which converts it into a bitmap at the output device's resolution and manages colour via ICC profiles. Key technologies include liquid electrophotography (as in HP Indigo presses), production inkjet systems, and dry-toner electrophotography.

Applications

  • Print on demand — books and documents printed only when ordered, reducing inventory.
  • Variable data printing — personalised direct mail and transactional documents in which text or images change from copy to copy.
  • Wide-format printing — banners, signage, exhibition graphics and vehicle wraps.
  • Photo printing — consumer and professional photographic output.
  • Textile and industrial printing — fabrics, labels, packaging prototypes and decorative surfaces.

Comparison with offset printing

Offset lithography remains cheaper per copy for long, static print runs and offers a wide gamut of spot colours and substrates. Digital printing is more economical for short runs, eliminates make-ready waste and setup time, and supports versioning and personalisation that offset cannot achieve. Industry analysts have long predicted a crossover point that moves steadily toward longer runs as digital press speeds and formats improve.
