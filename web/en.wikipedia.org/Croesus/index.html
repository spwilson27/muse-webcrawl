Croesus

Croesus (c. 585 – c. 546 BC) was the king of Lydia who reigned from about 585 BC until his defeat by the Persian king Cyrus the Great in 547 or 546 BC. His legendary wealth made his name a byword for riches — "rich as Croesus" — and he is famous for the story of his meeting with the Athenian lawgiver Solon, who warned him that no man should be counted happy until he is dead.

Reign

Croesus succeeded his father Alyattes and completed the Lydian conquest of the Greek cities of Ionia, making them tributary. His capital, Sardis, was one of the wealthiest cities of the ancient world, and Lydia is credited with issuing some of the earliest true coinage, electrum and later pure gold and silver coins. Croesus made lavish offerings to the oracle of Delphi, including gold statues and great quantities of precious metal, and consulted the oracle before attacking Persia. The oracle replied that if he crossed the river Halys he would destroy a great empire — which proved to be his own.

Fall

Cyrus the Great marched into Lydia, defeated Croesus at the Battle of Pteria and, after an indecisive engagement, pursued him to Sardis. Following the Battle of Thymbra, Sardis fell after a short siege and Lydia became a Persian satrapy. Later Greek accounts, notably Herodotus, tell of Cyrus preparing to burn Croesus on a pyre, from which he was saved — in some versions by the intervention of the god Apollo or by Cyrus's own change of heart. Croesus is said to have remained at the Persian court as an adviser to Cyrus and his son Cambyses II.

Legacy

The figure of Croesus became a moral exemplar in Greek literature on the instability of fortune. Archaeological excavation at Sardis has revealed the wealth of the Lydian kingdom, including its gold-refining workshops along the Pactolus river.
