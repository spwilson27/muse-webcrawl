Positive feedback

Positive feedback is a process in which the output of a system amplifies or reinforces a change in its input, so that the system moves further in the same direction. In a positive feedback loop, an initial disturbance produces effects that cause a still greater disturbance. Unlike negative feedback, which stabilises a system around an equilibrium, positive feedback is self-reinforcing and typically drives a system rapidly away from its starting state until some limiting factor intervenes.

Examples

Classic examples occur in many fields. In electronics, regenerative feedback in an amplifier produces oscillation or the "howl" of a microphone placed near its loudspeaker. In physiology, positive feedback accelerates processes to completion: uterine contractions in childbirth stimulate oxytocin release, which intensifies contractions; and the blood-clotting cascade recruits further platelets and clotting factors. In economics, speculative bubbles and bank runs are positive feedback processes, as is compound interest. In climate science, the ice–albedo feedback — melting ice exposing darker ocean, which absorbs more heat, melting more ice — is a major amplifier of warming.

Behaviour and stability

Mathematically, when the gain around a feedback loop exceeds unity, signals grow exponentially; in practice growth is eventually checked by saturation, depletion of resources, or a switch to another regime, as in the snap of a Schmitt trigger or the firing of a neuron's action potential. Engineers usually avoid unintended positive feedback in control systems because of its destabilising effect, but deliberately employ it in oscillators, comparators with hysteresis, and latching circuits.
