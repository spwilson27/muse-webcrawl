In astronomy, the interstellar medium (ISM) is the matter and radiation that exists in the space between the star systems in a galaxy. This matter includes gas in ionic, atomic, and molecular form, as well as dust and cosmic rays. It fills interstellar space and blends smoothly into the surrounding intergalactic space. The energy in the form of electromagnetic radiation in the same volume is the interstellar radiation field.

Composition and phases

By mass the ISM is roughly 70% hydrogen, 28% helium and 2% heavier elements ("metals") enriched by generations of stars; about 1% of the mass is in solid dust grains of silicates and carbonaceous material. The gas exists in several phases in rough pressure equilibrium: cold dense molecular clouds (10–100 K) where stars form; the cold and warm neutral medium of atomic hydrogen, observed through the 21-cm line; the warm ionised medium; and the hot ionised medium (around 106 K) filling much of the volume, created by supernova shock waves.

Role in star formation

The ISM is both the raw material of stars and the repository of material returned by stellar winds and supernovae. Dense molecular clouds collapse under gravity to form new stars, while ionising radiation and explosions from massive stars disperse clouds and stir the medium, regulating the cycle of star formation in galaxies. The Sun currently travels through the Local Interstellar Cloud within the low-density Local Bubble.

Observation

Interstellar matter reveals itself through emission and reflection nebulae, interstellar absorption lines, reddening and polarisation of starlight, infrared emission from dust, and radio observations of atomic and molecular spectral lines. More than 200 molecules have been detected in the ISM, including complex organic compounds.
