The past tense places events on the timeline before the present.

The past tense is a grammatical tense whose principal function is to place an action or situation in the past, at a time before the moment of speaking. Most languages with tense systems have at least one form for past reference, and many distinguish finer gradations — such as a remote past versus a recent past — or combine past tense with aspect, as in the distinction between the imperfect and the perfective past in Romance and Slavic languages.

In English

English has two morphological tenses, past and non-past. The simple past is formed with the suffix -ed for regular verbs ("walked") and by vowel change or other patterns for irregular verbs ("went", "sang", "wrote"). Combinations with auxiliaries produce further past constructions: the past progressive ("was walking"), the past perfect ("had walked"), used for an event anterior to another past event, and the past perfect progressive ("had been walking"). The modal would functions as the past of will in reported speech and conditionals. English also uses past-tense forms for non-temporal purposes: politeness ("I wanted to ask…") and hypothetical or counterfactual meaning ("If I knew…").

Cross-linguistic variation

Some languages, such as Mandarin Chinese, lack grammatical tense entirely and mark time with adverbs and aspect markers. Others have elaborate past systems: several Bantu languages distinguish multiple degrees of remoteness, and some languages mark whether a past event was witnessed by the speaker (evidential pasts). In Indo-European historical linguistics, the ancestral system of aorist, imperfect and perfect was reorganised in the daughter languages into the modern tense-aspect arrays.
