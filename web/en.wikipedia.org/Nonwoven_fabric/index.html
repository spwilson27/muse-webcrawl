Nonwoven fabric

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Nonwoven fabric is a fabric-like material made from staple fibres or continuous filaments bonded together by chemical, mechanical, heat or solvent treatment, rather than by weaving or knitting. Nonwovens are typically flat, porous sheets produced directly from fibres or molten polymer. They range from cheap, single-use products to durable engineered materials, and are defined by standard bodies such as EDANA and INDA as sheet or web structures bonded by entangling fibre or perforating films together.

Manufacture

Principal production routes include spunbond and meltblown processes, in which polymer granules are extruded into continuous filaments laid into a web and bonded — meltblown produces very fine fibres suited to filtration; drylaid carding or airlaying of staple fibres; wetlaid processes adapted from papermaking; and bonding methods such as needle-punching, hydroentanglement (spunlacing), thermal bonding and chemical bonding. Combinations such as SMS (spunbond–meltblown–spunbond) laminates are widely used for barrier fabrics.

Applications

Nonwovens are used in hygiene products (diapers, sanitary products, wipes), medical products (surgical masks, gowns, drapes and wound dressings — demand for meltblown mask media surged during the COVID-19 pandemic), filtration, geotextiles, agriculture crop covers, automotive interiors, construction membranes, interlinings and reusable shopping bags. Because many nonwovens are made of polypropylene and other plastics, their disposability raises environmental concerns, and biodegradable alternatives are an active area of development.
