Subjectivity and objectivity (philosophy)

Subjectivity and objectivity are paired concepts in philosophy. A subjective claim, judgment, or experience is one that depends on, or is shaped by, the perspective, feelings, or mental states of a subject; an objective claim purports to describe things as they are independently of any subject's viewpoint. The distinction runs through epistemology, ethics, aesthetics, and the philosophy of science.

Senses of the distinction

Philosophers distinguish several related contrasts. Ontological subjectivity concerns mode of existence: pains and sensations exist only as experienced by a subject, whereas mountains and molecules exist independently. Epistemic subjectivity concerns justification: a judgment is epistemically subjective if its truth depends on attitudes or values, and objective if settled by facts accessible from any standpoint. Thomas Nagel characterized objectivity as a method of understanding that detaches progressively from a particular individual viewpoint — a "view from nowhere."

History

The modern usage crystallized with Descartes, for whom the thinking subject was the foundation of certainty, and with Kant, who argued that experience is structured by the subject's own forms of intuition and categories — so that things-in-themselves remain beyond knowledge. In the 19th century Kierkegaard emphasized "subjectivity" as passionate inward commitment, especially in religious faith. In the 20th century, phenomenology and existentialism developed rich accounts of subjective experience, while analytic philosophy debated whether qualities such as color, value, and consciousness itself are subjective features of perceivers or objective features of the world.

Applications

In science, objectivity is associated with reproducibility, measurement invariance, and intersubjective agreement. In ethics, the debate between moral realism and expressivism turns partly on whether moral judgments can be objective. In aesthetics, judgments of beauty have classically been treated as subjective yet making a claim on others' agreement — Kant's "subjective universality."
