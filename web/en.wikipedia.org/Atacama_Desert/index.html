Atacama Desert

The Atacama Desert (Spanish: Desierto de Atacama) is a desert plateau in South America covering a roughly 1,600-kilometre (1,000 mi) strip of land on the Pacific coast, west of the Andes Mountains in northern Chile. It is the driest nonpolar desert in the world, and the second-driest overall, surpassed only by some locations in the McMurdo Dry Valleys of Antarctica. In some weather stations in the Atacama, rain has never been recorded; average annual rainfall in much of the core desert is about 15 mm (0.6 in).

Climate

The desert's hyper-aridity results from a combination of factors: the rain shadow of the Andes, the cold Humboldt Current offshore which stabilises the air and produces fog (camanchaca) rather than rain, and a subtropical high-pressure belt. Fog oases called lomas support unique vegetation and the chinchorro culture's settlements. Rare rain events can produce spectacular desert blooms (desierto florido).

Human history and economy

The Atacama has been inhabited for millennia; the Chinchorro people produced the world's oldest known artificial mummies (c. 7000 BCE). In the 19th century the desert's rich sodium nitrate (saltpeter) deposits were a cause of the War of the Pacific (1879–1884), after which Chile annexed the region. Today the desert hosts some of the world's largest copper mines, such as Chuquicamata and Escondida, and major lithium extraction from its salt flats (salares).

Astronomy

Its altitude, dryness, and clear skies make the Atacama the world's premier site for ground-based astronomy. Observatories include the ALMA millimetre array, the Paranal Observatory with the Very Large Telescope, and La Silla. NASA has also tested Mars rovers in the desert, whose soils are studied as analogues of Martian regolith.
