Venison

Venison originally meant the meat of a game animal, but now refers primarily to the meat of deer (and sometimes other hoofed game such as elk, moose, reindeer, and antelope). The word derives from the Latin venari, "to hunt", via Old French venaison. In earlier centuries the term applied to the flesh of any hunted animal, including wild boar and hare, but modern usage has narrowed it to cervids.

Production

Venison comes both from wild deer taken by hunters and from farmed deer raised commercially. New Zealand is the world's largest producer and exporter of farmed venison, followed by producers in Europe and North America. Farmed deer are typically red deer or hybrids with elk. Because deer are lean animals, venison contains less fat and fewer calories than comparable cuts of beef, though it is rich in protein, iron, and B vitamins.

Culinary use

Venison can be prepared as steaks, roasts, sausages, jerky, and ground meat. Prime cuts such as the loin and tenderloin are usually cooked quickly to medium-rare, since the leanness of the meat makes it prone to drying out when overcooked. Tougher cuts, such as the shoulder and neck, are suited to braising and stewing. Traditionally the meat was aged ("hung") to tenderise it and develop flavour, a practice still used for wild game. Venison has been a prestigious food since medieval times and features in the cuisines of Germany (where it is often served with red cabbage and lingonberries), France, and Scandinavia.

Health considerations

Wild deer may carry chronic wasting disease, a prion disease of cervids, and health authorities advise hunters in affected regions to test animals before consumption. Venison's low fat content and favourable nutrient profile make it popular among consumers seeking alternatives to red meat from domestic livestock.
