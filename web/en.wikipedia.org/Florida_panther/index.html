Florida panther

The Florida panther is a population of the North American cougar (Puma concolor couguar) found in South Florida. It lives in pinelands, tropical hardwood hammocks, and mixed freshwater swamp forests. Once ranging throughout the southeastern United States, by the 1970s it had been reduced by hunting and habitat loss to a small, inbred population in southwest Florida numbering perhaps 20–30 animals. It has been one of the most intensively managed endangered mammals in the United States.

Genetic restoration

The remnant population showed signs of inbreeding depression, including kinked tails, cowlicks, low sperm quality, and heart defects. In 1995, eight female Texas cougars were released into South Florida to restore genetic diversity. The intervention succeeded: heterozygosity increased, defects declined, and the population grew to an estimated 120–230 adults and subadults by the 2010s. The Florida panther remains listed as endangered under the Endangered Species Act.

Threats

Vehicle collisions are the leading recorded cause of death, and habitat loss to development and sea-level rise in South Florida is the principal long-term threat. Wildlife underpasses and fencing along Alligator Alley (Interstate 75) have reduced road mortality in places.

Status

The Florida panther is the state animal of Florida and the namesake of the Florida Panthers NHL team. Conservation land networks, including Florida Panther National Wildlife Refuge and Big Cypress National Preserve, protect its core habitat.
