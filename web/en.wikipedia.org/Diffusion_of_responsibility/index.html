Diffusion of responsibility

Diffusion of responsibility is a sociopsychological phenomenon whereby a person is less likely to take responsibility for action or inaction when other bystanders or witnesses are present. Considered a form of attribution, it is a key mechanism behind the bystander effect.

Research

The phenomenon was studied by John Darley and Bibb Latané following the 1964 murder of Kitty Genovese in New York. In their experiments, participants who believed others were also present were slower and less likely to report smoke filling a room or to help a person apparently having a seizure. The likelihood of intervention decreases as group size increases, the reverse of what common sense might predict.

Mechanisms

Explanations include pluralistic ignorance (looking to others to interpret the situation), evaluation apprehension, and the literal spreading of perceived responsibility across all present. Diffusion of responsibility also occurs in organisations, where it can enable harmful decisions no single person feels accountable for, and online, in phenomena such as cyberbullying and trolling.
