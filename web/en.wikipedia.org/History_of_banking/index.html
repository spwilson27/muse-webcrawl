History of banking


Bankers at work in Renaissance Florence

The history of banking traces the development of institutions that safeguard money, extend credit and facilitate payments, from grain banks in ancient Mesopotamia to the modern global financial system. Banking practices evolved alongside coinage, paper money, joint-stock companies and electronic finance.

Ancient origins

Temples and palaces in Mesopotamia stored grain and valuables and made loans as early as the 2nd millennium BCE; the Code of Hammurabi regulated lending. In ancient Greece, temples such as Delphi and Olympia held deposits, and private bankers (trapezitai) exchanged currency and extended credit. Roman bankers (argentarii) accepted deposits, made loans and transferred funds between accounts. In China, merchant houses developed paper drafts and, by the Song dynasty, the state issued paper currency (jiaozi) from the 11th century.

Medieval and Renaissance banking

Islamic finance developed sophisticated partnerships (mudarabah) and bills of exchange (hawala, suftaja) across the medieval Muslim world. In Europe, the Knights Templar operated an early international transfer network for pilgrims and crusaders. Italian merchant families — notably the Bardi, Peruzzi and Medici of Florence — built banking empires in the 14th and 15th centuries; the Medici Bank (founded 1397) pioneered double-entry bookkeeping, letters of credit and branch banking. The word "bank" derives from the Italian banco, the bench at which money changers worked. The super-companies of the Bardi and Peruzzi collapsed in the 1340s after loans to Edward III of England defaulted.

Early modern developments

Public banks appeared with the Taula de Canvi in Barcelona (1401) and Genoa's Banco di San Giorgio (1407). The Amsterdam Wisselbank (1609) stabilised coinage and payments in the Dutch Republic, while Amsterdam's stock exchange and the Dutch East India Company (1602) pioneered joint-stock finance. The Bank of England (1694) was founded to finance war against France and became the model central bank; it developed the banknote and the role of lender of last resort. In Scotland, competitive note-issuing banks flourished in the 18th century. The gold standard spread internationally in the 19th century, anchoring currencies to gold.

19th and 20th centuries

Industrialisation produced great private banking houses — Rothschilds, Barings, J.P. Morgan — and joint-stock deposit banks serving a mass clientele. In the United States, the National Banking Acts (1863–64) and the creation of the Federal Reserve (1913) structured the system. The Wall Street Crash of 1929 and subsequent bank failures led to deposit insurance and the Glass–Steagall separation of commercial and investment banking in the US (1933). The Bretton Woods system (1944) created the IMF and World Bank and pegged currencies to the dollar until its collapse in 1971–73 inaugurated floating exchange rates.

Modern banking

From the 1970s, deregulation, securitisation and computing transformed banking: ATMs, credit cards, electronic payments and global capital markets. The Basel accords (1988 onwards) set international capital standards. The financial crisis of 2007–08, triggered by US subprime mortgage defaults and the failure of Lehman Brothers, prompted bank rescues, stress testing and tighter regulation (Dodd–Frank, Basel III). Since then, digital and mobile banking, fintech competition and central bank digital currency experiments have continued to reshape the industry.
