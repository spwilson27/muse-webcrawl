Okinawan kobudo

Okinawan kobudō (沖縄古武道) is the weapon systems of Okinawan martial arts. It developed on the island of Okinawa, Japan, alongside the empty-hand art of karate, and encompasses a range of weapons traditionally said to have been adapted from farm implements and everyday tools.

Weapons

The best-known weapons of Okinawan kobudo include:

  • Bō — a long staff, about six feet (180 cm) in length.
  • Sai — a three-pronged truncheon-like weapon used in pairs.
  • Tonfa — a handled wooden club, originally the handle of a millstone grinder.
  • Nunchaku — two sticks connected by a chain or cord.
  • Kama — a sickle used for harvesting rice.
  • Eku — an oar; Tekko — a fist-load weapon; Tinbe-rochin — shield and short spear; Surujin — a weighted chain or rope.

History

Popular tradition holds that weapons practice flourished after the weapons bans imposed on Okinawa, first under King Shō Shin in the early 16th century and again after the Satsuma invasion of 1609, which forced peasants to improvise fighting tools from agricultural implements. Historians note the story is likely romanticised: many kobudo weapons have parallels in Chinese martial arts, and much of the practice was preserved within particular families and villages.

In the 20th century, masters such as Taira Shinken systematised the art, travelling Okinawa to collect and record kata (forms) that were in danger of being lost, and founding the Ryūkyū Kobudō Hozon Shinkō Kai. Kata remain the principal vehicle of transmission, alongside partner drills and conditioning exercises.
