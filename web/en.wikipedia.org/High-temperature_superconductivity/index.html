High-temperature superconductivity

High-temperature superconductors (abbreviated high-Tc or HTS) are defined as materials with critical temperatures above 77 K, the boiling point of liquid nitrogen. They were discovered by J. Georg Bednorz and K. Alex Müller in 1986 in a lanthanum barium copper oxide, work that won the 1987 Nobel Prize in Physics. Unlike the conventional superconductors explained by BCS theory, the high-Tc cuprates and iron-based superconductors are unconventional, and the microscopic mechanism of their superconductivity remains an open problem.

Cuprates

Within a year of the first discovery, yttrium barium copper oxide (YBCO) was found to superconduct at 92 K, the first material to work above liquid-nitrogen temperature. Cuprates share copper-oxide (CuO2) planes and display a rich phase diagram: the parent compounds are antiferromagnetic Mott insulators, and superconductivity appears on doping, alongside a still-debated "pseudogap" regime. The highest confirmed critical temperature at ambient pressure, in a mercury-based cuprate (HgBa2Ca2Cu3O8), is about 133 K, rising to roughly 164 K under high pressure.

Iron-based superconductors

In 2008, iron-based superconductors — iron pnictides and chalcogenides with critical temperatures up to about 56 K — became the second major high-Tc family, showing that strong magnetism and superconductivity coexist in layered materials beyond the cuprates.

Theory

Cuprate superconductors have d-wave pairing symmetry rather than the s-wave of BCS theory, and candidate pairing mechanisms involve antiferromagnetic spin fluctuations rather than phonons. Despite decades of work and phase-sensitive experiments, there is no consensus microscopic theory.

Applications

Because they can be cooled with inexpensive liquid nitrogen or cryocoolers, HTS materials — mainly YBCO coated conductors and bismuth-based BSCCO tapes — are used in high-field magnets, fault-current limiters, power cables, and prototype maglev and ship propulsion systems.
