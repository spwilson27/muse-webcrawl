Roman numerals

Roman numerals are a numeral system that originated in ancient Rome and remained the usual way of writing numbers throughout Europe into the Late Middle Ages. Numbers are written with combinations of letters from the Latin alphabet: I (1), V (5), X (10), L (50), C (100), D (500), and M (1,000). Roman numerals employ a largely additive notation with a subtractive convention for certain values (for example, IV for 4 and IX for 9).

Numbers are formed by combining symbols and adding their values, largest first: for example, 2024 = MMXXIV. A smaller symbol placed before a larger one is subtracted: I may precede V and X; X may precede L and C; C may precede D and M. Thus 4 = IV, 9 = IX, 40 = XL, 90 = XC, 400 = CD, 900 = CM. Generally no symbol is repeated more than three times in succession. Large numbers beyond several thousand were handled in antiquity by extensions such as the vinculum (a bar over a numeral multiplying it by 1,000) and the apostrophus system.

History

The system developed from Etruscan numerals and tally-mark conventions; early Roman forms differed from the classical ones, and the subtractive convention was not used consistently in antiquity — additive forms such as IIII for 4 and VIIII for 9 remained common on inscriptions, sundials, and clock faces. Through the Middle Ages Roman numerals were used for administration, commerce, and scholarship, while computation was generally done with counting boards and the abacus. They were gradually displaced by the Hindu–Arabic positional system after Leonardo Fibonacci's Liber Abaci (1202), though the changeover took centuries.

Modern use

Roman numerals persist in specialized contexts: regnal numbers of monarchs and popes (Elizabeth II, Benedict XVI), outlines and legal documents, book front matter pagination and volume numbering, film and game sequel titles and copyright years, sporting events (Super Bowl numbers, the Olympic Games), clock faces, periodic-table group labels in chemistry, and the names of chords in music analysis. The system has no zero of its own; medieval writers used the word nulla or the letter N for zero.
