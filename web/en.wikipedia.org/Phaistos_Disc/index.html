Phaistos Disc

The Phaistos Disc (also spelled Phaestos) is a disc of fired clay from the Minoan palace of Phaistos on the island of Crete, possibly dating to the middle or late Minoan Bronze Age (2nd millennium BC). Both sides are covered with stamped pictorial signs arranged in a spiral, making it a unique and famous undeciphered artefact.

Discovery and description

The disc was found on 3 July 1908 by the Italian archaeologist Luigi Pernier in a basement room of the palace complex at Phaistos, in a context associated with a tablet in Linear A. It measures about 15 cm across and is covered on both sides with 241 impressions of 45 distinct signs — plumed heads, shields, ships, fish, birds, lilies and human figures among them — pressed into the wet clay with individual stamps, an early example of reusable type. The signs are grouped by incised lines into 61 "words" and wind in a spiral toward the centre of each side.

Decipherment attempts

Because the corpus is a single short text in an otherwise unknown script, the disc remains undeciphered despite dozens of proposed readings — as a hymn, a ritual text, a calendar, a board game, an astronomical record, or a document in languages as various as Greek, Luwian, Semitic or Basque. There is broad agreement that the signs probably form a syllabary and that the text reads from the rim inward (or the reverse), but no proposal commands scholarly consensus. Occasional doubts about authenticity have been raised but are not generally accepted.

Unicode

The Phaistos Disc sign set was added to the Unicode Standard in 2008 (block U+101D0–U+101FF), enabling the signs to be represented in digital text.
