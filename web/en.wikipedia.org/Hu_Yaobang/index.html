Hu Yaobang (20 November 1915 – 15 April 1989) was a Chinese politician who served as a high-ranking official of the People's Republic of China. A close associate and chosen successor-designate of Deng Xiaoping, Hu was one of the leading reformers of the early reform era. He served as Chairman of the Chinese Communist Party from 1981 to 1982 and as General Secretary from 1982 until he was forced to resign in January 1987. His death in April 1989 helped spark the Tiananmen Square protests of 1989.

Early life and career

Hu was born into a poor peasant family in Liuyang, Hunan province. He joined the Communist Youth League in 1929 and the Communist Party in 1933, and participated in the Long March of 1934–35. He rose through the party's youth apparatus, heading the Communist Youth League for many years, and was known for his energy, relative openness, and sympathy for intellectuals.

Reform era

After the Cultural Revolution, during which he was purged, Hu was rehabilitated under Deng Xiaoping. As head of the party's Organization Department he oversaw the rehabilitation of millions of people persecuted during the Cultural Revolution and the Anti-Rightist Campaign. As General Secretary he promoted market-oriented economic reforms, loosened ideological controls, and encouraged freer discussion among intellectuals and students.

Downfall and death

Conservative party elders blamed Hu for being too lenient toward student protests in late 1986, accusing him of tolerating "bourgeois liberalization." He was forced to resign as General Secretary in January 1987 and was replaced by Zhao Ziyang. Hu died of a heart attack on 15 April 1989 during a Politburo meeting. Public mourning for Hu, widely seen as an honest and liberal-minded leader, escalated into the mass protests centered on Tiananmen Square in Beijing, which were suppressed on 4 June 1989.

Legacy

Hu remains a sensitive figure in China because of his association with the 1989 protests. Official commemorations of his birth centenary in 2015, attended by the top leadership, were interpreted by some observers as a partial rehabilitation of his reputation.
