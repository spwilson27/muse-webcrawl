Gandhara

Image placeholder: Greco-Buddhist sculpture of the Gandhara region

Gandhara (Sanskrit: Gandhāra; Urdu: گندھارا) was an ancient Indo-Aryan civilisation centred in what is today northern Pakistan and north-eastern Afghanistan, roughly corresponding to the Peshawar valley, the Pothohar plateau, and the Kabul River basin. It existed from the middle of the first millennium BCE until the beginning of the second millennium CE, and was a major centre of Buddhism and of trade between Central Asia, India, and the Mediterranean world.

History

Gandhara is mentioned in the Rigveda and was a satrapy of the Achaemenid Empire from the sixth century BCE. It was conquered by Alexander the Great in 327–326 BCE, after which it passed through the hands of the Mauryan Empire — under which Buddhism spread widely, especially under Ashoka — and of Indo-Greek, Indo-Scythian, and Indo-Parthian kingdoms. The region reached a high point under the Kushan Empire, particularly under Kanishka in the second century CE, when Gandhara became a great centre of Mahayana Buddhism and a hub of the Silk Road. The region was devastated by the Hephthalite (White Hun) invasions around 450–460 CE and later came under Hindu Shahi and then Ghaznavid rule.

Art and culture

Gandhara is renowned for the Greco-Buddhist art of Gandhara, in which Hellenistic artistic techniques were applied to Buddhist subjects. Gandharan sculptors produced some of the earliest anthropomorphic images of the Buddha, depicting him with wavy hair, heavy classical drapery, and features recalling Apollo. The region's monasteries and stupas — such as Taxila and Takht-i-Bahi — were major centres of learning, and Gandharan monks helped transmit Buddhism into Central Asia and China.

Language

The local language, Gandhari, was written in the Kharosthi script and is known from coins, inscriptions, and the birch-bark Gandharan Buddhist texts, among the oldest surviving Buddhist manuscripts.
