Tickling

Tickling is the act of touching a part of a body in a way that may cause involuntary twitching movements or laughter. The word evolved from the Middle English tikelen, perhaps frequentative of tickon, to touch lightly. The two types of tickle sensation identified by scientists are knismesis, a light, feather-like sensation that does not usually provoke laughter, and gargalesis, a harsher, deeper pressure that induces laughter in humans and other primates.

Physiology

Tickle sensations are carried by nerve fibres also involved in pain and itch. The most ticklish areas — such as the armpits, ribs, abdomen, feet, and neck — tend to be vulnerable regions rich in touch receptors. Tickling oneself is usually ineffective because the cerebellum predicts the sensory consequences of one's own movements and cancels the response.

Social and developmental aspects

Tickling is a common form of social play between parents and children and among peers, and parent–infant tickling games are thought to support bonding. Charles Darwin noted that tickle laughter requires a pleasant state of mind and a light spirit, and that the subject must not know the exact point of contact in advance. Chimpanzees and other great apes produce panting laughter-like vocalizations when tickled, and laboratory rats emit ultrasonic chirps when tickled by handlers — evidence that tickle responses are ancient in mammals.

Tickle torture

Unwanted prolonged tickling can be experienced as deeply unpleasant or distressing, because the laughter response is involuntary and may mask discomfort. Tickling has historically been used as a mild punishment or torment in various cultures, though accounts of it as a formal method of execution are generally considered legendary.
