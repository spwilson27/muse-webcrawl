Child tax credit


Tax credits for families

A child tax credit (CTC) is a tax credit for parents with dependent children given by various countries. The credit is often linked to the number of dependent children a taxpayer has and sometimes the taxpayer's income level. Child tax credits are one of a number of instruments used by governments to offset the costs of raising children and, in many countries, to reduce child poverty.

United States

In the United States, the child tax credit was introduced by the Taxpayer Relief Act of 1997 at $500 per qualifying child under 17. It was progressively increased and partly made refundable. The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 doubled the credit to $2,000 per child, of which up to $1,400 was refundable as the additional child tax credit. The American Rescue Plan Act of 2021 temporarily expanded the credit to $3,600 for children under 6 and $3,000 for children aged 6–17, made it fully refundable for that year, and provided for advance monthly payments during the second half of 2021. The expansion was credited with sharply reducing measured child poverty in 2021; after it lapsed, the credit reverted to its earlier form and the child poverty rate rose again.

United Kingdom

The United Kingdom's Child Tax Credit, introduced in 2003, was a means-tested payment for families with children, administered by HM Revenue and Customs. It was gradually replaced by Universal Credit for new claimants, and the tax credits system was closed entirely in 2025 as remaining claimants were migrated to Universal Credit.

Other countries

Canada provides the Canada Child Benefit, a tax-free monthly payment introduced in 2016. Germany provides Kindergeld, a monthly child allowance, along with a child tax allowance (Kinderfreibetrag). Many other developed countries operate similar payments, differing in whether they are universal or means-tested and whether they are delivered through the tax system or as direct benefits.
