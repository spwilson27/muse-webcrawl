Pausanias the Regent

Bornc. 510 BC, Sparta
Diedc. 470 BC, Sparta
AllegianceSparta
CommandsGreek allied army at the Battle of Plataea (479 BC)
RelationsNephew of Leonidas I; regent for Pleistarchus

Pausanias (Greek: Παυσανίας; died c. 470 BC) was a Spartan regent and general who commanded the combined Greek forces that defeated the Persian army of Mardonius at the Battle of Plataea in 479 BC, the decisive land victory of the Greco-Persian Wars. As regent for his young cousin Pleistarchus, son of King Leonidas I, Pausanias was effectively a leading man of Sparta in the war's final phase. His later arrogance, alleged treasonous dealings with Persia, and dramatic death made him a cautionary figure in Greek historiography, above all in Thucydides' account.

Plataea

After the Persian invasion force under Xerxes withdrew following Salamis (480 BC), his general Mardonius remained in Greece with a large army. In 479 BC Pausanias led the largest Greek army yet assembled—some 40,000 hoplites from Sparta and its allies—to Plataea in Boeotia. After days of manoeuvre and a disorderly night withdrawal, the Greeks won a crushing victory; Mardonius was killed and the Persian army destroyed. Pausanias famously refused to allow the mutilation of Mardonius's corpse in revenge for Leonidas, an act Herodotus praised as noble.

Byzantium and fall

In 478 BC Pausanias commanded the allied fleet that captured Byzantium from the Persians. There he was accused of arrogance, dressing and dining in Persian fashion, releasing Persian prisoners, and secretly negotiating with Xerxes—even offering marriage to the king's daughter—in hopes of becoming tyrant of Greece. Recalled to Sparta and tried, he was acquitted of treason for lack of proof but removed from command; Athenian leader Cimon and the growing Delian League took over the war at sea.

Returning privately to the Hellespont, Pausanias was expelled by the Athenians and settled at Colonae in the Troad before being recalled again. When a helot messenger betrayed his correspondence with Persia, the ephors trapped him: he fled into the temple of Athena of the Brazen House, where the Spartans walled up the doors and starved him to death around 470 BC. His fate illustrates the suspicion with which Sparta treated its own ambitious commanders abroad.
