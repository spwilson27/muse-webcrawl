Religion in ancient Rome

Religion in ancient Rome consisted of varying imperial and provincial religious practices, which were followed both by the citizens of Rome as well as those who were brought under its rule. The Romans thought of themselves as highly religious, and attributed their success as a world power to their collective piety (pietas) in maintaining good relations with the gods (pax deorum). Their polytheistic religion and mythology is known for being intermingled with Greek religion and mythology, as well as the Etruscan religion.

Cult and practice

Roman religion centred on ritual action rather than belief: prayers, vows, processions, and sacrifices performed correctly by priests and magistrates on behalf of the community. Major priesthoods included the pontifices, headed by the pontifex maximus, the augurs who read divine will from birds, and the Vestal Virgins who tended the sacred fire of Vesta. The calendar was crowded with festivals (feriae) such as the Saturnalia and Lupercalia, and every household honoured its lares and penates.

Gods and foreign cults

The Roman pantheon was headed by the Capitoline Triad of Jupiter, Juno, and Minerva; Greek gods were identified with Roman counterparts (Zeus/Jupiter, Aphrodite/Venus) through interpretatio romana. Rome readily imported foreign deities — Cybele from Anatolia, Isis from Egypt, Mithras — and in the imperial period the cult of the deified emperor became a unifying loyalty rite across the provinces.

Christianity and decline

Christianity spread within the empire despite intermittent persecutions, was legalized by Constantine's Edict of Milan (313), and became the state church under Theodosius I (380). Traditional cults were progressively defunded and banned in the 390s, though pagan practices persisted in rural areas and in philosophy for centuries.
