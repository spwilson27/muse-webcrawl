Soviet montage theory is an approach to film editing and a film theory developed by Soviet filmmakers in the 1920s. It holds that editing — the assembly of shots — is the foundation of cinema as an art form: the juxtaposition of two shots produces a meaning present in neither shot alone. The theory grew from the Kuleshov effect experiments and was systematised above all by Sergei Eisenstein, whose writings and films made montage central to film studies.

The Kuleshov effect

Lev Kuleshov's workshop demonstrated that viewers read an actor's neutral expression differently depending on the intercut image (a bowl of soup, a dead child, a woman) — evidence that context, created by editing, determines meaning. His students, including Vsevolod Pudovkin, built a practice of "linkage" montage that constructed scenes and emotions from fragments.

Eisenstein's montage

Eisenstein went further, theorising montage as collision: conflicting images clash to produce a new concept in the viewer's mind, an explicitly dialectical method. He categorised montage as metric, rhythmic, tonal, overtonal and intellectual — the last cutting between images to generate abstract ideas, as when October (1928) juxtaposes Kerensky with a mechanical peacock. Battleship Potemkin (1925), especially the Odessa steps massacre, remains the canonical demonstration.

Vertov and others

Dziga Vertov applied montage to documentary in his Kino-Eye manifestos and Man with a Movie Camera, while Dovzhenko developed a poetic montage in films such as Earth (1930). Stalinist cultural policy turned against "formalism" in the 1930s, curtailing the movement, but its influence on editing worldwide has been permanent.
