Respiratory tract

The respiratory tract is the subdivision of the respiratory system involved with the process of conducting air to and from the sites of gas exchange. It is traditionally divided into the upper respiratory tract and the lower respiratory tract.

Upper respiratory tract

The upper respiratory tract consists of the nose and nasal cavity, the paranasal sinuses, the pharynx (nasopharynx, oropharynx, and laryngopharynx), and the larynx. These structures filter, warm, and moisten incoming air, and the larynx contains the vocal cords used in speech.

Lower respiratory tract

The lower respiratory tract begins with the trachea, which divides into the two main bronchi entering the lungs. The bronchi branch repeatedly into smaller bronchioles, forming the bronchial tree, and terminate in alveolar ducts and alveoli, where gas exchange takes place. The conducting zone (airways that conduct but do not exchange gas) contrasts with the respiratory zone of the alveoli.

Lining and defences

Much of the tract is lined by respiratory epithelium — ciliated pseudostratified columnar epithelium with mucus-producing goblet cells. The mucociliary escalator traps inhaled particles and microorganisms in mucus and moves them upward to be swallowed or expelled, a key defence against infection. Infections of the tract are classified as upper respiratory tract infections (such as the common cold) and lower respiratory tract infections (such as pneumonia and bronchitis).
