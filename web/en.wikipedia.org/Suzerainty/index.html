Suzerainty

Suzerainty (from French suzerain, "overlord") is a relationship in which one state, the suzerain, exercises political control over the foreign relations and defence of a subordinate state — the vassal or tributary state — while allowing it substantial autonomy in internal affairs. It differs from full sovereignty and from outright annexation, and was a common framework in pre-modern and colonial diplomacy.

Historical examples

In medieval Europe, suzerainty described the feudal overlordship of a king or emperor over vassal lords and principalities. The Ottoman Empire exercised suzerainty over tributary states such as Wallachia, Moldavia, the Crimean Khanate and, nominally, Egypt. Imperial China maintained a tributary system in which neighbouring states such as Korea, Vietnam and the Ryukyu Kingdom acknowledged Chinese suzerainty through ritual tribute missions in exchange for recognition and trade. In the 19th century, Britain claimed suzerainty over the Transvaal (South African Republic) under the Pretoria (1881) and London (1884) Conventions — a claim whose ambiguity contributed to tensions leading to the Boer Wars.

Modern usage

Although largely obsolete as a formal legal category, the term has been applied retrospectively to arrangements such as Bhutan's acceptance of Indian guidance in foreign affairs under the 1949 Treaty of Peace and Friendship (revised in 2007 to remove that clause). Suzerainty is distinguished from a protectorate, in which the protecting power typically assumes more direct control, and from suzerainty-like arrangements in federacies and associated states.
