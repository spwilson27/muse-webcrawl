Domino effect

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A domino effect is the cumulative effect produced when one event sets off a series of similar or related events, a form of chain reaction. The term is an analogy to a falling row of dominoes: when the first domino falls, it knocks over the next, and so on in a linear sequence, each fall caused by the one before it.

Use of the term

The expression is used both literally (mechanical chain reactions, collapses) and figuratively, for cascading consequences in politics, economics, ecology and engineering. In international relations it underpinned the "domino theory" of the Cold War, which held that a communist takeover in one country would trigger similar takeovers in its neighbours — a rationale for United States intervention in Southeast Asia. In systems engineering, a domino effect describes failures propagating through coupled systems, as in cascading power-grid blackouts; in finance, it describes contagion between institutions. Related concepts include the butterfly effect, ripple effect and slippery slope.

Physics

In a well-spaced row, each falling domino converts potential energy into kinetic energy and can topple a successor considerably larger than itself, a fact used in demonstration chains in which dominoes grow geometrically in size. The speed of propagation depends on spacing, friction and domino dimensions, and has been studied as a model problem in mechanics.
