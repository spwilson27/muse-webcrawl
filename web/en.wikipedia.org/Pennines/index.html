Pennines

The Pennines, also known as the Pennine Chain or Pennine Hills, are a range of uplands running the length of Northern England. Often described as the "backbone of England" because of their length and position, they run from the Peak District in the south to the Scottish border in the north, separating North West England from Yorkshire and North East England.

Geography

The Pennines stretch about 414 km (257 miles). The highest point is Cross Fell in Cumbria at 893 m (2,930 ft). The range is characterized by gritstone edges, limestone dales, blanket bog, and heather moorland. Major rivers including the Tyne, Wear, Tees, Swale, Aire, Calder, and Trent rise in the hills.

Protected areas

The Pennines include three national parks — the Peak District, Yorkshire Dales, and Northumberland — and several Areas of Outstanding Natural Beauty. The Pennine Way, Britain's first National Trail, opened in 1965 and follows the range.

History

The hills were historically important for wool, coal, lead mining, and the textile mills of Lancashire and Yorkshire on either flank, linked by canals and railways crossing the passes.
