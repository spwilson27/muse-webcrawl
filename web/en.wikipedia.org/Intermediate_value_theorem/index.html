Intermediate value theorem

In mathematical analysis, the intermediate value theorem states that if f is a continuous function whose domain contains the interval [a, b], then it takes on any given value between f(a) and f(b) at some point within the interval. This captures the intuitive idea that the graph of a continuous function can be drawn without lifting the pencil from the paper.

Statement

If f is continuous on [a, b] and u is any number between f(a) and f(b), then there exists a number c in [a, b] such that f(c) = u. A useful special case, sometimes called Bolzano's theorem, states that if f(a) and f(b) have opposite signs, then f has a root in the interval (a, b).

Proof and foundations

The theorem depends on the completeness of the real numbers — it fails for functions defined only on the rational numbers (for example, f(x) = x² − 2 has no rational root). Standard proofs use the least-upper-bound property, the nested interval theorem, or a bisection argument. Bernard Bolzano gave a proof in 1817, seeking to remove appeals to geometric intuition; Augustin-Louis Cauchy provided a proof in 1821. The theorem generalises to connected topological spaces: continuous images of connected sets are connected.

Applications

The intermediate value theorem is used to prove the existence of roots of equations (and underlies numerical root-finding by bisection), to show that every odd-degree real polynomial has a real root, and in classical results such as the Borsuk–Ulam theorem and its corollaries — for instance, that at any moment there are antipodal points on Earth with equal temperature and pressure. A related concept is the Darboux property: derivatives always have the intermediate value property even when they are not continuous, a result known as Darboux's theorem.
