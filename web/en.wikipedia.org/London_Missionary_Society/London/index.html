The London Missionary Society (LMS) was an interdenominational Protestant missionary society founded in England in 1795 by evangelical Anglicans and Nonconformists, largely inspired by the Baptist Missionary Society founded three years earlier. It sent missionaries throughout the Pacific, Africa, and Asia during the 19th and 20th centuries, and its work was absorbed into the Council for World Mission in 1977.

History

The society's first mission sailed in the ship Duff in 1796, arriving in Tahiti in 1797; other parties went to Tonga and the Marquesas. The LMS later became known for work in Polynesia, where its missionaries and Tahitian teachers spread Christianity widely, and in southern Africa, where Robert Moffat and David Livingstone served. John Williams, the "Apostle of Polynesia", was killed on Erromango in 1839.

In China, LMS missionaries such as Robert Morrison, the first Protestant missionary to China (1807), and James Legge combined evangelism with translation and scholarship. In Madagascar, LMS converts faced persecution under Queen Ranavalona I before Christianity became established. The society also worked in India, the Caribbean, and Papua.

Legacy

The LMS is credited with major contributions to linguistics, printing, education, and the written forms of many Pacific and African languages, as well as with campaigning against slavery and the opium trade. Its archives are held at SOAS University of London.
