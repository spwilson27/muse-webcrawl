Alexander Vasilyevich Suvorov (24 November 1730 – 18 May 1800) was a Russian general and military theorist, counted among the greatest commanders in Russian history and one of the few great generals never to have lost a major battle. Catherine the Great made him a count and, near the end of his career, he received the rank of Generalissimo of the Russian Empire.

Early career

Suvorov entered the army as a private in 1748 and gained experience during the Seven Years' War. He distinguished himself in the Russo-Turkish War of 1768–1774, winning at Kozludzha, and subsequently campaigned against the Crimean Khanate and in the Caucasus. His fame rests largely on the Russo-Turkish War of 1787–1792, in which he won crushing victories at Focșani and Rymnik (1789) and stormed the fortress of Izmail (1790).

Later campaigns

In 1794 Suvorov suppressed the Kościuszko Uprising in Poland, storming the Praga suburb of Warsaw. Recalled by Paul I in 1799 despite their mutual dislike, the elderly Suvorov led Austro-Russian forces in northern Italy against Revolutionary France, winning at Cassano, the Trebbia and Novi. Ordered into Switzerland to join a second Russian army that had already been defeated, he fought his way across the Alps through the St Gotthard Pass and escaped encirclement — a retreat that cemented his legend. He died shortly after returning to Saint Petersburg in 1800.

Military thought

Suvorov's manual The Science of Victory stressed speed, surprise and the bayonet, summarised in his maxim "train hard, fight easy" and his emphasis on the soldier's initiative and morale. His name remains embedded in Russian military culture: a Soviet Order of Suvorov was created in 1942, and Suvorov military schools still train cadets.
