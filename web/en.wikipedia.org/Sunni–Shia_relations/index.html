Sunni–Shia relations

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sunni–Shia relations concern the historical and contemporary interactions between the two largest branches of Islam: Sunni Islam, followed by the majority of the world's Muslims, and Shia Islam, the largest minority branch. Relations have ranged from coexistence and cooperation to violent conflict, shaped by theological differences, politics, and regional rivalries.

Origins

The split originated in the succession crisis after the death of the Prophet Muhammad in 632. Those who became Sunnis accepted the caliphate of Abu Bakr and his successors, while those who became Shia held that leadership belonged to Muhammad's cousin and son-in-law Ali and his descendants. The killing of Ali's son Husayn at the Battle of Karbala in 680 became a defining event of Shia identity.

History

Over the centuries, Sunni and Shia communities have usually lived side by side, intermarried, and shared mosques in some regions, while at other times sectarian identity was mobilised by empires such as the Sunni Ottoman Empire and Shia Safavid Persia. In the modern era, sectarian tensions were sharpened by the Iranian Revolution of 1979, the Iran–Iraq War, the 2003 invasion of Iraq and its ensuing sectarian civil war, and proxy conflicts in Syria and Yemen, often described as a rivalry between Saudi Arabia and Iran.

Dialogue and demography

Efforts at reconciliation have included the 2004 Amman Message, which recognised the validity of the major schools of both traditions. Shia constitute a majority in Iran, Iraq, Bahrain, and Azerbaijan and significant minorities elsewhere; sectarian violence remains concentrated in particular countries and conflicts rather than being a universal feature of relations between the communities.
