Polydactyly

SpecialtyMedical genetics, orthopaedics
SymptomsExtra fingers or toes
CausesGenetic; sometimes syndromic
TreatmentSurgery (if desired/needed)

Polydactyly is a congenital condition in which a person or animal is born with extra fingers or toes. The extra digits may range from a small nub of soft tissue to a complete, functional digit, and the condition may affect one or more hands or feet.

Classification

Polydactyly is described by position: preaxial polydactyly involves the thumb or big-toe side, postaxial polydactyly the little-finger or little-toe side (the most common form), and central polydactyly the middle digits. Postaxial polydactyly occurs in roughly 1 in 1,000 births overall and is considerably more frequent in people of African ancestry, while preaxial polydactyly is more common in Asian and Native American populations.

Causes

The condition is often inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern with variable expression and may occur as an isolated trait. It is also a feature of more than a hundred syndromes, including Ellis–van Creveld syndrome, Bardet–Biedl syndrome, trisomy 13 (Patau syndrome) and Greig cephalopolysyndactyly, the latter associated with mutations in the GLI3 gene; limb-patterning involves the Sonic hedgehog (SHH) signalling pathway and its enhancer, the ZRS.

Treatment

Extra digits that are small soft-tissue nubbins may be tied off or removed in infancy; more developed digits, especially on the hand, are usually removed surgically, often between one and two years of age, with reconstruction to preserve function and appearance. Polydactyly also occurs in other animals and is famously common in Maine Coon cats and in the cats descended from Ernest Hemingway's pet Snow White in Key West, Florida.
