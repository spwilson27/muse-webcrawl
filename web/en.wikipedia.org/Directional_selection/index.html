Directional selection

In population genetics, directional selection is a mode of natural selection in which a single phenotype is favoured, causing the allele frequency to continuously shift in one direction over time. It contrasts with stabilizing selection, which favours intermediate phenotypes, and disruptive selection, which favours both extremes.

Mechanism

Under directional selection, individuals with traits on one side of the mean — for example, larger or smaller than average — survive and reproduce better than those with the average trait or the opposite extreme. As a result, the population mean of the trait shifts in the direction of the favoured extreme in succeeding generations.

Examples

A classic example is the peppered moth (Biston betularia) in Britain during the Industrial Revolution: dark (melanic) forms, previously rare, became predominant in polluted industrial areas because they were better camouflaged on soot-darkened trees. Other familiar cases include the evolution of antibiotic resistance in bacteria and pesticide resistance in insect pests, where selection consistently favours resistant individuals.

Consequences

Sustained directional selection reduces genetic variation at the loci involved and can lead to fixation of advantageous alleles; fossil examples include trends in body size in some lineages over geological time.
