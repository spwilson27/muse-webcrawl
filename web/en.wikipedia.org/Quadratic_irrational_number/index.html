Quadratic irrational number

In mathematics, a quadratic irrational number (also known as a quadratic irrational or quadratic surd) is a real number that is irrational and is the root of a quadratic polynomial with rational coefficients not all zero. Quadratic irrationals are a subset of the algebraic numbers.

Properties

Every quadratic irrational can be written in the form a + b√c, where a and b are rational numbers and c is a square-free integer. Joseph-Louis Lagrange proved in 1770 that a real number is a quadratic irrational if and only if its continued fraction expansion is eventually periodic, a theorem sometimes called Lagrange's theorem.

Examples

The golden ratio (1 + √5)/2 is a quadratic irrational, as are √2, √3, and all other square roots of non-square integers. Quadratic irrationals are exactly the irrational numbers whose continued fractions repeat with a finite period.
