GLP-1 receptor agonist

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists, also known as GLP-1 analogs, incretin mimetics, or GLP-1 drugs, are a class of drugs that mimic the action of the incretin hormone GLP-1. They are used in the treatment of type 2 diabetes and obesity. Well-known members include semaglutide (marketed as Ozempic, Wegovy, and Rybelsus), liraglutide, dulaglutide, exenatide, and the dual GIP/GLP-1 agonist tirzepatide.

Mechanism of action

GLP-1 receptor agonists activate receptors in the pancreas, increasing insulin secretion in a glucose-dependent manner and suppressing glucagon release. They also slow gastric emptying and act on appetite centers in the brain, promoting satiety and reduced food intake. Unlike native GLP-1, which is broken down within minutes by the enzyme DPP-4, these drugs are engineered to resist degradation, allowing dosing from twice daily to once weekly.

Uses and effects

The class lowers blood glucose and HbA1c in type 2 diabetes with a low intrinsic risk of hypoglycemia. Several agents have demonstrated reductions in major cardiovascular events, and large trials of semaglutide and tirzepatide showed substantial body-weight reductions, leading to approvals for chronic weight management. Research continues into uses including metabolic liver disease, sleep apnea, kidney disease, and addiction.

Adverse effects

Common side effects are gastrointestinal, including nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and constipation, particularly during dose escalation. Less common risks include gastroparesis, pancreatitis, gallbladder disease, and a boxed warning regarding thyroid C-cell tumors observed in rodents. Demand for these drugs for weight loss led to widespread shortages in the early 2020s and extensive media coverage.
