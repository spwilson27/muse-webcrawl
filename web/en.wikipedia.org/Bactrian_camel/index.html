Bactrian camel

Bactrian camel
Camelus bactrianus
Family: Camelidae
Status: Domesticated; wild form critically endangered

The Bactrian camel (Camelus bactrianus) is a large, even-toed ungulate native to the steppes of Central Asia. It has two humps on its back, in contrast to the single-humped dromedary camel, which lives in the Middle East, North Africa and Australia. The Bactrian camel has been domesticated for thousands of years and is used as a pack animal across the deserts and steppes of Mongolia, China, Kazakhstan and neighbouring regions.

Description

The Bactrian camel stands roughly 1.8 to 2.3 metres tall at the top of the humps and weighs between 300 and 1,000 kilograms. Its two humps store fat, which the animal can convert to water and energy when food and water are scarce. Unlike the dromedary, the Bactrian camel tolerates cold, drought and high altitude, and grows a thick, shaggy winter coat that it sheds in the spring.

Habitat and behaviour

Domesticated Bactrian camels live in the cold deserts and steppes of Central Asia, including the Gobi Desert. They can survive long periods without water and tolerate drinking water saltier than seawater. They feed on thorny plants, dry grasses and other sparse vegetation, and can lose up to a third of their body water without harm.

Wild Bactrian camel

The wild Bactrian camel (Camelus ferus) is genetically distinct from the domestic form and is critically endangered, with a small population surviving in the Gobi and Taklamakan deserts of Mongolia and China. It is considered one of the most endangered large mammals on Earth.

Domestication and uses

Bactrian camels were probably domesticated in Central Asia around 2500 BCE. They carried goods along the Silk Road, and today provide transport, meat, milk, wool and hair for textiles. Camel racing and tourism are also common in Mongolia.
