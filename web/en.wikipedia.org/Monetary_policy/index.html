Monetary policy

Central banks conduct monetary policy on behalf of the state

Monetary policy is the process by which a country's monetary authority — usually its central bank — controls the supply of money and interest rates to achieve macroeconomic goals such as price stability, full employment, and steady growth. It is distinct from fiscal policy, which concerns government taxation and spending. Since the late 20th century, most major central banks — the US Federal Reserve, the European Central Bank, the Bank of Japan, the Bank of England — have operated with formal independence from government and, in many cases, an explicit inflation target, commonly 2%.

Instruments

The principal tool is the short-term policy interest rate (such as the federal funds rate), which influences borrowing costs throughout the economy. Central banks also use open market operations — buying or selling government securities to adjust bank reserves — reserve requirements, and standing lending facilities. When rates reached near zero after the 2008 financial crisis, banks turned to unconventional tools: large-scale asset purchases ("quantitative easing"), forward guidance, and in some cases negative interest rates.

Expansionary and contractionary policy

Lowering rates and expanding the money supply stimulates spending, investment, and employment but risks inflation; raising rates restrains demand and prices at the cost of growth. Policy works with long and variable lags, and its effectiveness can be limited by the zero lower bound on interest rates, liquidity traps, and global capital flows.

History

Monetary policy in the modern sense grew with central banking in the 19th and 20th centuries. The gold standard constrained discretion; after Bretton Woods collapsed in 1971–73, fiat money and floating rates gave central banks freedom they used disastrously in the 1970s inflation, then — under figures such as Paul Volcker — to establish the low-inflation regime and the doctrine of central bank independence.
