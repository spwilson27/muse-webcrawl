Boeing 367-80

The Boeing 367-80, known simply as the Dash 80, is an American quadjet prototype aircraft built by Boeing to demonstrate the advantages of jet propulsion for commercial aviation. It served as the basis for both the KC-135 Stratotanker aerial refueling aircraft and the 707 passenger airliner family.

Development

In 1952, Boeing's board authorized the company to commit $16 million of its own funds — a substantial gamble — to build a prototype jet transport, at a time when most airlines were committed to piston-engined aircraft. The designation 367-80 derived from the Model 367, the C-97 Stratofreighter, whose 80th design study it nominally was; the number also helped conceal the radical nature of the project from competitors.

The Dash 80 first flew on 15 July 1954, piloted by Alvin "Tex" Johnston. It featured a swept wing of 35 degrees, four Pratt & Whitney JT3 turbojet engines in underwing pods, and tricycle landing gear.

The barrel roll

On 6 August 1955, during a demonstration flight over Seattle's Lake Washington for industry executives attending the Seafair hydroplane races, test pilot Tex Johnston performed two barrel rolls in the Dash 80 — a remarkable maneuver for a large transport aircraft. Boeing president Bill Allen had invited airline and industry representatives aboard a yacht to watch, unaware of Johnston's intentions. When summoned to explain himself, Johnston reportedly replied that he was "selling airplanes." He was never formally disciplined.

Legacy

The prototype led directly to the KC-135 Stratotanker, of which more than 800 were built for the U.S. Air Force, and to the Boeing 707, the airliner that inaugurated the Jet Age in commercial service in 1958. After years of test and development work, the Dash 80 was retired in 1972 and donated to the Smithsonian Institution. It is now displayed at the National Air and Space Museum's Steven F. Udvar-Hazy Center near Washington Dulles International Airport.
