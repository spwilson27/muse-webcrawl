Strangeness

In particle physics, strangeness (symbol S) is a property of particles, expressed as a quantum number, related to the number of strange quarks and strange antiquarks in the particle. It is defined as S = −(ns − ns̄), where ns is the number of strange quarks and ns̄ the number of strange antiquarks.

Conservation

Strangeness is conserved in the strong interaction and the electromagnetic interaction, but not in the weak interaction, in which it can change by one unit (ΔS = ±1). This pattern explains the behaviour that led to the quantum number's invention: particles containing strange quarks were produced copiously in pairs by strong interactions (so total strangeness stayed zero), but each then decayed only slowly through weak interactions, giving them anomalously long lifetimes that made them seem "strange".

Examples

The negatively charged kaon K− (an anti-up quark and a strange quark) has strangeness −1. The Lambda baryon Λ (uds) also has strangeness −1, while the Sigma baryons Σ have strangeness −1 and the Xi baryons Ξ, containing two strange quarks, have strangeness −2. The Omega baryon Ω−, made of three strange quarks, has strangeness −3; its prediction by Murray Gell-Mann in the Eightfold Way scheme and its discovery in 1964 was a landmark confirmation of the quark model.

History

The concept was introduced by Murray Gell-Mann (and independently by Kazuhiko Nishijima) in 1953–1955. In the Gell-Mann–Nishijima formula, the electric charge of a hadron is given by Q = I3 + (B + S)/2, where I3 is the third component of isospin and B is baryon number. With the advent of the quark model, strangeness was reinterpreted as simply counting strange quarks.
