Permanent revolution
OriginatorLeon Trotsky
Key textThe Permanent Revolution (1929); Results and Prospects (1906)
TraditionMarxism / Trotskyism
Contrasted withSocialism in one country; two-stage theory

The theory of permanent revolution, most fully developed by Leon Trotsky, holds that in countries of delayed capitalist development the tasks historically associated with a bourgeois-democratic revolution — such as land reform and national unification — can only be completed under the leadership of the proletariat, and that such a revolution must then "grow over" uninterruptedly into a socialist revolution and spread internationally.

Origins

The phrase appears in Marx and Engels's March 1850 Address to the Communist League. Trotsky, drawing on Alexander Parvus, formulated the theory after Russia's 1905 Revolution. In Tsarist Russia, he argued, the bourgeoisie was too weak and dependent on foreign capital and the state to carry through its own revolution; the young working class, concentrated in large factories, would lead the democratic revolution and be compelled to adopt socialist measures, but could survive only if revolution spread to advanced countries.

Debate within communism

After Lenin's death the theory was counterposed to Stalin's doctrine of "socialism in one country" and to the Comintern's two-stage strategy for colonial countries, becoming a defining doctrine of the Trotskyist movement and the Fourth International. Critics within and outside Marxism dispute both its account of 1917 and its prescriptions; supporters cite the Russian Revolution's trajectory as vindication of its central predictions.
