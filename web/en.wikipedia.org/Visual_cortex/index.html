Visual cortex

The visual cortex of the brain is the part of the cerebral cortex that processes visual information. It is located in the occipital lobe at the back of the brain. The primary visual cortex, designated V1 and also called the striate cortex, receives the main input from the eyes via the lateral geniculate nucleus of the thalamus; surrounding areas (V2, V3, V4, V5/MT and others) perform progressively more specialized analyses of colour, form, motion and depth.

Primary visual cortex (V1)

V1 is organized retinotopically: neighbouring neurons represent neighbouring points in the visual field, with a disproportionately large area devoted to the central fovea. Classic experiments by David Hubel and Torsten Wiesel showed that many V1 neurons respond selectively to edges of a particular orientation, and that cells are arranged in columns by orientation preference and ocular dominance. These discoveries earned them the Nobel Prize in 1981.

Extrastriate areas and processing streams

Information flows from V1 to V2 and onward along two broad pathways. The ventral stream, running into the temporal lobe, is concerned with object and face recognition — the "what" pathway; area V4 is important for colour. The dorsal stream, running into the parietal lobe, handles spatial location and visually guided action — the "where/how" pathway; area V5/MT is specialized for motion. Damage to these areas produces distinctive deficits, such as achromatopsia (loss of colour vision) from V4 lesions or akinetopsia (motion blindness) from V5 lesions.

Clinical aspects

Injury to the visual cortex, for example from stroke, produces visual field defects such as homonymous hemianopia, while unusual phenomena including visual hallucinations and cortical blindness (sometimes with denial of blindness, Anton syndrome) can follow occipital damage.
