Fynbos

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Fynbos (Afrikaans for "fine bush") is a distinctive type of shrubland vegetation found only in the Cape Floristic Region of South Africa, chiefly in the Western Cape and Eastern Cape. Growing on nutrient-poor soils in a winter-rainfall, Mediterranean-type climate, fynbos contains an extraordinary concentration of plant species — around 9,000 in the Cape flora, roughly two-thirds of them endemic — making it one of the world's most biodiverse temperate regions.

Characteristics

The archetypal fynbos is built from three elements: tall proteoid shrubs (proteas, including the king protea, South Africa's national flower), ericoid heath-like shrubs with tiny leaves (hundreds of Erica species), and restios — reed-like plants of the Restionaceae that take the place of grasses. Many plants are pollinated by sunbirds, sugarbirds, or specialist insects, and ants disperse the seeds of many species (myrmecochory), burying them safely underground.

Fire ecology

Fynbos is a fire-adapted system that burns every 10–30 years. Many species resprout from lignotubers or fire-proof bark; others are killed and regenerate from seed stored in the soil or held in woody cones in the canopy (serotiny). Some seeds germinate only in response to smoke chemicals (karrikins). Fire suppression and invasive alien trees — especially Australian acacias and pines — are major threats, along with agriculture and urban expansion around Cape Town.

Human use

The fynbos yields rooibos (Aspalathus linearis) and honeybush teas, buchu for traditional medicine and flavouring, and an important cut-flower trade in proteas and pincushions. Much of the biome is protected in reserves such as Table Mountain National Park and the Kogelberg Biosphere Reserve; the Cape Floral Region is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
