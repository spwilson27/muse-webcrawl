Monochrome painting

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Monochrome painting is painting executed in a single colour — or in values of a single hue. Although grisaille and other single-hue techniques are centuries old, the monochrome became a deliberate avant-garde proposition in the 20th century, when artists used it to ask what, at minimum, a painting is.

Early examples

Early provocations include Paul Bilhaud's black painting exhibited in 1882 and Alphonse Allais's humorous monochrome "albums" of the 1880s–90s (an all-white canvas titled First Communion of Anaemic Young Girls in the Snow). Kazimir Malevich's Black Square (1915) and Alexander Rodchenko's 1921 triptych of pure red, yellow, and blue panels — which Rodchenko declared marked the end of painting — made the monochrome a landmark of abstraction.

Post-war monochromes

After 1945 the monochrome flourished in many registers: Yves Klein's saturated International Klein Blue canvases from 1957; Robert Rauschenberg's White Paintings (1951), which John Cage called "airports for lights, shadows and particles"; Ad Reinhardt's nearly black paintings of the 1960s; and the textured white works of Robert Ryman. Later artists — from Ellsworth Kelly to Marcia Hafif, and movements such as the German ZERO group and Korean Dansaekhwa — have treated the single-colour canvas as a vehicle for materiality, meditation, and institutional critique rather than an endpoint.
