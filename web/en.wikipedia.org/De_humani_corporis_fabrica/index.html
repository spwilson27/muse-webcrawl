De humani corporis fabrica libri septem (Latin for "On the fabric of the human body in seven books") is a set of books on human anatomy written by Andreas Vesalius (1514–1564) and published in 1543 in Basel by Johannes Oporinus. It was a major advance in the history of anatomy and a landmark of Renaissance science, correcting many errors inherited from the ancient authority Galen and grounding the discipline in direct observation of dissected human cadavers.

The work

The Fabrica comprises seven books treating, in turn, the bones and joints; the muscles; the veins, arteries, and nerves; the organs of nutrition and generation; the heart and lungs; the brain and senses; and the manner of dissecting. Its several hundred woodcut illustrations — among the most famous images in the history of science — show skeletons and flayed "muscle men" posed against landscape backgrounds. The plates are attributed to artists of Titian's circle, possibly including Jan Stephan van Calcar. Vesalius also published a condensed companion volume, the Epitome, in the same year.

Departure from Galen

Vesalius, professor of surgery and anatomy at the University of Padua, dissected human bodies himself rather than relying on animal dissection and textual tradition. He showed, among other corrections, that the human lower jaw is a single bone rather than two, that the sternum has three parts rather than seven, and that Galen's description of the liver and the network of vessels at the base of the brain (the rete mirabile) did not apply to humans. A revised edition appeared in 1555.

Reception and influence

Dedicated to Emperor Charles V, the Fabrica met both acclaim and fierce resistance from Galenist physicians, yet within a generation its observational method had transformed anatomical teaching across Europe. The work is conventionally ranked with the publications of Copernicus's De revolutionibus (also 1543) as a foundation of the Scientific Revolution.
