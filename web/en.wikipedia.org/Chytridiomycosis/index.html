Chytridiomycosis

Chytridiomycosis is an infectious disease in amphibians, caused by the chytrid fungi Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis (Bd) and Batrachochytrium salamandrivorans (Bsal). It has been implicated in dramatic declines and extinctions of frog populations worldwide and is regarded as one of the most devastating infectious wildlife diseases ever recorded — studies have linked it to declines in at least 501 amphibian species and around 90 presumed extinctions.

Biology

The fungi colonise the keratinised skin of adult amphibians (and the mouthparts of tadpoles). Their motile, flagellated zoospores are released into water and swim to new hosts; inside epidermal cells they form sporangia that discharge further zoospores. Infection thickens the skin (hyperkeratosis) and, crucially, disrupts the skin's role in regulating water, sodium and potassium. Studies have shown that severe infection can reduce plasma electrolyte levels to the point of cardiac arrest, which is the usual cause of death. Bd grows best at cool-to-moderate temperatures (roughly 17–25 °C) and is inhibited or killed by sustained heat above about 28–30 °C.

History and spread

Mass amphibian die-offs from the 1970s onward in Australia and Central America were traced to chytridiomycosis in 1998, when Bd was formally described. The pathogen is thought to have originated in East Asia and to have spread globally through the international trade in live amphibians — including the African clawed frog, long exported for pregnancy testing and laboratory use, and the American bullfrog, farmed for food. In 2013 a second species, Bsal, was identified as the cause of catastrophic fire salamander declines in the Netherlands, prompting import bans on Asian salamanders in the EU and North America.

Conservation response

No method exists to eliminate the fungus from wild populations at landscape scale. Strategies include captive "assurance colonies" and amphibian arks, head-starting and reintroduction programmes, probiotic skin treatments, habitat manipulation to create warm, fungus-poor refuges, and strict biosecurity in the pet and food trades. A small number of species have shown signs of recovery, and some populations appear to be evolving resistance or tolerance.
