Herbivore

A herbivore is an animal anatomically and physiologically adapted to eating plant material — leaves, stems, roots, fruits, seeds, algae and other producers — as the main component of its diet. Herbivores form the second trophic level in most food chains, converting plant energy into animal biomass that supports carnivores and omnivores. They range in size from microscopic zooplankton grazing on phytoplankton to the African bush elephant, the largest living land animal.

Digestive adaptations

Plant tissue, especially cellulose-rich leaves and stems, is difficult to digest, and herbivores have evolved varied strategies to cope. Many rely on symbiotic microorganisms in the gut to ferment cellulose. Foregut fermenters, such as ruminants (cattle, sheep, deer), house microbes in a multi-chambered stomach, chew the cud, and extract nutrients after fermentation; hindgut fermenters, such as horses and rabbits, ferment material in an enlarged caecum and colon after the small intestine. Herbivores typically have long digestive tracts, broad flat molars for grinding, and — in many insects and other invertebrates — specialised mouthparts for piercing, sucking or chewing plant tissue.

Feeding categories

Herbivores are often classified by what they eat: grazers consume grasses; browsers feed on leaves and shoots of woody plants; frugivores eat fruit; granivores eat seeds; folivores specialise in leaves; nectarivores feed on nectar; and xylophages eat wood. Most herbivory in terrestrial ecosystems is by invertebrates, especially insects, though vertebrate herbivores include many fish, reptiles, birds and most hoofed mammals.

Ecology and coevolution

Herbivory is a major ecological force shaping plant evolution and community structure. Plants defend themselves with thorns, tough tissues, hairs and chemical compounds, while herbivores counter-adapt to detoxify or tolerate them — a coevolutionary arms race. Herbivores also disperse seeds, pollinate flowers, recycle nutrients and, at high densities, can transform landscapes, as with grazing ungulates maintaining grasslands. Because only about a tenth of plant energy typically passes to herbivore biomass, herbivores are generally far less abundant in energy terms than the plants they consume but more abundant than the carnivores that prey on them.
