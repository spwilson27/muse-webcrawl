Nucleotide sequence

Nucleotide sequence
Applies toDNA and RNA molecules
UnitsNucleotides (bases A, C, G, T in DNA; A, C, G, U in RNA)
DirectionWritten 5′ to 3′ by convention

A nucleotide sequence — more formally a nucleic acid sequence — is the order of nucleotides in a DNA or RNA molecule, and constitutes the primary structure of that molecule. It is conventionally written as a string of letters representing the nitrogenous bases: A (adenine), C (cytosine), G (guanine) and T (thymine) in DNA, with uracil (U) replacing thymine in RNA. Sequences are written from the 5′ end to the 3′ end.

Notation

Beyond the four standard letters, the IUPAC notation provides ambiguity codes for positions where more than one base may occur, such as R for a purine (A or G), Y for a pyrimidine (C or T), and N for any base. Digital sequences are stored in formats such as FASTA and FASTQ, and in public archives including GenBank, the European Nucleotide Archive and the DNA Data Bank of Japan.

Determination

Sequences are read experimentally by DNA sequencing. Landmark methods include Sanger chain-termination sequencing (1977), used for the first complete genomes and much of the Human Genome Project, and the massively parallel "next-generation" and long-read methods that followed, which made sequencing a routine, inexpensive tool of biology and medicine.

Biological significance

The nucleotide sequence of a gene determines, through transcription and translation, the amino acid sequence of the protein it encodes — three consecutive nucleotides (a codon) specifying each amino acid. Sequences also carry regulatory information in promoters, enhancers and splice sites, and non-coding RNAs derive their function from sequence alone. Comparing sequences between organisms reveals evolutionary relationships, identifies mutations causing disease, and underpins fields from phylogenetics to forensic science.
