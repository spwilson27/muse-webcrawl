ΔF508

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

ΔF508 (delta-F508), in current nomenclature p.Phe508del, is a genetic mutation — the deletion of three nucleotides in the CFTR gene that removes a single amino acid, phenylalanine, at position 508 of the CFTR protein. It is by far the most common cause of cystic fibrosis, accounting for roughly two-thirds of all cystic fibrosis cases worldwide and up to 85–90 per cent of cases in some northern European populations.

Mechanism

The CFTR protein is a chloride and bicarbonate channel that sits in the membrane of epithelial cells in the lungs, pancreas, sweat glands, and other organs. The ΔF508 deletion disrupts the folding of the protein's first nucleotide-binding domain. The cell's quality-control machinery recognises the misfolded protein and destroys most of it in the endoplasmic reticulum (a "processing" or class II defect), so very little channel reaches the cell surface; the small amount that does is also unstable and gates poorly. The result is defective salt and water transport, thick dehydrated mucus, chronic lung infection, and pancreatic insufficiency.

Genetics and distribution

The mutation is inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern: people with two copies develop cystic fibrosis, while carriers with one copy are healthy. Its frequency in Europeans suggests an ancient origin, possibly several thousand years ago, and hypotheses about a heterozygote advantage — for example resistance to secretory diarrhoeas such as cholera — remain debated. Among patients of northern European ancestry about half are homozygous for ΔF508; its frequency is lower in southern European, Ashkenazi Jewish, African, and Asian populations.

Therapy

Because the defect is mainly one of folding and trafficking, ΔF508 became the prime target of "corrector" drugs that help the protein reach the membrane, combined with "potentiators" that improve its opening: first lumacaftor/ivacaftor, then the triple combination elexacaftor/tezacaftor/ivacaftor (Trikafta/Kaftrio), approved in 2019, which benefits the roughly 90 per cent of patients who carry at least one ΔF508 allele.
