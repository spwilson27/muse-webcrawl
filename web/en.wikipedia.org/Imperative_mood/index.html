Imperative mood

Imperative mood
[Image: Road sign with the imperative "Stop" as a linguistic example]
CategoryGrammatical mood
FunctionExpressing commands, requests, prohibitions, advice
Example (English)"Be quiet!" / "Don't touch."

The imperative mood is a grammatical mood that forms a command or request. An imperative sentence typically uses the imperative mood and is often punctuated with an exclamation mark: "Go away!", "Read this book". It usually omits the grammatical subject, which is understood to be the person addressed ("you").

Formation across languages

In English the imperative uses the bare infinitive (base form) of the verb, and the negative imperative is formed with do not ("Don't go"). Many languages have dedicated imperative morphology distinguishing singular and plural, formal and informal address, and sometimes first- and third-person forms: Latin distinguished amā (singular) from amāte (plural); German uses geh! (informal singular) versus gehen Sie! (formal). Distinct forms sometimes exist for negative commands (a "prohibitive"), as in Spanish, where ¡habla! ("speak!") contrasts with ¡no hables! ("don't speak!"). Some languages, such as Finnish and Hungarian, possess a fully inflected imperative paradigm for all persons.

Usage

Besides commands, imperatives serve requests softened by "please", instructions (recipes, manuals), invitations ("Come in"), advice, warnings, and permissions ("Take as many as you like"). Because a bare imperative can sound abrupt, languages deploy politeness strategies — intonation, particles, or alternative constructions such as interrogatives ("Could you open the window?").
