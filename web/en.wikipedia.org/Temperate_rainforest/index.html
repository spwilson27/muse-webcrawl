Temperate rainforest

Temperate rainforests are coniferous or broadleaf forests that occur in the temperate zone and receive heavy rain. They are defined by high annual precipitation — generally at least 140 cm (55 in) — a cool, moist climate moderated by nearby ocean, and the frequent presence of epiphytes such as mosses and ferns growing on the trees. Temperate rainforests are far rarer than tropical rainforests and occur on every continent except Antarctica.

Distribution

The largest temperate rainforests form the Pacific temperate rain forests ecoregion along the western coast of North America, from Alaska to northern California, home to giant conifers including Sitka spruce, coast redwood and western hemlock. Other major regions include the Valdivian temperate rain forest of Chile and Argentina, the forests of Tasmania and southeastern Australia, New Zealand's West Coast, the Colchian rainforests of Turkey and Georgia, and fragments in Japan, Korea and Atlantic Europe.

Ecology

These forests are among the most productive and carbon-dense ecosystems on Earth. Their canopies support rich communities of epiphytes, and their streams sustain salmon and other wildlife. Many stands contain trees several hundred years old. Logging of old growth, particularly in North America and Tasmania, has been the subject of prolonged conservation campaigns, and substantial areas are now protected in national parks and World Heritage sites.
