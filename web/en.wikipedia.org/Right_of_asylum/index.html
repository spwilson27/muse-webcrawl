Right of asylum

The right of asylum, or right to seek asylum, is the right of people to seek protection in a country other than their own when they face persecution. Rooted in ancient practices of sanctuary, it is recognised in modern international human rights law, most prominently in Article 14 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948) and in the 1951 Refugee Convention and its 1967 Protocol.

History

Sanctuary in sacred places — temples, altars, churches — protected fugitives in ancient Greek, Roman, Hebrew and later medieval Christian practice. The modern, secular right of asylum developed with the law of nations: states asserted a sovereign prerogative to grant refuge on their territory, a principle tested in the International Court of Justice's Asylum Case (1950) concerning diplomatic asylum in Latin America.

Legal framework

Under the Refugee Convention, a refugee is a person outside their country of nationality with a well-founded fear of persecution for reasons of race, religion, nationality, membership of a particular social group or political opinion. The cornerstone obligation is non-refoulement (Article 33): no state may return a refugee to a territory where their life or freedom would be threatened. The Universal Declaration states that everyone has the right "to seek and to enjoy" asylum from persecution, though international law generally treats the grant of asylum as a right of states, with individual rights to seek it and not to be refouled. The UNHCR supervises the Convention's application.

Regional and national systems

The European Union operates a Common European Asylum System, with the Dublin Regulation allocating responsibility among member states; the EU Charter of Fundamental Rights guarantees the right to asylum. The United States grants asylum under the Immigration and Nationality Act as amended by the Refugee Act of 1980, with affirmative applications to USCIS and defensive claims in immigration court. Many constitutions — for example Germany's Basic Law — contain express asylum provisions.

Contemporary issues

Large displacement crises, mixed migration flows, offshore processing, safe-third-country agreements and disparities in recognition rates keep asylum at the centre of political controversy worldwide. By the mid-2020s the number of forcibly displaced people globally exceeded 110 million, including refugees, internally displaced persons and asylum seekers.
