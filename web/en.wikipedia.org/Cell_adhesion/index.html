Cell adhesion

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia. This article is about how cells stick to one another and to their surroundings — distinct from cellular respiration, the process by which cells generate energy.

Cell adhesion is the process by which cells interact with and attach to neighbouring cells or to the extracellular matrix (ECM) through specialized molecules on the cell surface called cell adhesion molecules (CAMs). Cell adhesion is fundamental to the formation and maintenance of tissues and organs in multicellular organisms, and it also plays roles in cell migration, embryonic development, inflammation, wound healing, and the spread of cancer (metastasis).

Adhesion molecule families

  • Cadherins — calcium-dependent molecules mediating homophilic cell–cell adhesion (like-with-like); E-cadherin is central in epithelia, and its loss is a hallmark of invasive carcinoma.
  • Integrins — heterodimeric receptors linking cells to ECM proteins such as fibronectin and collagen, and connecting to the cytoskeleton at focal adhesions.
  • Selectins — mediate transient adhesion of leukocytes to blood-vessel walls, enabling rolling before extravasation during inflammation.
  • Immunoglobulin superfamily CAMs — including NCAM and ICAMs, involved in neural development and immune interactions.

Junctional structures

Cells organize adhesion molecules into junctions: adherens junctions and desmosomes anchor cells to each other (via cadherins), hemidesmosomes and focal adhesions anchor cells to the matrix (via integrins), while tight junctions seal epithelial sheets and gap junctions allow direct communication between cells.

Clinical relevance

Defects in adhesion cause disease: mutations in desmosomal proteins produce blistering skin disorders and cardiomyopathies; leukocyte adhesion deficiency impairs immune responses; and altered adhesion is a key step in tumour invasion and metastasis. Adhesion molecules are also targets for drugs, e.g., integrin and selectin inhibitors.
