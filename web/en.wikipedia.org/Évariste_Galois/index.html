Évariste Galois (25 October 1811 – 31 May 1832) was a French mathematician and political activist. While still a teenager he created an entirely new branch of algebra — now called Galois theory — that determines when a polynomial equation can be solved by radicals, and in doing so laid the foundations of group theory and abstract algebra. He died at twenty from wounds suffered in a duel.

Life

Galois was born in Bourg-la-Reine, near Paris, the son of the town's mayor. After a dazzling but unruly school career at the Lycée Louis-le-Grand, he twice failed the entrance examination to the École Polytechnique and entered the less prestigious École Normale. His memoirs submitted to the Academy of Sciences were famously lost, rejected, or misunderstood — one by Cauchy, one by Fourier (who died), one returned by Poisson as incomprehensible. A fervent republican during the July Revolution of 1830, Galois was expelled from school, jailed twice for political activity, and acquitted of threatening the king's life.

The duel and the last night

On 30 May 1832 Galois was shot in a duel — possibly over a love affair, possibly a political provocation — and died the next day, reportedly refusing a priest and telling his brother "Don't cry; I need all my courage to die at twenty." The night before, famously, he wrote letters to friends and annotated his manuscripts, sketching the theory that Joseph Liouville finally published in 1846.

Mathematics

Galois's insight was to attach to each polynomial equation a group of permutations of its roots (the Galois group). An equation is solvable by radicals exactly when this group is "solvable" in a technical sense — explaining why the general quintic has no algebraic formula, a result Abel had approached differently. His ideas generalised into the Galois correspondence between field extensions and groups, now a cornerstone of modern algebra, number theory, and algebraic geometry.
