The Bank of Saint George (Italian: Banco or Casa di San Giorgio) was a financial institution of the Republic of Genoa, founded in 1407. It is often regarded as one of the oldest chartered banks in Europe — many historians consider it the first modern state deposit bank — and it was among the most powerful financial institutions of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries. Niccolò Machiavelli praised its stability and wrote that it effectively governed much of Genoa's affairs.

Origins

The bank grew out of the consolidation of Genoa's public debt. The republic had financed itself by assigning revenues to associations of creditors (called compere); in 1407 these were merged into the Casa di San Giorgio, which administered the debt and collected the taxes assigned to service it. Shares in the debt, called luoghi, were freely traded and functioned as a form of negotiable public security.

Activities

From its headquarters in the Palazzo San Giorgio, the bank managed customs and tax revenues, operated deposit and transfer banking (including early giro payments), and at times administered Genoese overseas possessions — including Corsica and trading posts in the Black Sea and Crimea — when the republic handed them over to its creditors. It financed monarchs and ventures across Europe and survived until 1805, when it was suppressed under Napoleonic rule.

Legacy

The Casa di San Giorgio is studied as a pioneering example of funded public debt, of a creditor institution exercising governmental functions, and of an early "state within a state." Its practices anticipated features of later central and national banks.
