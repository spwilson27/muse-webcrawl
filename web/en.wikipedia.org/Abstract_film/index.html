Abstract film

Abstract film is a subgenre of experimental film characterised by the absence of narrative, actors, and representational imagery. Instead, abstract films work with the plastic elements of cinema — colour, shape, movement, rhythm, and light — much as abstract painting and absolute music work in their media. Many were conceived as "visual music".

Early history

The movement emerged in Europe in the 1920s. In Germany, Hans Richter (Rhythmus 21, 1921), Viking Eggeling (Symphonie diagonale, completed 1924), and Walter Ruttmann (Lichtspiel: Opus I, 1921) animated painted and drawn geometric forms; Oskar Fischinger later refined the art in works synchronised to music. In France, Fernand Léger's Ballet Mécanique (1924) and the films of Man Ray and Marcel Duchamp (Anémic Cinéma, 1926) brought Dada and Cubist ideas to cinema.

Later developments

Émigrés such as Fischinger and Len Lye carried abstract animation to Britain and Hollywood; Lye pioneered painting and scratching directly on film stock (A Colour Box, 1935). In North America, Mary Ellen Bute, Norman McLaren (National Film Board of Canada), John and James Whitney (using mechanical and later computer techniques), Jordan Belson, and Stan Brakhage extended the field toward hand-painted film, optical printing, and computer graphics. Walt Disney's Fantasia (1940), with Fischinger's uncredited involvement, brought abstract sequences to mass audiences. Abstract methods persist in music videos, title sequences, and digital motion graphics.
