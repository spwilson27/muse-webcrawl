Mondo film

Mondo films are a subgenre of exploitation films, purportedly documentaries, that present sensationalist and taboo subjects from around the world — exotic customs, rituals, violence, sexuality and death — typically as a loosely connected series of vignettes united by travelogue-style narration. The genre takes its name from the Italian film Mondo Cane ("A Dog's World", 1962) by Gualtiero Jacopetti, Paolo Cavara and Franco Prosperi, whose international success spawned dozens of imitations.

Characteristics

Mondo films claim documentary authenticity while staging, re-enacting or misrepresenting much of their material. A detached, often ironic voice-over links scenes deliberately chosen to shock or titillate, juxtaposing "primitive" rites with the alleged decadence of modern Western life to suggest a world united in cruelty and absurdity. Titles frequently begin with Mondo ("world"), and posters and trailers emphasised the films' supposed banned or uncensored content.

History

The genre's roots lie in earlier travelogues and "jungle" exploitation pictures. Mondo Cane was nominated for an Academy Award for its song "More" and screened at Cannes, giving the format unusual respectability. Sequels and successors — Mondo Cane 2 (1963), Women of the World (1963), Africa Addio (1966), a controversial account of post-colonial Africa, and Goodbye Uncle Tom (1971), about American slavery — pushed the formula toward politics and ever stronger material.

In the late 1970s the genre shaded into the "snuff"-tinged shockumentary: Faces of Death (1978) and its sequels presented purported scenes of real death, many of them faked, to enormous home-video success. Similar series such as Traces of Death continued the format into the 1990s.

Criticism and legacy

Critics have charged mondo films with racism, voyeurism and fraud, while some scholars treat them as revealing documents of post-war Western anxieties and of the economics of exploitation cinema. Their compilation format and faux-documentary shock tactics anticipated reality television and internet shock content, and the films have been reappraised by cult-film audiences and released in restored editions.
