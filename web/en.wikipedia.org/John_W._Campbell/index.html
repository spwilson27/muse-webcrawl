John Wood Campbell Jr. (June 8, 1910 – July 11, 1971) was an American science fiction writer and editor. As editor of Astounding Science Fiction (later Analog) from 1937 until his death, he is credited with shaping the so-called Golden Age of Science Fiction.

Writing

Campbell studied physics at MIT and Duke University. He began publishing space-opera stories in the early 1930s, and under the pen name Don A. Stuart wrote more thoughtful stories, most famously the novella "Who Goes There?" (1938), later adapted as the films The Thing from Another World and The Thing.

Editorship

At Astounding, Campbell demanded plausible science and rigorous extrapolation, and discovered or nurtured writers including Isaac Asimov, Robert A. Heinlein, L. Sprague de Camp, Theodore Sturgeon and A. E. van Vogt. His editorials championed everything from nuclear power to, controversially, Dianetics and psionics. The posthumous Hugo Award for Best Editor was repeatedly won by him, and the award for best new writer long bore his name.

Legacy

Campbell's influence on science fiction is unmatched among editors, though his views on race and his editorial contrarianism have been widely debated in later assessments of his career.
