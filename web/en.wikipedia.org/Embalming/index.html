Embalming

Embalming is the art and science of preserving human remains by treating them with chemicals to forestall decomposition. The intention is to keep the remains suitable for public display at a funeral, for religious reasons, or for medical and scientific purposes such as their use as anatomical specimens.

History

The best-known ancient practitioners of embalming were the Egyptians, whose mummification practices removed internal organs, desiccated the body with natron, and wrapped it in linen. Embalming techniques were also practiced by other cultures, including the Chinchorro of South America, whose mummies predate those of Egypt. Modern embalming developed during the American Civil War, when the need to return dead soldiers home over long distances prompted surgeons such as Thomas Holmes to develop arterial embalming methods.

Modern practice

Modern embalming typically involves replacing the blood with a preservative fluid — usually a formaldehyde-based solution — injected into an artery while blood is drained from a vein, followed by cavity treatment of the internal organs. Different chemicals are used depending on the purpose and the condition of the remains. Embalming is most common in the United States and Canada for funeral viewings; it is generally temporary, delaying rather than preventing decomposition, and is not required by law in most circumstances.

Controversy

Embalming has been criticised on environmental grounds because of the release of formaldehyde into soil and groundwater, and some religions, including Judaism and Islam, generally prohibit the practice.
