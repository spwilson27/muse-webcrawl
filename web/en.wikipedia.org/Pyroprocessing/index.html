Pyroprocessing

Pyroprocessing is a general term for high-temperature processes. In the nuclear industry it specifically denotes a form of nuclear fuel reprocessing carried out at high temperatures in molten salt, using electrorefining to recover uranium and other actinides from spent nuclear fuel. The term is also used in cement manufacture, where pyroprocessing refers to the kiln stage of production.

Pyroprocessing
Field: Nuclear fuel cycle; cement
Key step: Electrorefining in molten salt
Developed at: Argonne National Laboratory
Associated reactor: Integral Fast Reactor

Nuclear fuel reprocessing

In pyroprocessing, spent oxide or metallic fuel is treated in a molten chloride salt bath at around 500 °C. In an electrorefiner, uranium deposits on a solid cathode while plutonium and other transuranic elements are collected together in a liquid cadmium cathode, so that plutonium is never separated in pure form. The process was developed at Argonne National Laboratory in the United States for the Integral Fast Reactor programme and has been pursued in South Korea and elsewhere.

Proliferation debate

Supporters argue pyroprocessing is more proliferation-resistant than the aqueous PUREX process because plutonium remains mixed with other highly radioactive actinides. Critics respond that the recovered material is still weapons-usable in principle and that safeguards accounting for bulk molten-salt processes is difficult; the question has been politically sensitive, notably in South Korea's negotiations with the United States.

Cement industry usage

In cement plants, pyroprocessing encompasses the preheater, calciner, and rotary kiln in which raw meal is converted to clinker at about 1450 °C — a high-temperature treatment analogous to those in pyrometallurgy.
