Cape Cod Canal

The Cape Cod Canal is an artificial waterway in Massachusetts connecting Cape Cod Bay in the north to Buzzards Bay in the south. Approximately 7 miles (11 km) long, it cuts across the base of the Cape Cod peninsula and is part of the Atlantic Intracoastal Waterway. It is crossed by two highway bridges and the Cape Cod Canal Railroad Bridge.

History

A canal across the isthmus was proposed as early as the 1620s by Miles Standish, and surveys continued for nearly three centuries. Construction finally began in 1909 under financier August Belmont Jr.; the canal opened as a private toll waterway in 1914. It was initially narrow and difficult to navigate, and after a German U-boat sank ships off the Cape in 1918 the United States government took control. The Army Corps of Engineers widened it to 480 feet and deepened it between 1935 and 1940, and the federal government purchased it outright.

Operation

The canal is operated toll-free by the US Army Corps of Engineers. Strong tidal currents reverse direction roughly every six hours and can exceed 5 knots, so vessel traffic is monitored from a marine traffic control centre in Sandwich. About 14,000 vessels, from cruise ships to tugboats and pleasure craft, transit annually, saving roughly 135 miles of open-ocean travel around the Cape's dangerous shoals.

Recreation

Paved service roads along both banks form popular paths for cycling, walking, fishing, and rollerblading. The Cape Cod Canal Visitor Center in Sandwich presents exhibits on the canal's construction and natural history.
