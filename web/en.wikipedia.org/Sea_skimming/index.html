Sea skimming

Sea skimming is a technique used by many anti-ship missiles — and formerly by aircraft — to fly very low over the surface of the sea in their terminal phase, in order to avoid detection by radar and infrared sensors and to reduce the time available for defensive fire.

Principle

A sea-skimming missile typically cruises at altitudes of only a few metres above the waves, guided by an inertial system and a radar altimeter, sometimes with satellite navigation updates. Because of the curvature of the Earth and the radar horizon, a ship's radar may not detect such a missile until it is relatively close, compressing reaction time. Sea clutter also makes tracking difficult.

Examples

Famous sea-skimming missiles include the French Exocet, the American Harpoon, the Norwegian Naval Strike Missile, and many Russian and Chinese designs. The Exocet's use in the Falklands War in 1982 demonstrated the threat posed to warships.

Countermeasures

Defences include airborne early warning aircraft looking down at the sea surface, close-in weapon systems such as Phalanx CIWS, surface-to-air missiles optimised for low targets, electronic warfare, and manoeuvre.
