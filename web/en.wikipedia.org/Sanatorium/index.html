Sanatorium

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sanatorium

Type: Long-stay medical facility

Principal association: Treatment of tuberculosis (19th–20th centuries)

Regimen: Rest, fresh air, heliotherapy, diet, graded exercise

Pioneer: Hermann Brehmer (Görbersdorf, 1854)

Declined with: Antibiotics (streptomycin, 1944)

A sanatorium (also spelled sanitorium) is a medical facility for the treatment of long-term illness, most famously associated with the care of tuberculosis patients in the decades before effective chemotherapy. At their height in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, sanatoria were built across Europe and North America, usually in mountain or countryside locations prized for pure air, and prescribed a regimen of rest, rich diet, sunlight and closely supervised routine lasting months or years.

History

The modern sanatorium movement began with Hermann Brehmer, who opened an institution at Görbersdorf in Silesia in 1854 based on his thesis that tuberculosis could be cured by rest, good nutrition and mountain air. At Davos in Switzerland, Alexander Spengler’s clinics turned the town into an international resort for wealthy consumptives — the setting of Thomas Mann’s novel The Magic Mountain (1924). In the United States, Edward Trudeau founded the Adirondack Cottage Sanitarium at Saranac Lake in 1885 after his own recovery in the mountains. Public and charitable sanatoria soon multiplied for working-class patients, and the fresh-air philosophy produced open-air schools and preventoria for children.

The sanatorium era also shaped architecture: Alvar Aalto’s Paimio Sanatorium (1933) in Finland and other modernist buildings were designed around sunlight, hygiene and the “cure porch”. The discovery of streptomycin in 1943–44 and the arrival of combination antibiotic therapy made the institutions obsolete within a generation; most were closed, converted to general hospitals or hotels, or demolished by the 1960s. In the Soviet Union and its successor states, by contrast, the sanatoriy survived as a state-provided spa-and-rest institution for workers, a meaning the word retains in much of Eastern Europe.
