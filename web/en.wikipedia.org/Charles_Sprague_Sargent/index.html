Charles Sprague Sargent (April 24, 1841 – March 22, 1927) was an American botanist, the foremost American dendrologist of his generation, and the first director of Harvard University's Arnold Arboretum in Boston, a post he held for 54 years. He was a leading figure in the early American conservation movement and the leading authority on North American trees.

Arnold Arboretum

Appointed in 1872, Sargent persuaded Harvard and the City of Boston to an innovative arrangement in which the university leased the arboretum's land to the city for a nominal sum while retaining control of the collections — a model that preserved the 281-acre landscape designed with Frederick Law Olmsted as part of Boston's "Emerald Necklace" park system. Sargent dispatched collectors such as E. H. Wilson to China and Japan, introducing thousands of Asian plants to Western horticulture, and built the arboretum's library and herbarium into world-class resources.

Work and conservation

Sargent authored the monumental Silva of North America (1891–1902), a fourteen-volume survey of the continent's trees illustrated by Charles Edward Faxon, along with the Manual of the Trees of North America (1905) and a catalogue of the forest trees of the United States for the 1880 census, produced with data collected nationwide. As chairman of commissions advising on the Adirondack forests and on a national forest policy, he helped shape the movement that produced the forest reserve system under presidents including Grover Cleveland, though his imperious style made him enemies among forestry professionals such as Gifford Pinchot, who favored managed use over Sargent's preservationist leanings.
