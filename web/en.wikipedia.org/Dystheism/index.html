Dystheism

Representation of a malevolent deity (placeholder image)

Dystheism (from Greek dys-, "bad", and theos, "god") is the belief that a god is not wholly good and may be evil. It is related to, but distinct from, misotheism, the hatred of God, and from the philosophical problem of evil, which asks how an omnipotent and wholly good God could permit suffering. A stronger position, sometimes called maltheism, holds that God is positively malevolent.

History and examples

Figures in mythology often embody morally ambiguous or hostile divinity: trickster gods, wrathful deities in ancient religions, and the Demiurge of many Gnostic systems, who created a flawed material world in ignorance or malice and is contrasted with a higher, benevolent God. In literature, dystheistic views have been attributed to some readings of the Marquis de Sade and to works that depict a cruel or indifferent creator; the poet Algernon Charles Swinburne and the philosopher Philipp Mainländer are also sometimes associated with such ideas.

Relation to the problem of evil

Where classical theodicy attempts to reconcile God's goodness with the existence of evil, dystheism simply denies one premise: God is not perfectly good. Philosophers distinguish dystheism from the view that God is indifferent (sometimes called amphitheism), and from atheism, which denies God's existence altogether.
