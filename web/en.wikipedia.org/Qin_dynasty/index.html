The Qin dynasty (Chinese: 秦朝; pinyin: Qín cháo) was the first imperial dynasty of China, lasting from 221 to 206 BCE. Growing out of the western state of Qin, which had been transformed by the Legalist reforms of Shang Yang in the fourth century BCE, it conquered the other six Warring States and unified China under an emperor for the first time.

Unification

King Ying Zheng of Qin completed the conquest of Han, Zhao, Wei, Chu, Yan, and Qi between 230 and 221 BCE, then proclaimed himself Qin Shi Huang, "First Emperor of Qin," and expected his line to rule for ten thousand generations. The name "China" in Western languages is generally thought to derive from "Qin."

Reforms

Guided by the chancellor Li Si, the dynasty centralized administration through commanderies and counties staffed by appointed officials; standardized the written script (small seal script), currency (the round banliang coin), weights, measures, and even axle lengths; built networks of roads and canals; and joined earlier frontier walls into a continuous northern defense, the precursor of the Great Wall. Legalist ideology underpinned the regime; the traditional account of the "burning of books and burying of scholars" (213–212 BCE) symbolized its suppression of dissent, though historians debate its scale.

Collapse

Massive corvée labor for the Great Wall, the Epang Palace, and the First Emperor's mausoleum — guarded by the Terracotta Army — together with crushing taxes and draconian punishments, bred widespread resentment. After Qin Shi Huang died in 210 BCE, a succession coup installed the weak Qin Er Shi under the eunuch Zhao Gao. Rebellions began in 209 BCE, led initially by Chen Sheng and Wu Guang, and were carried forward by Xiang Yu and Liu Bang; the dynasty fell in 206 BCE, and Liu Bang founded the Han dynasty after defeating Xiang Yu in the Chu–Han Contention.

Legacy

Though the shortest major dynasty, Qin's centralized bureaucratic empire, standardized script and measures, and imperial title endured as the template of Chinese government for two millennia.
