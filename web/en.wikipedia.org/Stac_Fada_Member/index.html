The Stac Fada ejecta blanket at Stoer on the north-west coast of Scotland.

The Stac Fada Member is a distinctive layer of rock in the Torridonian Stoer Group of northwest Scotland, interpreted as the ejecta blanket of a meteorite impact that occurred about 1.2 billion years ago. Named after the small promontory of Stac Fada on the Stoer peninsula, the unit consists of a chaotic mixture of shattered rock fragments (breccia) and melted material (suevite-like impactite) within the red sandstones of the Stoer Group.

Description

The layer, up to about 20 metres (66 ft) thick, contains shocked quartz, fragments of Lewisian gneiss basement, and distinctive green glassy material derived from impact melt. It can be traced along strike for at least 50 km (31 mi) and thins away from a maximum near Stoer, a pattern consistent with debris thrown out from an impact crater. Argon–argon dating of impact melt material has given an age of 1,177 ± 5 million years.

The crater

The original crater has never been directly observed. Gravity and magnetic evidence has been used to suggest a buried or eroded structure possibly under the Minch or near Lairg; in 2019 researchers proposed, from the distribution and thickness of ejecta and the direction of debris flow, that the crater — roughly 20 km (12 mi) wide — lies beneath the sea some 15–20 km west of Enard Bay. The event is among the largest known impacts to have struck the area of present-day Britain and predates complex land life by hundreds of millions of years.
