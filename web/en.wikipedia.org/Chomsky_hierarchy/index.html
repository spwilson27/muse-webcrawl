Chomsky hierarchy

The Chomsky hierarchy (infrequently referred to as the Chomsky–Schützenberger hierarchy) is a containment hierarchy of classes of formal grammars. The hierarchy was described by the linguist Noam Chomsky in 1956 and is fundamental to theoretical computer science and formal language theory.

The hierarchy

The hierarchy consists of the following levels, from most to least powerful:

TypeGrammarLanguageAutomaton
Type-0UnrestrictedRecursively enumerableTuring machine
Type-1Context-sensitiveContext-sensitiveLinear-bounded automaton
Type-2Context-freeContext-freeNondeterministic pushdown automaton
Type-3RegularRegularFinite-state automaton

Each class of languages is a proper subset of the class above it: every regular language is context-free, every context-free language is context-sensitive, and every context-sensitive language is recursively enumerable, but the converses do not hold.

Significance

The hierarchy connects formal grammars to the machines that recognize them and underlies parsing theory in computer science: regular expressions describe Type-3 languages, while the syntax of most programming languages is described by context-free (Type-2) grammars. Chomsky originally proposed the hierarchy as a framework for the study of natural language syntax; it helped launch the field of generative linguistics.
