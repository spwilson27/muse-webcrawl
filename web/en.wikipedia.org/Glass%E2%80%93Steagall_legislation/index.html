Glass–Steagall legislation

The Glass–Steagall legislation describes four provisions of the United States Banking Act of 1933 that separated commercial banking from investment banking. Named after Senator Carter Glass and Representative Henry B. Steagall, the provisions barred commercial banks from underwriting or dealing in securities (with limited exceptions), prohibited investment banks from taking deposits, and restricted affiliations between the two types of institution. The Act also created the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC).

Background

Following the stock market crash of 1929 and thousands of bank failures, Congress investigated conflicts of interest in banks that had used depositors' funds to underwrite and distribute securities. The separation was intended to restore confidence and reduce speculative risk in the banking system.

Repeal and debate

During the 1980s and 1990s regulators progressively relaxed the separation, and the Gramm–Leach–Bliley Act of 1999 repealed the affiliation restrictions, permitting financial conglomerates such as Citigroup. After the financial crisis of 2007–2008, some economists and politicians argued the repeal had contributed to the crisis — a claim others dispute — and the Volcker Rule of the 2010 Dodd–Frank Act partially reinstated separation by limiting proprietary trading by banks.
