March on Washington for Jobs and Freedom

The March on Washington for Jobs and Freedom, commonly known as the March on Washington or the Great March on Washington, was held in Washington, D.C., on August 28, 1963. The purpose of the march was to advocate for the civil and economic rights of African Americans. At the march, final speaker Dr. Martin Luther King Jr., standing in front of the Lincoln Memorial, delivered his historic "I Have a Dream" speech in which he called for an end to racism and racial segregation.

Background and organisation

The march revived the idea of A. Philip Randolph's threatened 1941 March on Washington, which had pressured President Roosevelt into desegregating the defence industries. Organised principally by Bayard Rustin, it united the "Big Six" organisations — among them the NAACP, SCLC, SNCC and the National Urban League — behind demands including comprehensive civil rights legislation, desegregation of schools, a federal jobs programme, and a $2 minimum wage. The Kennedy administration, initially wary, came to support the march once assured it would be peaceful and disciplined.

The day

An estimated 250,000 people — the largest demonstration yet seen in the capital — gathered between the Washington Monument and the Lincoln Memorial. Speakers included Randolph, NAACP leader Roy Wilkins, John Lewis of SNCC (whose sharp original text was moderated under pressure), and union leader Walter Reuther; performers included Mahalia Jackson, Bob Dylan and Joan Baez. King spoke last, setting aside his prepared text to develop the "dream" passage at Jackson's urging ("Tell them about the dream, Martin"). After the march, leaders met President John F. Kennedy at the White House.

Legacy

The march is credited with helping to build the political momentum that produced the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965. King's speech became one of the most famous in American history. Later anniversaries — the 20th anniversary march of 1983, the 50th in 2013 and the Commitment March of 2020 — consciously echoed its themes of jobs, voting rights and equality.
