Śūnyatā

Sanskritशून्यता (śūnyatā)
Palisuññatā
Literal meaningEmptiness, voidness
TraditionBuddhism (esp. Mahāyāna)

Śūnyatā (Sanskrit: शून्यता; Pali: suññatā), often translated as "emptiness", "voidness", or "openness", is a central Buddhist concept holding that all phenomena are empty (śūnya) of inherent, independent existence (svabhāva). Nothing — including the self — exists by its own nature; things arise only in dependence on causes, conditions, and conceptual designation. Śūnyatā is not a claim that nothing exists, but that things lack the self-sufficient essence that unenlightened perception attributes to them.

Early Buddhism

In the early discourses, "empty" is first applied to the sense-fields and to meditative attainment: in the Cūḷasuññata Sutta the Buddha describes a progressive emptying of perception, and the senses and their objects are said to be "empty of a self or of anything pertaining to a self". Emptiness here is closely tied to the doctrines of impermanence (anicca), suffering (dukkha), and not-self (anattā), and to dependent origination (pratītyasamutpāda).

Madhyamaka

The philosopher Nāgārjuna (c. 150–250 CE), founder of the Madhyamaka ("Middle Way") school, made śūnyatā the keystone of his Mūlamadhyamakakārikā: whatever is dependently arisen is emptiness, and emptiness itself is a dependent designation — "the middle way". Nāgārjuna equated dependent origination, emptiness, and the middle path, and argued that emptiness too is empty (śūnyatā-śūnyatā), so that emptiness must not be grasped as a metaphysical absolute; those who reify it, he warned, are "incurable". The Prajñāpāramitā sutras, especially the Heart Sutra's famous declaration "form is emptiness, emptiness is form", gave the teaching wide currency throughout Mahāyāna Buddhism.

Later developments

In Yogācāra and later traditions, emptiness was elaborated through such schemes as the three natures and the "emptiness of emptiness". In East Asian Buddhism, śūnyatā interacted with Chinese thought to shape Chan/Zen, where it is expressed in the imagery of the empty circle (ensō) and in koan practice; in Tibetan Buddhism it remains the centrepiece of philosophical debate, interpreted variously by the Rangtong and Shentong schools. Realising emptiness — the wisdom (prajñā) that sees things as they are — is held to cut the root of ignorance and liberate beings from saṃsāra.
