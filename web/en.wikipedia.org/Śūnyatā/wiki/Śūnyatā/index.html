Śūnyatā

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Śūnyatā (Sanskrit: शून्यता; Pali: suññatā), often translated as emptiness, voidness, or openness, is a central concept in Mahāyāna Buddhism. It holds that all phenomena are empty (śūnya) of inherent existence or essence (svabhāva): things exist only dependently, in relation to causes, conditions, parts, and the mind that designates them.

Development

The idea grows out of the early Buddhist doctrines of impermanence (anicca), non-self (anattā), and dependent origination (pratītyasamutpāda). It received its classical philosophical form in the Prajñāpāramitā sutras (such as the Heart Sutra, which declares "form is emptiness, emptiness is form") and in the treatises of Nāgārjuna (c. 2nd century CE), founder of the Madhyamaka school. In his Mūlamadhyamakakārikā, Nāgārjuna argued that whatever arises dependently is empty of intrinsic nature, and that emptiness itself is not a thing — it too is empty.

Interpretation

Śūnyatā is not a doctrine of nothingness or nihilism. Nāgārjuna's "middle way" denies both eternalism (things exist in themselves) and annihilationism (things do not exist at all): phenomena function and can be experienced precisely because they lack fixed essence. Understanding emptiness is held to dissolve attachment and aversion, since nothing grasped at can bear the weight of permanence the mind projects onto it.

In other traditions

The term also appears in Hindu philosophy — for instance in some readings of Advaita Vedanta and in the Śaiva traditions — and in Jain thought, with meanings shaped by each system's metaphysics. In East Asian Buddhism the concept merged with Daoist ideas of the void, informing Chan and Zen aesthetics, ink painting, and garden design.
