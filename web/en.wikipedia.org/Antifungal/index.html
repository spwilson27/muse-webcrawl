Antifungal

An antifungal medication, also known as an antimycotic medication, is a pharmaceutical fungicide or fungistatic used to treat and prevent mycosis such as athlete's foot, ringworm, candidiasis (thrush), serious systemic infections such as cryptococcal meningitis, and others. Such drugs are usually obtained by a doctor's prescription, but a few are available over the counter.

Classes

The major classes of antifungals are: azoles (such as fluconazole, itraconazole and clotrimazole), which inhibit synthesis of ergosterol, the main sterol of fungal cell membranes; polyenes (such as amphotericin B and nystatin), which bind ergosterol and create pores in the membrane; echinocandins (such as caspofungin), which inhibit cell-wall glucan synthesis; and allylamines (such as terbinafine), used especially against dermatophytes.

Resistance and challenges

Antifungal development is difficult because fungi, like humans, are eukaryotes, so drugs risk harming the host. Antifungal resistance is rising, notably in Candida auris and azole-resistant Aspergillus fumigatus, and invasive fungal infections carry high mortality in immunocompromised patients.
