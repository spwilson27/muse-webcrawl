Genitive case

Genitive case
AbbreviationGEN
TypeGrammatical case
Typical functionsPossession, origin, composition, partitivity, description

In grammar, the genitive case (abbreviated GEN) is the grammatical case that marks a word, usually a noun, as modifying another word, also usually a noun — thus indicating an attributive relationship of one noun to the other noun. A genitive can also serve purposes indicating other relationships. For example, some verbs may feature arguments in the genitive case; and the genitive case may also have adverbial uses (see adverbial genitive).

Genitive constructions typically mark possession and other noun-to-noun relations.

The genitive most typically marks a noun as being the possessor of another noun, as in "the student's book". However, across languages it covers a much wider family of relations: origin ("a man of Rome"), composition ("a cup of gold"), the object of an action ("the destruction of the city"), partitivity ("some of the water") and description ("a man of honour").

In particular languages

English

English marks the genitive with the clitic -'s (the "possessive" form, as in John's book) and with the preposition of (the roof of the house). Traditional grammars call the English -'s form the possessive case. Modern English also has a "double genitive" (a friend of mine).

Latin

Latin genitives end in endings such as -ae (first declension singular, puellae), -ī (second declension, dominī) and -is (third declension, rēgis). Latin uses the genitive for possession, partitive expressions, and with certain adjectives and verbs.

German

German has a full genitive case marked on articles and adjectives (e.g. des Mannes, "of the man"), used for possession and after certain prepositions, though in colloquial speech it is often replaced by von plus the dative.

Russian and other Slavic languages

Russian uses the genitive extensively: possession, negation of existence, quantity (after numerals and words like mnogo, "many"), and after many prepositions.

Formation and related constructions

Many languages that lack a morphological genitive case use other strategies: prepositional phrases (French de), possessive determiners, or construct-state forms as in Semitic languages (see iḍāfa in Arabic and smikhut in Hebrew).
