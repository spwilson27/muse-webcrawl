Comfort object

A comfort object, transitional object, or security blanket is an item used to provide psychological comfort, especially for children in unfamiliar or stressful situations, but also by adults. Common examples are a favourite stuffed animal, blanket or piece of cloth that a child carries and sleeps with.

Transitional object theory

The concept was introduced by paediatrician and psychoanalyst Donald Winnicott in 1951. In his theory of child development, the transitional object is the infant's first "not-me" possession, occupying an intermediate space between the child's inner world and external reality. It helps the child manage the anxiety of separating from the primary caregiver and is a normal step toward independence; attachment usually peaks in toddlerhood and fades as the child grows, though the object may be kept for years.

In adults

Surveys suggest a substantial minority of adults still own and use comfort objects, such as childhood soft toys or blankets, reporting that they aid sleep and reduce stress, with no association with psychopathology. Comfort objects and weighted blankets are also used therapeutically with people with anxiety, dementia and post-traumatic stress, and comfort items such as plush toys are provided by emergency services to children after traumatic incidents.

Related concepts

"Security blanket" entered popular culture through Linus van Pelt in Charles M. Schulz's comic strip Peanuts, who is rarely seen without his blanket. The idea is related to attachment theory, which studies the bonds between children and caregivers, and to the use of transitional phenomena such as thumb-sucking and favourite songs.
