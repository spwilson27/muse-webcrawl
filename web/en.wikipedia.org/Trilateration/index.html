Trilateration

Trilateration is the use of distances (or measurements of absolute distance) for determining the unknown position coordinates of a point of interest, often in geometry and navigation. Unlike triangulation, which uses angles, trilateration relies solely on measured distances from known reference points.

Principle

If the distance from a point to a known reference point is measured, the unknown point must lie on a circle (in two dimensions) or sphere (in three dimensions) centred on the reference point. A second distance narrows the location to the intersection of two circles — generally two points. A third measurement selects between them. In practice, measurement errors mean the circles rarely meet exactly, so least-squares methods are used to find the best-fit position.

Applications

Trilateration is the core principle behind satellite navigation systems such as the Global Positioning System (GPS). A GPS receiver measures signal travel times from several satellites, converts them to distances, and computes its position — a technique more precisely termed pseudo-range multilateration, since an additional unknown (receiver clock error) must be solved using a fourth satellite. Trilateration is also used in surveying, robotics, seismology for locating earthquake epicentres, and in mobile phone and Wi-Fi positioning using signal strengths or travel times from known transmitters.

History

Distance-based positioning was used in surveying networks long before electronics, and the method was adapted to radio navigation systems such as LORAN in the 20th century before becoming ubiquitous with satellite navigation.
