Electric car

An electric car or electric vehicle (EV) is an automobile propelled by one or more electric motors, using energy stored in rechargeable battery packs. Unlike a conventional internal combustion engine vehicle, or a hybrid that carries both systems, a battery electric vehicle (BEV) runs purely on electricity and produces no tailpipe emissions.

History

Electric cars were among the first automobiles: in the 1890s and 1900s they competed with steam and gasoline cars and held early speed records, but cheap mass-produced petrol cars such as the Ford Model T, together with the electric starter, drove them from the market. Interest revived with the oil crises of the 1970s and again in the 1990s (General Motors EV1). The modern era began with the Tesla Roadster (2008), the Nissan Leaf and Chevrolet Volt (2010), and was transformed by falling lithium-ion battery costs; by the early 2020s annual global EV sales exceeded ten million, with China the largest market.

Technology

Modern electric cars use lithium-ion traction batteries, power electronics (inverters) and AC induction or permanent-magnet motors. Energy can be recovered during braking by regeneration. Charging takes place at home via AC chargers or at public DC fast-charging stations that can add hundreds of kilometres of range in under an hour. Typical ranges of contemporary models are 300–600 km. Key issues include battery cost, lifetime and recycling, charging infrastructure, electricity generation mix, and the environmental footprint of mining battery materials.

Advantages and disadvantages

EVs offer high energy efficiency, instant torque, low operating and maintenance costs, and no local air pollution; total CO₂ emissions depend on grid carbon intensity but are generally well below those of comparable petrol cars over the vehicle's life. Drawbacks include higher purchase prices (though these have narrowed), charging time, range in cold weather, and battery weight.
