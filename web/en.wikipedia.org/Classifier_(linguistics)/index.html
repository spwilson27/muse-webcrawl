Classifier (linguistics)

A classifier (abbreviated clf or cl) is a word or affix that accompanies nouns and can be considered to "classify" a noun depending on its type, for example by shape, animacy, or function. Classifiers are typically required in numeral phrases — so-called numeral classifiers — where a number cannot directly modify a noun: Mandarin Chinese sān běn shū ("three [bound-volume] book") uses the classifier 本 běn for bound volumes.

Distribution

Numeral classifier systems are characteristic of East and Southeast Asian languages (Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Vietnamese, Thai, Malay, Burmese), many Austronesian and Australian Aboriginal languages, and Mesoamerican languages such as Yucatec Maya. Japanese counters (josūshi) include 本 hon for long cylindrical objects and 匹 hiki for small animals; Korean and Vietnamese have similar inventories numbering in the dozens to hundreds.

Types and semantics

Classifiers categorise nouns by salient properties: shape (long, flat, round), animacy (human, animal), function (vehicles, machines), arrangement, and social status (honorific classifiers for royalty or deities in Thai and Khmer). Some classifiers are "repeaters", echoing part of the noun. Classifiers differ from noun classes and grammatical gender in being chosen per act of counting or reference rather than fixed lexically, though the boundaries between the systems are debated.

Other uses

In sign languages, "classifier" handshapes represent categories of objects (vehicles, people, flat things) and their movement in signing space. In library and information science, and in grammar generally, the term also appears in other senses, but in linguistics the numeral-classifier meaning predominates.
