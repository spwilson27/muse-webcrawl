A truffle is the fruiting body of a subterranean ascomycete fungus, predominantly of the genus Tuber. Truffles are ectomycorrhizal fungi, growing in close association with the roots of certain trees such as oak, hazel, beech and poplar. Many species are highly prized as food for their powerful aroma and flavour, and the most celebrated—the white truffle of Alba (Tuber magnatum) and the black Périgord truffle (Tuber melanosporum)—command some of the highest prices of any foodstuff in the world.

Biology

Unlike mushrooms, truffles mature entirely below ground and do not forcibly discharge their spores. Instead they rely on animals to dig them up and disperse their spores. To attract these animals, ripe truffles emit strong volatile compounds, including androstenol, a pheromone also produced by boars, which is why pigs and trained dogs have traditionally been used to locate them.

Culinary use

Truffles are generally shaved or grated raw over dishes such as pasta, risotto and eggs, since cooking diminishes their aroma. They have been esteemed since antiquity, and were famously celebrated by the French gastronome Jean Anthelme Brillat-Savarin, who called them "the diamond of the kitchen".

Cultivation

Because wild harvests have declined, truffles are increasingly cultivated in orchards ("truffières") of trees inoculated with truffle spores, a practice developed in France in the nineteenth century and since spread to Spain, Italy, Australia, New Zealand and North America.
