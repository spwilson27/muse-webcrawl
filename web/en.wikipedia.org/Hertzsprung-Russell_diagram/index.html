Hertzsprung–Russell diagram

Createdc. 1911–1913
CreatorsEjnar Hertzsprung; Henry Norris Russell
AxesSpectral type / temperature vs. luminosity
RegionsMain sequence, giants, supergiants, white dwarfs

The Hertzsprung–Russell diagram (H–R diagram) is a scatter plot of stars showing the relationship between their luminosities (or absolute magnitudes) and their stellar classifications (spectral types, or equivalently surface temperatures and colors). It is one of the most important tools in astronomy for understanding stellar evolution.

Form of the diagram

Temperature increases to the left on the horizontal axis, so hot blue stars appear at the left and cool red stars at the right, while luminosity increases upward. Most stars fall along a diagonal band, the main sequence, where stars spend most of their lives fusing hydrogen. Above the main sequence lie the red giants and supergiants — luminous but cool — and below it the white dwarfs, hot but faint.

History

Danish astronomer Ejnar Hertzsprung first plotted the relation around 1911, and Henry Norris Russell published his version in 1913. The diagram's structure, initially puzzling, was explained by twentieth-century theories of stellar structure: a star's position on the diagram is determined mainly by its mass and age, and stars trace predictable evolutionary tracks across it.

Uses

By fitting the main sequence of a star cluster to that of known clusters, astronomers estimate cluster distances (spectroscopic and main-sequence fitting) and ages, since the point where stars leave the main sequence — the turn-off point — depends on the cluster's age.
