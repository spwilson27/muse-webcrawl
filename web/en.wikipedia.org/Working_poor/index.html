Working poor

The working poor are working people whose incomes fall below a given poverty line due to low-income jobs and low familial household income. These are people who spend at least 27 weeks in a year working or looking for employment, but remain under the poverty threshold.

Low-wage workers, such as food service and retail employees, make up a large share of the working poor.

Definition and measurement

In the United States, the Bureau of Labor Statistics defines the working poor as individuals who spent at least 27 weeks in the labor force (working or looking for work) but whose incomes still fell below the official poverty level. Similar concepts are used in Europe, where Eurostat measures "in-work poverty" using a threshold of 60 percent of median equivalised income.

Causes

Common causes include low minimum wages, part-time or irregular employment, single-earner households, large family size, and a lack of access to higher-paying occupations. The phenomenon is closely linked to the growth of low-wage service-sector jobs and to income inequality.

Policy responses

Proposed and implemented responses include raising the minimum wage, earned income tax credits such as the Earned Income Tax Credit, subsidized childcare, and expanded access to education and job training.
