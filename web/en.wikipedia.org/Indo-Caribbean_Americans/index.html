Indo-Caribbean Americans

Indo-Caribbean Americans are Americans of Indian ancestry who trace their roots to the Caribbean, principally Guyana, Trinidad and Tobago, and Suriname, as well as Jamaica, Grenada and other islands. Their ancestors were brought from India — mainly from the Bhojpuri-speaking regions of present-day Uttar Pradesh and Bihar, and from South India — as indentured labourers to British, Dutch and French colonies between 1838 and 1917 to work on sugar plantations after the abolition of slavery.

Immigration and communities

Large-scale immigration to the United States began after the Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965, accelerating in the 1970s and 1980s as Indo-Guyanese and Indo-Trinidadians left political instability and economic hardship. The largest community is in New York City, particularly Richmond Hill, Queens — nicknamed "Little Guyana" — along Liberty Avenue; other major concentrations are in the New York metropolitan area, Florida (Orlando and South Florida), and Schenectady, New York.

Culture

Indo-Caribbean Americans maintain a distinctive culture blending Indian and Caribbean elements: Hinduism, Islam and Christianity are all widely practised; Diwali and Phagwah (Holi) are publicly celebrated; chutney and chutney soca music, tassa drumming and foods such as roti, doubles and curry reflect the fusion. Many Indo-Caribbean Americans are sometimes described as "twice migrants" (having migrated from India to the Caribbean and then again to North America), and community identity often distinguishes them from direct Indian immigrants while sharing much common heritage.
