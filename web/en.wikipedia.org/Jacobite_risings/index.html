Jacobite risings

The Jacobite risings were a series of rebellions and wars in Britain and Ireland between 1688 and 1746, aimed at restoring the exiled Stuart king James II of England and VII of Scotland and his descendants to the thrones from which they had been deposed in the Glorious Revolution. The movement took its name from Jacobus, the Latin form of James.

Background

James II was deposed in 1688–89 and replaced by William III and Mary II. His supporters, called Jacobites, were concentrated in the Scottish Highlands, Ireland, and Tory and Catholic circles in England, and received intermittent support from France and Spain, Britain's rivals.

The risings

Major risings occurred in 1689 under Viscount Dundee, who won at Killiecrankie but was killed; in 1715, when James Edward Stuart ("the Old Pretender") landed in Scotland but the rising collapsed after the drawn Battle of Sheriffmuir and the surrender at Preston; in 1719, with a small Spanish-supported landing defeated at Glenshiel; and finally in 1745, when Charles Edward Stuart ("Bonnie Prince Charlie") raised his standard at Glenfinnan, took Edinburgh, won at Prestonpans, and marched into England as far as Derby before retreating. The campaign ended in the Jacobites' catastrophic defeat at the Battle of Culloden on 16 April 1746.

Aftermath

After 1746 the British government suppressed Highland clan society through measures including the Disarming Act and the banning of Highland dress, and built roads and forts such as Fort George to pacify the region. Jacobitism faded as a political force, though it survived in romantic literature and song, notably through the works of Sir Walter Scott.
