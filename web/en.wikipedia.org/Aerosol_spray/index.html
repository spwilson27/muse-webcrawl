Aerosol spray

Aerosol spray is a type of dispensing system which creates an aerosol mist of liquid particles. It comprises a can or bottle that contains a payload and a propellant under pressure. When the container's valve is opened, the payload is forced out of a small opening and emerges as an aerosol or mist. One of the most common types is spray paint, alongside products such as deodorants, insecticides, cooking sprays, and hair sprays.

History

The concept dates to the 18th century, when carbonated drinks were dispensed from pressurised containers. The modern aerosol can is credited to Norwegian engineer Erik Rotheim, who patented a design in 1927, and to American inventor Julian Kahn. During the Second World War, the United States government funded research into a portable insecticide spray for troops — the "bug bomb" — which led to mass production. After the war, aerosol products spread rapidly through consumer markets.

How it works

An aerosol can contains the product and a liquefied or compressed gas propellant. In liquefied-gas systems, the propellant exists partly as a liquid in equilibrium with its vapour, maintaining near-constant pressure as the product is used. Pressing the actuator opens a valve; propellant vapour pushes the product up a dip tube and out through a nozzle, where it atomises into fine droplets.

Propellants

Chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) were once the standard propellants, but after the discovery that they damage the ozone layer, they were phased out under the Montreal Protocol (1987). Modern propellants include hydrocarbons such as propane and butane, compressed gases such as nitrogen and carbon dioxide, and hydrofluoroalkanes in medical inhalers.

Environmental and safety concerns

Hydrocarbon propellants are volatile organic compounds that contribute to ground-level ozone, and many are flammable. Aerosol cans must not be punctured or exposed to heat, as residual pressure can make them explode. Most cans are made of steel or aluminium and are widely recyclable once empty.
