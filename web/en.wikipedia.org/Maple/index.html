Maple

Maple is a genus of trees and shrubs, Acer, in the soapberry family Sapindaceae. There are roughly 130 accepted species, most native to Asia, with others in Europe, northern Africa and North America. Maples are known for their distinctive palmately lobed leaves, their paired winged fruits called samaras, their brilliant autumn colour, and the sweet sap of the sugar maple from which maple syrup is made.

Characteristics

Most maples are deciduous trees growing 10–45 metres tall, though some are shrubs. Leaves are opposite and usually palmately veined and lobed, with 3 to 9 veins each leading to a lobe. The flowers are small and often inconspicuous. The fruit is a double samara — two winged seeds joined together, popularly called "helicopters" or "whirlybirds" — which spins as it falls, slowing descent and aiding wind dispersal.

Uses

The sugar maple (Acer saccharum) of eastern North America is tapped in late winter for sap, which is boiled down into maple syrup; Canada produces the great majority of the world supply, and the maple leaf is Canada's national symbol, appearing on its flag. Maple wood — especially hard ("rock") maple — is valued for flooring, furniture, bowling pins, baseball bats and musical instruments such as violins, guitars and drums. Many species and hundreds of cultivars, particularly of the Japanese maple (Acer palmatum), are planted as ornamentals for their form and foliage colour.

Ecology

Maples are important forest trees across the temperate Northern Hemisphere, providing food and habitat for wildlife; their seeds are eaten by birds and mammals, and their flowers are an early source of nectar for bees. Some species, notably Norway maple, have become invasive in North American forests, shading out native regeneration. Maple decline and diseases such as tar spot and verticillium wilt affect the genus, and the Asian longhorned beetle is a serious pest in some regions.
