De-extinction

De-extinction (also known as resurrection biology, or species revivalism) is the process of generating an organism that either resembles or is an extinct species. Proposed methods include cloning from preserved DNA, genome editing of a close living relative to reproduce the extinct species' traits, and selective back-breeding of descendants carrying ancestral characteristics.

Notable efforts

In 2003, scientists in Spain briefly cloned a Pyrenean ibex (bucardo) from frozen tissue; the newborn survived only minutes, making it the first — and so far only — extinct animal to be temporarily "brought back." Projects have targeted the passenger pigeon, the thylacine, the gastric-brooding frog, and the woolly mammoth, the latter pursued by geneticist George Church's laboratory and the company Colossal Biosciences, which aims to produce a cold-resistant elephant bearing mammoth traits. Back-breeding programs such as the Quagga Project and the Tauros Programme attempt to recover lost phenotypes without genetic engineering.

Debate

Supporters argue de-extinction could restore lost ecological functions, such as the grazing that maintained the mammoth steppe, and enrich conservation. Critics contend that revived organisms would be proxies rather than true members of the extinct species, that habitats have often changed beyond suitability, and that resources would be better spent protecting endangered species still living. Questions of welfare, disease risk, and legal status under endangered-species law remain unresolved.
