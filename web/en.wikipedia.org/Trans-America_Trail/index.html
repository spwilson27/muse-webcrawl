Trans-America Trail

The Trans-America Trail (TAT) is a transcontinental recreational route across the United States composed primarily of dirt roads, gravel roads, forest roads and farm roads, intended for dual-sport motorcycles, off-road motorcycles and four-wheel-drive vehicles. The route runs roughly from the east coast of the United States to the Pacific Northwest, crossing the Great Plains, the Rocky Mountains and the deserts of the Great Basin.

Route

The trail traditionally begins near the Atlantic coast in North Carolina (with a connector starting in West Virginia in some versions) and proceeds west through Tennessee, Mississippi, Arkansas, Oklahoma, New Mexico, Colorado, Utah, Nevada, Idaho and Oregon, ending at the Pacific coast near Port Orford, Oregon. The full route is approximately 4,200 miles (6,800 km) long, though exact mileage varies with annual revisions.

Notable sections include the high mountain passes of Colorado, the red-rock country of Utah around Moab, the open rangeland of Wyoming and Idaho (on some routings), and the sagebrush deserts of Nevada. Much of the route follows public land, including national forests and Bureau of Land Management roads.

History

The route was conceived and developed by Sam Correro, who scouted and mapped it over many years beginning in the 1980s and 1990s, selling roll charts and later GPS tracks to riders. The trail is not an officially designated government trail; it is a user-maintained assemblage of public roads and legally accessible tracks. Because it relies on public roads, sections can change as roads close, are paved, or become impassable, and riders are advised to carry current GPS tracks.

Use

Most riders complete the trail on lightweight or midweight dual-sport motorcycles over several weeks, though the full route has also been driven in modified 4x4 vehicles. The trail is generally ridden from east to west in late spring through early autumn, when mountain passes are free of snow.
