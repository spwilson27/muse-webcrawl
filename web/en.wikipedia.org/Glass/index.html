Glass is used for windows, containers, optics and fibre.

Glass is an amorphous (non-crystalline) solid material, most commonly a transparent, brittle substance made by melting silica (sand) with other ingredients and cooling it without crystallization. Glass has been made since antiquity and is used in an enormous range of applications — windows, bottles and tableware, lenses and optical instruments, insulation, electronic displays and the optical fibres that carry global communications.

Composition and structure

The most common type, soda–lime glass, consists chiefly of silicon dioxide (~75%) networked in a disordered, random arrangement, with soda (sodium carbonate) added to lower the melting temperature and lime (calcium oxide) to improve chemical durability. Because its atoms lack the long-range order of a crystal, glass is described as a supercooled liquid frozen into rigidity through the glass transition — although, contrary to popular myth, old windowpanes do not flow perceptibly at room temperature. Other families include borosilicate glass (e.g. Pyrex), prized for thermal-shock resistance; lead glass ("crystal") with high refractive index; fused quartz; and aluminosilicate glasses strengthened for phone screens.

History

Glass beads and vessels were first produced in Mesopotamia and Egypt around 1500 BC (with earlier glassy faience), and core-forming allowed hollow vessels. The invention of glassblowing in the Syro-Palestinian region in the 1st century BC made glass cheap and ubiquitous in the Roman world. Medieval craftsmen produced stained glass for cathedrals and, on the Venetian island of Murano, clear cristallo. Industrial revolutions followed: cast plate glass, the Siemens regenerative furnace, the Owens automatic bottle machine (1903), and Pilkington's float-glass process (1959), in which molten glass is floated on molten tin to make flawless flat sheets — the basis of nearly all modern window and mirror glass.

Manufacture and working

Modern container and flat glass is made in continuous furnaces melting thousands of tonnes of batch with recycled cullet. Glass can be blown, pressed, drawn, cast, rolled or floated; it can be tempered (toughened) by controlled cooling or chemical ion exchange, laminated with plastic interlayers for safety, coated for low-emissivity insulation, and melted and re-formed repeatedly. Glass is in principle infinitely recyclable without loss of quality.

Uses

Beyond architecture and containers, specialized glasses serve as optical lenses and telescope mirrors, laboratory ware, glass-reinforced plastic (fibreglass), fibre-optic cables, microscope slides and smartphone cover glass; vitrification is also used to immobilize high-level nuclear waste. Natural glasses include obsidian, fulgurites formed by lightning, and impact glasses such as tektites.
