Vicarious liability

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Vicarious liability is a form of strict, secondary liability that arises under the common law doctrine of agency, respondeat superior ("let the superior answer"), under which a superior is responsible for the acts of their subordinate, or in a broader sense, any third party that had the ability, duty and authority to control the actions of a violator and failed to do so.

The tort is often applied to employers, who may be held liable for the negligent or otherwise tortious acts of their employees committed within the course and scope of employment, even though the employer itself committed no wrongdoing.

Employment law

Employers are vicariously liable for the torts committed by their employees in the course of their employment. The classic test asks whether the employee's act was so closely connected with the employment that it would be fair and just to hold the employer liable. Courts commonly consider factors such as the time, place and purpose of the act, the extent to which the act furthered the employer's interests, and whether the employee was "on a frolic of his own" — a substantial deviation from assigned duties that takes the act outside the scope of employment.

Liability may extend to intentional wrongdoing, including assaults by employees such as security staff, where the employment materially enhanced the risk of the harm occurring. By contrast, employers are generally not vicariously liable for the torts of independent contractors, though exceptions exist where the duty owed is non-delegable.

Rationale

The doctrine is usually justified on grounds of loss distribution and deterrence: the employer, who profits from the enterprise and is better placed to absorb or insure against losses, is made to bear the cost of risks created by that enterprise. It also gives employers an incentive to select, train and supervise employees carefully.

Other contexts

Vicarious liability also appears in other areas of law. In criminal law, statutes sometimes impose liability on corporations or employers for offences committed by employees. In copyright and patent law, a party may be liable for infringement by another where it had the right and ability to supervise the infringing activity and a direct financial interest in it. Vehicle owners may also be made statutorily liable for the negligence of persons driving with their permission.
