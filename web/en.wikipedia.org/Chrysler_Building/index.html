The Chrysler Building is an Art Deco skyscraper on the East Side of Manhattan in New York City, at 405 Lexington Avenue. Standing 1,046 feet (319 m) tall, it was the world's tallest building for eleven months after its completion in 1930, until it was surpassed by the Empire State Building. It remains the tallest brick building in the world with a steel framework, and is regarded as a masterpiece of Art Deco architecture.

The building was commissioned by automobile magnate Walter Chrysler, who paid for it personally so that his children would inherit it; the Chrysler Corporation itself was never headquartered there. Architect William Van Alen designed its distinctive crown of seven radiating terraced arches clad in stainless steel, decorated with sunburst patterns, eagles modelled on Chrysler hood ornaments, and stylised automobiles.

The race for the sky

Van Alen was competing with his former partner H. Craig Severance, who was building 40 Wall Street. To secure the title of world's tallest, Van Alen secretly assembled a 125-foot (38 m) spire, or "vertex," inside the building's crown and hoisted it into place in a single operation on 23 October 1929, surprising the public and surpassing Severance's tower. The completed building was dedicated on 27 May 1930.

Later history

The Chrysler family sold the building in 1953. It was designated a New York City landmark in 1978 and added to the National Register of Historic Places in 1976. The ground beneath it is owned by Cooper Union, which leases the land to the building's owners.
