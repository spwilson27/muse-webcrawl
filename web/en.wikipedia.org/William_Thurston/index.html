William Thurston

William Paul Thurston (October 30, 1946 – August 21, 2012) was an American mathematician. A pioneer in the field of low-dimensional topology, he was awarded the Fields Medal in 1982 and the first Leroy P. Steele Prize for Seminal Contribution to Research in 2009.

Contributions

Thurston revolutionized the study of 3-manifolds by showing that hyperbolic geometry — using his "geometrization conjecture" — could unify their classification. He proposed that every closed 3-manifold decomposes into pieces each carrying one of eight model geometries; this framework inspired Richard Hamilton's Ricci flow and Grigori Perelman's eventual proof of both Thurston's conjecture and the Poincaré conjecture in 2003. His earlier work on foliations, the Nielsen–Thurston classification of surface automorphisms, and orbifolds was equally influential, and his vivid geometric intuition reshaped how mathematicians visualize topology.

Career

Born in Washington, D.C., Thurston earned his doctorate from UC Berkeley in 1972 and taught at Princeton, Berkeley, UC Davis, and Cornell, where he directed the Mathematical Sciences Research Institute (MSRI) from 1992 to 1997. His essay "On Proof and Progress in Mathematics" (1994) is widely read. He also designed the origami paper for the "Mathematical Fashion" projects and died of melanoma in Rochester, New York, in 2012.
