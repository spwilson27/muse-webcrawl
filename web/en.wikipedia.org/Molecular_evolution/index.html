Molecular evolution

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Comparing molecular sequences reveals evolutionary relationships and timescales.

Molecular evolution is the study of how biological molecules — DNA, RNA, and proteins — change over evolutionary time, and of the forces that drive those changes: mutation, genetic drift, recombination, gene flow, and natural selection. The field emerged in the 1960s when protein and nucleic-acid sequences first became available, notably through the work of Emile Zuckerkandl and Linus Pauling, who proposed the molecular clock in 1962.

Molecular clock

Zuckerkandl and Pauling observed that differences in amino-acid sequences of haemoglobin and cytochrome c between species accumulate roughly in proportion to the time since their divergence. This "molecular clock" allows researchers to estimate divergence times, though rates vary between genes, lineages, and sites, and modern methods use relaxed-clock models calibrated with fossils.

Neutral theory

In 1968 Motoo Kimura proposed the neutral theory of molecular evolution: most evolutionary changes at the molecular level are caused by random drift of selectively neutral mutations, not by positive selection. It predicts that the rate of molecular evolution equals the neutral mutation rate, that functionally constrained regions evolve slowly, and that pseudogenes evolve fastest. The nearly neutral theory (Tomoko Ohta) extended it to slightly deleterious mutations whose fate depends on population size.

Patterns and mechanisms

Key measures include the ratio of nonsynonymous to synonymous substitutions (dN/dS) for detecting selection on proteins, codon usage bias, gene duplication followed by divergence (Ohno's model), exon shuffling, horizontal gene transfer (common in bacteria), and concerted evolution of gene families. Genomic data reveal that most of the genome in many eukaryotes is non-coding and evolves nearly neutrally.

Applications

Molecular evolution underlies phylogenetics and the tree-of-life reconstruction (e.g., Woese's three-domain system based on rRNA), forensic DNA analysis, epidemiology and virus tracking, conservation genetics, and the detection of genes under selection in adaptation and disease.
