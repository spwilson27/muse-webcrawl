The Amarna letters (sometimes referred to as the Amarna correspondence or Amarna tablets) are an archive, written on clay tablets, primarily consisting of diplomatic correspondence between the Egyptian administration and its representatives in Canaan and Amurru, or neighbouring kingdom leaders, during the New Kingdom of Egypt. The letters were found in Upper Egypt at el-Amarna, the modern name for the ancient Egyptian capital of Akhetaten, founded by pharaoh Akhenaten (1350s–1330s BC) during the Eighteenth Dynasty of Egypt.

Contents of the letters

The letters are written in Akkadian cuneiform, the diplomatic language of the Late Bronze Age Near East, although the scribes were mostly Canaanites and their writing shows heavy influence from their local Canaanite languages — a mixed dialect sometimes called Canaano-Akkadian. The corpus consists of over 300 tablets, a precious few of which are literary or lexical texts; the great majority are letters.

The correspondence spans the reigns of Amenhotep III, Akhenaten, and possibly the first years of Tutankhamun. Roughly three dozen letters come from great kings — the rulers of Babylon, Assyria, Mitanni, Hatti, Alashiya (Cyprus) and Arzawa — who address the pharaoh as a peer, "my brother." The rest come from Egyptian vassal rulers in Syria and Canaan, who prostrate themselves rhetorically and report on local intrigues, requests for troops, and complaints against rivals.

Discovery

The tablets were first found in 1887 by a local woman digging for sebakh (fertiliser) among the ruins of Amarna. After an initial period in which many tablets were damaged or dispersed through the antiquities market, excavations by William Matthew Flinders Petrie in 1891–92 located the remains of the records office — the "Place of the Letters of Pharaoh" — in the central city.

Significance

The Amarna letters are of central importance for the study of the Late Bronze Age international system, the history of the Levant, and the background of the Hebrew Bible. They preserve some of the earliest references to groups and places such as the Habiru, and they provide the main primary source for Egyptian imperial administration in Asia. Famous individual letters include the complaints of Rib-Hadda of Byblos, the scheming of Aziru of Amurru, and the Babylonian king Burna-Buriash II's protests about the treatment of his messengers and merchants.
