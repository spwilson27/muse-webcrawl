The First Jewish–Roman War (66–73/74 CE), sometimes called the Great Jewish Revolt or the Great Revolt, was the first of three major rebellions by the Jews of Judaea against the Roman Empire. It began amid escalating tensions between Jewish and Greek populations, resentment of oppressive Roman procurators, and messianic ferment, and it ended with the destruction of Jerusalem and the Second Temple — a catastrophe whose commemoration persists in Judaism to this day.

Course of the war

The revolt began in 66 CE; the rebels first defeated the forces of the Syrian legate Cestius Gallus at Beth Horon. Emperor Nero dispatched Vespasian, who methodically reconquered Galilee in 67 — where the rebel commander Josephus was captured and defected — and by 69 Rome's civil war (the Year of the Four Emperors) had made Vespasian emperor. His son Titus besieged Jerusalem in 70 CE, breaching its walls after a brutal siege; the Temple was burned, the upper city taken, and much of the population killed or enslaved. The Arch of Titus in Rome depicts the procession of the Temple's menorah and treasures.

Masada

The last stronghold, the mountaintop fortress of Masada held by Sicarii under Eleazar ben Ya'ir, fell in 73 or 74 CE to Flavius Silva's Tenth Legion after the construction of a great assault ramp. Josephus reports that the defenders chose mass suicide over capture — a story that made Masada an enduring symbol.

Consequences

The war devastated Judaea, ended the Temple cult, and redirected Judaism toward rabbinic forms centred on synagogue and study. The fiscus Judaicus tax was imposed on Jews empire-wide. Further revolts — the Kitos War (115–117) and the Bar Kokhba revolt (132–136) — completed the Roman suppression of Jewish political independence in the province.
