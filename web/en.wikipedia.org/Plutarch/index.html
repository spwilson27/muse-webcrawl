Plutarch

Plutarch (c. AD 46 – after AD 119) was a Greek Middle Platonist philosopher, historian, biographer, essayist, and priest at the Temple of Apollo in Delphi. He is known primarily for his Parallel Lives, a series of biographies of famous Greeks and Romans designed to illuminate their common moral virtues and failings, and for the Moralia, a collection of essays and speeches on ethics, religion, and philosophy.

Life

Plutarch was born to a prominent family in Chaeronea, in Boeotia, and studied mathematics and philosophy in Athens. He travelled widely in the Roman world, served as a magistrate in his home town, and for the last thirty years of his life was a priest at Delphi. Under the name Lucius Mestrius Plutarchus he became a Roman citizen. He wrote in Greek, and his works were immensely influential on European literature — Shakespeare drew the plots of Julius Caesar, Coriolanus, and Antony and Cleopatra from Thomas North's English translation of the Lives.

Parallel Lives

The Parallel Lives pair a Greek with a Roman of similar career — Alexander with Caesar, Demosthenes with Cicero — followed by a formal comparison. Twenty-three pairs survive, plus four single lives. Plutarch famously disclaimed writing history: "I am writing lives, not histories; and noble deeds do not always reveal virtue or vice."
