The three-field system is a regime of crop rotation that was used in medieval and early-modern Europe. Under this system, the arable land of a community was divided into three large fields: one sown in autumn with wheat or rye, one sown in spring with peas, beans, barley or oats, and one left fallow each year. The fields rotated so that each rested one year in three.

Advantages

Compared with the older two-field system, in which half the land lay fallow each year, the three-field system put two-thirds of the land under cultivation annually. Planting legumes in the spring field helped restore nitrogen to the soil, while the spring sowing reduced the risk that a single bad season would destroy the whole harvest. The system spread across much of Europe from about the 8th to the 11th centuries, contributing to population growth in the High Middle Ages.

Organisation

The system was closely tied to the open-field system and the manorial economy: peasant families held scattered strips in each of the three fields and were obliged to follow the common rotation, with the fallow field serving as grazing for communal livestock, whose manure fertilised the soil.

Decline

From the 16th century onward, convertible husbandry and, in the 18th century, the Norfolk four-course rotation using turnips and clover eliminated the need for fallow, while enclosure dissolved the communal fields. The three-field system nonetheless persisted in parts of France, Germany and Russia (as the trekhpolie) into the 19th century.
