Raskol

Raskol (Russian: Раскол, "schism") was the great schism in the Russian Orthodox Church that began in the 17th century, when Patriarch Nikon introduced reforms to Russian liturgical books and practices intended to bring them into line with contemporary Greek usage. Those who rejected the reforms and clung to the old rites became known as Old Believers or raskolniki (schismatics).

Background

In 1652 Nikon became Patriarch of Moscow and, with the support of Tsar Alexis, began correcting Russian service books against Greek originals. The changes were outwardly small — the spelling of the name of Jesus, the number of alleluias sung, the direction of processions, and making the sign of the cross with three fingers instead of two — but many Russians regarded ritual exactness as essential to salvation and saw the corrections as heresy and a sign of the approaching Antichrist.

The schism

The archpriest Avvakum led the opposition, arguing that the Russian Church, not the Greeks, had preserved the true faith. Church councils of 1666–1667 confirmed the reforms and anathematised the old rite; Avvakum and other leaders were exiled, and Avvakum was burned at the stake in 1682. Resistance flared into revolts, notably the Solovetsky Monastery uprising (1668–1676), and some Old Believers resorted to mass self-immolation rather than submit.

Consequences

The schism permanently divided Russian Orthodoxy. Old Believers split into priestly and priestless branches and developed distinctive communities, often in remote regions or abroad, noted for conservatism, distinctive chant, icons and dress, and later for commercial success as merchants and industrialists. Persecution continued for centuries, though toleration gradually increased; in 1971 the Moscow Patriarchate lifted the anathemas on the old rites, recognising them as equally salvific.
