Constitutio Antoniniana

The Constitutio Antoniniana (Latin for "Constitution [or Edict] of Antoninus"), also known as the Edict of Caracalla, was an edict issued in AD 212 by the Roman emperor Caracalla (Marcus Aurelius Antoninus) which declared that all free men in the Roman Empire were to be given full Roman citizenship and all free women the same rights as Roman women.

Background and text

Before the edict, citizenship was held by all inhabitants of Italy and by varying proportions of provincials — Roman colonies, retired legionaries, and individuals or communities granted citizenship for service or favour. The text of the edict survives in damaged form on a papyrus (P. Giess. 40) held at Giessen, Germany. It appears to have excluded at least one category, the dediticii (surrendered enemies), though the reading is debated.

Motives

The historian Cassius Dio, a hostile contemporary, attributed the grant to fiscal motives: extending citizenship made more people liable to inheritance and manumission taxes, which Caracalla simultaneously doubled. Other explanations include religious gratitude (Caracalla claimed to have been saved from a plot), administrative simplification, and the culmination of a centuries-long process by which citizenship had already spread widely, making the distinction increasingly formal.

Consequences

The edict effectively universalised Roman citizenship for the free population of the empire, one of the most sweeping grants of citizenship in history. In practice, local laws and customs continued to operate, and legal status distinctions shifted toward the divide between honestiores (the more honourable) and humiliores (the humbler), which carried different legal penalties. The edict also made the emperor's nomen, Aurelius, ubiquitous in inscriptions from new citizens.
