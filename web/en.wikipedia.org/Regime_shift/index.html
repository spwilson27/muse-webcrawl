Regime shift

In ecology, a regime shift is a large, abrupt, persistent change in the structure and function of an ecosystem. Regime shifts occur when an ecosystem crosses a threshold, or tipping point, moving from one relatively stable state to another that is maintained by different internal feedbacks. Because the new state is self-reinforcing, returning to the original condition is often difficult — a property known as hysteresis.

Examples

Well-documented regime shifts include the collapse of the Newfoundland cod fishery, the shift of shallow lakes from clear, plant-dominated water to turbid, algae-dominated water under nutrient loading (eutrophication), coral reefs flipping to algal-dominated states after overfishing of herbivores and coral bleaching, and the conversion of savannas to shrublands through overgrazing and fire suppression.

Causes and detection

Regime shifts are typically triggered by a combination of slow changes in underlying conditions (such as nutrient accumulation or climate warming) and sudden disturbances (storms, disease outbreaks, harvesting). Researchers look for early-warning signals of approaching thresholds, including rising variance and autocorrelation in time-series data, under the theory of critical slowing down.

The concept overlaps with research on tipping points in the climate system and ecological resilience, and it informs ecosystem-based management of fisheries, lakes and rangelands.
