Hershey–Chase experiment

The Hershey–Chase experiment, conducted in 1952 by Alfred Hershey and Martha Chase at the Cold Spring Harbor Laboratory, helped confirm that DNA is genetic material. While DNA had been known to biologists since 1869, many scientists still assumed at the time that proteins carried the information for inheritance, because DNA appeared to be an inert molecule with a monotonous structure, whereas proteins were known to be highly diverse.

Conducted1952
ResearchersAlfred Hershey, Martha Chase
InstitutionCold Spring Harbor Laboratory
SystemT2 bacteriophage and E. coli
ConclusionDNA, not protein, is the genetic material (in phage T2)

Method

Hershey and Chase used the T2 bacteriophage, a virus that infects the bacterium Escherichia coli and is composed solely of a protein shell (capsid) containing DNA. They grew two batches of phage in media containing different radioactive isotopes so as to label one component selectively:

  • Phosphorus-32 (32P) labels DNA, because DNA contains phosphorus but phage protein essentially does not.
  • Sulfur-35 (35S) labels protein, because the amino acids cysteine and methionine contain sulfur, while DNA contains none.

Each batch of labelled phage was allowed to infect bacteria. The cultures were then agitated in a Waring blender to shear the empty phage coats off the bacterial surface, and centrifuged so that the bacteria formed a pellet while the lighter phage coats remained in the supernatant.

Results

In the 32P (DNA-labelled) experiment, most of the radioactivity was found in the bacterial pellet, and radioactive DNA was also detected in the progeny phages produced after infection. In the 35S (protein-labelled) experiment, nearly all the radioactivity remained in the supernatant with the detached protein coats, and the progeny phage contained no significant 35S. Hershey and Chase concluded that DNA, not protein, entered the bacterial cell and directed the production of new viruses — strong evidence that DNA is the hereditary material.

Significance

Together with the earlier Avery–MacLeod–McCarty experiment (1944), which had been met with scepticism, the Hershey–Chase experiment convinced most biologists that DNA was the genetic material, paving the way for the determination of the double-helix structure by Watson and Crick the following year. Hershey shared the 1969 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine (with Max Delbrück and Salvador Luria) for work on the genetic structure of viruses. The experiment is popularly known as the "blender experiment" after the kitchen appliance used to separate phage coats from bacteria.
