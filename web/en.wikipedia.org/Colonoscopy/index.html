Colonoscopy

Colonoscopy is the endoscopic examination of the large bowel and the distal part of the small bowel with a camera on a flexible tube passed through the anus. It can provide a visual diagnosis, for example of ulceration or precancerous polyps, and grants the opportunity for biopsy or removal of suspected colorectal cancer lesions.

Indications

Colonoscopy is a mainstay of colorectal cancer screening, recommended periodically for adults beginning around age 45–50 in many countries, and is used to investigate symptoms such as rectal bleeding, chronic diarrhea, iron-deficiency anemia and unexplained abdominal pain. It is also used in the diagnosis and monitoring of inflammatory bowel diseases such as Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis.

Preparation and procedure

The colon must be emptied of solid matter, so patients follow a low-fiber diet and fast, drinking a strong laxative bowel preparation the day before. The procedure is usually performed with sedation and takes about 20–60 minutes. The colonoscope — a flexible tube about the thickness of a finger, with a light, camera, and channels for air, water and instruments — is advanced to the cecum. Polyps found during the examination are commonly removed (polypectomy) with a wire snare.

Risks

Colonoscopy is generally safe. Complications include perforation of the colon (roughly 1 in 1,000–2,000 procedures, more likely when polyps are removed), bleeding after polypectomy, and adverse reactions to sedation. Virtual colonoscopy by CT scan is a less invasive alternative but cannot remove polyps and still requires bowel preparation.

Effectiveness

By detecting and removing adenomatous polyps before they turn malignant, colonoscopy reduces both the incidence of and mortality from colorectal cancer; large cohort studies associate screening colonoscopy with substantial long-term reductions in cancer deaths.
