Weather front

A weather front is a boundary separating air masses of different densities, usually differing in temperature and humidity. Fronts are the principal cause of significant weather in the middle latitudes and are drawn on surface weather maps with standardized symbols: a line with triangles for a cold front, semicircles for a warm front, alternating symbols for a stationary front, and combined symbols for an occluded front.

Types

Cold front

A cold front is the leading edge of a cooler, drier air mass replacing warmer air. Cold fronts move faster than warm fronts and lift warm air steeply, often producing a narrow band of showers and thunderstorms, a sharp temperature drop, and a shift in wind direction. On maps they are drawn in blue with triangles pointing in the direction of movement.

Warm front

A warm front occurs where a warm air mass advances over retreating cold air. The warm air slides gently upward over a broad region, typically producing extensive cloudiness and steady, light precipitation well ahead of the surface front, drawn in red with semicircles.

Stationary and occluded fronts

A stationary front stalls between two air masses and can bring days of cloudy, wet weather. An occluded front forms when a fast-moving cold front overtakes a warm front, lifting the warm sector entirely off the ground—a stage in the life cycle of a mid-latitude cyclone described by the Norwegian cyclone model.

Frontogenesis

The formation and strengthening of fronts, called frontogenesis, occurs where deformation and convergence in the wind field concentrate temperature gradients, often near the jet stream. Fronts weaken by frontolysis when the temperature contrast relaxes.
