Crossing number (graph theory)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In graph theory, the crossing number cr(G) of a graph G is the lowest number of edge crossings in a plane drawing of the graph. For instance, a graph is planar if and only if its crossing number is zero. Determining the crossing number is a classical problem that originated with Pál Turán's "brick factory problem" during the Second World War, in which he sought to minimize the crossings of tracks connecting kilns and storage yards.

Results and complexity

Exact crossing numbers are known only for particular families of graphs, such as complete graphs and complete bipartite graphs; Guy's conjecture gives a formula for complete graphs that has been verified for small cases but remains unproven in general. Computing the crossing number is NP-hard, which has motivated heuristic and approximation algorithms used in graph drawing and VLSI layout. The crossing lemma, which relates the number of crossings to the numbers of vertices and edges, is a fundamental tool in combinatorial geometry and incidence theory.

Variants

Related concepts include the rectilinear crossing number, in which edges must be drawn as straight line segments, and the pairwise or odd crossing numbers, which count crossings under different conventions and were brought together by the Hanani–Tutte theorem.
