Symmetry breaking

In physics, symmetry breaking is a phenomenon where a disordered but symmetric state collapses into an ordered, but less symmetric state. This collapse is understood as symmetry breaking when the state, governed by the laws of physics and lying somewhere within the symmetric space of the system, actually perceives less symmetry than the system itself and the governing laws.

Commonly, symmetry breaking refers to spontaneous symmetry breaking, in which the underlying laws are invariant under a symmetry transformation, but the chosen ground state (vacuum) is not. In explicit symmetry breaking, by contrast, the defining equations of the system themselves fail to exhibit the symmetry.

Examples

A classic mechanical example is a pencil balanced on its tip: the setup is rotationally symmetric, but the pencil must fall in one particular direction, breaking the symmetry. In condensed matter physics, a ferromagnet above its Curie temperature has magnetisation pointing in no preferred direction; cooled below the transition, it picks a direction, spontaneously breaking rotational symmetry. Water freezing into ice breaks continuous translational and rotational symmetry into the discrete symmetry of a crystal lattice.

In particle physics

Spontaneous symmetry breaking plays a central role in the Standard Model: through the Higgs mechanism, the electroweak gauge symmetry SU(2)×U(1) is broken to the electromagnetic U(1), giving mass to the W and Z bosons while leaving the photon massless. When a continuous symmetry is spontaneously broken, Goldstone's theorem predicts the appearance of massless scalar excitations (Goldstone bosons), which in the gauge case are "eaten" to become the longitudinal components of the massive vector bosons.

Phase transitions

Symmetry breaking is the organising concept for the Landau theory of phase transitions: the order parameter is zero in the symmetric phase and nonzero in the broken phase, and transitions are classified by which symmetries are broken. Explicit breaking, such as an external magnetic field applied to a ferromagnet, tilts the energy landscape so that one state is preferred, rounding the sharp transition.
