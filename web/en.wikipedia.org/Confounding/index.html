Confounding

In causal inference, a confounder is a variable that influences both the dependent variable and the independent variable, causing a spurious association. Confounding is a causal concept, and as such, cannot be described in terms of correlations or associations. The existence of confounders is an important quantitative explanation why correlation does not imply causation.

A classic example is the observed association between ice-cream sales and drowning deaths: both increase in summer, because hot weather (the confounder) causes more ice-cream consumption and more swimming.

Definition

Traditionally, a confounder was required to be associated with both the exposure and the outcome, and not to lie on the causal pathway between them. Modern causal frameworks, using directed acyclic graphs (DAGs), define confounding in terms of open "back-door" paths between treatment and outcome that must be blocked for causal identification.

Control of confounding

Confounding can be addressed at the design stage by randomization, restriction, or matching, and at the analysis stage by stratification, regression adjustment, propensity score methods, or instrumental variables. Random assignment in experiments balances both measured and unmeasured confounders in expectation, which is why randomized trials are considered a gold standard.

Related concepts

Confounding must be distinguished from effect modification (interaction), mediation, and collider bias. Simpson's paradox, in which an association reverses when data are pooled, can arise when a confounder is ignored.
