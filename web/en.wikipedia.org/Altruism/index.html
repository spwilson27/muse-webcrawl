Altruism

Altruism is the principle and practice of concern for the well-being of others, in which a person acts to promote someone else's welfare even at a risk or cost to themselves. The term was coined by the French philosopher Auguste Comte, from the Italian altrui ("of others"), as an antonym of egoism. Altruistic behaviour is found in humans and many other animals, and is studied in psychology, evolutionary biology, philosophy and economics.

Evolutionary explanations

In biology, altruism refers to behaviour that increases the fitness of another organism while decreasing the actor's own fitness. Explanations include kin selection (helping genetic relatives), reciprocal altruism (helping those likely to reciprocate), and group selection. W. D. Hamilton's rule states that a costly helping behaviour can evolve when the benefit to the recipient, weighted by relatedness, exceeds the cost to the actor.

Psychology

Psychologists distinguish altruistic motivation — helping with the ultimate goal of benefiting another — from helping done for reward or to avoid punishment. The empathy-altruism hypothesis, associated with C. Daniel Batson, holds that empathic concern produces genuinely altruistic motivation. Social norms such as the norm of reciprocity and social responsibility also encourage helping.

Philosophy and religion

Many ethical traditions praise selfless concern for others; the Golden Rule appears in numerous religions. Philosophers debate whether pure altruism is possible and whether morality requires it. Effective altruism applies evidence and reason to maximise the good one's resources can do.
