Earthwork (archaeology)

Defensive earthworks of an Iron Age hillfort

In archaeology, an earthwork is any artificial change in land level, typically made from piles of artificially placed or sculpted rocks and soil. Earthworks can be raised banks (tumuli, ramparts, mottes), sunken features (ditches, moats, hollow ways), or flattened terraces and platforms. Because they survive where masonry does not, earthworks are among the most widespread traces of past societies, from Neolithic causewayed enclosures to medieval castles and Civil War fortifications.

Types

Common classes include burial mounds and barrows; henges and stone-circle banks; hillforts with their ramparts and ditches; linear boundaries such as Offa's Dyke; Roman marching camps; motte-and-bailey castles; ridge-and-furrow cultivation; lynchets; moated sites; and the monumental platform mounds of the Mississippian culture, such as Cahokia's Monks Mound, the largest earthen structure in the Americas. Geoglyphs such as the Nazca Lines are negative earthworks cut into desert surfaces.

Investigation

Earthworks are studied by topographic survey, aerial photography — especially in raking light — and lidar, which can reveal relief beneath forest canopy. Excavation is often unnecessary for interpretation, and earthworks are valued as protected monuments: in England many are scheduled monuments recorded in county historic environment records. Related military usage gives the term to field fortifications, from Roman camps to the trench systems of the First World War.
