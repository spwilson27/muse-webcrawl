Al-Azhar Mosque (Arabic: الجامع الأزهر, "the resplendent congregational mosque") is an Egyptian mosque in Islamic Cairo, founded by the Fatimid general Jawhar al-Siqilli shortly after the Fatimid conquest of Egypt. Its name is commonly thought to derive from Fatimah al-Zahra, daughter of the Prophet Muhammad. It is the mosque attached to Al-Azhar University, one of the oldest continuously operating universities in the world and a leading centre of Sunni Islamic scholarship.

History

Construction began in 970 and the mosque was completed in 972; the first prayers were held in Ramadan of that year. Originally built as the congregational mosque of the new Fatimid capital, al-Qahira (Cairo), it served an Ismaili Shi'a state. After Saladin's abolition of the Fatimid Caliphate in 1171, the mosque's fortunes declined under the Sunni Ayyubids, but it was restored and expanded under the Mamluks, who made it a major centre of learning.

Architecture

The mosque shows a layered architectural history spanning more than a millennium. The original Fatimid prayer hall, with its keel arches and carved stucco, is surrounded by later Mamluk additions — including madrasas, the minaret of Qaitbey (1483) and the double-finialled minaret of al-Ghuri (1501) — and Ottoman-era expansions such as the façade and prayer hall added by Abd al-Rahman Katkhuda in the 18th century. The complex today has five minarets, several domes and a vast marble-paved sahn (courtyard).

Al-Azhar University

Teaching at the mosque began in the 10th century, and al-Azhar developed into a university teaching Islamic law, theology, Arabic language and later secular subjects. In 1961 it was reorganised by law as a modern university, though its scholars (ulama) continue to issue influential fatwas and to represent a mainstream, centrist tradition in Sunni Islam. The Grand Imam of al-Azhar is among the most respected religious authorities in the Muslim world.
