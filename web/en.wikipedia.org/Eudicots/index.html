Eudicots

The eudicots are a large clade of flowering plants (angiosperms) containing about three-quarters of all angiosperm species, including most familiar trees, shrubs, herbs and crops such as roses, beans, sunflowers, oaks and apples. The name refers to their typical possession of two seed leaves (cotyledons), though the group is defined phylogenetically rather than by that trait alone.

Defining features

Eudicots were first recognised from their distinctive tricolpate pollen — pollen grains with three furrows — which appears in the fossil record about 125 million years ago in the Early Cretaceous. Molecular phylogenetics confirmed the clade, adopted in the APG classification systems. Most also have net-veined leaves and floral parts in fours or fives.

Major groups

Early-diverging eudicots include Ranunculales (buttercups, poppies) and Proteales. The core eudicots split into the superrosids (rosids plus Saxifragales) and superasterids (asterids and relatives), which together contain the great majority of species, including the fabids, malvids, lamiids and campanulids.

Evolution and importance

Eudicots radiated rapidly during the Cretaceous and came to dominate most terrestrial ecosystems. Economically they provide most human food crops other than cereals. See also Flowering plant, Rosids and Asterids.
