Sicilian Vespers

The outbreak of the revolt outside the Church of Santo Spirito, Palermo, at vespers on Easter Monday 1282.

The Sicilian Vespers (Italian: Vespri siciliani) was a successful rebellion on the island of Sicily that broke out at Easter 1282 against the rule of the French-born king Charles I of Anjou. Beginning in Palermo at the hour of vespers (evening prayer) on 30 March 1282, the revolt spread across the island; within six weeks most of Sicily's French residents — officials, soldiers and their families, perhaps several thousand people — had been killed or expelled.

Background

Charles of Anjou had conquered the Kingdom of Sicily from the Hohenstaufen in 1266–1268 with papal backing, executing the young Conradin. His rule — heavy taxation, arrogant French officials and plans to use Sicily as a base for conquering the Byzantine Empire — bred deep resentment. The exiled Byzantine emperor Michael VIII Palaiologos, the Aragonese court (whose queen Constance was heir of the Hohenstaufen Manfred), and the Sicilian exile John of Procida are traditionally said to have conspired against him.

The uprising

According to the traditional account, during Easter Monday vespers at the Church of the Holy Spirit outside Palermo, a French sergeant insulted or molested a Sicilian woman; her husband killed him, and the crowd turned on the French, ringing the church bells as the signal for a general massacre. The cry "Morte ai francesi!" ("Death to the French!") spread through the city and then the island. Sicilians allegedly identified Frenchmen by asking them to pronounce cìciri ("chickpeas"), a shibboleth the French could not pronounce correctly.

Consequences

The rebels offered the Sicilian crown to Peter III of Aragon, who landed in August 1282, beginning the War of the Sicilian Vespers (1282–1302). The Peace of Caltabellotta (1302) left Sicily an independent kingdom under Frederick III of the House of Barcelona, while the Angevins retained the mainland south as the Kingdom of Naples — a division of southern Italy that endured, in various forms, for centuries. The revolt destroyed Charles's ambitions in the eastern Mediterranean and weakened papal-Angevin power. Verdi's 1855 opera Les vêpres siciliennes dramatised the event.
