International Style (architecture)

The International Style is a major architectural style that developed in the 1920s and 1930s and was dominant in Western architecture until the 1970s. Defined by an emphasis on volume over mass, balance rather than preconceived symmetry, and the rejection of applied ornament, it produced buildings characterised by smooth, planar surfaces, flat roofs, glass curtain walls, and open, flexible floor plans made possible by steel and reinforced concrete frame construction.

Name and origins

The term derives from the 1932 exhibition "Modern Architecture: International Exhibition" at the Museum of Modern Art in New York, organised by Henry-Russell Hitchcock and Philip Johnson, and their accompanying book The International Style: Architecture Since 1922. The style's pioneers included Walter Gropius and the Bauhaus in Germany, Le Corbusier in France, Ludwig Mies van der Rohe, and J. J. P. Oud and De Stijl in the Netherlands.

Characteristics

Hitchcock and Johnson identified three principles: expression of volume rather than mass; regularity and balance rather than axial symmetry; and reliance on the inherent elegance of materials and proportions rather than ornament. Buildings typically sit on pilotis (piles) or recessed ground floors, use ribbon windows and curtain walls, and present white or neutral rendered facades. Classic examples include the Bauhaus building in Dessau (1926), Le Corbusier's Villa Savoye (1931), Mies van der Rohe's Barcelona Pavilion (1929), and later the Lever House (1952) and Seagram Building (1958) in New York.

Spread and decline

Fleeing Nazi Germany, Gropius and Mies emigrated to the United States, where the style was widely adopted for corporate headquarters and postwar reconstruction, becoming the default idiom of the modern city skyline. From the 1960s it was increasingly criticised — notably by postmodernists such as Robert Venturi — for monotony and indifference to context, and its dominance gave way to postmodernism, high-tech architecture and other movements, though its influence persists in contemporary minimalist design.
