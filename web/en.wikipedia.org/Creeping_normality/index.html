Creeping normality

Creeping normality (also called landscape amnesia) is the process by which a major change can be accepted as normal and natural if it happens slowly, through small, often unnoticeable, increments of change.

The concept was articulated by Jared Diamond in his 2005 book Collapse: How Societies Choose to Fail or Succeed, in a discussion of how the Norse Greenlanders and other societies failed to notice slow environmental degradation — such as progressive soil erosion and deforestation — because each generation took the slightly worsened landscape of its youth as the normal baseline.

Related concepts

Creeping normality is closely related to the idea of shifting baseline syndrome in ecology and fisheries science, in which each generation recalibrates its sense of a healthy ecosystem to its own experience, and to the fable of the boiling frog. It is frequently invoked in discussions of climate change and biodiversity loss.
