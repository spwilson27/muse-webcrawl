Tooth

Diagram of a human molar tooth in cross-section.

A tooth (plural teeth) is a hard, calcified structure found in the jaws (or mouths) of many vertebrates and used to break down food. Some animals, particularly carnivores and omnivores, also use teeth for catching and killing prey, tearing flesh, and for grooming or defence. The roots of teeth are covered by gums. Teeth are not made of bone, but rather of multiple tissues of varying density and hardness that originate from the embryonic germ layer, the ectoderm.

The general structure of teeth is similar across the vertebrates, although there is considerable variation in their form and position. The teeth of mammals have deep roots, a pattern also found in some fish and crocodilians. In most teleost fish, however, the teeth are attached to the outside surface of the bone, while in lizards they are attached to the inner surface of the jaw by one side. In cartilaginous fish, such as sharks, the teeth are attached by tough ligaments to hoops of cartilage that form the jaw.

Structure

A tooth consists of a crown, which is the part visible above the gum line, and one or more roots embedded in the jawbone. The main tissues are:

  • Enamel — the hard, white outer covering of the crown. Composed almost entirely of hydroxyapatite crystals, it is the hardest substance in the human body, but it cannot regenerate once formed.
  • Dentin — a softer, yellowish calcified tissue beneath the enamel that forms the bulk of the tooth and can be produced throughout life.
  • Pulp — the soft central chamber containing nerves, blood vessels, and connective tissue.
  • Cementum — a bone-like tissue covering the root, anchoring the tooth via the periodontal ligament to the jawbone.

Human teeth

Humans have two sets of teeth during their lifetime. The first set, the deciduous (primary or "baby") teeth, consists of 20 teeth that begin to erupt at around six months of age. The permanent set consists of up to 32 teeth: eight incisors, four canines, eight premolars, and twelve molars, including the four third molars or "wisdom teeth". The arrangement of teeth is described by a dental formula; for adult humans it is 2.1.2.3 in each quadrant of the mouth.

Different teeth have different functions: incisors cut food, canines tear it, and premolars and molars crush and grind it. The shapes of an animal's teeth are closely related to its diet, a relationship that makes teeth especially valuable in palaeontology and archaeology, where they often survive long after other tissues have decayed.

Development

Tooth development, or odontogenesis, begins in the embryo with the formation of the dental lamina. Tooth buds pass through bud, cap, and bell stages, during which ameloblasts produce enamel and odontoblasts produce dentin. Genetic signalling involving families of genes such as BMP, FGF, and SHH controls the number, position, and shape of teeth, and disruptions can cause missing, extra, or malformed teeth.

Diseases and care

The most common tooth diseases are dental caries (tooth decay), caused by acids produced by bacteria fermenting sugars in dental plaque, and periodontal disease, inflammation of the tissues supporting the teeth. Fluoride strengthens enamel against decay and is widely added to toothpaste and, in some countries, to drinking water. Dentistry is the branch of medicine concerned with the diagnosis, prevention, and treatment of oral diseases; common treatments include fillings, root canal therapy, crowns, and extraction.

Teeth in other animals

Tooth form varies enormously. Rodents have continuously growing incisors; elephants' tusks are elongated upper incisors; sharks continually shed and replace their teeth throughout life, sometimes thousands in a lifetime. Some animals, such as turtles and modern birds, have lost teeth entirely in favour of beaks. In many invertebrates, hardened mouthparts are informally called teeth, though they differ developmentally from true vertebrate teeth.
