Gold Clause Cases

The Gold Clause Cases were a series of decisions by the Supreme Court of the United States in 1935 concerning the constitutional power of the federal government to abrogate "gold clauses" in public and private contracts during the Great Depression. The principal cases were Norman v. Baltimore & Ohio Railroad Co., Nortz v. United States, and Perry v. United States.

Background

Many bonds and contracts issued before 1933 contained gold clauses requiring payment in gold coin or its equivalent value, protecting creditors against currency depreciation. After taking office, President Franklin D. Roosevelt took the United States off the gold standard, and Congress passed a Joint Resolution on 5 June 1933 declaring gold clauses contrary to public policy and unenforceable. The dollar was subsequently devalued from $20.67 to $35 per troy ounce of gold. Creditors sued, arguing that the abrogation violated the Constitution.

Decisions

On 18 February 1935, in a set of closely divided 5–4 rulings delivered by Chief Justice Charles Evans Hughes, the Court upheld the government's action as applied to private contracts: Congress's power to regulate the currency allowed it to nullify gold clauses in private agreements. In Perry v. United States, the Court held that Congress could not constitutionally repudiate the government's own obligations, but ruled that the plaintiff bondholder had suffered no recoverable damages, since he could not legally have held or exported the gold in any event.

Significance

The decisions removed a major legal threat to the Roosevelt administration's monetary policy and confirmed broad congressional authority over the currency. Justice James McReynolds, dissenting orally, declared that the Constitution "is gone." Gold clauses remained unenforceable in the United States until Congress legalised them again for contracts issued after 1977.
