RS-68

An RS-68 engine during testing at NASA's Stennis Space Center.

The RS-68 (Rocket System-68) is a liquid-fuel cryogenic rocket engine built by Aerojet Rocketdyne (formerly Rocketdyne). Developed in the late 1990s, it burns liquid hydrogen and liquid oxygen and was designed to power the Delta IV launch vehicle family. The RS-68 is the most powerful hydrogen/oxygen rocket engine currently in service.

Unlike the Space Shuttle Main Engine (RS-25), from whose technology it partly descends, the RS-68 was designed for low manufacturing cost rather than reusability, accepting somewhat lower performance in exchange for far fewer parts and easier fabrication.

Design

The engine uses a gas-generator cycle: a portion of the propellants is burned in a gas generator to drive the turbopumps, and the exhaust is ducted away rather than passed through the main combustion chamber. Its large nozzle uses ablative cooling — layers of silica-phenolic material that char and erode away, carrying heat with them — instead of the hundreds of brazed cooling tubes used on the RS-25. This reduced the part count by roughly 80 percent compared with the Shuttle engine.

The RS-68 produces about 705,000 pounds-force (3.14 MN) of thrust at sea level in its original form, with a vacuum specific impulse of about 410 seconds. The uprated RS-68A, first flown in 2012, increased sea-level thrust to approximately 705,000–710,000 lbf and improved performance for heavy missions. The further improved RS-68B was proposed for NASA's Ares V cargo launcher but was never flown.

Operational history

The RS-68 first flew on 20 November 2002 powering the first Delta IV launch. A single RS-68 powered each Delta IV Medium and the common booster core of the Delta IV Heavy, which used three engines. The engine flew exclusively on Delta IV until that vehicle's retirement in 2024, accumulating a long record of successful launches for the US Air Force, NRO and NASA, including the Parker Solar Probe and the Orion EFT-1 test flight.
