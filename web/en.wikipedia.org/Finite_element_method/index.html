The finite element method (FEM) is a popular method for numerically solving differential equations arising in engineering and mathematical modeling. Typical problem areas of interest include the traditional fields of structural analysis, heat transfer, fluid flow, mass transport, and electromagnetic potential.

FEM is a general numerical method for solving partial differential equations in two- or three-space variables (i.e., some boundary value problems). To solve a problem, the FEM subdivides a large system into smaller, simpler parts called finite elements, a process known as discretisation or meshing. The equations modelling these finite elements are then assembled into a larger system of equations that models the entire problem. FEM then approximates a solution by minimizing an associated error function via the calculus of variations.

History

The method's practical origins lie in the 1940s and 1950s work of aeronautical engineers, notably the 1956 paper by Turner, Clough, Martin, and Topp on stiffness analysis of aircraft structures, and Ray Clough's 1960 paper that introduced the name "finite element". Mathematicians independently developed the underlying variational theory; Richard Courant had used piecewise polynomial approximation over triangular subregions as early as 1943. NASA funded the development of the general-purpose program NASTRAN in the 1960s, and commercial packages such as ANSYS, Abaqus, and COMSOL Multiphysics later made FEM a standard engineering tool.

Method

A typical FEM workflow consists of pre-processing (defining the geometry, material properties, boundary conditions, and generating a mesh of elements connected at nodes), solution (assembling element stiffness matrices into a global sparse linear system and solving for nodal unknowns), and post-processing (computing derived quantities such as stress, strain, temperature gradients, or field fluxes, and visualising them). Accuracy improves with mesh refinement, at the cost of more computation; convergence studies and adaptive refinement guide the choice of mesh.

Formulation

Mathematically, FEM seeks an approximate solution in a finite-dimensional subspace of the solution space of a variational (weak) formulation of the PDE. The Galerkin method, in which test functions are drawn from the same space as the trial functions, is the most common choice. Error estimates bound the difference between the finite element solution and the exact solution in terms of element size and polynomial degree.

Applications

FEM is used throughout engineering: stress and vibration analysis of bridges, aircraft, and automobiles; crash simulation; thermal analysis of electronics; groundwater and CFD-adjacent flow modelling; electromagnetic design of motors and antennas; and biomechanics, including patient-specific modelling of bones and implants.
