The Zhuangzi is an ancient Chinese text from the late Warring States period (476–221 BCE) which is, together with the Tao Te Ching, one of the two foundational works of Daoism. It is named after its purported author, Master Zhuang (Zhuang Zhou, c. 369–286 BCE), and consists of stories, parables, and dialogues celebrated for their literary brilliance and philosophical humour.

Text and authorship

The received text has 33 chapters, divided since the edition of the commentator Guo Xiang (died 312 CE) into 7 "Inner Chapters" — generally accepted as the work of Zhuang Zhou himself — 15 "Outer Chapters", and 11 "Miscellaneous Chapters", composed by his followers and later hands. Guo Xiang's commentary fixed the text and profoundly shaped its interpretation.

Famous passages

The first Inner Chapter, "Free and Easy Wandering" (Xiaoyao you), opens with the giant fish Kun transforming into the bird Peng. Other celebrated episodes include Zhuangzi dreaming he was a butterfly (and wondering whether he was a man dreaming of being a butterfly or a butterfly dreaming of being a man), the skilled Cook Ding whose blade never dulls, the debate with Huizi on the happiness of fish, the fasting of the mind, and the giant useless tree that survives because it is good for nothing.

Philosophy

The text urges alignment with the spontaneous way of nature (ziran), scepticism about fixed distinctions and conventional knowledge, and freedom from the constraints of ritual, fame, and utility — themes that deeply influenced Chinese Buddhism, poetry, and painting.
