Intermediate disturbance hypothesis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The intermediate disturbance hypothesis (IDH) suggests that local species diversity is maximized when ecological disturbance is neither too rare nor too frequent. At low levels of disturbance, more competitive organisms will push subordinate species to extinction and dominate the ecosystem. At high levels of disturbance, due to frequent forest fires or human impacts like deforestation, all species are at risk of going extinct.

According to IDH theory, at intermediate levels of disturbance, diversity is thus maximized because species that thrive at both early and late successional stages can coexist. The hypothesis was proposed by Joseph Connell in 1978, drawing on observations of tropical rain forests and coral reefs, where storms, wave action and other disturbances create a mosaic of patches at different successional stages.

Mechanism

Disturbances such as fires, floods, storms and grazing open up gaps in a community. These gaps are colonised first by fast-growing, opportunistic species. If disturbance is rare, slower-growing competitive dominants eventually exclude the pioneers. If disturbance is very frequent, only the hardiest pioneers persist. At intermediate frequencies, both groups—and species specialised on intermediate conditions—can be maintained in the landscape, yielding the highest overall diversity.

Criticism and evidence

The hypothesis has been influential but is debated. Meta-analyses have found the predicted hump-shaped relationship in some systems but not others, and the pattern depends on the productivity of the environment, the scale of measurement, and the type of disturbance. Some ecologists argue the hypothesis is too vague about what counts as "intermediate" disturbance to be readily falsifiable.
