Ctesiphon (Middle Persian: Tyspwn; Arabic: al-Madāʾin) was an ancient city in Mesopotamia, on the eastern bank of the Tigris about 35 km southeast of modern Baghdad. It served as a royal capital of the Parthian Empire and later the Sasanian Empire for more than eight centuries, becoming one of the largest cities in the world.

History

Founded as a Parthian camp opposite Hellenistic Seleucia, Ctesiphon grew into the Parthians' winter capital; Roman armies captured it repeatedly — under Trajan (116), Avidius Cassius (164) and Septimius Severus (198) — yet it always recovered. Under the Sasanians (from 224 AD) it formed, with Seleucia and later suburbs, the metropolitan complex the Arabs called al-Mada'in, "the cities". The Sasanian royal palace included the great audience hall whose soaring brick iwan, the Taq Kasra ("Arch of Khosrow"), still stands — built probably under Khosrow I in the 6th century, its 25-metre span was the largest free-standing vault of its age.

Decline

After the Battle of al-Qadisiyyah (636) and the Arab capture of Ctesiphon in 637, the court fled east and the city declined; its population and building materials were partly absorbed by Baghdad, founded in 762 by the Abbasid caliph al-Mansur. The site, at modern Salman Pak, was fought over in the First World War (Battle of Ctesiphon, 1915) and excavated by German and American-Italian teams in the 20th century.
