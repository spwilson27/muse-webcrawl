The Clarendon Code was a series of four English statutes passed between 1661 and 1665 that established the religious settlement of the Restoration and penalized Protestant Nonconformists who refused to conform to the restored Church of England. Though named after Edward Hyde, 1st Earl of Clarendon, the chief minister of Charles II, the code was driven largely by the Cavalier Parliament rather than by Clarendon himself.

The four acts

The Corporation Act 1661 required municipal officeholders to take Anglican communion and renounce the Solemn League and Covenant. The Act of Uniformity 1662 required all ministers to assent to the Book of Common Prayer in its entirety; about 2,000 clergy who refused were ejected from their livings on St Bartholomew's Day 1662, an event remembered as the Great Ejection. The Conventicle Act 1664 forbade religious meetings of five or more persons outside the Church of England. The Five Mile Act 1665 prohibited ejected ministers from coming within five miles of any incorporated town or their former parishes.

Effects

The code created a permanent division between the established church and the dissenting churches, and its enforcement varied with political circumstances. Attempts by Charles II and James II to suspend the penal laws by royal declaration of indulgence provoked constitutional crises, contributing to the Glorious Revolution of 1688.

Repeal

The Toleration Act 1689 gave limited relief to Protestant dissenters, though the Corporation and Test Acts remained until their repeal in 1828.
