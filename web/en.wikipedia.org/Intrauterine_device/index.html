An intrauterine device (IUD), also known as an intrauterine contraceptive device (IUCD) or coil, is a small, often T-shaped birth control device that is inserted into the uterus to prevent pregnancy. IUDs are a form of long-acting reversible contraception (LARC) and are among the most effective methods of birth control available.

Types

Two broad types are in widespread use. Copper IUDs (such as the copper T380A) contain no hormones; copper ions create an environment toxic to sperm and eggs, preventing fertilization. They are effective for up to 10–12 years and can also be used as emergency contraception when inserted within five days of unprotected intercourse. Hormonal IUDs release a small amount of the progestin levonorgestrel, which thickens cervical mucus, thins the uterine lining, and may suppress ovulation. Depending on the product they last 3–8 years and often reduce menstrual bleeding.

Effectiveness and safety

With failure rates under 1% per year, IUDs rival sterilization in effectiveness while remaining fully reversible; fertility returns promptly after removal. Possible side effects include irregular bleeding, cramping after insertion, expulsion of the device, and, rarely, uterine perforation at the time of insertion (about 1–2 per 1,000 insertions). Contrary to older concerns raised by the Dalkon Shield of the 1970s, modern IUDs are not associated with a significant increase in pelvic inflammatory disease after the first few weeks following insertion.

Use

IUDs are used by more than 150 million women worldwide and are recommended by organizations such as the World Health Organization and the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists for most women, including adolescents and women who have never been pregnant. Insertion is a brief office procedure performed by a trained clinician.
