A water footprint shows the extent of water use in relation to consumption by people. The water footprint of an individual, community, or business is defined as the total volume of fresh water used to produce the goods and services consumed. The concept was introduced in 2002 by Arjen Hoekstra of UNESCO-IHE as an indicator of water use that includes both direct and indirect consumption, extending the earlier idea of "virtual water" developed by John Anthony Allan.

Components

The water footprint is conventionally divided into three components, standardized in the Water Footprint Assessment Manual of the Water Footprint Network (founded 2008):

Blue water footprint — consumption of surface or groundwater resources (evaporated, incorporated into a product, or returned to a different catchment or time period).

Green water footprint — consumption of rainwater stored in soil as soil moisture, chiefly relevant for agriculture and forestry products.

Grey water footprint — the volume of freshwater required to assimilate pollutants and meet specific water quality standards.

Examples and statistics

Agriculture accounts for roughly 92% of humanity's water footprint. Producing one kilogram of beef requires on the order of 15,000 litres of water, mostly for feed crops; a kilogram of rice requires roughly 2,500 litres; a single cup of coffee embodies about 130–140 litres. The global average water footprint has been estimated at about 1,385 cubic metres per person per year. National footprints vary widely, and wealthy countries often externalize much of their footprint by importing water-intensive goods from water-stressed regions, a pattern captured by the concept of virtual water trade.

Uses and criticism

Water footprints are used by businesses, governments, and consumers to identify water risks, set reduction targets, and label products; an international standard, ISO 14046, was published in 2014. Critics note that a simple volumetric footprint ignores local scarcity — a litre consumed in a water-rich region is not equivalent to a litre in a desert basin — and weighted "water scarcity footprints" have been developed to account for local impacts.
