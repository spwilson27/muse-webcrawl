Modem

A modem (a portmanteau of modulator–demodulator) is a hardware device that converts data from a digital format into a format suitable for an analog transmission medium such as a telephone line or radio link, and converts incoming analog signals back into digital data. Modems enabled computers to communicate over the existing telephone network and were the primary means of home Internet access in the 1980s and 1990s.

History

Commercial modems emerged in the 1950s and 1960s for military and airline-reservation networks. The Bell 103 modem (1962) offered 300 bits per second over ordinary phone lines. Successive ITU standards raised speeds through 1,200, 2,400, 9,600, and 14,400 bit/s to the 33.6 kbit/s of V.34 and the 56 kbit/s of V.90/V.92 in the late 1990s, which approached the theoretical limit of the analog voice channel. The characteristic screeching handshake of dial-up modems became a cultural soundmark of the early consumer Internet.

Types

Dial-up modems use the voice band of telephone lines. DSL modems use higher frequencies on the same copper lines to deliver much faster broadband while leaving the voice band free. Cable modems operate over coaxial cable television networks under the DOCSIS standard. Fibre-optic terminals (ONTs) and cellular modems perform analogous conversion for optical and radio media, though the term "modem" persists even where modulation schemes differ greatly from the original audio tones.

Operation

A modem modulates a carrier signal to encode digital information — using techniques such as frequency-shift keying in early devices and quadrature amplitude modulation in later ones — and applies error correction (such as V.42) and data compression (V.42bis) to improve effective throughput and reliability.
