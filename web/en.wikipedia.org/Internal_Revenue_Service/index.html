The Internal Revenue Service (IRS) is a bureau of the United States Department of the Treasury responsible for administering the federal tax laws enacted by Congress — the Internal Revenue Code. The IRS collects the bulk of federal revenue (over $4 trillion annually in recent years), processes more than 260 million tax returns, and issues refunds, while enforcing compliance through audits and criminal investigation.

History

The office originated in 1862, when President Abraham Lincoln created the Commissioner of Internal Revenue to administer an income tax levied to fund the Civil War. The modern income tax followed the Sixteenth Amendment (1913). The Bureau of Internal Revenue was reorganized and renamed the Internal Revenue Service in 1953, and substantially restructured by the IRS Restructuring and Reform Act of 1998, which reorganized the agency around categories of taxpayers rather than geography.

Organization and work

The IRS is headed by a Commissioner appointed by the President and confirmed by the Senate for a five-year term. Operating divisions handle wage and investment income, large businesses and international taxpayers, small businesses and the self-employed, and tax-exempt and government entities. The IRS Criminal Investigation division investigates tax fraud and financial crimes. Services include issuing regulations and guidance, the e-file electronic filing system, taxpayer assistance, and the Taxpayer Advocate Service, an independent organization within the IRS.

Controversies

The agency has periodically faced controversy, including the 2013 revelations that it gave extra scrutiny to some political groups seeking tax-exempt status, recurring funding and staffing disputes, and debates over audit rates for low- and high-income taxpayers.
