Appian Way

The Appian Way (Latin: Via Appia) is one of the earliest and most strategically important Roman roads. Construction began in 312 BCE under the censor Appius Claudius Caecus, initially linking Rome with Capua; it was later extended across southern Italy to Brundisium (Brindisi), the main port for travel to Greece and the East, giving a total length of roughly 560 km (350 miles).

History

Called the "queen of roads" (regina viarum), the Via Appia was vital for moving troops and supplies during the Samnite Wars and later conflicts. In 71 BCE the road near Rome was lined with the crucified followers of Spartacus after the suppression of his slave revolt. Roman law forbade burial within the city, so the road outside the walls became lined with tombs, monuments and catacombs, including those associated with early Christianity.

Today

Long stretches of the original basalt paving survive, flanked by umbrella pines and ruins. The first miles out of Rome form the Appian Way Regional Park, a protected archaeological and natural area popular with walkers and cyclists. In 2024 the Via Appia was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List.
