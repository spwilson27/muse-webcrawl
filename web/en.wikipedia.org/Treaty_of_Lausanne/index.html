The Treaty of Lausanne (French: Traité de Lausanne) is a peace treaty negotiated during the Lausanne Conference of 1922–23 and signed in the Palais de Rumine in Lausanne, Switzerland, on 24 July 1923. The treaty officially resolved the conflict that had originally existed between the Ottoman Empire and the Allied French Republic, British Empire, Kingdom of Italy, Empire of Japan, Kingdom of Greece, Kingdom of Romania and the Serb-Croat-Slovene State since the onset of World War I.

Background

The treaty superseded the unimplemented Treaty of Sèvres of 1920, which had partitioned the Ottoman Empire. After the Turkish victory in the Greco-Turkish War (1919–1922), the government of the Grand National Assembly of Turkey, led by Mustafa Kemal (Atatürk), rejected Sèvres. Negotiations opened at Lausanne on 20 November 1922, chaired by British foreign secretary Lord Curzon; the Turkish delegation was led by Ìsmet Pasha.

Terms

The treaty defined the borders of the modern Turkish Republic, confirmed Turkish sovereignty over Anatolia and Eastern Thrace, and established Turkey as the successor state to the Ottoman Empire. Turkey renounced all claims to the former Ottoman territories outside those borders, including Cyprus, Egypt, Iraq and Syria. Greece recognized the Turkish annexation of Ìzmir (Smyrna) and Eastern Thrace. The Turkish Straits were demilitarized and placed under international administration (superseded by the Montreux Convention of 1936). Articles 37–44 protected the rights of non-Muslim minorities in Turkey, and a separate convention mandated a population exchange between Greece and Turkey.

Aftermath

The treaty was registered in the League of Nations Treaty Series on 5 September 1924. It led to the international recognition of the sovereignty of the new Republic of Turkey, which was proclaimed on 29 October 1923. It remains a foundational document of Turkish foreign policy and is still invoked in debates over Turkish borders, the status of Mosul, and minority rights in Turkey and Greece.
