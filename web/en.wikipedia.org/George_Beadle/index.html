George Wells Beadle (22 October 1903 – 9 June 1989) was an American geneticist. In 1958 he shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Edward Tatum for demonstrating that genes regulate specific chemical processes — the "one gene–one enzyme" hypothesis — and with Joshua Lederberg for work on bacterial genetics.

Career and research

Raised on a Nebraska farm, Beadle studied at the University of Nebraska and earned his PhD at Cornell in 1931 on maize genetics. After postdoctoral work at Caltech with Thomas Hunt Morgan's group and in Paris with Boris Ephrussi — where experiments transplanting eye tissue in fruit fly larvae hinted at gene-controlled chemistry — he moved to Stanford in 1937.

There, with biochemist Edward Tatum, Beadle turned to the bread mould Neurospora crassa. They irradiated spores to produce mutants, then showed that individual mutations knocked out single steps in metabolic pathways: each mutant failed to make one enzyme and could be rescued by supplying the product of that step. Published in 1941, this "one gene–one enzyme" concept (later refined to one gene–one polypeptide) launched biochemical genetics.

Later life

Beadle chaired biology at Caltech and served as president of the University of Chicago from 1961 to 1968. He was an enthusiastic gardener and an authority on teosinte, the wild ancestor of maize. He died in 1989.
