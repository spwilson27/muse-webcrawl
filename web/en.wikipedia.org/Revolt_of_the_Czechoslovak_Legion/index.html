Revolt of the Czechoslovak Legion

The Revolt of the Czechoslovak Legion was an armed uprising in the summer of 1918 by the Czechoslovak Legion — volunteer units of Czechs and Slovaks, many of them former prisoners of war — against the Bolshevik authorities in Russia. It became a major catalyst for the escalation of the Russian Civil War and Allied intervention.

Background

After the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk, the Legion, some 40,000–60,000 strong, sought to leave Russia via the Trans-Siberian Railway to Vladivostok and sail to France to continue fighting on the Western Front. Tensions with the Bolsheviks grew as the Legion was ordered to disarm; a violent incident at Chelyabinsk in May 1918, in which a Legionnaire was lynched by a mob, triggered open conflict.

The revolt

Following Leon Trotsky's order to disarm and arrest the Legion, its units seized stretches of the Trans-Siberian Railway, capturing Chelyabinsk, Novonikolaevsk, Samara, Kazan and other towns. In Samara the anti-Bolshevik Committee of Members of the Constituent Assembly (Komuch) was established under Legion protection. The Legion's capture of the Imperial gold reserve at Kazan was a major prize.

Aftermath

The revolt strengthened anti-Bolshevik forces and prompted the Allied landings at Vladivostok and elsewhere. Eventually the Legion withdrew along the railway and was evacuated from Vladivostok in 1920, returning to the newly independent Czechoslovakia.
