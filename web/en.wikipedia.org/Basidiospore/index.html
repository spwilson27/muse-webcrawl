Basidiospore


Basidia bearing basidiospores on sterigmata.

A basidiospore is a reproductive spore produced by Basidiomycete fungi, a grouping that includes mushrooms, shelf fungi, rusts, and smuts. Basidiospores are typically unicellular and haploid, and they are formed by the process of meiosis in specialized end cells known as basidia. Millions of basidiospores can be produced by a single mushroom; some species release them in enormous quantities, and they are dispersed primarily by wind and gravity.

Structure and development

A basidium is a microscopic, spore-producing structure found on the hymenophore of fruiting bodies. In a typical basidium, karyogamy and meiosis occur, producing four haploid nuclei that migrate into four spores borne externally on slender projections called sterigmata. Each spore typically contains one nucleus. Certain fungi deviate from the four-spore pattern, producing two-spored basidia.

Discharge and germination

Many gilled mushrooms forcibly discharge their basidiospores by a surface-tension catapult mechanism involving a droplet of fluid (Buller's drop) at the spore's base, propelling it a short distance into the space between gills so it can fall free of the cap. On landing in a suitable environment, a basidiospore germinates to produce a primary mycelium, which may fuse with a compatible partner to form the dikaryotic secondary mycelium that eventually fruits. In rust and smut fungi, basidiospores (sporidia) form part of complex multi-host life cycles and agricultural diseases.
