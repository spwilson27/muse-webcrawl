Home front during World War II

The home front during World War II covers the activities of civilians in the warring nations in support of their military effort, and the effects of total war on everyday life between 1939 and 1945. The Second World War blurred the line between front line and home more than any previous conflict: civilians were mobilised for war production, subjected to rationing and evacuation, and — through aerial bombing and occupation — became targets on an unprecedented scale.

War production and labour

All major belligerents converted their economies to war production. In Britain, women were conscripted into factories, the services and the Land Army; by 1943 some 90 per cent of single women of working age were in work or uniform. Germany, despite ideological reluctance, increasingly relied on millions of foreign forced labourers. The United States became the "Arsenal of Democracy": its factories produced some 300,000 aircraft and 86,000 tanks, and six million women entered the workforce, symbolised by "Rosie the Riveter".

Rationing and everyday life

Britain introduced food rationing in January 1940 — bacon, butter and sugar first, followed by meat, tea, cheese and most other staples — along with clothing coupons and petrol rationing. "Make Do and Mend", the Dig for Victory gardening campaign, and utility design defined civilian life; rationing continued in some form until 1954. Evacuation moved about 1.5 million people, mostly children, from British cities in September 1939 alone. The blackout, air-raid shelters and the Home Guard shaped the experience of the "People's War".

Bombing and civil defence

Strategic bombing brought the war to civilians directly. The German Blitz killed about 43,000 British civilians in 1940–41; Allied bombing of Germany killed several hundred thousand, including an estimated 35,000 in Dresden in February 1945; Allied raids on Japan killed perhaps 100,000 in Tokyo in a single night in March 1945. Civil defence organisations — air raid wardens, fire watchers, ambulance and rescue services — were overwhelmingly staffed by volunteers.

Occupied Europe

In German-occupied Europe, daily life meant food requisitioning, curfews, forced labour drafts and terror. The Hunger Winter of 1944–45 starved the western Netherlands. Resistance movements grew from intelligence networks to armed uprisings, while collaboration ranged from opportunism to ideology. Jews across occupied Europe were deported and murdered in the Holocaust; Roma, Poles, Soviet prisoners and others were also persecuted and killed in vast numbers.

The Soviet Union

The Soviet home front endured the war's harshest conditions. Some 1,700 factories were dismantled and moved east beyond the Urals in 1941. Leningrad's 872-day siege killed around a million civilians, most by starvation, as rations fell to 125 grams of adulterated bread a day. Women made up the majority of the industrial and agricultural workforce, and teenagers and pensioners were mobilised wholesale.

The United States

The continental United States was spared attack (apart from small incidents and the Aleutian Islands campaign), but the war ended the Great Depression, drew millions into war plants and the armed forces, and brought rationing of gasoline, rubber, sugar and meat. Around 120,000 Japanese Americans, most of them citizens, were forcibly interned — a policy later officially apologised for. The war years also accelerated the Great Migration of Black Americans to industrial cities and laid groundwork for the postwar civil rights movement.

Propaganda and morale

Governments managed information and morale through ministries of propaganda, censored press, newsreels and posters — Britain's "Keep Calm and Carry On" (printed but scarcely displayed at the time), America's Office of War Information films, and Goebbels's apparatus in Germany. Despite hardship, civilian morale in the major belligerents proved more resilient than pre-war theorists had feared.
