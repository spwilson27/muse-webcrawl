Limit (mathematics)

In mathematics, a limit is the value that a function (or sequence) approaches as its input (or index) approaches some value. Limits are fundamental to calculus and mathematical analysis, providing the rigorous basis for continuity, derivatives and integrals. The modern precise definition uses the language of "epsilon–delta" arguments developed in the nineteenth century.

Limit of a function

The statement limx→c f(x) = L means that f(x) can be made arbitrarily close to L by taking x sufficiently close to, but not equal to, c. Formally: for every ε > 0 there exists a δ > 0 such that whenever 0 < |x − c| < δ, then |f(x) − L| < ε. One-sided limits restrict x to approach c from the left (x → c⁻) or from the right (x → c⁺); the two-sided limit exists precisely when both one-sided limits exist and are equal.

Limit of a sequence

A sequence (an) has limit L if its terms eventually lie and remain arbitrarily close to L: for every ε > 0 there is an index N such that |an − L| < ε for all n > N. For example, the sequence 1, 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, … has limit 0. Convergent sequences are Cauchy sequences in complete spaces such as the real numbers.

Uses

The derivative of a function is defined as the limit of the slope of secant lines, and the integral as the limit of sums of areas of rectangles. Limits also define infinite series, continuity, and the behaviour of functions at infinity. Techniques for evaluating limits include algebraic simplification, the squeeze theorem, and L'Hôpital's rule for indeterminate forms.

History

Greek mathematicians used limiting arguments in the method of exhaustion; Newton and Leibniz handled limits intuitively in the seventeenth century. Augustin-Louis Cauchy gave a verbal definition in the 1820s, and Karl Weierstrass supplied the arithmetised ε–δ formulation that became standard in the later nineteenth century, placing analysis on a rigorous footing.
