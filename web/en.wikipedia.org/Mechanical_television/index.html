Mechanical television

Mechanical television or mechanical scan television is an obsolete television system that relies on a mechanical scanning device, such as a rotating disk with holes in it or a rotating mirror system, to scan the scene and generate the video signal, and a similar mechanical device at the receiver to display the picture. This contrasts with vacuum tube electronic television technology, using electron beam scanning methods, as in cathode-ray tube televisions. Subsequently, modern solid-state liquid-crystal displays (LCD) and LED displays are now used.

History

The earliest television systems, developed in the 1920s and early 1930s, were mechanical. Paul Nipkow patented the Nipkow disk — a rotating disk pierced by a spiral of holes — in 1884; as the disk rotated, the holes swept across the scene line by line, and a photocell behind the disk converted the varying light into an electrical signal. John Logie Baird gave the first public demonstration of a working television system in London in January 1926, transmitting images at 30 lines. In 1928 Baird demonstrated colour transmission and transatlantic transmission, and the BBC began experimental broadcasts using his 30-line system in 1929.

Charles Francis Jenkins developed mechanical systems in the United States, and by the early 1930s several stations broadcast low-definition mechanical television. Mechanical receivers, such as Baird's "Televisor", were sold to hobbyists and early adopters.

Decline

Mechanical systems were limited in resolution: increasing the number of lines required ever more precisely made disks spinning at high speed, and the small apertures passed very little light, giving dim pictures. Electronic systems developed by Philo Farnsworth, Vladimir Zworykin, and the EMI team in Britain overcame these limits. In 1936 the BBC opened the world's first regular high-definition television service using the 405-line electronic system, and mechanical television was abandoned.
