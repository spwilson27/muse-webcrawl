Fowler's position

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Fowler's position is a standard patient position in medicine in which the patient is seated in a semi-sitting position (45–60 degrees) with knees either bent or straight. Variations in the angle are denoted as low Fowler's (15–30 degrees), semi-Fowler's (30–45 degrees), standard Fowler's, and high Fowler's (80–90 degrees), in which the patient sits nearly upright. The position is named for George Ryerson Fowler (1848–1906), an American surgeon who used it in the treatment of peritonitis.

Uses

Fowler's position is used to promote oxygenation by allowing maximum chest expansion: gravity pulls the diaphragm downward and permits the lungs to expand more fully, so it is a common intervention for patients with breathing difficulty, including those with pneumonia, heart failure, or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. It is also used after certain surgeries, during nasogastric tube insertion and feeding (to reduce the risk of aspiration), and to facilitate drainage after abdominal operations — Fowler originally adopted it to encourage infected fluid to pool in the pelvis, where absorption into the circulation is slower.

Considerations

Prolonged use can increase pressure over the sacrum and heels, raising the risk of pressure injuries, and sliding down in bed can create shearing forces on the skin; proper support of the feet and repositioning are standard nursing measures.
