Convention on the Conservation of Migratory Species

The Convention on the Conservation of Migratory Species of Wild Animals, also known as the CMS or the Bonn Convention, is an international treaty concluded in Bonn, Germany, in 1979 under the auspices of the United Nations Environment Programme. It provides a global platform for the conservation of migratory animals and their habitats across the full extent of their ranges, which frequently cross many national jurisdictions.

Structure

The Convention distinguishes between two appendices. Appendix I lists migratory species threatened with extinction; parties that are range states for these species must strictly protect them, conserve or restore their habitats, and prohibit their taking, subject to narrow exceptions. Appendix II lists migratory species whose conservation status would benefit significantly from international cooperation, and encourages range states to conclude subsidiary agreements for their management.

Subsidiary agreements

A family of agreements and memoranda of understanding has been negotiated under the CMS umbrella, covering, among others, European bats (EUROBATS), seals in the Wadden Sea, small cetaceans of the Baltic and North Seas (ASCOBANS), African-Eurasian migratory waterbirds (AEWA), albatrosses and petrels (ACAP), and migratory sharks and raptors.

Membership and meetings

The Convention entered into force on 1 November 1983. Its secretariat is administered by UNEP and based in Bonn. The Conference of the Parties meets roughly every three years to review implementation, amend the appendices, and adopt resolutions; more than 130 states are parties.
