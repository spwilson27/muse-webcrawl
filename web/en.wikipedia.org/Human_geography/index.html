Human geography

Human geography
Branch of: Geography
Subfields: Cultural, economic, political, population, urban, health geography
Key figures: Friedrich Ratzel, Ellen Semple, Vidal de la Blache, David Harvey
Methods: Fieldwork, GIS, quantitative and qualitative analysis

Human geography, also known as anthropogeography, is the branch of geography that studies humans and their communities, cultures, economies, and interactions with the environment, interrelating them by space and place. Along with physical geography, it is one of the two major subdivisions of the discipline.

History

The field took shape in the late 19th century with Friedrich Ratzel's Anthropogeographie and the environmental determinism of Ellen Churchill Semple, countered by Paul Vidal de la Blache's possibilism and regional geography. Mid-20th-century human geography adopted quantitative, model-building methods (the "quantitative revolution"), before successive waves — behavioural, humanistic, Marxist and radical, feminist, and postmodern geographies — broadened its questions and methods from the 1970s onward.

Subfields

Major subfields include cultural geography (landscapes, identity, place), economic geography (industry, trade, development), political geography and geopolitics, population geography and demography, urban geography, social geography, health geography, historical geography and tourism geography. Contemporary work makes heavy use of geographic information systems (GIS), remote sensing and big spatial data, alongside ethnography and archival research.
