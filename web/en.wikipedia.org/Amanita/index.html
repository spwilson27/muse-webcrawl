Amanita

Amanita is a genus of basidiomycete fungi comprising roughly 600 species worldwide, including some of the most toxic mushrooms known as well as prized edible species. Amanitas are typically recognized by a cap bearing pale warty patches (remnants of the universal veil), free white gills, a ring (annulus) on the stem, and a cup-like volva at the base.

Toxicity

The genus includes the death cap (Amanita phalloides), responsible for the great majority of fatal mushroom poisonings, and the destroying angels (such as A. virosa and A. bisporigera). Their toxins, chiefly α-amanitin and other amatoxins, inhibit RNA polymerase II and cause potentially lethal liver and kidney failure days after ingestion. The fly agaric (A. muscaria), by contrast, is psychoactive rather than usually deadly: its active compounds are ibotenic acid and muscimol.

Edible species and ecology

Some species, including Caesar's mushroom (A. caesarea) and the blusher (A. rubescens), are eaten, but because of the danger of confusion with toxic lookalikes, eating wild amanitas is strongly discouraged for non-experts. Most Amanita species are mycorrhizal, forming symbiotic associations with trees such as pine, oak and birch, and they play an important role in forest nutrient cycling.
