The Albaicín (also spelled Albayzín) is a district of Granada, in the autonomous community of Andalusia, Spain, which retains the narrow, winding streets of its medieval Moorish past. Facing the Alhambra across the valley of the River Darro, it was declared a World Heritage Site in 1994 as an extension of the Alhambra and Generalife listing.

History

The hill was settled in Iberian and Roman times, but the district took shape under Muslim rule. After the fall of the Zirid taifa and through the Nasrid period, the Albaicín became a densely populated quarter of mosques, palaces, cisterns and public baths. Its population swelled with refugees from other parts of al-Andalus reconquered by Christian kingdoms. After the fall of Granada in 1492, the quarter remained largely Morisco; following the Morisco revolt of 1568–71 and the subsequent expulsion, it declined and many mosques were replaced by churches.

Landmarks

The viewpoint of the Mirador de San Nicolás offers the celebrated panorama of the Alhambra against the Sierra Nevada. Other sights include El Bañuelo, an 11th–12th-century Arab bathhouse; the remains of the Zirid walls and gates such as Puerta de Elvira; the converted mosques now serving as the churches of San Salvador and San Miguel Bajo; numerous historic cisterns (aljibes); and the traditional carmenes — walled houses with gardens — characteristic of the district. The Carrera del Darro and Paseo de los Tristes run along the river below.

Character today

The Albaicín preserves a strong neighbourhood identity, with Moroccan tea houses, flamenco venues in the adjoining Sacromonte quarter, and a mix of long-term residents, students and tourists. Its street plan and vernacular architecture are among the best surviving examples of a Hispano-Muslim medina.
