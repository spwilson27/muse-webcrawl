The Prime Minister of Canada (French: premier ministre du Canada) is the head of government of Canada and the country's chief executive. The Prime Minister chairs the Cabinet, directs the federal administration, and is usually the leader of the political party holding the most seats in the House of Commons.

Appointment and tenure

By constitutional convention, the Prime Minister is appointed by the Governor General, representing the monarch, and must be able to command the confidence of the elected House of Commons. There is no fixed term: a Prime Minister serves while retaining that confidence and while remaining leader of their party. The office is not established explicitly in the Constitution Acts, resting instead on convention modelled on the British premiership.

History

The first Prime Minister was Sir John A. Macdonald, who took office at Confederation in 1867 and dominated Canadian politics for most of the following quarter-century. William Lyon Mackenzie King, the longest-serving Prime Minister, held the office for over 21 years across three periods. Other notable holders include Wilfrid Laurier, Robert Borden, Lester B. Pearson, Pierre Trudeau, Brian Mulroney, Jean Chrétien, and Justin Trudeau, who served from 2015 to 2025 before being succeeded by Mark Carney.

Role

The Prime Minister selects Cabinet ministers, advises on the appointment of senators, judges, and the Governor General, directs foreign and defence policy, and represents Canada internationally.
