The Permanent Settlement, also known as the Permanent Settlement of Bengal or the Zamindari system, was an agreement between the East India Company and Bengali landlords (zamindars) to fix revenues to be raised from land, enacted in 1793 by the Governor-General Lord Cornwallis under the Bengal Code.

Terms

Under the Settlement, zamindars — previously tax collectors with customary rights — were recognised as hereditary proprietors of the land, provided they paid a land revenue demand fixed in perpetuity to the Company, amounting to ten-elevenths of the rental they collected. If a zamindar failed to pay by the appointed date, his estate could be sold under the so-called "sunset law". The raiyats (cultivators) became tenants of the zamindars, with their rents initially regulated but only weakly protected.

Consequences

The Settlement gave the colonial state a stable, predictable income and created a loyal landed class, but the demand was set punitively high: many old estates were auctioned in the first decades, tenancy insecurity and subinfeudation spread, and absentee landlordism flourished. Unlike the ryotwari and mahalwari systems used elsewhere in British India, Bengal's revenue was never revised upward with rising prices and cultivation, so the state's share steadily shrank in real terms. The zamindari system was abolished after independence by land-reform legislation in India in the 1950s and in East Pakistan (now Bangladesh) under the East Bengal State Acquisition and Tenancy Act of 1950.
