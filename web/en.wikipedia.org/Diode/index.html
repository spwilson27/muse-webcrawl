Diode

A diode is a two-terminal electronic component that conducts current primarily in one direction (asymmetric conductance); it has low (ideally zero) resistance in one direction and high (ideally infinite) resistance in the other. A semiconductor diode, the most commonly used type today, is a crystalline piece of semiconductor material with a p–n junction connected to two electrical terminals.

Common diode packages and the schematic symbol.

Operation

When the anode is positive relative to the cathode (forward bias), a silicon diode begins to conduct appreciably above about 0.6–0.7 volts. In reverse bias, only a tiny leakage current flows until the breakdown voltage is reached. The current–voltage relationship is described approximately by the Shockley diode equation. Vacuum tube diodes, historically used in early electronics, employ thermionic emission instead.

Types and applications

Varieties include rectifier diodes for converting alternating to direct current, Zener diodes for voltage regulation, light-emitting diodes (LEDs), photodiodes, Schottky diodes with low forward voltage drop, and varactor diodes used as voltage-controlled capacitors. Diodes are fundamental to power supplies, radio demodulation, signal clipping, and logic in digital electronics.
