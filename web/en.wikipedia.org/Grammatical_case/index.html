Grammatical case

Case is a special grammatical category of a noun, pronoun, adjective, participle or numeral whose value reflects the grammatical function the word performs in a phrase, clause, or sentence. In some languages, nouns, pronouns, adjectives, determiners, participles, prepositions, numerals, articles and their modifiers take different inflected forms depending on their case. Common cases include the nominative (subject), accusative (direct object), genitive (possession), and dative (indirect object).

Overview

As a language evolves, cases can merge or disappear — a process called syncretism. Ancient Greek had five cases; modern Greek retains four. Latin had six (plus vestiges of a seventh, the locative); its Romance descendants lost noun case almost entirely, keeping case distinctions only in pronouns. English retains case only in pronouns (I/me/my, he/him/his), while languages such as Russian (six), Finnish (fifteen), and Hungarian maintain rich case systems.

Common cases

  • Nominative — the subject of a verb.
  • Accusative — the direct object of a transitive verb.
  • Genitive — possession or relation ("the book of Mary").
  • Dative — the indirect object, the recipient of an action.
  • Ablative — movement away from, or means/source, in Latin.
  • Vocative — direct address.
  • Locative and instrumental — place, and means/instrument.

Alignment

Case marking interacts with morphosyntactic alignment. In nominative–accusative languages the subject is nominative and the object accusative; in ergative–absolutive languages such as Basque, the subject of a transitive verb takes the ergative case while the object and intransitive subject share the absolutive.
