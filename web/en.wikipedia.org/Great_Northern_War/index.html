The Great Northern War (1700–1721) was a conflict in which a coalition led by the Tsardom of Russia successfully contested the supremacy of the Swedish Empire in Northern, Central and Eastern Europe. The initial leaders of the anti-Swedish alliance were Peter I of Russia, Frederick IV of Denmark–Norway and Augustus II the Strong of Saxony–Poland. The war ended with the defeat of Sweden, the rise of Russia as a great power, and the founding of Saint Petersburg on territory conquered from Sweden.

Background

Between 1560 and 1658 Sweden built a Baltic empire (Dominium maris baltici) encompassing Finland, Estonia, Livonia, Ingria, and parts of northern Germany. Its neighbors resented and coveted these gains. When the teenage Charles XII ascended the throne in 1697, Russia, Denmark–Norway and Saxony formed a secret alliance to partition the Swedish empire.

Charles XII's early victories

The coalition's opening attacks miscarried spectacularly. Charles knocked Denmark out of the war within months (Peace of Travendal, 1700) and then crushed Peter's much larger army at Narva (November 1700), one of the most lopsided victories of the era. Turning south, he spent six years in Poland, deposing Augustus II and installing Stanisław Leszczyński (Treaty of Altranstädt, 1706). Meanwhile, Peter used the respite to rebuild his army on Western lines and founded Saint Petersburg (1703) in conquered Ingria.

Turning point: Poltava

In 1708 Charles invaded Russia. Scorched-earth tactics, a brutal winter (the Great Frost of 1709), and the diversion of his supply column at Lesnaya starved the Swedish army. At the Battle of Poltava (8 July 1709, NS), Peter destroyed the Swedish field army; the remnants surrendered at Perevolochna. Charles fled to Ottoman exile at Bender, urging the Porte into a brief war with Russia. Poltava is conventionally regarded as the decisive turning point and the beginning of Sweden's decline as a great power.

End of the war

Denmark and Saxony rejoined the war; Hanover and Prussia later entered to claim Swedish German possessions. Charles, returning to Sweden in 1714, was killed besieging Fredriksten in Norway in 1718 — by enemy fire or, as has been speculated ever since, by assassination. His sister Ulrika Eleonora and then Frederick I made peace piecemeal: with Hanover and Prussia (Stockholm, 1719–20), Denmark (Frederiksborg, 1720), and finally Russia.

Consequences

By the Treaty of Nystad (1721), Russia gained Estonia, Livonia, Ingria and part of Karelia — its long-sought Baltic window — while Sweden retained Finland. Peter proclaimed the Russian Empire and took the title Emperor. Sweden's Age of Greatness (stormaktstiden) was over; Russia dominated the Baltic thereafter.
