Indian indenture system

Period1834–1917 (recruitment ended)
Labourers transportedc. 1.2–1.6 million
Main destinationsMauritius, British Guiana, Trinidad, Natal, Fiji, Suriname

The Indian indenture system was a system of bonded labour under which more than a million Indians were transported to European colonies across the Caribbean, Africa, Southeast Asia and the Pacific between the 1830s and early 20th century, largely to work on sugar plantations after the abolition of slavery.

Origins

After Britain abolished slavery in 1834, planters sought a new labour supply. The first shipments reached Mauritius in 1834 and British Guiana in 1838. Labourers signed agreements (girmit) typically for five years, with a promise of wages and, eventually, a return passage — conditions often misrepresented by recruiters.

Conditions

Workers faced harsh discipline, penal sanctions for absence, low wages and poor housing; historians have debated the system's closeness to slavery, with Hugh Tinker famously calling it "a new form of slavery". Emigration depots at Calcutta and Madras processed recruits, and voyages aboard sailing ships and later steamers could be deadly.

End and legacy

Indian nationalist pressure, including campaigns by Mahatma Gandhi in South Africa and C. F. Andrews's reports from Fiji, led to recruitment being halted in 1917 and the system abolished by the early 1920s. Descendants of the girmitiyas form major communities in Mauritius, Guyana, Trinidad and Tobago, Suriname, South Africa and Fiji, shaping those societies' cultures, cuisines and politics.
