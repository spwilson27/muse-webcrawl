The free software movement is a social and political movement that advocates for software users' freedom to run, study, modify, and share software. Launched by Richard Stallman with the GNU Project in 1983, it holds that software should respect users' freedom and community, in contrast to proprietary software, which the movement regards as an unjust restriction on users.

The four freedoms

The movement defines free software by four essential freedoms: (0) to run the program for any purpose; (1) to study how it works and change it, requiring access to source code; (2) to redistribute copies; and (3) to distribute modified versions. "Free" refers to liberty, not price — often summarized as "free as in free speech, not free beer".

History

Stallman announced the GNU Project in 1983 to build a complete free operating system, published the GNU Manifesto in 1985, and founded the Free Software Foundation the same year. The GNU General Public License (1989) introduced "copyleft", using copyright law to guarantee that derivative works remain free. With Linus Torvalds' Linux kernel (1991), the GNU/Linux system became a complete free operating system. In 1998, part of the community rebranded similar practices as "open source", emphasizing practical benefits over the movement's ethical framing — a distinction the free software movement continues to stress.

Impact

Free software now underpins much of the internet's infrastructure, and its philosophy has influenced related movements for free culture, open access, and open hardware.
