West African Vodun

A Vodun shrine in Benin, the heartland of the religion.

West African Vodun (also spelled Vodon, Vodoun, Vodou, Vudu, or Voodoo) is a religion practiced by the Gbe peoples (Fon, Ewe, Aja, Mina) of Benin, Togo, and Ghana, and by related communities in southwestern Nigeria. The word vodun derives from the Fon-Gbe languages and means a spirit, deity, or sacred power.

Vodun centers on veneration of a pantheon of spirits (vodun) subordinate to a remote creator, Mawu-Lisa, and on maintaining harmonious relations between the living, the ancestors, and the spirit world through offerings, divination (notably Ifá/Fa), initiation, and ceremony. Deities include Legba, guardian of crossroads and gateways; Sakpata, associated with the earth and smallpox; Xevioso, of thunder; and Mami Wata, spirit of the waters.

History and diaspora

Centered historically on the kingdom of Dahomey, Vodun was suppressed at times by colonial and post-independence governments, but in 1992 Benin formally recognized it as an official religion, and 10 January is a national Vodun Day festival, centered on Ouidah. Through the Atlantic slave trade, Vodun was carried to the Americas, where it gave rise to Haitian Vodou, Louisiana Voodoo, and influenced Cuban Santería and Brazilian Candomblé — religions often distorted in popular culture into sensational caricatures.

Practice

Priests (vodunon) and priestesses lead ceremonies involving drumming, dance, song, and spirit possession, in which a vodun is believed to mount and speak through a devotee. Shrines accumulate offerings and power objects; the faith also encompasses ancestor veneration through Egungun masquerades.
