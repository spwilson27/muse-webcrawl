Liberal feminism

Liberal feminism is a tradition of feminism that emphasizes the equality of men and women through political and legal reform within the framework of liberal democracy. It holds that gender inequality results chiefly from legal restrictions and social customs that deny women equal opportunity.

History

The tradition traces its roots to Mary Wollstonecraft's A Vindication of the Rights of Woman (1792) and John Stuart Mill's The Subjection of Women (1869). Liberal feminists led the campaigns for women's education, property rights, and suffrage in the 19th and early 20th centuries, and later for equal pay, reproductive rights, and anti-discrimination law.

Principles

Liberal feminism works within existing institutions, seeking equal rights under the law, equal access to education and employment, and the removal of formal barriers to women's advancement. Organizations such as the National Organization for Women (founded 1966) exemplify this approach, campaigning for measures such as the Equal Rights Amendment in the United States.

Criticism

Radical, socialist, and intersectional feminists have criticized liberal feminism for focusing on formal equality while neglecting deeper structural inequalities of class, race, and economic power. Defenders reply that legal and political equality is a necessary foundation for broader social change.
