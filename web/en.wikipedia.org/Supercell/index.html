Supercell

A supercell is a thunderstorm characterized by the presence of a mesocyclone: a deep, persistently rotating updraft. For this reason, these storms are sometimes called rotating thunderstorms. Supercells are the least common type of thunderstorm but are the most likely to produce severe weather, including large hail, damaging winds, and strong to violent tornadoes.

Characteristics

Supercells form in environments with strong vertical wind shear, instability, and moisture. The rotating updraft tilts and stretches horizontal vorticity into the vertical, allowing the storm to sustain itself for hours and travel long distances. On radar, a classic supercell often shows a hook echo. Visual features include a rain-free base, a wall cloud beneath the updraft, and a large anvil. Types include classic, high-precipitation (HP), and low-precipitation (LP) supercells, as well as miniature supercells.

Hazards

Supercells are responsible for the majority of strong and violent (EF2+) tornadoes, hail exceeding 5 cm in diameter, and extreme straight-line winds. They occur worldwide but are most frequent in the Great Plains of the United States — "Tornado Alley" — as well as in Argentina's Pampas, Bangladesh, Australia, and parts of Europe and South Africa.
