On the Origin of Species (or, more completely, On the Origin of Species by Means of Natural Selection, or the Preservation of Favoured Races in the Struggle for Life) is a work of scientific literature by Charles Darwin that is considered to be the foundation of evolutionary biology. It was published on 24 November 1859. Darwin's book introduced the scientific theory that populations evolve over the course of generations through a process of natural selection, although Lamarckism was also included as a mechanism of lesser importance.

The book presented a body of evidence that the diversity of life arose by common descent through a branching pattern of evolution. Darwin included evidence that he had collected on the Beagle expedition in the 1830s and his subsequent findings related to geology, geographical distribution, morphology, embryology and palaeontology.

Background

Darwin's ideas developed during the second voyage of HMS Beagle (1831–1836), where observations of finches and tortoises in the Galápagos Islands, and of fossils in South America, led him to question the fixity of species. On his return he began developing his theory of natural selection, influenced by Thomas Malthus's Essay on the Principle of Population. He worked on the theory for two decades, but moved to publish quickly in 1858 when Alfred Russel Wallace sent him an essay describing the same idea, prompting a joint presentation to the Linnean Society.

Content

The book opens with chapters on variation under domestication and in nature, then presents the struggle for existence and natural selection. Later chapters address difficulties of the theory (including the imperfection of the geological record), instinct, hybridism, the geological succession of organic beings, geographical distribution, and morphology, embryology and rudimentary organs. Darwin concluded by envisaging all life descending, with modification, from a common ancestor: "There is grandeur in this view of life..." The book contains only one illustration — a branching diagram of divergence, the famous "tree of life".

Reception

The first edition of 1,250 copies sold out on the day of publication. The book generated extensive scientific, philosophical and theological debate. Six editions appeared in Darwin's lifetime (the last in 1872), with the word "evolution" first appearing in the sixth edition. By the 1870s the scientific community and much of the general public had accepted evolution as a fact, although natural selection as its primary mechanism achieved broad acceptance only with the modern evolutionary synthesis of the 1930s–1950s.
