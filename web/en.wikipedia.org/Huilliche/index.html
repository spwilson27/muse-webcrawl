Huilliche people

The Huilliche (Spanish: Huilliche, Huiliches or Veliches) are an Indigenous people of southern Chile and neighbouring areas of Argentina. Together with the Mapuche and the Picunche, they belong to the broader Mapuche cultural and linguistic group, though they speak their own variety known as Huilliche or Chesungun, a dialect — or by some classifications a separate language — of Mapudungun.

Historically the Huilliche inhabited the territory south of the Toltén River, through the Valdivian and Osorno regions to the Chiloé Archipelago and the Reloncaví Sound. Their traditional economy combined agriculture — notably potatoes, of which the Chiloé area is a center of origin — with fishing, shellfish gathering, and livestock.

History

Unlike the Mapuche of Araucanía, the Huilliche were incorporated into the Spanish colonial system relatively early, though the destruction of the seven Spanish cities around 1600 pushed the frontier north and left much Huilliche territory outside direct Spanish control for two centuries. In 1793 the Treaty of Las Canoas (Parlamento de Negrete) formalized relations with the Spanish Crown. During the 19th and 20th centuries Huilliche communities lost much of their land to Chilean and foreign settlers, a process whose effects are still contested in land claims today.

Culture

Huilliche culture shares many features with Mapuche culture, including the machi (shaman), the nguillatún ceremony, and the cuisines of the south. In Chiloé, Huilliche traditions blended with Spanish colonial influences to form the distinctive Chilote culture of wooden churches, mythology, and maritime livelihoods.
