Video nasty

A video nasty was a colloquial term popularized by the British press and campaigners in the early 1980s for a number of low-budget horror films released on video cassette that were widely alleged to be corrupting, especially to children. The resulting moral panic led to the passage of the Video Recordings Act 1984.

[Image: A wall of lurid 1980s horror videocassette covers in a British video shop.]

Background

In the early years of home video, films could be released on cassette in the UK without the classification required for cinema releases. Campaigners such as Mary Whitehouse pressed for prosecution under the Obscene Publications Act. The Director of Public Prosecutions eventually issued a list of 72 titles liable for prosecution — the famous "video nasties" list, which included films such as Cannibal Holocaust, The Evil Dead, and I Spit on Your Grave.

Legacy

The Act introduced compulsory BBFC classification of home video. Many listed titles have since been released uncut, and the episode is now studied as a classic moral panic.
