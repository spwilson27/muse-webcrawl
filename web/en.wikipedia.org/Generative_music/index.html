Generative music

Generative music
Term popularised byBrian Eno (1990s)
Genre / approachMusic created by a system or process, ever-different and changing
Key worksMusic for Airports (1978, tape-loop systems); In C (1964); Koan software (1990s)
Related fieldsAmbient music, algorithmic composition, systems music

Generative music is a term popularised by Brian Eno to describe music that is ever-different and changing, and that is created by a system. Rather than being fixed on a recording or score in a single definitive form, a generative piece consists of rules, processes or software from which performances unfold in real time, sometimes never repeating exactly.

Origins

Precursors include Mozart's musical dice game (Musikalisches Würfelspiel), in which pre-composed fragments are assembled by dice rolls; the chance procedures of John Cage; and the minimal systems pieces of Steve Reich and Terry Riley — Riley's In C (1964) lets performers advance through 53 short modules at their own pace, producing an unrepeatable result. Eno's Discreet Music (1975) and Ambient 1: Music for Airports (1978) used unsynchronised tape loops of different lengths that drift in and out of phase, generating an ever-changing texture from simple materials.

Software era

In the 1990s Eno collaborated with the company SSEYO on the Koan generative music engine, releasing works such as Generative Music 1 (1996) that play differently on every listening. He compared the composer of generative works to a gardener who plants seeds and tends the conditions rather than building the finished structure directly. Later examples include Eno's apps Bloom (2008, with Peter Chilvers) and Reflection (2017), which generates an endless, seasonally varying version of a single piece.

Contemporary practice

Today generative techniques span modular-synthesis patches, live coding, cellular automata, Markov models and machine-learning systems, and are used in installations, games (with adaptive scores), functional music for focus and sleep, and contemporary composition. Debates continue over authorship and creativity where systems — and, more recently, AI models — produce the surface of the music.
