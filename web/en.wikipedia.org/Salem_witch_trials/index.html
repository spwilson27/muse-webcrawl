The Salem witch trials were a series of hearings and prosecutions of people accused of witchcraft in colonial Massachusetts between February 1692 and May 1693. More than 200 people were accused; thirty were found guilty, nineteen of whom were executed by hanging, while Giles Corey was pressed to death for refusing to plead. It remains the deadliest witch hunt in colonial North American history.

Background

The trials took place amid the anxieties of a frontier society traumatised by King William's War, religious conflict within Salem Village (now Danvers), and popular belief in the Devil's active presence. In January 1692, the daughter and niece of the Reverend Samuel Parris began suffering fits, screaming and contortions; doctors suggested bewitchment.

The trials

The first accused were Sarah Good, Sarah Osborne and the enslaved woman Tituba, who confessed and named others. Accusations spread across Essex County. Governor William Phips established a special Court of Oyer and Terminer, which admitted "spectral evidence" — testimony that an accused person's spirit had appeared to the victim. Executions began in June 1692 on Gallows Hill in Salem Town. The accused included respected church members and even a former minister, George Burroughs.

End and aftermath

As accusations reached the colony's elite, public opinion turned. Phips dissolved the court in October 1692; its successor largely acquitted or reprieved the remaining prisoners, and the hysteria ended by May 1693. In 1697 Massachusetts held a day of fasting and repentance; jurors and a judge publicly apologised. Over the following decades the colony reversed attainders and paid restitution to victims' families. In 2001 the last victims were formally exonerated.

Legacy

"Witch hunt" has entered the language as a metaphor for baseless persecution, most famously applied to McCarthyism through Arthur Miller's 1953 play The Crucible. Historians continue to debate the causes, from ergot poisoning (a theory now largely discounted) to social factionalism and the psychology of persecution.
