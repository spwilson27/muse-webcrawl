Asymptotic analysis

In mathematical analysis, asymptotic analysis, also known as asymptotics, is a method of describing limiting behaviour. It studies how functions behave as an argument tends toward a limit, usually infinity, in order to obtain simpler approximations that become arbitrarily accurate in the limit. Asymptotic results are expressed using notation such as the Bachmann–Landau family — big O, little o, Θ, Ω and ω.

Notation

Writing f(x) ∼ g(x) means f/g → 1 as x → ∞. Big O notation, f(x) = O(g(x)), states that |f| is eventually bounded by a constant multiple of g. In computer science the same notation classifies algorithm complexity, such as O(n log n) sorting or O(2ⁿ) exponential algorithms.

Asymptotic expansions

An asymptotic expansion approximates a function by a partial sum of a (often divergent) series whose first few terms give an excellent approximation for large arguments. Adding more terms improves accuracy only up to an optimal point, after which the series diverges — a subtlety noted by Poincaré, who formalised the concept in 1886.

Examples

Classic results include Stirling's approximation n! ∼ √(2πn)(n/e)ⁿ; the prime number theorem π(x) ∼ x / ln x; the Bessel function asymptotics used in wave problems; and the expansion of the exponential integral.

Applications

Asymptotic methods are essential in applied mathematics (boundary-layer theory, WKB approximation), statistics (large-sample theory), analytic number theory, combinatorics (singularity analysis), and the analysis of algorithms.
