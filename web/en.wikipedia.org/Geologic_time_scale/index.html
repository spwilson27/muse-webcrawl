The geologic time scale (GTS) is a system of chronological dating that classifies geological strata (stratigraphy) in time. It is used by geologists, palaeontologists, and other Earth scientists to describe the timing and relationships of events in geologic history. The time scale is hierarchically divided into eons, eras, periods, epochs and ages, and spans the 4.54 billion years since Earth's formation.

Structure

The Phanerozoic Eon ("visible life", 541 million years ago to the present) is divided into the Paleozoic, Mesozoic and Cenozoic eras — "old", "middle" and "new life" respectively — whose boundaries are marked by major changes in the fossil record, including the great mass extinctions. The immense earlier span, from Earth's formation to the Cambrian, is grouped as Precambrian time, divided into the Hadean, Archaean and Proterozoic eons.

How boundaries are defined

Modern boundaries are fixed at Global Stratotype Sections and Points (GSSPs) — physical reference points in rock sections, informally called "golden spikes" — and dated absolutely using radiometric methods such as uranium–lead and argon–argon dating. The International Chronostratigraphic Chart is periodically revised by the International Commission on Stratigraphy.

History of the scale

The relative scale was assembled in the 19th century from the work of William Smith, Georges Cuvier, Charles Lyell and others, who ordered rock layers by superposition and fossil content long before absolute ages were known. Radiometric dating in the 20th century attached numerical ages to the framework.
