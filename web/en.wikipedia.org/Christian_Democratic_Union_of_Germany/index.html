Christian Democratic Union of Germany

The Christian Democratic Union of Germany (German: Christlich Demokratische Union Deutschlands, CDU) is a Christian democratic and conservative political party in Germany. Founded in 1945 after the Second World War, it has been one of the country's two major parties for most of the Federal Republic's history and supplied chancellors including Konrad Adenauer, Ludwig Erhard, Helmut Kohl, and Angela Merkel. It contests elections in all German states except Bavaria, where its sister party, the Christian Social Union (CSU), operates; together they form the CDU/CSU grouping, commonly called "the Union," in the Bundestag.

History

The CDU emerged in 1945 from interdenominational efforts to unite Catholic and Protestant voters, drawing on traditions of the former Centre Party and conservative parties. Under Konrad Adenauer, its first chairman and the Federal Republic's first chancellor (1949–1963), the party anchored West Germany in NATO and the European Communities and presided over the "economic miracle" guided by Ludwig Erhard's social market economy.

In government

Helmut Kohl led the party back to power in 1982 and oversaw German reunification in 1990. After defeat in 1998, Angela Merkel became leader in 2000 and chancellor in 2005, heading governments — mostly "grand coalitions" with the Social Democrats — for sixteen years until 2021. The party then spent a period in opposition before returning to government; it has governed for the majority of the Federal Republic's existence and is often described as a Volkspartei, a broad "people's party."

Positions

The CDU combines support for the social market economy, fiscal restraint, and European integration with socially conservative-to-moderate positions that have liberalized over time, particularly under Merkel. It maintains a strict separation from the far-right Alternative for Germany, known as the "firewall" policy.
