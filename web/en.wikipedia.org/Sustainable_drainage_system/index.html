Sustainable drainage system

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sustainable drainage systems (also known as SuDS, SUDS, or sustainable urban drainage systems) are a collection of water management practices that aim to align modern drainage systems with natural water processes and are part of a larger green infrastructure strategy. SuDS efforts make urban drainage systems more compatible with components of the natural water cycle such as storm surge overflows of water, soil percolation, and bio-filtration. These efforts hope to mitigate the effect human development has had (or may have) on the natural water cycle, particularly in surface runoff and water effluent quality.

Background

Conventional drainage conveys stormwater away as quickly as possible through pipes to watercourses, which can cause downstream flooding, carry pollutants (oils, heavy metals, sediments) into rivers, and deprive groundwater of recharge. SuDS instead manage rainfall close to where it falls — following the principles of managing quantity, quality, amenity, and biodiversity, sometimes called the "four pillars" of SuDS design in the United Kingdom. They gained prominence following major floods in the UK in 2007 and subsequent legislation and guidance such as the Flood and Water Management Act 2010 and the CIRIA SuDS Manual. Analogous approaches include low-impact development (LID) in North America and Water Sensitive Urban Design (WSUD) in Australia.

Management train

SuDS components are combined in a "management train", a sequence of measures that progressively treat runoff:

  • Source control: green roofs, rainwater harvesting, permeable paving, and rain gardens on individual plots;
  • Site control: swales, filter strips, infiltration basins, and bioretention areas serving a development;
  • Regional control: detention basins, retention ponds, and constructed wetlands handling runoff from larger areas.

Benefits

Properly designed SuDS reduce peak flows and flood risk, improve water quality through sedimentation, filtration, and biological processes, recharge groundwater, and provide habitat and attractive open space. They are often cheaper in whole-life terms than conventional piped systems and can increase the resilience of developments to climate change by storing water during extreme rainfall events.

Challenges

Barriers to adoption have included uncertainty over long-term maintenance responsibilities, land-take for surface features, and the need for cooperation among developers, water companies, and local authorities; several jurisdictions now require SuDS approval and adoption arrangements for new developments.
