Remittance

Remittance flows are a major source of external finance for many developing countries.

A remittance is a non-commercial transfer of money by a foreign worker, a member of a diaspora community, or a person with familial ties abroad, for household income in their home country or locality of origin. Money sent home by migrant workers competes with international aid as one of the largest financial inflows to developing countries. Workers' remittances are a significant part of international capital flows, especially with regard to labour-exporting countries.

Remittances play an increasingly large role in the economies of many countries. They contribute to economic growth and to the livelihoods of those countries' residents. According to the World Bank, remittance flows to low- and middle-income countries reached an estimated $669 billion in 2023. The largest recipients in absolute terms have long included India, Mexico, China, the Philippines, and Egypt, while smaller economies such as Tonga, Tajikistan, and Lebanon receive the largest shares relative to gross domestic product.

History

Remittances have existed for centuries in various forms, but they expanded rapidly with the growth of international labour migration in the late twentieth century and with the spread of money-transfer operators, banking networks, and later mobile money services. The costs of sending remittances have historically been high — often exceeding 6 per cent of the amount sent on average — prompting international targets, such as Sustainable Development Goal 10.c, to reduce transaction costs to less than 3 per cent.

Economic effects

Remittances can reduce poverty, finance education and health care, smooth consumption during economic shocks, and provide capital for small businesses. Critics note that heavy dependence on remittances may discourage labour-force participation, appreciate the real exchange rate (a form of Dutch disease), and expose households to fluctuations in the economies where migrants work. Because remittances tend to remain stable or even rise during crises in the home country, economists often describe them as countercyclical.
