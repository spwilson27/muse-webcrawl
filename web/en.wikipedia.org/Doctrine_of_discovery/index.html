Doctrine of discovery

The doctrine of discovery is a legal concept stating that a government acquires title to the lands it "discovers" and the dominion and political capacity of the original inhabitants are diminished to mere occupancy with their title subject to alienation only to the sovereign discovering nation. The doctrine was applied by European colonial powers from the Age of Discovery onward to claim sovereignty over territories inhabited by Indigenous peoples throughout the Americas, Africa, Asia and the Pacific, and it was later adopted into the law of the successor nations, notably the United States.

Origins

The doctrine traces to papal bulls of the fifteenth century, notably Pope Nicholas V's Romanus Pontifex (1455) and Alexander VI's Inter caetera (1493), which purported to grant Portugal and Spain possession of lands "discovered" and to be discovered beyond Europe, and to Christian dominion over the peoples encountered. European states developed the practice of "discovery" as a mode of acquiring title among themselves, treating the discovery, occupation and treaty-making of colonising powers as determinative of sovereignty, while Indigenous peoples were viewed as occupants with limited rights of their own.

United States law

In the United States, the doctrine was formally adopted by the Supreme Court in Johnson v. M'Intosh (1823), in which Chief Justice John Marshall held that European "discovery" gave the discovering nation ultimate title to the land, subject to the Indigenous peoples' right of occupancy; that title could be extinguished only by the federal government. The doctrine underpins the federal law of Indian title and has been invoked in later cases. Similar reasoning shaped colonial and post-colonial law in Canada, Australia (overturned in part by Mabo v Queensland in 1992, which rejected terra nullius) and New Zealand.

Criticism and repudiation

The doctrine of discovery has been widely criticised by Indigenous peoples, scholars and religious bodies as a racist and colonialist fiction that treated non-Christian inhabitants as lesser rights-holders and legitimised dispossession. The United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (2007) implicitly rejected doctrines of discovery. In March 2023, the Vatican formally repudiated the doctrine of discovery, stating that the fifteenth-century papal bulls "did not adequately reflect the equal dignity and rights of indigenous peoples" and were manipulated for political purposes.
