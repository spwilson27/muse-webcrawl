Shor's algorithm

Inventor: Peter Shor (1994)
Problem: Integer factorisation
Complexity: Polynomial time on a quantum computer
Impact: Threatens RSA encryption

Shor's algorithm is a quantum algorithm for finding the prime factors of an integer. It was devised in 1994 by the mathematician Peter Shor and runs in polynomial time — dramatically faster than the best known classical factorisation algorithms, whose running time grows super-polynomially with the size of the number.

How it works

The algorithm reduces factorisation to finding the period of a modular exponential function. A quantum computer evaluates this function in superposition and applies the quantum Fourier transform to extract the period efficiently; classical post-processing (continued fractions and greatest common divisors) then yields the factors.

Significance

Because the security of the widely used RSA cryptosystem rests on the difficulty of factoring large numbers, a sufficiently large fault-tolerant quantum computer running Shor's algorithm could break much of today's public-key cryptography. This prospect is a major motivation for the development of post-quantum cryptography. Experimental demonstrations have so far factored only small numbers.
