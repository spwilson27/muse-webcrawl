Apportionment

Apportionment is the process by which seats in a legislative body, or resources more generally, are distributed among constituencies in proportion to population or some other measure. The term is most familiar in the context of allocating seats in the United States House of Representatives among the states after each decennial census, and in the apportionment of European Parliament seats among member states.

Methods

Numerous mathematical methods have been devised. Largest remainder methods allocate seats according to quotas, while divisor methods (highest averages methods) such as the Huntington–Hill method used in the United States, the D'Hondt method, and the Sainte-Laguë method divide populations successively by a sequence of divisors. Each method embodies trade-offs among fairness criteria, and results such as the Balinski–Young theorem show that no method can satisfy all reasonable criteria simultaneously.

Paradoxes

Apportionment is subject to paradoxes, including the Alabama paradox (a state losing a seat when the house size increases), the population paradox, and the new state paradox. These phenomena were observed in practice in the history of United States congressional apportionment.
