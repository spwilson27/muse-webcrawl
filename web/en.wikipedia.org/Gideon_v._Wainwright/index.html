Clarence Gideon's handwritten petition reached the U.S. Supreme Court.

Gideon v. Wainwright, 372 U.S. 335 (1963), is a landmark decision of the Supreme Court of the United States holding that the Sixth Amendment's guarantee of the right to counsel is a fundamental right, binding on the states through the Fourteenth Amendment, and that states must provide an attorney to criminal defendants who cannot afford one in felony cases.

Background

Clarence Earl Gideon was charged in Florida with breaking and entering with intent to commit petty larceny, a felony. At his 1961 trial he asked the court to appoint him a lawyer because he was too poor to hire one. The judge refused, citing state law, and Gideon represented himself and was convicted. From prison, Gideon filed a handwritten in forma pauperis petition for habeas corpus with the U.S. Supreme Court, arguing his conviction violated his constitutional rights. The Court agreed to hear the case and appointed a prominent Washington attorney — the future Supreme Court justice Abe Fortas — to represent him.

Decision

In a unanimous opinion by Justice Hugo Black, the Court held that the Sixth Amendment right to counsel is "fundamental and essential to a fair trial," and therefore obligatory upon the states. The ruling expressly overruled Betts v. Brady (1942), which had required appointed counsel only in "special circumstances." Black reasoned that lawyers in criminal courts are "necessities, not luxuries," and that a fair trial cannot be assured where a poor defendant is left to face the prosecution alone.

Aftermath

Gideon received a new trial, this time with court-appointed counsel W. Fred Turner, and was acquitted by a jury after only an hour of deliberation. The decision led to the release or retrial of thousands of indigent prisoners in Florida and other states, and spurred the growth of public defender systems across the United States. The right to appointed counsel was later extended to misdemeanours carrying possible imprisonment (Argersinger v. Hamlin, 1972) and to juveniles (In re Gault, 1967). Anthony Lewis's book Gideon's Trumpet (1964) popularized the case.
