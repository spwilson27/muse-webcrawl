Speed of sound

The speed of sound is the distance travelled per unit of time by a sound wave as it propagates through an elastic medium. At 20 °C (68 °F), the speed of sound in air is about 343 metres per second (1,125 ft/s; 1,235 km/h; 767 mph; 667 kn), or one kilometre in 2.91 s or one mile in 4.69 s. It depends strongly on the temperature as well as the medium through which a sound wave is propagating.

In air

For dry air, the speed increases with temperature, approximately as c ≈ 331.3 + 0.606·T m/s, where T is the temperature in degrees Celsius. Humidity has a small but measurable effect. Isaac Newton first computed the speed of sound in his Principia (1687), underestimating it because he assumed isothermal compression; Laplace later corrected the calculation by accounting for adiabatic heating.

In other media

Sound travels much faster in liquids and solids: about 1,480 m/s in fresh water and roughly 5,960 m/s in steel, because these media are far stiffer.

Mach number

The ratio of an object's speed to the speed of sound in the surrounding medium is its Mach number. Aircraft exceeding Mach 1 fly supersonically; Chuck Yeager first broke the sound barrier in level flight in 1947.
