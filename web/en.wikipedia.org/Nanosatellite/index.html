Nanosatellite

A nanosatellite, or nanosat, is an artificial satellite with a wet mass between 1 and 10 kilograms (2.2 and 22 lb). Nanosatellites are part of the broader family of small satellites, which also includes microsatellites (10–100 kg), picosatellites (0.1–1 kg) and femtosatellites (below 0.1 kg). Advances in miniaturised electronics have made nanosatellites a cheap route to space for universities, startups and government agencies.

CubeSats

The most common nanosatellite format is the CubeSat, built from standardised 10 cm cube units (1U, 2U, 3U, 6U and larger). The standard, developed in 1999 by California Polytechnic State University and Stanford University, allows off-the-shelf components and rideshare launches as secondary payloads. Thousands of CubeSats have been launched for Earth observation, communications, technology demonstration and education, including commercial constellations imaging the Earth daily.

Applications and concerns

Applications include remote sensing, IoT data relay, scientific experiments and amateur radio. The rapid growth of nanosatellite launches has raised concerns about orbital debris and collision risk, since most nanosatellites lack propulsion and cannot deorbit actively; regulators increasingly require disposal plans.
