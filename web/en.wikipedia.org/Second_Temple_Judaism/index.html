Second Temple Judaism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Second Temple, rebuilt by Herod the Great, stood at the center of Jewish religious life.

Second Temple Judaism is the Jewish religion as it developed during the Second Temple period, which began with the construction of the Second Temple around 516 BCE and ended with the Roman siege of Jerusalem and the destruction of the temple in 70 CE. The period encompasses the Persian, Hellenistic, Hasmonean, and early Roman eras of Jewish history.

History

After the Babylonian exile, returning Jews rebuilt the Temple in Jerusalem under Persian rule. Alexander the Great's conquests brought Judea into the Hellenistic world, provoking tension between Hellenizing Jews and traditionalists that erupted in the Maccabean Revolt (167–160 BCE) against the Seleucid Empire and led to the independent Hasmonean kingdom. Rome under Pompey intervened in 63 BCE, and Herod the Great later rebuilt the Temple on a grand scale. The destruction of the Temple in 70 CE by Titus during the First Jewish–Roman War ended the sacrificial cult and transformed Judaism.

Religious life and groups

Worship centered on Temple sacrifice, pilgrimage festivals, Torah study, and synagogue prayer. Distinct parties emerged: the Sadducees, aristocratic priests who accepted only the written Torah; the Pharisees, whose oral law and belief in resurrection shaped later Rabbinic Judaism; the Essenes, a separatist movement associated with the Dead Sea Scrolls; and Zealots, who advocated armed resistance to Rome. Apocalyptic literature — expectations of a messiah, final judgment, and resurrection — flourished in this period.

Legacy

Second Temple Judaism is the matrix from which both Rabbinic Judaism and early Christianity emerged. The canon of the Hebrew Bible was largely settled in this period, and its debates over law, purity, and eschatology profoundly influenced the religious landscape of the ancient world.
