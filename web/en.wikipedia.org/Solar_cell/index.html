A solar cell (also called a photovoltaic cell) is an electronic device that converts the energy of light directly into electricity by the photovoltaic effect. Solar cells are the building blocks of solar panels (photovoltaic modules). Most commercial cells are made from crystalline silicon; other technologies use thin films of cadmium telluride or copper indium gallium selenide (CIGS), or multi-junction stacks for space applications.

Working principle

When photons strike a semiconductor such as silicon, they can excite electrons across the band gap, creating electron–hole pairs. The internal electric field of a p–n junction separates the charges, producing a voltage and, in a closed circuit, a current. The maximum theoretical efficiency for a single-junction cell under sunlight is given by the Shockley–Queisser limit, about 33.7%.

History

The photovoltaic effect was observed by Edmond Becquerel in 1839. The first practical silicon solar cell, with about 6% efficiency, was demonstrated at Bell Laboratories in 1954 by Daryl Chapin, Calvin Fuller, and Gerald Pearson. Early uses were in space satellites, beginning with Vanguard 1 in 1958. Falling manufacturing costs since the 2000s have made photovoltaics one of the cheapest sources of new electricity generation.

Applications

Solar cells power utility-scale solar farms, rooftop systems, calculators, satellites, and remote installations. Record research cells using perovskite–silicon tandems have exceeded 33% efficiency.
