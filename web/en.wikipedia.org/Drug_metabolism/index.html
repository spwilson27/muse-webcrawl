Drug metabolism

Drug metabolism is the metabolic breakdown of drugs by living organisms, usually through specialized enzymatic systems. More generally, xenobiotic metabolism (from the Greek xenos "stranger" and biotic "related to living beings") is the set of metabolic pathways that modify the chemical structure of xenobiotics, which are chemical compounds foreign to an organism's normal biochemistry, such as any drug or poison. These pathways are a form of biotransformation present in all major groups of organisms and are considered to be of ancient origin. These reactions often act to detoxify poisonous compounds (although in some cases the intermediates in xenobiotic metabolism can themselves cause toxic effects).

The study of drug metabolism is called pharmacokinetics. Metabolism usually converts lipid-soluble chemical compounds into more readily excreted polar products, which can be eliminated through urine, bile, or sweat.

Phases of detoxification

The metabolism of a drug is conventionally divided into three phases. In phase I, enzymes such as cytochrome P450 oxidases introduce reactive or polar groups into xenobiotics. These modified compounds are then conjugated to polar compounds in phase II reactions, catalysed by enzymes such as glutathione S-transferases. Finally, in phase III, the conjugated xenobiotics may be further processed before being recognised by efflux transporters and pumped out of cells.

Phase I reactions

Phase I reactions include oxidation, reduction, and hydrolysis. The most important enzyme system of phase I is cytochrome P450, a superfamily of isozymes localised in the smooth endoplasmic reticulum of hepatocytes. Microsomal mixed-function oxidases use NADPH and molecular oxygen to oxidise substrates. Oxidation usually makes the compound more water-soluble and may create a site for conjugation, but it can also activate a harmless prodrug or produce a reactive, toxic metabolite; for example, the analgesic paracetamol (acetaminophen) is partly converted to the hepatotoxic metabolite NAPQI.

Phase II reactions

Phase II (conjugation) reactions attach a large polar group to the drug or its phase I metabolite, greatly increasing water solubility. Common conjugations include glucuronidation (the most frequent, catalysed by UDP-glucuronosyltransferases), sulfation, glutathione conjugation, acetylation, and methylation. Glucuronidation of morphine, for instance, produces morphine-6-glucuronide, which is a more potent analgesic than morphine itself.

Cytochrome P450

Humans have 57 genes coding for various cytochrome P450 enzymes. CYP enzymes account for roughly 75% of total drug metabolism. The most important isoforms in human drug metabolism include CYP3A4, CYP2D6, CYP2C9, CYP2C19, CYP1A2, and CYP2E1. Many drugs inhibit or induce these enzymes, producing clinically important drug–drug interactions; CYP2D6 is also highly polymorphic, dividing populations into poor, intermediate, extensive, and ultrarapid metabolisers.

Factors affecting metabolism

Drug metabolism rates vary greatly among patients because of age, sex, genetics, liver disease, diet, and co-administered drugs. Newborns and the elderly generally have reduced metabolic capacity. Enzyme induction (for example by St John's wort, rifampicin, or cigarette smoke) can lower drug levels below the therapeutic range, whereas enzyme inhibition (for example by grapefruit juice, ketoconazole, or cimetidine) can raise them to toxic concentrations. Genetic polymorphisms of enzymes such as CYP2D6 and the acetyltransferase NAT2 underlie much of the inter-individual variability studied by pharmacogenomics.
