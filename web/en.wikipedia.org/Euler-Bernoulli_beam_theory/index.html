Euler–Bernoulli beam theory

A cantilever beam deflecting under an end load, as described by the Euler–Bernoulli equation.

Euler–Bernoulli beam theory (also known as engineer's beam theory or classical beam theory) is a simplification of the linear theory of elasticity which provides a means of calculating the load-carrying and deflection characteristics of beams. It was first enunciated circa 1750 by Leonhard Euler and Daniel Bernoulli, and became a cornerstone of structural and mechanical engineering during the Industrial Revolution.

Assumptions

The theory assumes that cross-sections of the beam that are plane and perpendicular to the beam's axis before bending remain plane and perpendicular to the (bent) neutral axis after bending; consequently, transverse shear deformation is neglected. It is therefore accurate for slender beams, where the length is much greater than the cross-sectional dimensions, and deflections are small. The central result is a fourth-order differential equation relating the deflection w(x) to the distributed load q(x):

EI d4w/dx4 = q(x),

where E is Young's modulus and I the second moment of area of the cross-section. Bending stress follows from the flexure formula σ = −My/I.

Extensions and successors

For short, deep beams where shear deformation matters, Timoshenko–Ehrenfest beam theory relaxes the normality assumption and adds a shear correction term. Euler–Bernoulli theory remains the standard first model for beams in bridges, buildings, machine elements and MEMS devices, and forms the basis of the stiffness method taught in undergraduate structural analysis.
