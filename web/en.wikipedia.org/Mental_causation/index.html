Mental causation

Mental causation is a conceptual problem in the philosophy of mind concerning how mental states — beliefs, desires, intentions and sensations — can cause physical effects, such as bodily movements, and how physical events can in turn cause mental states. It is a central puzzle because an adequate theory of mind must explain how thought can move matter without abandoning the apparent causal completeness of physics.

The problem

Descartes, a substance dualist, held that the mind interacts with the body at the pineal gland; his critics, notably Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia, asked how an immaterial substance could affect a material one. In the modern form, the philosopher Jaegwon Kim posed the "causal exclusion problem": if every physical event has a sufficient physical cause, then either mental causes are identical to physical causes, or behaviour is systematically overdetermined, or the mental is causally inert (epiphenomenalism).

Approaches

Identity theories dissolve the problem by identifying mental states with brain states. Functionalism treats mental states as realisable in many physical systems while still accepting their causal roles. Donald Davidson's anomalous monism holds that mental events are identical with physical events but that there are no strict laws linking descriptions in mental vocabulary to those in physical vocabulary. Interactionist dualists accept non-physical causation, while epiphenomenalists such as T. H. Huxley likened consciousness to the whistle of a steam engine — produced by, but not influencing, the mechanism. Interventionist and proportionality-based accounts of causation in the philosophy of science have been used to defend a robust causal role for the mental without competing with physical causes at the same level.

Significance

The debate bears on free will, moral responsibility and the explanation of action: if reasons never cause behaviour, the commonsense picture of agents acting on their beliefs and desires is threatened.
