George S. Patton

George Smith Patton Jr. (November 11, 1885 – December 21, 1945) was a general in the United States Army who commanded the Seventh Army in the Mediterranean theatre and the Third Army in France and Germany during World War II. Born in San Gabriel, California, he attended the Virginia Military Institute and the United States Military Academy at West Point, and competed in the modern pentathlon at the 1912 Summer Olympics.

Early career

Patton served in the Pancho Villa Expedition of 1916 and, in World War I, was a founder of the U.S. Army's tank arm, commanding the U.S. tank corps in France and being wounded at the Meuse-Argonne offensive. Between the wars he was a leading advocate of armored warfare despite institutional resistance.

World War II

In 1942 Patton commanded forces in the Allied invasion of French North Africa. He led the Seventh Army in the invasion of Sicily in 1943, where his slapping of two shell-shocked soldiers nearly ended his career. Reassigned to command the Third Army in 1944, he conducted a celebrated rapid armored advance across France, relieved Bastogne during the Battle of the Bulge, and drove into Germany and Czechoslovakia in 1945. His aggressive style earned him the nickname "Old Blood and Guts".

Patton died on December 21, 1945, from injuries sustained in an automobile accident near Mannheim, Germany, and was buried at the Luxembourg American Cemetery.
