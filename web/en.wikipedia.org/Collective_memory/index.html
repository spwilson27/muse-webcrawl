Collective memory

Collective memory refers to the shared pool of knowledge, images and narratives about the past that is held by a social group — a family, community, nation or culture — and that shapes the group's identity. Unlike individual memory, it is produced and maintained through communication, ritual, education, monuments, media and other social practices.

Origins of the concept

The term was developed by the French sociologist Maurice Halbwachs in works such as The Social Frameworks of Memory (1925) and the posthumously published The Collective Memory (1950). Halbwachs argued that individual recollection is framed by the groups to which a person belongs, and that groups reconstruct the past in light of present concerns. Later scholars extended the idea: Pierre Nora's work on lieux de mémoire ("sites of memory") examined monuments, archives and commemorations, while Jan Assmann distinguished communicative memory, passed informally between generations, from cultural memory, fixed in texts, rituals and institutions.

Applications

Collective memory is central to the study of nationalism, commemoration of wars and atrocities, truth commissions, museums and heritage, and debates over statues and public history. Because collective memories are selective and contested, different groups within a society may hold rival versions of the same events, making memory itself an object of political struggle.
