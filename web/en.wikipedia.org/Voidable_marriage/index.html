Voidable marriage

Voidable marriage
Legal areaFamily law
EffectValid until annulled by a court at a party's petition
ContrastVoid marriage (never valid in law)

A voidable marriage is a marriage that is valid in law but may be declared invalid (annulled) by a court at the request of one of the parties, typically because of a defect existing at the time the marriage was celebrated. Until a decree of nullity is granted, the marriage is treated as fully valid, and unlike a void marriage — which is deemed never to have existed — a voidable marriage can be affirmed by the parties once the defect is removed or condoned.

Grounds

Grounds vary by jurisdiction but commonly include non-consummation, lack of valid consent due to duress, mistake, or mental incapacity, one party being under the age of consent, concealment of a serious fact such as an existing marriage-adjacent condition or venereal disease, or the wife being pregnant by another man at the time of marriage. In England and Wales, the grounds are set out in the Matrimonial Causes Act 1973, as amended (including by the Marriage (Same Sex Couples) Act 2013).

Effects of annulment

Historically, annulment could affect the legitimacy of children and property rights retrospectively; modern statutes in most jurisdictions protect children of annulled marriages and allow courts to make financial orders similar to those on divorce. In canon law of the Catholic Church, a declaration of nullity states that a valid sacramental marriage never came into being, and the grounds and process are correspondingly strict.
