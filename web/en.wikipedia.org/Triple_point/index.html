Triple point

A typical phase diagram; the solid green lines show water's anomalous behaviour

In thermodynamics, the triple point of a substance is the temperature and pressure at which the three phases — gas, liquid, and solid — of that substance coexist in thermodynamic equilibrium. It is the point at which the sublimation curve, fusion curve, and vaporisation curve of a phase diagram meet.

Triple point of water

Temperature273.16 K (0.01 °C, 32.018 °F)
Pressure611.657 Pa (0.00604 atm)

The triple point of water is 273.16 K at a partial vapour pressure of 611.657 pascals. Until the 2019 redefinition of SI base units, it was used to define the kelvin: the unit of thermodynamic temperature was defined as 1/273.16 of the temperature of the triple point of water. Triple-point cells containing pure water remain a practical standard for calibrating thermometers.

For most substances the triple point is also the minimum pressure at which the liquid can exist. Water's triple-point pressure is so low that liquid water cannot exist in open conditions on Earth's surface below it — ice sublimes directly to vapour instead. On Mars, where surface pressures hover near the triple point of water, this behaviour governs the stability of water ice.

Other substances

Many substances have multiple triple points involving different crystal polymorphs. Notable examples include carbon dioxide (216.55 K at 5.185 bar), which is why solid CO2 ("dry ice") sublimes at atmospheric pressure, and argon (83.81 K), which is used as a temperature calibration fixed point. The Gibbs phase rule predicts that a single-component system can have at most one triple point where three specified phases meet, but systems with several solid phases can exhibit several such points.
