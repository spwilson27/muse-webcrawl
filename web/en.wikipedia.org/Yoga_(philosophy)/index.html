Yoga (philosophy)

Yoga, as a philosophical school, is one of the six orthodox (āstika) darśanas of Indian philosophy, systematised in the Yoga Sutras attributed to the sage Patañjali (compiled between the 2nd century BCE and the 4th century CE). Closely allied to the dualist metaphysics of Samkhya, Yoga provides the practical discipline of meditation by which the mind's fluctuations are stilled and the self (puruṣa) is realised as distinct from matter (prakṛti), attaining liberation (kaivalya).

Sources and history

Yogic practices of breath control, sense-withdrawal and meditative absorption are described in the early Upanishads, the Bhagavad Gita (which frames yoga as disciplined action, devotion and knowledge) and Jain and Buddhist literature. Patañjali's Yoga Sutras condensed this heritage into 196 aphorisms, on which Vyāsa's Yogabhāṣya became the foundational commentary. Later developments include medieval haṭha yoga, with its emphasis on bodily posture and energy channels, and the modern, largely postural yoga transmitted worldwide from 20th-century India.

Doctrine

The Sutras define yoga as citta-vritti-nirodha — "the cessation of the turnings of thought." Suffering arises because consciousness mistakenly identifies with the material mind; liberation follows from discriminative discernment (viveka-khyāti). Unlike Samkhya, classical Yoga accepts a special Lord, Īśvara, devotion to whom aids concentration.

The eight limbs

Patañjali's path (aṣṭāṅga yoga) comprises eight limbs:

  • Yama — ethical restraints (non-violence, truthfulness, non-stealing, continence, non-possessiveness)
  • Niyama — observances (purity, contentment, austerity, study, devotion)
  • Āsana — a steady, comfortable seat
  • Prāṇāyāma — regulation of the breath
  • Pratyāhāra — withdrawal of the senses
  • Dhāraṇā — concentration
  • Dhyāna — meditation
  • Samādhi — absorption, culminating in liberation
