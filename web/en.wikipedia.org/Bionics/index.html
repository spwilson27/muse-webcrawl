Bionics

Bionics is the application of biological methods and systems found in nature to the study and design of engineering systems and modern technology. The word — a blend of biology and electronics — is credited to US Air Force officer Jack E. Steele, who used it around 1958–1960. In medicine, bionics more specifically denotes the replacement or enhancement of organs and body parts with mechanical or electronic versions, such as cochlear implants and prosthetic limbs. The related term biomimetics (or biomimicry) emphasises imitating natural designs to solve engineering problems.

Examples in engineering

Classic examples of biomimetic design include Velcro, inspired by the hooked burrs of the burdock plant observed by George de Mestral; drag-reducing surfaces modelled on shark skin; the lotus effect, in which micro-structured surfaces repel water and dirt; and the aerodynamic redesign of Japan's Shinkansen bullet train nose after the kingfisher's beak, which reduced tunnel sonic booms. Gecko adhesion — millions of microscopic hairs exploiting van der Waals forces — has inspired dry adhesives and climbing robots.

Medical bionics

Implantable devices that interface with the nervous system are the best-known medical application. Cochlear implants restore a sense of hearing by stimulating the auditory nerve directly; retinal implants aim to do the same for vision. Myoelectric prosthetic arms read electrical signals from residual muscles to control motors, and research on brain–computer interfaces seeks to let users control prostheses or computers by thought. Artificial hearts, pacemakers, and insulin pumps are sometimes included under the broader bionics umbrella.

Relationship to other fields

Bionics overlaps with bioengineering, biomechatronics, and biomaterials science. In popular culture the term became famous through 1970s television series The Six Million Dollar Man and The Bionic Woman, whose heroes had superhuman electromechanical limbs — capabilities far beyond current technology, though modern prosthetics continue to narrow the gap.
