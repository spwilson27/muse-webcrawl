Single-payer healthcare

Single-payer healthcare is a type of universal healthcare system in which the cost of essential healthcare for all residents is covered by a single public system, financed through taxes, rather than by competing private insurers. Medical services are still delivered by a mix of public and private providers; it is the payment that is single. The term distinguishes such systems from multi-payer models like those of Germany or the Netherlands, and from wholly state-run delivery systems such as Britain's National Health Service (though the NHS is also often described as single-payer).

Examples

Canada's Medicare system is the classic example: each province administers a public insurance plan covering medically necessary hospital and physician services, while doctors mostly remain in private practice. Taiwan's National Health Insurance, introduced in 1995, is another well-studied single-payer system. In the United States, "Medicare for All" proposals would extend the public Medicare programme — itself a single-payer system for Americans over 65 — to the whole population; the merits and costs of such expansion are the subject of continuing political debate.

Arguments in debate

Supporters emphasise lower administrative overhead (a single payer needs no marketing, underwriting, or profit margin), universal coverage, and bargaining power over prices. Critics point to higher tax burdens, the risk of longer waiting times for elective care, reduced incentives for innovation, and the transition costs of displacing private insurance.
