Battlecruiser

The battlecruiser (also written battle cruiser or battle-cruiser) was a type of capital ship of the first half of the 20th century. Battlecruisers were similar in size, cost and armament to battleships, but they typically carried fewer guns and thinner armour, trading protection for higher speed. The first battlecruisers were developed in the United Kingdom in 1907, shortly after the revolutionary battleship HMS Dreadnought.

Origins and design

The battlecruiser was the brainchild of Admiral Sir John "Jacky" Fisher, the British First Sea Lord. Fisher envisaged a fast capital ship, armed like a battleship but fast enough to hunt down enemy cruisers and commerce raiders anywhere in the world, and to scout for the battle fleet. The Invincible class, laid down in 1906, mounted eight 12-inch guns but carried armour only proof against cruiser guns, achieving speeds around 25 knots. Germany, Japan, and briefly other navies built comparable ships.

Service

Battlecruisers performed their intended cruiser-hunting role at the Battle of the Falkland Islands in 1914, destroying Admiral von Spee's squadron. However, their use in the line of battle exposed their thin armour: at the Battle of Jutland in 1916, three British battlecruisers — Indefatigable, Queen Mary and Invincible — blew up with heavy loss of life. In the Second World War, HMS Hood, the pride of the Royal Navy, was sunk by the German battleship Bismarck in 1941.

Decline

The Washington Naval Treaty of 1922 halted most battlecruiser construction; some hulls were converted into aircraft carriers, such as the Japanese Akagi and American Lexington class. Post-war "fast battleships" combined heavy armour with high speed, making the separate battlecruiser type obsolete. The Soviet/Russian Kirov-class large missile cruisers, sometimes described as battlecruisers, are the only ships of comparable type in modern service.
