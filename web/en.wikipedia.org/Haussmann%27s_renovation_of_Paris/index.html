Haussmann's renovation of Paris

Haussmann's renovation of Paris was a vast public works programme commissioned by Emperor Napoleon III and directed by Baron Georges-Eugène Haussmann, prefect of the Seine, between 1853 and 1870. It demolished much of medieval Paris and rebuilt the capital with wide tree-lined boulevards, new parks, sewers, and waterworks, giving Paris much of the appearance it retains today.

The programme

Haussmann cut dozens of broad avenues — including the boulevards Saint-Michel, Saint-Germain, Haussmann, and Sébastopol, and the avenue de l'Opéra — through the dense old quarters, connecting railway stations and easing the movement of traffic and troops. Building codes imposed uniform limestone façades, aligned cornices, and height limits, producing the characteristic "Haussmann" apartment blocks. The city annexed its surrounding communes in 1860, growing from 12 to 20 arrondissements. Engineer Eugène Belgrand built an extensive new sewer system and aqueducts, while Jean-Charles Alphand laid out parks such as the Bois de Boulogne, Bois de Vincennes, and Parc des Buttes-Chaumont. Markets (Les Halles), hospitals, schools, and the Palais Garnier opera house were among the major new buildings.

Motives and criticism

Beyond beautification and sanitation, the renovation aimed to prevent the street barricades that had marked earlier revolutions and to assert imperial prestige; it also provided employment during economic downturns. The works displaced hundreds of thousands of poorer residents to peripheral districts and were financed by speculative borrowing that drew accusations of corruption — Jules Ferry famously attacked the "fantastic accounts of Haussmann." Haussmann was dismissed in 1870, shortly before the fall of the Second Empire, though construction continued for decades along his lines.

Legacy

The renovation profoundly influenced urban planning worldwide, from Vienna and Brussels to Buenos Aires and Mexico City, and "Haussmannian" remains a standard term for the Parisian urban fabric. Critics such as the Situationists and historians of the Paris Commune have debated whether its social costs outweighed its achievements in public health and civic beauty.
