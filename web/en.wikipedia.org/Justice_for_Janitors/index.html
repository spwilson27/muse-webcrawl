Justice for Janitors

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Justice for Janitors is a labour campaign of the Service Employees International Union (SEIU) that organises janitors and other building-service workers, many of them immigrants, in cities across the United States and Canada. Launched in the mid-1980s, it became one of the most prominent examples of social-movement unionism and of organising low-wage service workers outside traditional workplace-by-workplace elections.

Background

During the 1970s and 1980s, building owners increasingly contracted out cleaning work to non-union firms, driving down wages and benefits for janitors who had previously held union jobs. Because individual cleaning contractors were small and interchangeable, the campaign targeted the building owners and major contractors at the level of whole urban markets, using public pressure, demonstrations, and civil disobedience to win master agreements.

Major campaigns

The campaign began in Denver in 1985 and gained national attention in Los Angeles. On 15 June 1990, striking janitors and supporters marching in Century City were attacked by Los Angeles police; the widely reported violence galvanised public support, and the janitors won a contract soon after. The date is still commemorated by the union as Justice for Janitors Day. Campaigns followed in Washington, D.C., Houston, Miami, and other cities, frequently raising wages and winning health benefits for thousands of cleaners, and the model was adapted in the United Kingdom's "Justice for Cleaners" campaign.

Legacy

Justice for Janitors is credited with revitalising the SEIU, demonstrating that immigrant service workers were organisable, and influencing later campaigns for low-wage workers, including fast-food organising. Critics have debated its top-down structure and bargaining compromises, but historians of the labour movement regard it as a landmark of late twentieth-century American unionism. The 2000 Ken Loach film Bread and Roses dramatised the Los Angeles campaign.
