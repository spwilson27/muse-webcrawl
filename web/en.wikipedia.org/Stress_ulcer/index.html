Stress ulcer

A stress ulcer is a single or multiple mucosal defect in the stomach (and sometimes the duodenum or oesophagus) that occurs during the physiologic stress of serious illness. Unlike ordinary peptic ulcers, which usually form deep single lesions, stress ulcers are typically multiple, superficial erosions that develop in the acid-producing part of the stomach within hours to days of a critical insult.

Causes

Stress ulceration is associated with severe trauma, major surgery, burns, sepsis, shock, and prolonged mechanical ventilation in intensive care patients. The key mechanism is reduced blood flow to the gastric mucosa, which impairs its protective barrier and bicarbonate secretion and allows acid to injure the surface. Named forms include Curling's ulcers, occurring after severe burns in the duodenum, and Cushing's ulcers, associated with head injury or brain surgery, which are linked to increased acid secretion driven by raised intracranial pressure.

Presentation and treatment

Many stress erosions cause no symptoms, but clinically important cases present with upper gastrointestinal bleeding, which carries significant mortality in critically ill patients. Prevention (stress ulcer prophylaxis) with acid-suppressing drugs such as proton pump inhibitors or H2 receptor antagonists, and early enteral feeding, is standard for high-risk ICU patients; evolving guidance restricts prophylaxis to those at genuinely high risk because of possible adverse effects. Established bleeding is managed with resuscitation, endoscopic therapy, and, rarely, surgery or embolisation.
