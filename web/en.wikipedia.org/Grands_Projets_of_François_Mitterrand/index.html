Grands Projets of François Mitterrand

The Grands Projets (officially Grandes Opérations d'Architecture et d'Urbanisme) were a programme of monumental architectural projects launched by French president François Mitterrand during his presidency (1981–1995). Intended to modernise Paris and to leave a lasting cultural legacy comparable to the works of earlier French rulers, the projects reshaped several districts of the capital and became emblematic of Mitterrand's fourteen years in power.

The projects

  • The Louvre Pyramid and the "Grand Louvre" expansion, designed by I. M. Pei, inaugurated in 1989.
  • The Grande Arche de la Fraternité at La Défense, designed by Johann Otto von Spreckelsen, completed for the bicentennial of the French Revolution in 1989.
  • The Opéra Bastille, designed by Carlos Ott, opened in 1989.
  • The Institut du Monde Arabe, designed by Jean Nouvel, opened in 1987.
  • The Bibliothèque nationale de France (site François-Mitterrand), designed by Dominique Perrault, the largest and last of the projects, opened in 1996.
  • The Parc de la Villette and the Cité des Sciences et de l'Industrie, on the site of former abattoirs.
  • The Ministry of Finance (Bercy), which moved the ministry out of the Louvre.
  • The Musée d'Orsay, begun under Valéry Giscard d'Estaing and completed under Mitterrand, opened in 1986.

Design and politics

Most of the projects were awarded through architectural competitions, several of them won by relatively unknown architects, and a number of competitions were decided personally by Mitterrand. The programme was criticised for its cost, its top-down character — critics dubbed Mitterrand "Tonton" playing the pharaoh — and in the case of the Bibliothèque nationale, for functional problems in its design, including the exposure of books to light in its glass towers. Supporters credit the Grands Projets with renewing Paris's cultural infrastructure and architectural ambition.

Legacy

The Grands Projets are frequently compared to the grands travaux of Napoleon III and Baron Haussmann. Many of the buildings have become fixtures of the Parisian landscape, and the model of presidential monument-building influenced later French presidents' cultural programmes.
