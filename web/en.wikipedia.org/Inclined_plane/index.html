Inclined plane

An inclined plane, also known as a ramp, is a flat supporting surface tilted at an angle, with one end higher than the other, used as an aid for raising or lowering a load. It is one of the six classical simple machines defined by Renaissance scientists, and it trades increased distance for reduced force: a load can be moved up a slope with less effort than lifting it vertically.

Mechanical advantage

The ideal mechanical advantage of a frictionless inclined plane is the ratio of the length of the slope to its vertical rise, L/h, equivalently 1/sin θ, where θ is the angle of inclination. The force required to push a load of weight W up the plane (neglecting friction) is W sin θ.

History

Ramps were used in antiquity, including in the construction of the Egyptian pyramids and prehistoric monuments. Galileo Galilei used inclined planes in his experiments on falling bodies, slowing the acceleration of rolling balls so it could be measured, and Simon Stevin and Jordanus de Nemore gave early analyses of the forces involved.

Applications

Applications include loading ramps, roads and railways switchbacking up mountainsides, wheelchair access ramps, chutes and slides, and, in modified form, the wedge and the screw, both of which can be understood as inclined planes wrapped or driven into material.
