The Bataan Death March was the forcible transfer by the Imperial Japanese Army of approximately 75,000 American and Filipino prisoners of war from the Bataan Peninsula to prison camps around 65 miles (105 km) away, following the surrender of Bataan on 9 April 1942 during the Second World War. The march was characterized by severe physical abuse and wanton killings, and was later judged a Japanese war crime.

Background

After months of fighting and with supplies exhausted, Major General Edward P. King surrendered the Bataan garrison on 9 April 1942. The Japanese, who had expected far fewer prisoners, were unprepared to move or feed so many men, most of whom were already sick and starving.

The march

Prisoners were grouped and marched north from Mariveles and Bagac toward San Fernando, from where they were packed into boxcars for transport to Camp O'Donnell. Along the way they were beaten, bayoneted, beheaded or left to die; those who fell behind or stopped for water were routinely killed. Estimates of deaths vary widely, from around 5,500 to 18,650 total, including between roughly 500 and 650 Americans.

Aftermath

Survivors were held in brutal conditions at Camp O'Donnell and later Cabanatuan. After the war, the march was prosecuted as a war crime; Japanese commander Masaharu Homma was convicted by a U.S. military commission and executed in 1946. Memorials in the Philippines and the United States commemorate the victims, and annual memorial marches are held, notably at White Sands Missile Range in New Mexico.
