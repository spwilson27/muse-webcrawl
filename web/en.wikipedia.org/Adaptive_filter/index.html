Adaptive filter

An adaptive filter is a digital filter whose coefficients change over time to optimise a performance criterion, usually the minimisation of the mean square of an error signal. Unlike a fixed filter, it "learns" the statistics of its input and environment, and can track slowly varying conditions. The classical framework was developed by Bernard Widrow and Marcian Hoff, who introduced the least mean squares (LMS) algorithm in 1960.

Structure

A typical adaptive system has two parts: a digital filter (often a finite impulse response filter) that processes the input, and an adaptation algorithm that adjusts the filter's coefficients from the difference between the filter output and a desired response. The four basic configurations are system identification, inverse modelling, prediction, and interference cancelling.

Algorithms

  • LMS (least mean squares) — simple and robust; updates coefficients proportional to the product of the error and the input. Its normalised variant, NLMS, is widely used in practice.
  • RLS (recursive least squares) — converges faster by minimising a weighted sum of squared errors, at greater computational cost.
  • Kalman filters — provide an optimal state-space formulation for time-varying systems.

Applications

Adaptive filters are fundamental to modern signal processing: acoustic echo cancellation in telephony and conference systems, active noise control, channel equalisation in modems and mobile communications, adaptive beamforming in radar and microphone arrays, noise removal in biomedical signals such as ECG, and hearing aids that adapt to their acoustic environment.
