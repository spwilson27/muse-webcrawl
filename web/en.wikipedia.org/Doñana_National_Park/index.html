Doñana National Park

Doñana National Park is a natural reserve in Andalusia, southern Spain, in the provinces of Huelva, Seville and Cádiz. It covers 54,252 hectares, in addition to a surrounding natural park, and occupies the right bank of the Guadalquivir river at its estuary on the Atlantic Ocean. The park is an area of marshes, shallow streams, and sand dunes in the Guadalquivir delta, and is noted for its great ecological wealth. It was designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1994.

Ecology

Doñana is one of Europe's most important wetland reserves and a major site for migrating birds. Its habitats include seasonal marshes that flood in winter and dry out in summer, mobile and stabilised sand dunes, scrubland, and stone pine and cork oak woodland. The park supports species such as the Iberian lynx and the Spanish imperial eagle, both endangered, along with large numbers of greater flamingos, spoonbills, and wintering waterfowl. Herds of red deer, fallow deer and wild boar roam the scrub and marsh edges.

History

The area takes its name from Doña Ana de Silva y Mendoza, wife of a 16th-century duke of Medina Sidonia, whose family used the land as a hunting estate. Conservationists including José Antonio Valverde campaigned for its protection in the 1950s and 1960s; the World Wildlife Fund helped purchase land, and the national park was created in 1969. In 1998 the park was threatened by the Aznalcóllar mine disaster, when a burst tailings dam released toxic sludge toward its northern boundary; the spill was diverted and a major clean-up followed.

Threats

The wetlands depend on water from the aquifer system around the park, which is under pressure from intensive agriculture — particularly strawberry farming in the surrounding region — and from drought. Falling water tables and dry years have left marshes unflooded, harming breeding birds, and water management remains a subject of political dispute.
