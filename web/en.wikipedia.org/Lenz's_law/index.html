Lenz's law

Lenz's law, formulated by the Baltic German physicist Emil Lenz in 1834, states that the direction of the electric current induced in a conductor by a changing magnetic field is such that the magnetic field created by the induced current opposes the change that produced it. It supplies the negative sign in Faraday's law of induction, ε = −dΦB/dt, and is a direct consequence of the conservation of energy: without the opposition, a small change would amplify itself indefinitely.

Explanation

If the magnetic flux through a loop increases, the induced current flows so that its own magnetic field points against the increase; if the flux decreases, the current acts to sustain it. A bar magnet pushed toward a coil is repelled by the coil's induced field, so the work done pushing against that repulsion is what becomes electrical energy. Conversely, withdrawing the magnet makes the coil attract it. This opposition underlies eddy-current braking, induction cooktops' loading effects, and the back-EMF in electric motors.

Applications

  • Generators and transformers: the load current always opposes the change driving it, which is why generators become harder to turn under load.
  • Eddy current brakes in trains and roller coasters exploit the opposing forces on moving conductors.
  • Magnetic levitation and damping: induced currents in conductors resist changes in flux, damping oscillation in balances and galvanometers.
