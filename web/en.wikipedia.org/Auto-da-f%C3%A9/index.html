Auto-da-fé
Meaning"Act of faith" (Portuguese)
ContextSpanish and Portuguese Inquisitions
Period15th–19th centuries

An auto-da-fé (from Portuguese auto da fé, "act of faith") was the ritual public penance and sentencing carried out by the Spanish and Portuguese Inquisitions. After a trial, those convicted of heresy and other offences were presented in a solemn public ceremony at which sentences were read and penances imposed.

Ceremony

Autos-da-fé were elaborate public spectacles, often held in a main square with processions of penitents wearing garments (sanbenitos) marked according to their offences. Those reconciled with the Church received penances; those "relaxed" to the secular arm — the unrepentant or relapsed — were handed over for execution, usually by burning, carried out by civil authorities outside the ceremony itself.

History

The first recorded auto of the Spanish Inquisition took place in Seville in 1481. The practice spread through Spain, Portugal and their empires, including in Mexico, Peru, Brazil and Goa. The last autos were held in the early nineteenth century, and the public image of the auto-da-fé has remained a potent symbol of religious persecution, depicted in art and literature such as Voltaire's Candide.
