Motion picture film format

[Image: Common motion picture film formats and gauges]

A motion picture film format is defined by a set of characteristics that specify how images are recorded on film stock: principally the film gauge (width of the film), the size and shape of the image area (aspect ratio), the arrangement of perforations, the pulldown (number of perforations advanced per frame), and the placement of the soundtrack. Formats arise to meet practical needs - economy, image quality, compatibility of cameras, printers and projectors worldwide.

Major formats

The industry standard for most of cinema history has been 35 mm film, introduced by Edison's engineers and standardized in 1909, shot with four-perforation pulldown in the Academy ratio (1.37:1) and later in widescreen variants including anamorphic (CinemaScope, 2.39:1) and Super 35. 16 mm (1923) served education, television and independent film; 70 mm offered larger, sharper images for roadshow epics, with 65 mm origination used by Todd-AO, Super Panavision and IMAX. 8 mm and Super 8 served home movies. Other systems include 9.5 mm Pathé Baby, VistaVision (35 mm run horizontally), Technirama, and multi-strip processes such as Cinerama.

Sound and decline of film

Sound formats added optical tracks beside the image; 70 mm used magnetic stripes, and later 35 mm prints carried digital sound data for Dolby Digital, DTS and SDDS. Since the 2000s, digital cinematography and digital projection have displaced most film formats, though 35 mm and 65/70 mm persist in prestige productions.
