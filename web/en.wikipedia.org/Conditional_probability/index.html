Conditional probability

In probability theory, conditional probability is a measure of the probability of an event occurring, given that another event has already occurred. If the event of interest is A and the event B is known or assumed to have occurred, the conditional probability of A given B is usually written as P(A|B).

Definition

If P(B) > 0, the conditional probability of A given B is defined as:

P(A|B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B)

That is, the probability of A within the reduced sample space in which B has occurred.

Bayes' theorem

Conditional probability is connected to the probability of the reverse conditioning by Bayes' theorem:

P(A|B) = P(B|A) P(A) / P(B)

Bayes' theorem underpins Bayesian inference and is widely used in statistics, medicine (interpreting diagnostic tests), machine learning (naive Bayes classifiers) and law.

Independence

Events A and B are independent if and only if P(A|B) = P(A) (when P(B) > 0) — learning that B occurred does not change the probability of A. Conditional independence extends this idea to conditioning on a third event.

Common fallacies

Confusing P(A|B) with P(B|A) is known in legal contexts as the prosecutor's fallacy. The base rate fallacy arises when prior probabilities are ignored, for example when assessing a positive result from a screening test for a rare disease.

Multiplication rule and total probability

The definition rearranges to the multiplication rule P(A ∩ B) = P(A|B) P(B), generalised by the chain rule to many events. Combined with a partition of the sample space, the law of total probability expresses P(A) as a weighted sum of conditional probabilities.
