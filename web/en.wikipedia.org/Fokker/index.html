Fokker

Fokker was a Dutch aircraft manufacturer that operated from 1912 until its bankruptcy in 1996. Founded by the aviation pioneer Anthony Fokker, the company became famous for its First World War fighter aircraft built for Germany, its interwar trimotor airliners, and its post-war family of turboprop and jet regional airliners, the F27 Friendship, F28 Fellowship, Fokker 50, Fokker 70, and Fokker 100.

First World War

Working in Germany, Fokker produced some of the war's most famous fighters. The Fokker Eindecker monoplanes of 1915–16, fitted with a synchronisation gear that let a machine gun fire through the propeller arc, gave Germany air superiority in the "Fokker Scourge." Later designs included the Dr.I triplane flown by Manfred von Richthofen, the "Red Baron," and the D.VII, widely considered the war's finest fighter.

Interwar and civil aviation

After the war Fokker moved production to the Netherlands. Its F.II and F.VII trimotors became workhorses of early airlines; an F.VII carried Richard Byrd over the North Pole (as claimed) in 1926 and flew the first trans-Pacific flight of the Southern Cross in 1928. A Fokker subsidiary in the United States built the F-10 trimotor, whose 1931 crash killing football coach Knute Rockne led to tighter airliner regulation.

Post-war airliners

The turboprop F27 Friendship (1958) became one of the best-selling Western turboprops, and the F28 Fellowship jet followed in 1967. Their successors, the Fokker 50 and the Fokker 70/100 jets, sold well in the 1980s–90s, but development costs, a strong guilder, and competition from Bombardier and Embraer overwhelmed the company, and Fokker was declared bankrupt in March 1996.

Legacy

Fokker's maintenance and services businesses survived as Fokker Technologies (later part of GKN Aerospace), supporting the hundreds of Fokker aircraft that remain in service, and the name has been periodically attached to revival ventures.
