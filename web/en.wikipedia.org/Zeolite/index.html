Zeolite

Zeolites are microporous aluminosilicate minerals commonly used as commercial adsorbents and catalysts. The term was coined in 1756 by Swedish mineralogist Axel Fredrik Cronstedt, who observed that rapidly heating stilbite produced large amounts of steam from water that had been adsorbed by the material; from the Greek words meaning "to boil" and "stone", he called it a zeolite.

Zeolites have a rigid, three-dimensional framework of SiO4 and AlO4 tetrahedra linked by shared oxygen atoms, creating regular channels and cavities of molecular dimensions — typically 0.3 to 1 nanometre across. This structure allows them to act as molecular sieves, selectively sorbing molecules by size and polarity.

Occurrence and synthesis

Natural zeolites form where volcanic rocks and ash layers react with alkaline groundwater; well-known species include clinoptilolite, mordenite, chabazite and analcime. Because natural materials vary in composition, industry relies heavily on synthetic zeolites manufactured hydrothermally, whose pore structures and acidity can be precisely tailored. Well over 200 distinct framework types are known.

Applications

Zeolites are used in water softening and purification, as detergent builders (zeolite A replaced phosphates in many laundry detergents), in petrochemical cracking catalysts (such as ZSM-5 and zeolite Y in fluid catalytic cracking), in oxygen concentrators that separate nitrogen from air, in cat litter and odour control, and in agriculture as soil conditioners and feed additives.
