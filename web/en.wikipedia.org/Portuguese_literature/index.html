Portuguese literature

Portuguese literature is the literature written in the Portuguese language, chiefly in Portugal and Brazil, but also in Portuguese-speaking Africa and Asia. It is generally divided by era into medieval, Renaissance, baroque, neoclassical, romantic, realist, modernist, and contemporary literatures.

Origins and the Renaissance

The earliest Portuguese texts are the Galician-Portuguese lyric poems (cantigas) of the twelfth to fourteenth centuries. Gil Vicente founded Portuguese theatre in the early sixteenth century. The supreme figure of the Renaissance is Luís de Camões, whose epic Os Lusíadas (1572), celebrating Vasco da Gama's voyage to India, is the national poem of Portugal.

Nineteenth and twentieth centuries

Romanticism was led by Almeida Garrett and Alexandre Herculano. The great realist novelist is Eça de Queirós, author of The Maias and The Crime of Father Amaro. In the twentieth century, Fernando Pessoa — writing under numerous heteronyms — came to be regarded as one of the greatest poets of modernism. José Saramago, author of Blindness and Baltasar and Blimunda, won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1998. Other notable modern writers include António Lobo Antunes, Agustina Bessa-Luís, and the poet Sophia de Mello Breyner Andresen.

Brazilian literature

Brazil developed a major literature of its own, from Machado de Assis and Euclides da Cunha through the modernists to Clarice Lispector and João Guimarães Rosa, and is often treated as a parallel tradition within Lusophone letters.
