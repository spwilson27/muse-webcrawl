William Morris (24 March 1834 – 3 October 1896) was an English textile designer, artist, and writer associated with the Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood and the Arts and Crafts movement. His designs for wallpaper, fabrics, and furniture — dense, intricate patterns drawn from nature — and his firm Morris & Co. reshaped Victorian decorative art.

Life and work

Morris studied at Oxford, where he met Edward Burne-Jones, and briefly trained as an architect before joining the circle of Dante Gabriel Rossetti. In 1861 he co-founded the decorative arts firm that became Morris & Co., producing stained glass, tiles, embroidery, and printed textiles by handcraft methods in reaction against industrial mass production. His home, Red House in Bexleyheath, designed with Philip Webb, became an Arts and Crafts landmark.

Morris was also a prolific writer: the epic poem The Earthly Paradise, translations of Icelandic sagas and Homer, and the utopian socialist novel News from Nowhere (1890). Politically he became a committed revolutionary socialist, founding the Socialist League in 1884 and lecturing and writing pamphlets such as Useful Work versus Useless Toil. In his final years he founded the Kelmscott Press (1891), whose hand-printed books, above all the Kelmscott Chaucer, revived fine book design.
