Unique factorization domain

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In mathematics, a unique factorization domain (UFD), also called a factorial ring, is a commutative ring in which every non-zero non-unit element can be written as a product of irreducible elements, and this factorisation is unique up to order and multiplication by units. The concept generalises the fundamental theorem of arithmetic, which states that the integers form a UFD: every integer factors uniquely into prime numbers.

Definition and properties

Formally, an integral domain R is a UFD if every non-zero element that is not a unit can be written as a product of irreducible elements, and any two such products differ only in the order of the factors and by units. In a UFD, the notions of irreducible element and prime element coincide. UFDs can be characterised as integral domains in which every height-one prime ideal is principal, and a Noetherian domain is a UFD if and only if it is integrally closed and every divisor class is trivial.

Examples

Every principal ideal domain is a UFD, and hence so is every Euclidean domain: examples include the integers, fields, polynomial rings in one variable over a field, and the Gaussian integers ℤ[i]. By the Gauss lemma, a polynomial ring over a UFD is again a UFD, so ℤ[x₁, …, xₙ] and k[x₁, …, xₙ] are factorial. Not every ring of algebraic integers is a UFD: the classic counterexample is ℤ[√−5], in which 6 = 2·3 = (1 + √−5)(1 − √−5) gives two genuinely different factorisations — a failure of unique factorisation that motivates the theory of ideals and the class group, which measures how far a Dedekind domain is from being a UFD.

History

Investigations into non-unique factorisation in the 19th century, prompted by attempted proofs of Fermat's Last Theorem in cyclotomic integers, led Kummer to invent "ideal numbers" and Dedekind to develop ideal theory — turning the failure of the UFD property into one of the most fruitful episodes in the history of algebra.
