Anomie

[Image placeholder]

Anomie (from French anomie, from Ancient Greek ἀνομία, "lawlessness," from ἀ- "without" and νόμος "law") is a social condition defined by an uprooting or breakdown of any moral values, standards or guidance for individuals to follow. It may evolve from a mismatch between personal or group standards and wider social standards, or from the lack of a social ethic — particularly during rapid social change, when old norms lose their force before new ones are established.

Durkheim

The term was popularised by the French sociologist Émile Durkheim, who introduced it in The Division of Labour in Society (1893) and developed it in Suicide (1897). Durkheim argued that economic crises — both crashes and sudden booms — weaken the collective regulation of appetites, producing "anomic suicide," one of his four types of suicide alongside egoistic, altruistic and fatalistic. For Durkheim, anomie is a property of the social structure, not merely of individuals.

Merton and later theory

Robert K. Merton reformulated anomie as strain: in his 1938 essay "Social Structure and Anomie," deviance arises when culturally prescribed goals (such as monetary success) are emphasised but the legitimate institutional means of achieving them are unequally available, leading to adaptations he labelled innovation, ritualism, retreatism and rebellion. Later criminologists developed strain theory further, and psychologists such as Leo Srole operationalised the individual-level analogue, "anomia," in survey research.
