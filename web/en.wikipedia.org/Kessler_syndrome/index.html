The Kessler syndrome (also called the Kessler effect) is a scenario proposed by NASA scientist Donald J. Kessler in 1978 in which the density of objects in low Earth orbit becomes high enough that collisions between objects generate debris that causes further collisions — a cascade that could render some orbits unusable for generations.

Background

Every satellite, spent rocket stage, and fragment in orbit travels at several kilometres per second, so even a centimetre-sized particle carries the energy of an explosive charge. As the population of objects grows, the expected number of collisions rises faster than linearly, and each collision can create thousands of trackable fragments.

Debris-generating events

The 2007 Chinese anti-satellite test against the FengYun-1C weather satellite created more than 3,000 pieces of trackable debris, the largest single debris-generating event on record. In 2009 the operational Iridium 33 communications satellite collided with the defunct Russian Cosmos 2251 — the first accidental hypervelocity collision between two intact satellites — adding over 2,000 more trackable fragments.

Consequences and mitigation

A full cascade would threaten weather, communications, and navigation services and could make certain altitude bands hazardous for human spaceflight, although experts debate how close the current environment is to instability. Mitigation measures include passivating spent stages, deorbiting satellites at end of life, improved tracking and collision avoidance, and proposals for active debris removal of the largest derelict objects, which dominate the long-term risk.
