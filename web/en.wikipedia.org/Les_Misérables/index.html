Les Misérables (French: [le mizeʁabl]) is an 1862 French novel by Victor Hugo, widely regarded as one of the greatest novels of the 19th century. Set in France between 1815 and the June Rebellion of 1832, it follows the ex-convict Jean Valjean in his struggle for redemption, interwoven with the lives of the factory worker Fantine, her daughter Cosette, the police inspector Javert, the innkeepers the Thénardiers, and the revolutionary student Marius. The novel examines law and grace, poverty, justice and redemption, and contains famous digressions on subjects including the Battle of Waterloo, the Paris sewers, argot, and convent life.

Structure and plot

The novel is divided into five volumes — Fantine, Cosette, Marius, The Idyll in the Rue Plumet and the Epic in the Rue St. Denis, and Jean Valjean — comprising 365 chapters in the unabridged French text, one of the longest novels ever written.

Jean Valjean is released from the Toulon prison after nineteen years — five for stealing a loaf of bread for his sister's starving family, and fourteen more for escape attempts — embittered and unable to find work with a convict's passport. Shown mercy by Bishop Myriel of Digne, who gives him silver candlesticks and tells the police they were a gift, Valjean resolves to live honestly. Under the name Madeleine he prospers as a manufacturer and becomes mayor of Montreuil-sur-Mer. When another man is mistakenly identified as Valjean, he reveals himself in court and is re-imprisoned, escaping to rescue Cosette, the daughter of the dying Fantine, from the abusive Thénardiers. Pursued relentlessly by Inspector Javert, Valjean raises Cosette in Paris. Years later Cosette falls in love with Marius Pontmercy, and Valjean saves Marius's life at the barricades of the 1832 June Rebellion by carrying him through the sewers — sparing Javert along the way, an act of mercy that shatters Javert's rigid moral code and drives him to suicide. Valjean reveals his past to Marius, withdraws from Cosette's life, and dies reconciled, with the bishop's candlesticks beside him.

Themes

Hugo wrote in his preface that the book addressed "the degradation of man by the proletariat, the ruin of woman by hunger, the atrophy of childhood by night" — so long as ignorance and misery exist, he argued, such books would not be useless. The novel contrasts Javert's legalism with the bishop's grace, and became a plea for social reform: Hugo's fame from it helped campaigns against the death penalty and child labour.

Publication and reception

Hugo, in exile in Guernsey, completed the novel in 1861; it was published on 3–4 April 1862 in one of the largest publicity campaigns of the century, with simultaneous editions across Europe. It was an enormous popular success, though critical reaction was mixed — some reviewers, such as Baudelaire publicly praising it while privately disparaging it, and Flaubert and the Goncourt brothers, found it overwrought. It has never been out of print.

Adaptations

The novel has been adapted countless times for film, radio and television, including celebrated French and international film versions. Its best-known adaptation is the stage musical by Claude-Michel Schönberg and Alain Boublil (Paris 1980; London 1985), which ran for decades in the West End and on Broadway and was filmed in 2012 with Hugh Jackman as Valjean — bringing songs such as "I Dreamed a Dream" and "One Day More" to a global audience.
