Trappist beer

Trappist ales are traditionally served in wide goblet-style glasses branded to each abbey.

OriginTrappist (Cistercian) monasteries, mainly Belgium
Governing bodyInternational Trappist Association (founded 1997)
Label"Authentic Trappist Product"
Typical stylesEnkel, dubbel, tripel, quadrupel

Trappist beer is beer brewed by Trappist monks — members of the Order of Cistercians of the Strict Observance — within the walls of their monasteries. Of the monasteries that brew beer, most are in Belgium, where abbeys such as Westmalle, Chimay, Rochefort, Orval, Westvleteren and Achel became famous for strong, bottle-conditioned ales. Other recognised Trappist breweries include La Trappe in the Netherlands, Stift Engelszell in Austria, Tre Fontane in Italy, Spencer in the United States and Tynt Meadow in England.

The Authentic Trappist Product label

In 1997 eight Trappist abbeys founded the International Trappist Association (ITA) and created the hexagonal "Authentic Trappist Product" (ATP) label to protect the name against commercial imitators. To carry the label, products must be made within the immediate surroundings of the abbey, production must be carried out or supervised by the monastic community, and profits must be destined for the monks' upkeep, charitable work and the needs of the monastery. Beers brewed in a Trappist style by secular breweries are marketed as "abbey beers" instead.

Styles

Belgian Trappist breweries named their beers by a simple hierarchy of strength. An enkel (single) is the lightest, once the monks' own daily beer; a dubbel is a brown ale of around 6–7% ABV typically brewed with dark candy sugar; a pale, golden tripel — popularised by Westmalle in 1934 — reaches about 9%; and quadrupel denotes the strongest, darkest ales, such as Rochefort 10 and Westvleteren 12, the latter often rated among the world's finest beers. Orval is distinctive for its use of the wild yeast Brettanomyces, which gives the beer a dry, complex character as it ages.

History

Monasteries have brewed since the Middle Ages, both for the monks and for travellers. The French Revolution and the two World Wars closed or destroyed many abbey breweries; the survivors revived brewing after 1945, and Chimay (from 1862) pioneered large-scale sale of abbey-brewed beer. The growing popularity of "Trappist" as a marketing term for unrelated breweries led to the legal protections and the ITA label of 1997.
