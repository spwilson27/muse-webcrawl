The Landsknechte (singular Landsknecht) were German mercenary soldiers who formed the bulk of the Holy Roman Empire's Imperial Army during the late 15th and 16th centuries. Raised by Emperor Maximilian I from 1486 onward in imitation of the formidable Swiss mercenary pikemen, they fought all over Europe — above all in the Italian Wars — and became famous both for their battlefield effectiveness and for their flamboyant, slashed and parti-coloured clothing, which was exempt from the sumptuary laws of the time.

Organisation and warfare

Landsknechte fought in dense pike squares modelled on Swiss practice, supported by halberdiers, arquebusiers, and the Doppelsöldner — "double-pay men" who took the most dangerous front-rank positions, some wielding the great two-handed Zweihänder sword to break into enemy pike formations. Their short sidearm, the Katzbalger, became emblematic. Companies were recruited by contractor-colonels under an Imperial patent and were notorious for indiscipline and mutiny when pay fell into arrears.

Decline

Landsknechte achieved lasting notoriety for the Sack of Rome in 1527, when unpaid Imperial troops, many of them Landsknechte, mutinied and plundered the city. As warfare professionalised and their Swiss-style tactics were copied everywhere, their distinctiveness faded in the later 16th century, though German mercenary infantry in their tradition served throughout the Thirty Years' War and beyond.
