Pilate Stone

The Pilate Stone is a damaged block of carved limestone (82 cm × 65 cm) discovered in 1961 at Caesarea Maritima in Israel. It bears a partially preserved Latin inscription mentioning Pontius Pilate, the prefect of Judaea who, according to the Gospels, presided at the trial of Jesus. It is the only known archaeological artifact that contemporaneously attests Pilate's existence and office.

Discovery and inscription

The stone was found by an Italian archaeological team directed by Antonio Frova, reused as a step in the Roman theatre of Caesarea. The inscription, in four lines, is usually reconstructed as a dedication of a building (a "Tiberieum") by "[Pont]ius Pilate, [prefect] of Judaea". From it scholars learned that Pilate's official title was praefectus (prefect), not the procurator later used by Tacitus—a detail matching the Gospels' vaguer term for governor.

Significance

Dated to Pilate's governorship (AD 26–36), the stone provides independent confirmation of a central New Testament figure and illustrates Roman administration in Judaea. The original is held by the Israel Museum in Jerusalem; a replica stands at Caesarea.
