Instrumentation amplifier

An instrumentation amplifier (sometimes shorthanded as in-amp or InAmp) is a type of differential amplifier that has been outfitted with input buffer amplifiers, which eliminate the need for input impedance matching and thus make the amplifier particularly suitable for use in measurement and test equipment. Additional characteristics include very low DC offset, low drift, low noise, very high open-loop gain, very high common-mode rejection ratio, and very high input impedances. Instrumentation amplifiers are used where great accuracy and stability of the circuit both short- and long-term are required.

Circuit

The most commonly used instrumentation amplifier circuit is the three op-amp design. Two non-inverting amplifiers form a differential input stage whose gain is set by a single resistor Rgain connected between the two inverting inputs; a third op-amp wired as a differential (subtractor) stage converts the differential signal to a single-ended output and rejects the common-mode component. The gain of the first stage is 1 + 2R/Rgain, and because only one resistor must be changed, the overall gain can be adjusted precisely without disturbing the symmetry of the circuit.

Properties

  • High input impedance at both inputs, typically 109 Ω or more, so sensor sources are not loaded.
  • High common-mode rejection ratio (CMRR), often exceeding 100 dB, rejecting interference such as mains hum picked up equally by both leads.
  • Gain set by a single external resistor, or programmed digitally in integrated versions.
  • Low offset voltage and drift, important for amplifying microvolt-level signals.

Applications

Instrumentation amplifiers are widely used in industrial and medical measurement: amplifying the output of strain-gauge bridges, thermocouples and pressure transducers, and in biomedical front ends for ECG and EEG signals, where tiny differential signals sit on large common-mode voltages. They are available as integrated circuits such as the classic INA128 and AD620 families.
