Externality

In economics, an externality is an indirect cost or benefit to an uninvolved third party that arises as an effect of another party's activity. Externalities are a central example of market failure, because the party creating them does not bear their full consequences, so market prices misstate true social costs and benefits.

Types

Negative externalities impose costs: air and water pollution, traffic congestion, noise, antibiotic resistance, and second-hand smoke. Positive externalities confer benefits: education spillovers, vaccination (which protects others through herd immunity), beekeeping that pollinates neighbouring orchards, and research whose results spread beyond the firm that funded it. Where positive externalities exist, markets underproduce the good; where negative ones exist, they overproduce it.

Remedies

Arthur Pigou proposed corrective taxes — Pigouvian taxes — equal to the external damage, a logic embodied in carbon taxes and congestion charges. Ronald Coase argued in "The Problem of Social Cost" (1960) that, with well-defined property rights and negligible transaction costs, private bargaining can reach efficient outcomes regardless of who holds the rights; where bargaining is impractical, governments turn to regulation, tradable permits such as cap-and-trade systems, subsidies for positive externalities, and liability rules. Policies like extended producer responsibility internalize disposal costs into product prices.
