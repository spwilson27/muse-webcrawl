Stereotype (printing)

Invented: 18th century (William Ged, c. 1725)
Material: Type metal cast from a mould
Uses: Newspapers, books, reprints

In printing, a stereotype is a solid plate of type metal, cast from a mould taken from a surface composed of set type and other elements. The plate is a single rigid copy of the whole page or forme, which can be printed from repeatedly instead of the original movable type.

Process and use

A mould (or matrix) is taken of the set type — historically in plaster, later and most commonly in papier-mâché, called a flong — and type metal is cast against it. Stereotyping, experimented with by William Ged in Edinburgh around 1725 and widely adopted in the 19th century, allowed publishers to preserve pages for reprinting, to distribute duplicate plates to printers in different cities (enabling simultaneous newspaper editions), and to free the expensive movable type for new composition. Curved stereotype plates were essential to the high-speed rotary presses of mass-circulation newspapers.

Legacy of the word

Because a stereotype reproduced the same text identically and unchangeably, the word — like its synonym cliché, originally a French printers' term for such a plate — passed into general use for a fixed, widely held, oversimplified idea or image, the sense familiar today in social psychology.
