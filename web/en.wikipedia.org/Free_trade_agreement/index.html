Free trade agreement

Free trade agreement
TypeTrade pact between two or more countries
EffectReduces or eliminates tariffs and other trade barriers among members
ExamplesUSMCA, CPTPP, RCEP, EU single market (deeper integration)

A free trade agreement (FTA) is a pact between two or more countries to reduce or eliminate barriers to trade among them — principally tariffs and import quotas — usually alongside rules on services, investment, intellectual property, government procurement, and dispute settlement. Unlike a customs union, members of a free trade area keep their own external tariffs toward non-members, which makes rules of origin necessary to prevent goods entering through the lowest-tariff member.

Scope and structure

Modern FTAs go far beyond border taxes. Chapters typically cover sanitary and phytosanitary measures, technical standards, competition, labour and environmental commitments, digital trade, and investor protection. Agreements may be bilateral (such as the Australia–United States FTA) or regional and "mega-regional", such as the Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership (CPTPP) and the Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership (RCEP) in Asia-Pacific, the world's largest by GDP share.

Economics

Economists generally find that FTAs create trade among members while also diverting some trade from more efficient outsiders — Viner's classic distinction between trade creation and trade diversion. Net welfare effects depend on the members' economic structures; adjustment costs fall unevenly on workers and industries exposed to new competition, fuelling political debate. FTAs are permitted under WTO rules (GATT Article XXIV and GATS Article V) provided they cover substantially all trade and do not raise barriers to outsiders.

History

The 1860 Cobden–Chevalier Treaty between Britain and France is an early landmark. Post-1945 liberalisation proceeded mainly through the multilateral GATT/WTO rounds, but from the 1980s and especially after the stalling of the Doha Round, countries increasingly turned to bilateral and regional deals, producing a dense "spaghetti bowl" of overlapping agreements. Political backlash in the 2010s — including the renegotiation of NAFTA as the USMCA — showed FTAs' continuing controversy.
