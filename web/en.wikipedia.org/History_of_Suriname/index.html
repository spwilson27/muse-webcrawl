History of Suriname

The history of Suriname traces South America's smallest sovereign state from its Indigenous peoples through English and Dutch colonization, a plantation economy built on enslaved labor, and immigration from India, Indonesia, and China, to independence from the Netherlands in 1975 and the turbulent decades that followed.

Colonization

Arawak and Carib peoples inhabited the Guianas long before European arrival. The English established the first lasting colony in 1650 under Francis Willoughby. In 1667, by the Treaty of Breda, the Dutch retained Suriname in exchange for New Netherland (including New Amsterdam, present-day New York) — a famous colonial swap. Jewish refugees from Brazil and elsewhere also settled, founding Jodensavanne, one of the oldest Jewish agricultural communities in the Americas.

Plantation era and emancipation

Suriname's plantations produced sugar, coffee, cocoa, and cotton using enslaved Africans, whose harsh treatment provoked Maroon communities formed by escapees in the interior; treaties with Maroon nations were signed in the 1760s. Slavery was abolished in 1863, followed by a decade of state supervision. Labor shortages were met with indentured workers from British India (from 1873) and Dutch Indonesia (from 1890), giving Suriname its characteristic ethnic diversity.

Autonomy and independence

Suriname became an autonomous country within the Kingdom of the Netherlands in 1954. Full independence followed on 25 November 1975 under Prime Minister Henck Arron. A military coup in 1980 brought Dési Bouterse to power; the December murders of 1982 and the Interior War (1986–1992) against the Jungle Commando marked the decade. Democracy was restored in the 1990s, though Bouterse later returned as elected president (2010–2020) before being convicted for his role in the December murders.

Recent developments

Offshore oil discoveries announced in the 2020s promised a transformation of the economy, with first production expected later in the decade.
