Airy disk

In optics, the Airy disk (or Airy disc) and Airy pattern describe the best-focused spot of light that a perfect lens with a circular aperture can produce, limited by the diffraction of light. Instead of a point, a point source is imaged as a bright central disk surrounded by concentric rings of decreasing intensity.

Description

The pattern was first described by George Biddell Airy in 1835. The angular radius of the first dark ring is given by sin θ ≈ 1.22 λ/D, where λ is the wavelength of light and D is the aperture diameter. About 84% of the light falls within the central disk. The size of the Airy disk determines the resolving power of a telescope or camera: two point sources closer than roughly one Airy-disk radius cannot be distinguished, the Rayleigh criterion.

Applications

Airy disk size governs the performance of microscopes, cameras, and the human eye, and it is central to astronomy, where atmospheric seeing usually blurs star images well beyond the diffraction limit. Adaptive optics systems attempt to restore images toward the Airy pattern.
