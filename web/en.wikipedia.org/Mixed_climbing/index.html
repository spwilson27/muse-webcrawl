Mixed climbing

Mixed climbing is a combination of ice climbing and rock climbing, in which the climber ascends a route that alternates between bare rock, ice, and snow while wearing crampons and using ice tools. The techniques used on the rock portions — hooking tool picks on edges and torqueing them in cracks — are known as dry-tooling when practiced on dry rock alone.

History

Mixed ground has always been part of alpine climbing, but modern mixed climbing as a discipline emerged in the 1990s, when climbers such as Jeff Lowe climbed routes like Octopussy (1994) in Vail, Colorado, pushing standards with leashless tools, monopoint crampons, and heel spurs. Competition mixed climbing and winter climbing events further developed the sport, and purpose-built dry-tooling crags and gyms appeared.

Grading

Mixed routes are graded on the M scale (M1–M16+), comparable in difficulty conceptually to the rock climbing Yosemite Decimal System: the grade reflects the strength, technique, and commitment required, including the awkwardness of tool placements. The hardest confirmed mixed ascents reach around M14–M16.

Equipment and ethics

Climbers use technical ice axes (often leashless, with ergonomic grips), crampons with mono- or dual front points and heel spurs, helmets, and sometimes specialized fruit boots. Using metal tools and crampons on rock damages the surface, so dry-tooling is restricted to designated crags and generally considered unethical on established free-climbing routes.
