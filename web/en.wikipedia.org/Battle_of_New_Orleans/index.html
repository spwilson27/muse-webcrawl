The Battle of New Orleans was fought on 8 January 1815 between the British Army under Major General Edward Pakenham and the United States Army under Brevet Major General Andrew Jackson, roughly 8 km southeast of New Orleans in present-day Chalmette, Louisiana. The lopsided American victory—the last major battle of the War of 1812—made Jackson a national hero and eventually propelled him to the presidency.

Background

Britain's Gulf Coast campaign aimed to seize New Orleans and possibly challenge American control of the Louisiana Purchase. In December 1814 a British fleet landed troops below the city. Jackson, commanding a patchwork of regulars, militia, free men of colour, Choctaw allies, and even Jean Lafitte's Baratarian pirates, fortified a line along the Rodriguez Canal between the Mississippi River and a cypress swamp. Preliminary fighting occurred on 23 and 28 December and 1 January.

The battle of 8 January

Before dawn Pakenham launched a two-pronged assault on the American earthworks. The main column advanced into concentrated artillery and rifle fire in the open; fascines and ladders needed to cross the ditch had been forgotten by the regiment assigned to carry them. Pakenham and his second-in-command, General Gibbs, were killed leading the attack, and the assault collapsed with some 2,000 casualties in about half an hour. A smaller British force across the river achieved a local success but withdrew when the main attack failed.

Aftermath

Unknown to both sides, the Treaty of Ghent ending the war had been signed on 24 December 1814, though it was not yet ratified; the battle therefore did not change the war's terms, but it decisively secured American possession of New Orleans in fact. The anniversary, 8 January, was celebrated as a national holiday for decades, and the battle entered American folklore in song and story.
