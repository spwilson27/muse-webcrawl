Cosmic distance ladder
PurposeMeasuring distances to astronomical objects
First rungsRadar ranging, stellar parallax
Middle rungsCepheid variables, RR Lyrae stars, red-giant branch tip
Far rungsType Ia supernovae, Tully–Fisher relation, redshift (Hubble's law)

The cosmic distance ladder (also known as the extragalactic distance scale) is the succession of methods by which astronomers determine the distances to celestial objects. No single method works at all scales, so each technique is calibrated on nearer objects and then used to measure farther ones, like the rungs of a ladder: errors at each step propagate to the next, making the ladder's calibration a central concern of cosmology.

The ladder builds from direct geometric methods to standard candles calibrated step by step.

Rungs of the ladder

The foundation is geometric: radar ranging measures distances within the Solar System, and stellar parallax — refined enormously by the Gaia satellite — gives distances to nearby stars. These calibrate standard candles, objects of known intrinsic brightness: Cepheid variable stars, whose period–luminosity relation Henrietta Swan Leavitt discovered in 1912; the tip of the red-giant branch; and Type Ia supernovae, which reach uniform peak luminosities and are visible across billions of light-years. Other secondary indicators include the Tully–Fisher relation for spiral galaxies and surface brightness fluctuations. At the greatest distances, redshift combined with Hubble's law supplies the distance.

The Hubble constant tension

The ladder underpins measurements of the Hubble constant, the present expansion rate of the universe. Distance-ladder measurements led by the SH0ES programme give about 73 km/s/Mpc, while values inferred from the cosmic microwave background under the standard ΛCDM model are about 67 km/s/Mpc. This persistent discrepancy, known as the Hubble tension, may indicate systematic errors in the ladder's rungs or new physics beyond the standard cosmological model.
