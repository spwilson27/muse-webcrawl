Honor code

An honor code or honour code is a set of rules or ethical principles governing an academic community based on the idea that people (in this case, students) can be trusted to act honourably. Those who are in violation of the honor code can be subject to various sanctions, including expulsion from the institution. Other forms of honor codes appear in the military, in professions, and in historical codes of conduct such as chivalry or bushido.

Academic honor codes

At many American colleges and universities, students pledge on enrollment not to lie, cheat, or steal, and often also to report violations by others (the "non-toleration" clause). Traditions associated with strict honor codes include unproctored examinations, take-home exams, and student-run honor councils or judiciary boards that investigate and sanction offences. The oldest such system in continuous operation is widely considered to be at the College of William & Mary, established under Thomas Jefferson's influence in the late 18th century. Other well-known examples include the University of Virginia, Princeton University, the California Institute of Technology, and the United States service academies.

Research by Donald McCabe and others has found that self-reported cheating is substantially lower at schools with traditional, well-supported honor codes than at comparable institutions without them, though the effect depends on a campus culture in which the code is genuinely embraced by students.

Military honor codes

The United States Military Academy at West Point maintains the Cadet Honor Code: "A cadet will not lie, cheat, steal, or tolerate those who do." Similar formulations are used at the other federal service academies and at state military colleges such as the Virginia Military Institute and The Citadel. Violations are adjudicated by cadet honor boards and can result in expulsion ("separation").
