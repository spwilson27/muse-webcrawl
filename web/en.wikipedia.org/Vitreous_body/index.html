Vitreous body

The vitreous body (Latin: corpus vitreum), also called the vitreous humor, is the clear gel that fills the space between the lens and the retina of the eyeball in humans and other vertebrates. It makes up about 80% of the volume of the eye.

[Image: A diagram of the human eye showing the vitreous body filling the posterior segment.]

Composition and function

The vitreous is about 99% water, with a network of collagen fibrils and hyaluronic acid giving it a gelatinous consistency. It helps maintain the eye's spherical shape, holds the retina against the retinal pigment epithelium, and acts as a shock absorber for the globe.

Clinical significance

With age the vitreous liquefies (syneresis) and may separate from the retina — a posterior vitreous detachment — producing "floaters" (muscae volitantes). Vitreous hemorrhage and its involvement in retinal tears can threaten vision, and a surgical procedure called vitrectomy removes and replaces diseased vitreous.
