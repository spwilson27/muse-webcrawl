Capital controls

Capital controls
FieldInternational economics, finance
ExamplesTransaction taxes, capital quotas, dual exchange rates, outright bans
See alsoCapital account, financial repression

Capital controls are residency-based measures such as transaction taxes, other limits, or outright prohibitions that a nation's government uses to regulate flows from capital markets into and out of the country's capital account. These measures may be economy-wide, sector-specific, or industry-specific, and may apply to equity, bonds, or foreign exchange trades.

History

Capital controls were a normal feature of the Bretton Woods system after the Second World War. They were progressively dismantled from the 1970s onward as financial liberalisation spread, but several countries reinstated them in response to financial crises, including Malaysia in 1998 and Iceland in 2008.

Arguments

Supporters argue controls can curb destabilising speculative inflows and give monetary policy more autonomy. Critics argue they distort markets, encourage corruption and evasion, and raise the cost of capital. The International Monetary Fund softened its earlier opposition in the 2010s, accepting temporary controls in some circumstances.
