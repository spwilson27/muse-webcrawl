Unit of selection

A unit of selection is a biological entity — such as a gene, cell, individual organism, group, or species — that natural selection is proposed to act upon. The question of which level or levels selection acts on has been one of the central debates in evolutionary biology since Darwin.

The debate

Darwin's original theory focused on the individual organism. In the 1960s, George C. Williams argued in Adaptation and Natural Selection that most adaptations could be explained at the level of the individual or gene, undermining loose appeals to group benefit. Richard Dawkins popularized the gene-centred view in The Selfish Gene (1976), distinguishing replicators (genes) from vehicles (organisms).

Multilevel selection

Later theorists, including David Sloan Wilson and Elliott Sober, developed multilevel selection theory, holding that selection can act simultaneously at several levels and that group-level selection can matter under defined conditions — for instance in the evolution of altruism and of eusocial colonies. Inclusive fitness and kin selection offer complementary accounting frameworks.

Related concepts

The debate connects to discussions of selfish genetic elements, cancer as cell-level selection, species selection in paleontology, and the major transitions in evolution, in which lower-level units became integrated into higher-level individuals.
