The Corinth Canal is a canal in Greece that cuts through the narrow Isthmus of Corinth, connecting the Gulf of Corinth in the west with the Saronic Gulf in the Aegean Sea in the east. Cut through solid limestone to a depth of up to 90 metres below the top of the cutting, it separates the Peloponnese peninsula from the rest of mainland Greece, technically making the Peloponnese an island. At only 21–25 metres wide, it is too narrow for most modern ships and is used mainly by tourist vessels, yachts, and small craft.

History

Rulers from Periander of Corinth in the 7th century BC dreamed of the canal; instead the ancients built the Diolkos, a paved trackway over which ships were hauled across the isthmus. Julius Caesar and Caligula considered digging, and Nero ceremonially began excavations with a golden shovel in AD 67 using thousands of prisoners, but the project was abandoned after his death.

Construction finally began in 1881 under a French company after designs by Hungarian engineers István Türr and Béla Gerster; the company went bankrupt and a Greek company completed the work, inaugurating the canal on 25 July 1893 in the presence of King George I. The canal saved small ships the 300 km circumnavigation of the Peloponnese but was plagued by landslides of its unstable walls, which have repeatedly closed it. In 1944 retreating German forces sabotaged the canal; American engineers cleared it by 1948. Distinctive submersible bridges at each end sink below the water to let ships pass.
