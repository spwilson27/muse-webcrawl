Debridement


Debridement of a necrotic wound

Debridement is the medical removal of dead, damaged, or infected tissue to improve the healing potential of the remaining healthy tissue. Removal may be surgical, mechanical, chemical, autolytic (self-digestion), or by maggot therapy.

The word comes from the French débridement, meaning to remove a constraint, originally used in the sense of unbridling. In wound care, debridement is a key step in managing chronic wounds such as pressure ulcers, diabetic foot ulcers, and venous leg ulcers, as well as burns and traumatic injuries.

Types

Surgical and mechanical

Surgical or "sharp" debridement uses sterile instruments such as a scalpel, curette, or scissors to cut away dead tissue, and is typically the fastest method. Mechanical debridement includes irrigation, hydrotherapy, and wet-to-dry dressings, which remove debris when dressings are changed.

Chemical and autolytic

Chemical (enzymatic) debridement uses agents such as collagenase to break down necrotic tissue. Autolytic debridement uses the body's own enzymes and moisture-retentive dressings to liquefy dead tissue; it is selective but slow.

Biological

Maggot therapy uses live, disinfected maggots of the green bottle fly to consume necrotic tissue precisely while leaving healthy tissue largely intact.
