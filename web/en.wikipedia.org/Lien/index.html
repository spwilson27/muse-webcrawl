Lien

A lien (from French lien, "bond" or "tie", from Latin ligamen) is a legal claim or charge upon property, granted by its owner or imposed by law, that secures the payment of a debt or the performance of an obligation. If the underlying obligation is not satisfied, the lienholder may enforce the lien, commonly through a judicial foreclosure sale of the property.

Types

Liens arise by agreement (consensual liens, such as mortgages), by statute, or by court judgment. Common varieties include:

  • Mechanic's liens (construction liens) securing builders, contractors, and suppliers for work or materials that improve real property.
  • Tax liens, imposed by governments for unpaid taxes, often enjoying priority over private claims.
  • Judgment liens, arising when a court judgment is recorded against a debtor's property.
  • Maritime liens, attaching to vessels for necessaries, crew wages, and salvage.
  • Possessory liens, such as an artisan's or innkeeper's right to retain a chattel until charges are paid.

Priority and perfection

The order in which competing liens are paid — their priority — is generally determined by the time of recording or "perfection", subject to statutory exceptions (for example, purchase-money security interests and tax liens). Under the Uniform Commercial Code in the United States, liens on personal property are typically perfected by filing a financing statement. A lien is discharged when the debt is paid, and the holder normally must record a release so the title is clear.
