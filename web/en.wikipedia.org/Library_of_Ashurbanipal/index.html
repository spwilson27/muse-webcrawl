The Library of Ashurbanipal, also known as the Royal Library of Nineveh, is a collection of more than 30,000 clay tablets and fragments inscribed with cuneiform texts, assembled in the 7th century BC by the Assyrian king Ashurbanipal at his palace in Nineveh. It is considered the first systematically collected library in the ancient world and one of the most important archaeological finds for the study of ancient Mesopotamia.

Contents

Ashurbanipal, who prided himself on his literacy, ordered scribes to copy and collect texts from across his empire and from conquered Babylonia. The collection includes royal inscriptions, chronicles, religious texts, hymns, prayers, incantations, medical treatises, astronomical and astrological reports, omen series, lexical lists, contracts and letters. Its most famous items are tablets of the Epic of Gilgamesh, including the "Flood Tablet" whose deluge narrative strikingly parallels the biblical flood story; George Smith's translation of it in 1872 caused a sensation.

Destruction and rediscovery

When Nineveh fell to a coalition of Babylonians and Medes in 612 BC, the palace burned; paradoxically, the fire baked many of the clay tablets hard, preserving them. The ruins were excavated from 1849 by Austen Henry Layard and later by Hormuzd Rassam, who in 1853 uncovered further thousands of tablets. The bulk of the collection is now held by the British Museum in London, where the Ashurbanipal Library Project continues to catalogue and publish the texts.

Significance

Because the library preserved comprehensive copies of Mesopotamian literature and scholarship, it underpins much of modern Assyriology; many works, such as the creation epic Enūma Eliš and the myth of Adapa, are known principally or entirely through its tablets.
