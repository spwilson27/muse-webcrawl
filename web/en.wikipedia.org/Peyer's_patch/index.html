Peyer's patch

SystemImmune system / lymphatic system
LocationSmall intestine, especially the ileum
Part ofGut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT)
Named afterJohann Conrad Peyer

Peyer's patches are organized lymphoid nodules found in the wall of the small intestine, particularly in the ileum. They are a major component of the gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT) and play a central role in immune surveillance of the intestinal tract. They are named after the Swiss anatomist Johann Conrad Peyer, who described them in 1677.

Structure

Humans typically have 30–40 Peyer's patches, elongated thickenings of the intestinal mucosa each containing numerous lymphoid follicles rich in B cells, surrounded by T-cell regions and antigen-presenting cells. The epithelium overlying a patch — the follicle-associated epithelium — contains specialized M cells (microfold cells) that lack the dense brush border of absorptive enterocytes and instead sample material from the gut lumen.

Function

M cells transcytose antigens, bacteria, and particles from the intestinal lumen to the immune cells beneath, allowing the gut immune system to monitor intestinal contents and mount responses — including production of secretory IgA — against pathogens, while maintaining tolerance to food antigens and commensal microbes. Peyer's patches are among the principal inductive sites for intestinal immune responses.

Clinical significance

Because they sample gut contents, Peyer's patches can serve as entry points for pathogens: Salmonella Typhi, Yersinia, and poliovirus exploit M cells, and prions are thought to replicate in Peyer's patches in variant Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease. In typhoid fever the patches may ulcerate and perforate. Their enlargement in Crohn's disease and the earliest lesions of that condition often appear over lymphoid follicles.
