Maurice Harold Macmillan, 1st Earl of Stockton (10 February 1894 – 29 December 1986) was a British statesman and Conservative politician who was Prime Minister of the United Kingdom from 1957 to 1963. Popularly nicknamed "Supermac", he became known for his unflappability, his pragmatic "One Nation" conservatism, and for presiding over a period of rising consumer affluence.

Early life and career

Born into the Macmillan publishing family, Macmillan was educated at Eton and Balliol College, Oxford, and served with distinction in the Grenadier Guards in the First World War, being wounded three times. He was MP for Stockton-on-Tees (1924–1929 and 1931–1945), where unemployment made him a critic of his party's orthodoxy; his book The Middle Way (1938) argued for a planned, mixed economy. After wartime service as Minister Resident in North Africa, he returned as MP for Bromley in 1945, and as Housing Minister (1951–1954) delivered the pledge of 300,000 houses a year. He then served as Defence Minister, Foreign Secretary and Chancellor of the Exchequer under Churchill and Eden.

Premiership

Macmillan succeeded Anthony Eden after the Suez Crisis in January 1957, restored relations with the United States, and won a large majority in the 1959 general election under the slogan echoing his 1957 observation that most Britons had "never had it so good". His government oversaw rapid decolonisation — in his 1960 "Wind of Change" speech in South Africa he acknowledged the growth of African national consciousness — and applied, unsuccessfully, to join the European Economic Community, an application vetoed by French president Charles de Gaulle in 1963. His final year was shaken by the Profumo affair, and he resigned on health grounds in October 1963, succeeded by Alec Douglas-Home.

Later life

Macmillan became chancellor of the University of Oxford and chairman of Macmillan Publishers, published six volumes of memoirs, and in 1984 accepted a hereditary earldom as Earl of Stockton. In old age he emerged as a critic of Margaret Thatcher's privatisation programme, famously likening it to "selling the family silver".
