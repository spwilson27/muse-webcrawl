William Shockley

William Bradford Shockley Jr. (February 13, 1910 – August 12, 1989) was an American physicist and inventor. As head of the Bell Labs solid-state group, he shared the 1956 Nobel Prize in Physics with John Bardeen and Walter Brattain "for their researches on semiconductors and their discovery of the transistor effect."

Transistor and Silicon Valley

Born in London to American parents and raised in California, Shockley earned his doctorate at MIT and joined Bell Telephone Laboratories in 1936. After wartime work on radar and anti-submarine operations research, he led the team whose semiconductor program produced the point-contact transistor in December 1947 and then his own junction transistor design, the foundation of modern electronics. In 1955 he founded Shockley Semiconductor Laboratory in Mountain View, California, recruiting a brilliant young staff; in 1957 eight of them — the "traitorous eight" — resigned over his abrasive management and founded Fairchild Semiconductor, seeding what became Silicon Valley.

Later controversy

From the 1960s Shockley became notorious for advocating eugenics and arguing that intelligence differences between races were substantially hereditary — positions condemned by mainstream science and damaging to his reputation. He died of prostate cancer in Stanford, California, in 1989.
