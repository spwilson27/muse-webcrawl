Phenomenalism

Phenomenalism is the view that physical objects cannot justifiably be said to exist in themselves, but only as perceptual phenomena or sensory stimuli situated in time and space. In particular, the view associates the existence of a physical object with being perceived — a modern descendant of George Berkeley's dictum esse est percipi ("to be is to be perceived").

The view

In its classical form, phenomenalism holds that statements about physical objects can be translated, without loss of meaning, into statements about actual and possible sense-data. A table, on this view, is a "logical construction" out of actual and hypothetical experiences: to say the table still exists unperceived is to say that observers would have table-like experiences under specified conditions. John Stuart Mill expressed a related idea in calling matter "the permanent possibility of sensation".

History

The view grows out of British empiricism: Berkeley's idealism, David Hume's scepticism about substance, and Kant's distinction between phenomena and noumena. In the 20th century it was developed within logical positivism and empiricism by Ernst Mach (whose "elements" were sensations), Bertrand Russell (logical constructions in Our Knowledge of the External World), C. I. Lewis, and A. J. Ayer, who defended a linguistic version in Language, Truth and Logic (1936) and later writings.

Criticisms

G. E. Moore's "A Defence of Common Sense" and his earlier "refutation of idealism" pressed the claims of realism. Roderick Chisholm argued that the translation programme fails: sense-datum statements themselves presuppose reference to perceivers and their bodies, and the required hypothetical statements cannot be fully specified without circularity. Phenomenalism fell from favour with the decline of verificationism, though related ideas survive in constructive empiricism, structuralism about perception, and some versions of idealism and pragmatism.
