Pueblo Revolt

The Pueblo Revolt of 1680, also known as Popé's Rebellion or Popay's Rebellion, was an uprising of most of the indigenous Pueblo people against the Spanish colonizers in the province of Santa Fe de Nuevo México, in present-day New Mexico. Coordinated by the Tewa religious leader Popé (Po'pay), the revolt killed some 400 Spaniards, including 21 Franciscan missionaries, and drove the remaining 2,000 settlers south to El Paso. The Pueblos then governed themselves independently for twelve years, until the Spanish reconquest under Diego de Vargas began in 1692.

Causes

Decades of grievances fueled the revolt: the encomienda system's demands for tribute and labour, drought and famine in the 1670s, Apache and Navajo raids the Spanish could not prevent, and aggressive suppression of Pueblo religion — in 1675, Governor Juan Francisco Treviño arrested 47 Pueblo medicine men, hanging three and publicly whipping the rest, including Popé.

The revolt

Popé organised a pan-Pueblo alliance from Taos, using runners carrying knotted cords to synchronise the rising for August 10, 1680. Missions were burned, churches desecrated, and Santa Fe besieged; the survivors fled south. It remains one of the most successful Indigenous uprisings against a European colonial power in North America.

Legacy

The revolt secured lasting concessions: Spanish authorities thereafter tolerated Pueblo religion and land grants and reduced labour demands. In 2005, a statue of Popé became one of New Mexico's two contributions to the National Statuary Hall in the U.S. Capitol.
