French Polish

French polishing is a wood finishing technique that results in a very high gloss surface, with a deep colour and chatoyancy. It consists of applying many thin coats of shellac dissolved in alcohol using a rubbing pad lubricated with oil. The technique originated in France in the early 19th century and became the standard finish for fine furniture and musical instruments during the Victorian era.

Process

The finish is built up with a pad known as a rubber, fad, or tampon, made of wadding wrapped in cotton or linen cloth. The pad is charged with shellac and a small amount of oil, then applied to the wood in circular and figure-eight motions. Stages of the process include charging, bodying up, and spiriting off, in which a nearly dry pad removes oil and produces the final mirror-like sheen. The work is slow and highly skilled, and the resulting film is very thin compared with modern varnishes or lacquers.

Characteristics

French polish produces a finish prized for its depth and clarity, and it is readily repairable because new shellac dissolves and bonds with the old. Its disadvantages are vulnerability to heat, water, and alcohol, all of which can mark the surface, and its labour intensity, which has led most commercial furniture production to adopt sprayed finishes. The technique remains in use by restorers and luthiers, and shellac-based French polish is standard on many classical guitars.
