Flamboyant

Flamboyant (from French flamboyer, "to flame") is a form of late Gothic architecture that flourished in France from about 1370 to the mid-16th century, and in Spain and Portugal for somewhat longer. It is characterised by extravagant, flame-like tracery in windows, double curves forming ogee shapes, and a profusion of carved ornament. The term was coined — originally with condescending intent — in the early nineteenth century, but has since become the standard label for the style, known in England partly in parallel with the Perpendicular style, and related to the German Sondergotik.

Characteristics

The hallmark of Flamboyant design is the soufflet and mouchette: flowing, teardrop- and flame-shaped openings in window tracery generated by double-curved (ogee) arcs meeting in sharp points. Other features include the three-centred "basket-handle" arch (arc en anse de panier), pendant keystones, lierne star vaults, steeply pitched gables filled with tracery, and pinnacles, crockets, and finials covering vertical surfaces. Flamboyant buildings often dissolve the wall into a screen of stone lacework, as in the west front of Rouen Cathedral, the church of Saint-Maclou in Rouen, the transept facades of Beauvais and Sens, and the Sainte-Chapelle of Vincennes. Notable late examples include the church of Brou at Bourg-en-Bresse and parts of the Basilica of Saint-Denis.

Spread and decline

The style spread from France to Spain, where it blended with local traditions in the Isabelline Gothic of the Catholic Monarchs' era (as at San Juan de los Reyes in Toledo), and to Portugal, where its exuberant maritime variant at the Jerónimos Monastery and Batalha is called Manueline. It reached the Low Countries, Bohemia, and Italy in modified forms. From the 1520s, Renaissance classicism imported from Italy gradually displaced it in France, though Flamboyant design persisted in parish churches and choir screens well into the sixteenth century, sometimes side by side with classical elements.

Revival

The Gothic Revival of the nineteenth century revived Flamboyant motifs, and the style's name passed into general English usage meaning "showy, exuberant, ostentatious" — a sense now more familiar than the architectural one, and also the source of the name of the royal poinciana tree, Delonix regia, called the "flamboyant tree" for its spectacular red-orange blossom.
