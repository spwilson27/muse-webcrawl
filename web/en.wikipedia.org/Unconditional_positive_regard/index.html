Unconditional positive regard

Unconditional positive regard, a concept initially developed by Stanley Standal in 1954, was popularised and given its best-known formulation by humanistic psychologist Carl Rogers. It is one of the three core conditions of person-centred (client-centred) therapy, alongside congruence and empathic understanding: warm acceptance of and caring for the client as a person, without conditions, judgement or disapproval of particular feelings or behaviours.

In Rogers's theory

Rogers argued that psychological difficulties arise when a person's self-concept is shaped by "conditions of worth" — the belief that one is lovable only if one meets others' standards. In therapy, unconditional positive regard provides an environment in which the client feels safe to explore experiences previously denied to awareness, allowing the self-concept and experience to come into congruence and the client's inherent actualising tendency to operate. Rogers described nearly unconditional regard as prizing the client as a separate person with permission to have their own feelings and experiences.

Role in development

The concept also applies to child-rearing: children who receive unconditional positive regard are thought to develop healthier self-regard, whereas conditional regard can produce contingent self-worth and introjected values. Research in motivational psychology has broadly supported links between perceived unconditional acceptance and well-being.

Criticism

Critics note the practical difficulty of sustaining entirely unconditional acceptance and question whether the core conditions are sufficient, rather than merely helpful, for therapeutic change.
