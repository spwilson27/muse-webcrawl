Cellulitis

Cellulitis is usually a bacterial infection involving the inner layers of the skin. It specifically affects the dermis and subcutaneous fat. Signs and symptoms include an area of redness which increases in size over a few days. The borders of the area of redness are generally not sharp and the skin may be swollen. While the redness often turns white when pressure is applied, this is not always the case. The area of infection is usually painful.

Lymphatic vessels may occasionally be involved, and the person may have a fever and feel tired. The legs and face are the most common sites involved, though cellulitis can occur on any part of the body.

Cause

Cellulitis is usually caused by a bacterial infection. The most common bacteria are streptococci and Staphylococcus aureus. Bacteria enter through a break in the skin such as an insect bite, cut, or athlete's foot. Risk factors include a weakened immune system, eczema, diabetes, and obesity.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is usually based on symptoms and physical examination; blood cultures rarely identify the organism. Conditions that mimic cellulitis include deep vein thrombosis, stasis dermatitis, and necrotizing fasciitis, the last being a medical emergency with pain out of proportion to findings.

Treatment

Treatment is with antibiotics such as cephalexin, amoxicillin, or cloxacillin. In those allergic to penicillin, erythromycin or clindamycin may be used. When methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) is a concern, doxycycline or trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole may, in addition, be recommended. Elevation of the affected part and pain relief are also advised. Most cases improve within 48 hours of starting antibiotics; complications include abscess formation and sepsis.

Epidemiology

In 2013, cellulitis was the cause of about 1.5% of all hospital admissions in the United States, and globally it resulted in about 28,000 deaths in 2023.
