Statute of limitations

Legal area: Civil and criminal procedure
Purpose: Time limit for bringing legal claims
Related concepts: Laches, tolling, repose

A statute of limitations, known in civil law systems as a prescriptive period, is a law passed by a legislative body to set the maximum time after an event within which legal proceedings may be initiated. When the time specified in a statute of limitations passes, a claim can no longer be filed or, if filed, may be liable to be struck out.

Rationale

Limitation periods are intended to ensure that claims are brought while evidence is still available and witnesses' memories are fresh, to give potential defendants certainty, and to encourage plaintiffs to pursue claims diligently. The underlying principle is often expressed in the maxim that the law assists the vigilant, not those who sleep on their rights.

Application

Limitation periods vary by jurisdiction and by type of claim: contract and personal injury claims commonly carry periods of a few years, while the most serious crimes, such as murder, frequently have no limitation period at all. The running of the clock may be paused or "tolled" in circumstances such as the claimant being a minor, the defendant concealing the wrongdoing, or the harm not being discovered until later — the so-called discovery rule. Related but distinct doctrines include statutes of repose and the equitable doctrine of laches.
