Aaron Temkin Beck (July 18, 1921 – November 1, 2021) was an American psychiatrist and professor in the department of psychiatry at the University of Pennsylvania. He is regarded as the father of cognitive therapy and cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT). His pioneering methods, set out in the 1960s and codified in Cognitive Therapy and the Emotional Disorders (1976), are widely used to treat clinical depression and various anxiety disorders, and his self-report instruments — the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI), Beck Hopelessness Scale, Beck Anxiety Inventory, and Beck Scale for Suicidal Ideation — are among the most used measures in clinical psychology and psychiatry research.

Early life and education

Beck was born in Providence, Rhode Island, to Russian Jewish immigrant parents, the youngest of five children. After a childhood illness that nearly killed him, he developed fears he later worked to overcome. He graduated from Brown University in 1942 and earned his M.D. from Yale Medical School in 1946, then trained in neurology and psychiatry, initially practicing psychoanalysis.

Development of cognitive therapy

In the late 1950s and early 1960s, Beck set out to validate psychoanalytic accounts of depression empirically. His studies of patients' dreams and free associations instead found pervasive negative thoughts about the self, the world, and the future — the "cognitive triad" — leading him to posit that distorted, automatic negative thinking drives depressive symptoms. Turning against orthodox psychoanalysis, he developed a structured, collaborative, present-focused therapy in which patients identify, test, and modify maladaptive beliefs ("collaborative empiricism"). He extended the model to anxiety disorders, personality disorders, substance use, schizophrenia (in later recovery-oriented work), and suicide prevention, and demonstrated efficacy in some of the first randomized controlled trials of psychotherapy.

Later career and honors

In 1994 Beck and his daughter Judith S. Beck founded the Beck Institute for Cognitive Behavior Therapy in the Philadelphia suburbs to train clinicians worldwide. His awards included the Albert Lasker Award for Clinical Medical Research (2006) and the Gustav O. Lienhard Award. He was frequently described as one of the most influential psychotherapists of all time. Beck continued writing and research into his late nineties, publishing a recovery-oriented cognitive therapy for schizophrenia in 2020, and died at home in Philadelphia in 2021 at age 100.
