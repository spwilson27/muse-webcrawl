Plymouth Colony (sometimes Plimouth) was the first permanent English colony in New England, founded in 1620 and absorbed into the Province of Massachusetts Bay in 1691. The colony was established by a group of Puritan Separatists known in history as the Pilgrims, who had fled religious persecution in England and had first sought refuge in Leiden, Holland.

The Pilgrims sailed aboard the Mayflower, landing first near present-day Provincetown on Cape Cod in November 1620 before settling at Plymouth. Before disembarking they signed the Mayflower Compact, an agreement to form a self-governing body politic, which became a foundational document of American self-government.

History

The first winter was devastating: roughly half of the 102 passengers died from disease, exposure, and scurvy. In the spring of 1621, the survivors were aided by Native Americans of the Wampanoag Confederacy, notably Squanto (Tisquantum), who taught them to cultivate corn, extract sap from maple trees, catch fish, and avoid poisonous plants. That autumn the colonists celebrated a harvest feast with the Wampanoag that is traditionally regarded as the first Thanksgiving.

In 1621 the colony received a patent from the Council for New England. William Bradford served as governor for most of the colony's history, for a total of some 30 years. The colony gradually expanded across southeastern Massachusetts, founding towns such as Duxbury, Marshfield, Scituate, and Barnstable.

Relations with Native peoples deteriorated over time, culminating in King Philip's War (1675–1678), one of the bloodiest conflicts per capita in American history, which devastated both the colony and the Wampanoag and their allies. In 1691, Plymouth Colony was merged with the Massachusetts Bay Colony and other territories to form the Province of Massachusetts Bay, losing its separate existence.

Government and society

Plymouth was governed under the Mayflower Compact and later patents, with a governor and assistants elected annually by the freemen of the colony. Its legal code, compiled in the 1636 Book of the General Laws, was notable for guaranteeing trial by jury and rejecting primogeniture in inheritance. The colony's economy rested on agriculture, fishing, fur trading, and later timber.

Legacy

Plymouth Colony occupies a central place in American historical memory. Plymouth Rock, the Mayflower, and the Thanksgiving narrative became enduring national symbols, and Bradford's journal Of Plymouth Plantation remains a key primary source for early American history.
