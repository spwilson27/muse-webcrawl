Parchment

Parchment is a writing material made from specially prepared animal skins — principally calf, sheep, or goat — that have been limed, scraped, and dried under tension. Unlike leather, parchment is not tanned. It was the dominant writing surface in Europe and the Near East from late antiquity through the Middle Ages, until paper gradually displaced it.

Etymology and origins

The word derives from the Latin pergamena, traditionally linked to the city of Pergamon in Asia Minor, where, according to a story repeated by Pliny the Elder, parchment was developed when Egypt restricted exports of papyrus. In practice, prepared skins had been used for writing much earlier, but Pergamon became a celebrated centre of production.

Manufacture

Skins are soaked in a lime solution to loosen hair and flesh, scraped clean with a crescent-shaped knife (a lunellum), then stretched on a wooden frame and dried under tension, which aligns the collagen fibres and produces a smooth, pale surface. The finest parchment, made from calfskin, is called vellum.

Use

Parchment was used for scrolls and, later, for codices — the bound-book format that spread with Christianity. Because it is durable and can be scraped and reused, erased and overwritten sheets are known as palimpsests. Parchment is still used today for ceremonial documents, Torah scrolls, diplomas, and, historically, acts of the British Parliament. See also manuscript and paper.
