Pays de la Loire (French pronunciation: [pe.i də la lwaʁ], "Loire Lands") is one of the eighteen administrative regions of France, in the west of the country on the Atlantic coast. Created in the post-war period from parts of the historic provinces of Brittany, Anjou, Maine, and Poitou, it comprises five departments: Loire-Atlantique, Maine-et-Loire, Mayenne, Sarthe, and Vendée. Its capital and largest city is Nantes, followed by Angers and Le Mans.

Geography

The region takes its name from the Loire, France's longest river, which crosses it east to west before reaching the Atlantic at Saint-Nazaire. The landscape is mostly low-lying: the bocage of the Vendée and Mayenne, the vineyards and troglodyte villages of Anjou around Saumur and Angers, the Sarthe valley, and the marshlands of the Guérande peninsula and the Marais Poitevin fringe. The Atlantic coastline, about 450 km including sandy beaches and resorts such as Les Sables-d'Olonne and La Baule, supports a major tourism industry.

History and identity

Because its boundaries were drawn administratively in the 1950s rather than from a single historic province, Pays de la Loire has an unusually debated identity. Its capital, Nantes, was historically the chief city of Brittany, and a persistent movement campaigns for the reunification of Loire-Atlantique with the Brittany region; the Breton flag is commonly flown in Nantes, and its castle houses the museum of the city's history. The Vendée is remembered for the counter-revolutionary Wars of the Vendée during the French Revolution, a heritage showcased by the Puy du Fou historical theme park, one of France's most visited attractions.

Economy

The region has one of France's lowest unemployment rates and a diversified economy: agri-food processing, shipbuilding at Saint-Nazaire (Chantiers de l'Atlantique, builders of some of the world's largest cruise ships), aeronautics, insurance and services in Nantes, and the automotive industry around Le Mans, famous for the 24 Hours of Le Mans endurance race first held in 1923. The Loire Valley's western vineyards—Muscadet, Anjou, and Saumur—form part of the wider Loire wine region.
