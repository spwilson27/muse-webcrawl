Rulemaking

In administrative law, rulemaking is the process by which a government agency creates, amends or repeals regulations (rules) having the force of law. Because modern legislatures cannot specify every detail of policy, statutes commonly delegate authority to agencies to fill in the details within stated bounds. Rulemaking is distinguished from adjudication, in which an agency decides individual cases.

United States procedure

In the United States, informal rulemaking under the Administrative Procedure Act of 1946 follows "notice and comment": the agency publishes a notice of proposed rulemaking in the Federal Register, receives written public comments (now largely through the Regulations.gov portal), considers them, and issues a final rule with a statement of basis and purpose. Rules generally take effect at least 30 days after publication and may be challenged in court as arbitrary, capricious or beyond the agency's statutory authority.

Significant rules are reviewed by the Office of Information and Regulatory Affairs (OIRA) within the Office of Management and Budget, and major rules require cost–benefit analysis under executive orders dating to 1981. Congress may disapprove a rule under the Congressional Review Act of 1996. Other procedures include formal rulemaking (trial-type hearings, rarely required), negotiated rulemaking, in which affected interests jointly draft a proposal, and the issuance of interpretive rules and guidance, which are exempt from notice and comment but do not bind the public.

Other jurisdictions

Equivalent processes exist elsewhere: the European Union adopts delegated and implementing acts under comitology procedures; the United Kingdom uses statutory instruments subject to affirmative or negative parliamentary procedure; and many countries require public consultation for subordinate legislation. Critics debate whether rulemaking offers genuine democratic participation or merely advantages organised interests, while courts increasingly scrutinise the reasoning and evidence behind major rules.
