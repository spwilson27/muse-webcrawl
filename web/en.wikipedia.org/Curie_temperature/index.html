In physics and materials science, the Curie temperature (TC), or Curie point, is the temperature above which certain materials lose their permanent magnetic properties — a ferromagnetic material becomes paramagnetic. It is named after Pierre Curie, who showed in 1895 that magnetism disappears at a definite temperature.

Explanation

Below TC, neighbouring atomic magnetic moments align spontaneously within magnetic domains because exchange coupling overcomes thermal agitation. As temperature rises, thermal motion increasingly disrupts the alignment; at the Curie point the spontaneous magnetisation falls continuously to zero in a second-order phase transition. Above TC the material follows the Curie–Weiss law, retaining only field-induced (paramagnetic) magnetisation. The analogous transition in antiferromagnets is the Néel temperature, and in ferroelectrics the loss of spontaneous polarisation also occurs at a Curie temperature.

Applications

The Curie point is exploited in temperature self-regulation: Curie-point soldering irons and rice cookers use alloys whose magnetism vanishes at the desired temperature; in geology, the Curie depth (where rocks reach magnetite's Curie temperature) constrains models of the Earth's crust.
