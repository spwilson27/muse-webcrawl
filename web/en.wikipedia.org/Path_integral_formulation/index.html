Path integral formulation

The path integral formulation is a description of quantum theory that generalises the action principle of classical mechanics. Developed by Richard Feynman in the 1940s, building on work by Paul Dirac, it replaces the single classical trajectory of a system with a sum — a functional integral — over all possible trajectories, each weighted by a phase factor eiS/ħ, where S is the action.

Key idea

To compute the probability amplitude for a particle to travel from one point to another, one sums the contributions of every conceivable path between them. Paths near the classical trajectory have similar phases and interfere constructively; wildly different paths tend to cancel. In this way classical mechanics emerges in the limit where the action is large compared to ħ. The formulation gives an intuitive picture of phenomena such as the double-slit experiment, where the particle's amplitude explores both slits.

Quantum field theory

In quantum field theory the path integral sums over all field configurations. It provides the natural framework for perturbation theory and Feynman diagrams, for gauge theories, and for non-perturbative methods such as lattice QCD, where the integral (rotated to imaginary time) is evaluated by Monte Carlo simulation. The formulation also connects quantum mechanics to statistical mechanics through the Wick rotation.

History

Feynman developed the approach in his 1942 PhD thesis and his 1948 paper "Space-Time Approach to Non-Relativistic Quantum Mechanics", partly inspired by Dirac's 1933 remarks linking the propagator to the classical action. Although mathematically less rigorous than operator methods, it proved equivalent to the Schrödinger and Heisenberg formulations and often far more powerful, especially in particle physics.
