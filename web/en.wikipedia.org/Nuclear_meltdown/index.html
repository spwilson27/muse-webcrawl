Nuclear meltdown

A nuclear meltdown, also known as a core meltdown or a partial core melt, is a severe nuclear reactor accident that results in core damage from overheating. The term was popularised in reference to the hypothetical event in which intensely hot nuclear fuel rods melt through the containment and, in the worst case, the ground beneath — colloquially the "China syndrome".

Causes

A meltdown generally begins with a loss-of-coolant accident. Even after a reactor is shut down, radioactive decay continues to generate heat — about 6–7% of full power initially, falling over hours and days. If this decay heat is not removed, fuel cladding oxidises in steam, producing hydrogen, and the fuel itself eventually liquefies, forming a molten mixture called corium that can breach the reactor pressure vessel.

Notable incidents

Partial meltdowns occurred at Three Mile Island Unit 2 in Pennsylvania in 1979, where roughly half the core melted but releases were small. The Chernobyl disaster in 1986 involved a steam explosion and fire rather than a classic meltdown, though corium formed. In the Fukushima Daiichi accident of 2011, loss of power after a tsunami caused meltdowns in three reactors. Earlier incidents include the Lucens reactor in Switzerland and the Fermi 1 fast reactor near Detroit.

Consequences

Consequences range from a contained core melt with negligible release to a large release of volatile fission products if containment fails. Modern reactor designs include core catchers and passive cooling systems intended to prevent or mitigate melting.
