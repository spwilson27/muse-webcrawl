Balassa–Samuelson effect

The Balassa–Samuelson effect, named after economists Béla Balassa and Paul Samuelson, who published the idea independently in 1964, explains why price levels tend to be higher in richer countries than in poorer ones, and why fast-growing countries often experience real exchange rate appreciation.

Mechanism

The economy is divided into a traded-goods sector (manufactures and other goods that compete internationally) and a non-traded sector (most services, housing, haircuts). Productivity growth tends to be much faster in the traded sector. As productivity and wages rise in traded industries, competition for labour pulls up wages across the whole economy, including the non-traded sector, where productivity has grown little. Service prices therefore rise. Because traded goods prices are pinned down by world markets, the overall consumer price level ends up higher in richer countries — a systematic deviation from purchasing power parity related to the Penn effect.

Implications

The effect implies that comparing incomes across countries at market exchange rates understates real living standards in poor countries, that catching-up economies should expect equilibrium real appreciation, and that inflation differentials within currency unions can arise from productivity differences rather than loose policy. Empirical studies generally find the relationship between income and price levels robust, though the size and sectoral details of the effect are debated.
