Man with a Movie Camera (Russian: Человек с киноаппаратом) is a 1929 Soviet experimental documentary film directed by Dziga Vertov. It presents urban life in Kyiv, Kharkiv, Moscow and Odesa over the course of a day, without actors, sets, intertitles or a conventional narrative, illustrating Vertov's "Kino-Eye" theory that the camera can capture truths the human eye cannot.

Techniques

The film is famous for its inventive editing and cinematography: double exposure, fast and slow motion, freeze frames, jump cuts, split screens, Dutch angles and tracking shots. Cameraman Mikhail Kaufman appears in the film, filming the city from daring vantage points.

Reception and legacy

Initially divisive in the USSR, the film is now widely regarded as one of the greatest documentaries ever made. In the 2012 Sight & Sound poll it was voted the eighth greatest film of all time, and it topped the magazine's documentary poll in 2014. Many later scores have been composed for it, including by the Alloy Orchestra and Michael Nyman.
