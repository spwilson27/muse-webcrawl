Abu Bakr Abdullah ibn Abi Quhafa (c. 573–634), known as Abu Bakr al-Siddiq ("the Truthful"), was the closest companion of Muhammad and, after the Prophet's death in 632, the first caliph of the Rashidun Caliphate. His daughter Aisha married Muhammad. Sunni Muslims revere him as the greatest person after the prophets; Shia tradition criticises his succession over Ali and his treatment of Fatimah's inheritance.

Early life and conversion

A wealthy Qurayshi merchant of the Taym clan, Abu Bakr was among the first converts to Islam and is credited with bringing many early Muslims — including Uthman and Zubayr — to the faith. He freed enslaved converts such as Bilal, accompanied Muhammad on the Hijra to Medina in 622, hiding in the Cave of Thawr, and fought in the early battles.

Caliphate

Chosen at the Saqifa of Banu Sa'ida on the day Muhammad died, Abu Bakr faced the Ridda wars as Arabian tribes renounced allegiance or withheld zakat; under generals including Khalid ibn al-Walid, his forces reunified Arabia. He then launched the first conquests into Sasanian Iraq and Byzantine Syria, setting the stage for the expansion continued under Umar and later the Umayyads. Tradition credits him with ordering the first collection of the Qur'an into a single manuscript after heavy losses of reciters at Yamama.

Death and legacy

Abu Bakr died in 634 after a two-year caliphate and was buried beside Muhammad in Medina, in what is now the Prophet's Mosque. Before dying he nominated Umar as successor. His brief reign preserved the Muslim community's unity and initiated its transformation into an empire.
