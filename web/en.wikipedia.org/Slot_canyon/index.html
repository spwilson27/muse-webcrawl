Slot canyon


Light beams in Upper Antelope Canyon, Arizona

A slot canyon is a long, narrow channel or drainageway with sheer rock walls that are typically eroded into either sandstone or limestone. A slot canyon has depth-to-width ratios that typically exceed 10:1 over most of its length and can approach 100:1. The term is especially used in the semiarid southwestern United States and particularly the Colorado Plateau. Slot canyons are subject to flash floods and commonly contain pools of water, some of them many meters deep.

Formation

Slot canyons are formed when a distinctive layer of rock is especially resistant to erosion but not to cutting downward, such as sandstone or limestone. They develop in arid regions where infrequent but intense rain events produce sudden flash floods. The water, carrying sand and debris, scours the rock and carves a narrow channel. Over thousands of years, differential erosion of harder and softer layers creates the fluted, sculpted walls characteristic of these canyons.

Distribution

Slot canyons are found all over the world in areas with low rainfall. Some of the best-known are in the Colorado Plateau of the American Southwest, in northern Arizona and southern Utah—including Antelope Canyon near Page, Arizona, perhaps the most famous slot canyon in the world, and the Narrows in Zion National Park. Other examples exist in Spain's Sierra de Guara, the Blue Mountains of Australia, and the mountains of Jordan, including the Siq at Petra.

Hazards

Slot canyons are subject to flash flooding. Rain falling many miles away can send a wall of water through a canyon with little warning; rainfall need not occur over the canyon itself. Hikers are advised to check weather forecasts for the whole watershed and to avoid slot canyons when rain is possible. In 1997, eleven tourists were killed by a flash flood in Lower Antelope Canyon.

Canyoneering

Exploring slot canyons, a pursuit known as canyoneering, may require technical skills including rappelling, swimming, and chimneying. Many slot canyons require permits, and some, including Antelope Canyon on Navajo land, can only be visited on guided tours.
