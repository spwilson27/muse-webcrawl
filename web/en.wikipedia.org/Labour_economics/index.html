Labour economics

Labour economics, or labor economics, seeks to understand the functioning and dynamics of the markets for paid labour. Labour is a commodity that is supplied by labourers in exchange for a wage paid by demanding firms.

Because labourers exist as parts of a social, institutional, or political system, labour economics is often regarded as a sociology or political science as well as economics.

Labour markets

The labour market functions through the interaction of workers and employers. In the neoclassical model, firms demand labour up to the point where the marginal cost of hiring equals the value of the marginal product of labour, while workers supply labour until the marginal disutility of work equals the wage. Fluctuations in demand, wage levels, and the bargaining power of workers and firms all shape outcomes.

Human capital and education

Human capital theory, developed by economists such as Gary Becker and Jacob Mincer, treats education and training as investments that raise a worker's productivity and hence earnings. The Mincer earnings function relates wages to years of schooling and experience.

Unemployment and job search

Labour economists study the causes of unemployment, including frictional unemployment arising from job search, structural unemployment from skill mismatch, and cyclical unemployment from aggregate demand shortfalls. Search and matching theory, associated with Peter Diamond, Dale Mortensen and Christopher Pissarides, models how unemployed workers and vacancies meet.

Institutions and policy

Trade unions, minimum wages, employment protection legislation, and collective bargaining are central topics. The empirical literature, including the influential work of David Card and Alan Krueger on minimum wages, has challenged simple competitive-model predictions.
