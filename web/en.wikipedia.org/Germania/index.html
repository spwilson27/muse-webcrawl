Germania was the Roman term for the historical region in north-central Europe inhabited mainly by the Germanic peoples. It extended from the Rhine in the west to beyond the Vistula in the east, and from the Danube in the south to the Baltic and North Seas. The name is used both for the Roman provinces of Germania Superior and Germania Inferior along the Rhine and, in the phrase Germania Magna ("Greater Germania"), for the unconquered territory beyond the imperial frontier.

Germania Magna, east of the Rhine, was never permanently conquered by Rome.

History

Roman contact intensified under Augustus, whose armies campaigned deep into Germania until the disaster of the Battle of the Teutoburg Forest (AD 9), in which Arminius of the Cherusci destroyed three legions under Varus; thereafter the Rhine became the frontier, defended by the limes. Two provinces west of the Rhine, Germania Superior and Germania Inferior, were formally organised around AD 83–90, with capitals at Mainz (Mogontiacum) and Cologne (Colonia Agrippina).

The fullest ancient description is Tacitus's ethnographic treatise Germania (De origine et situ Germanorum, c. AD 98), which depicts the Germani as a distinct people (indigenae), famed — in Tacitus's moralising mirror for Roman decadence — for freedom, martial valour and marital fidelity. Julius Caesar's De Bello Gallico had earlier drawn the Rhine as the boundary between Gauls and Germani. From the 3rd century AD, Germanic confederations such as the Alemanni and Franks pressed on the frontier, and during the Migration Period Germanic peoples reshaped the map of post-Roman Europe.

Other uses

"Germania" is also the name of the female national personification of Germany (a sword-bearing figure prominent in 19th-century art and monuments such as the Niederwald Germania), of Tacitus's book itself, and — in Nazi planning — of Albert Speer's projected redesign of Berlin as the world capital "Welthauptstadt Germania".
