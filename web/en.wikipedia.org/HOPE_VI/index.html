HOPE VI

HOPE VI is a program of the United States Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD). It is intended to revitalize the worst public housing projects in the United States into mixed-income developments. Its philosophy is largely based on New Urbanism and the concept of defensible space.

The program began in 1992, with formal recognition by law in 1998. As of 2005, the program had distributed $5.8 billion through 248 grants. By 2010, the program had essentially wound down, with HUD proposing to replace it with the Choice Neighborhoods program, which was authorized in that year.

History

The program's name is an acronym for "Housing Opportunities for People Everywhere". It grew out of the work of the National Commission on Severely Distressed Public Housing, established by Congress in 1989, which reported in 1992 that approximately 86,000 public housing units across the country were so deteriorated as to be uninhabitable.

HOPE VI grants funded the demolition of severely distressed public housing and its replacement with lower-density, mixed-income communities, often combined with housing vouchers for displaced residents. The program also funded community and supportive services intended to help residents achieve self-sufficiency.

Criticism

The program has been criticized for reducing the total stock of public housing: demolished developments were typically replaced with fewer units than were torn down, and only a portion of the new units were reserved for very low-income households. Critics argued that many original residents were displaced and never returned, and that the program contributed to gentrification. Supporters countered that the program replaced dangerous, isolated projects with safer, better-integrated neighborhoods and improved living conditions for those who remained.
