The Cambridge Apostles, also known as the Cambridge Conversazione Society, is an intellectual secret society at the University of Cambridge founded in 1820. Members, elected from among undergraduates, meet to discuss papers on philosophical and other questions in an atmosphere of complete candour.

History

The society was founded by George Tomlinson and a small group of friends; its original twelve members gave rise to the name "Apostles". Members who graduate become "Angels" and retain links to the society. Meetings traditionally took place on Saturday evenings, with sardines on toast — jokingly called "whales" — served as refreshment.

Members

Notable members have included the philosophers G. E. Moore, Bertrand Russell and Ludwig Wittgenstein, the economist John Maynard Keynes, the writers Lytton Strachey and E. M. Forster, and members of the Bloomsbury Group. In the twentieth century the society became notorious through the "Cambridge spies", as Guy Burgess and Anthony Blunt had both been Apostles.

Ethos

The society was shaped by Moore's Principia Ethica (1903), with its emphasis on personal relationships and aesthetic experience, and by a culture of absolute honesty in discussion — members were expected to pursue the truth regardless of convention, a spirit the society called "the Real".
