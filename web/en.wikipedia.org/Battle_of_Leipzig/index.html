The Battle of Leipzig, also known as the Battle of the Nations, was fought from 16 to 19 October 1813 at Leipzig in Saxony. The Sixth Coalition armies of Russia, Prussia, Austria, and Sweden, led by Karl Philipp, Prince of Schwarzenberg, and Gebhard Leberecht von Blücher, decisively defeated the French army of Napoleon I. With over 600,000 soldiers involved, it was the largest battle in Europe before the First World War, and Napoleon's most consequential defeat of 1813.

Background

After the destruction of his Grande Armée in Russia in 1812, Napoleon rebuilt his forces and fought the spring and autumn campaigns of 1813 in Germany. Following the expiry of the Truce of Pläswitz, the Coalition's strategy of avoiding battle with Napoleon personally while defeating his marshals steadily strained French resources. In October the converging Coalition armies closed on Napoleon around Leipzig, where he chose to give battle with his back to a limited number of river crossings.

Battle

Fighting raged around a ring of villages — Wachau, Möckern, Probstheida among them — from 16 October. On 17 October both sides paused largely to bring up reinforcements, which favoured the more numerous Coalition. On 18 October a massive Coalition assault compressed the French into Leipzig's suburbs; during the fighting, Saxon and Württemberg contingents defected to the Coalition. Napoleon began withdrawing across the single Elster bridge on the night of 18–19 October, but the bridge was blown prematurely on 19 October, trapping some 30,000 French troops in the city, including Marshal Józef Poniatowski, who drowned in the river.

Aftermath

The defeat ended French domination of Germany: the Confederation of the Rhine dissolved, and the Coalition advanced to the Rhine and into France itself the following year, leading to Napoleon's first abdication in April 1814. The enormous Monument to the Battle of the Nations (Völkerschlachtdenkmal), completed in 1913 for the centenary, still dominates the battlefield site.
