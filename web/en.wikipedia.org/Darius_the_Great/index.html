Darius the Great

Darius I (Old Persian: Dārayavaʰuš; c. 550 – 486 BCE), commonly known as Darius the Great, was the third King of Kings of the Achaemenid Empire, reigning from 522 BCE until his death in 486 BCE. He ruled the empire at its terrestrial peak, when it included much of Western Asia, parts of the Balkans and the Caucasus, most of the Black Sea's coastal regions, Central Asia, the Indus Valley, and Egypt.

Accession

On the death of Cambyses II in 522 BCE, a man Darius identified as the impostor Gaumata seized the throne claiming to be Bardiya, the king's brother. Darius, a member of a collateral line of the royal family, overthrew him with six Persian nobles and was proclaimed king. His own account, the great trilingual Behistun Inscription carved on a cliff in western Iran, then describes a year of rebellions across the empire, which he suppressed in nineteen battles.

Administration and reforms

Darius reorganized the empire into satrapies governed by satraps, with royal inspectors ("the king's eyes") to check them. He standardized coinage with the gold daric, reformed taxation, codified laws, built the Royal Road from Susa to Sardis with relay stations, and completed a canal linking the Nile to the Red Sea. He moved the administrative language to Imperial Aramaic and sponsored vast building programmes at Susa, Babylon, and Persepolis, which he established as a ceremonial capital.

Expansion and the Greek wars

Darius annexed the Indus Valley, Thrace, and Macedon, and campaigned against the Scythians across the Danube. After Athens supported the Ionian Revolt (499–494 BCE), he dispatched an expedition to punish Athens and Eretria; it was defeated at the Battle of Marathon in 490 BCE. Darius died in 486 BCE while preparing a larger campaign, leaving the invasion of Greece to his son Xerxes I.

Legacy

Darius is remembered as one of Persia's greatest administrators, remembered in Greek sources as the "shopkeeper" king for his fiscal organization, and in Iranian tradition as a lawgiver and a devout follower of Ahura Mazda and Zoroastrian ethics. His tomb is at Naqsh-e Rostam.
