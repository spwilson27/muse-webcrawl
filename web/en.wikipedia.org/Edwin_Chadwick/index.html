Edwin Chadwick

Sir Edwin Chadwick KCB (24 January 1800 – 6 July 1890) was an English social reformer noted for his work to reform the Poor Laws and to improve sanitation and public health in Victorian Britain.

Poor Law reform

A disciple of the utilitarian philosopher Jeremy Bentham, Chadwick served as secretary to the Royal Commission on the Poor Laws and was a principal author of its 1834 report, which led to the Poor Law Amendment Act 1834. The new system, administered by the Poor Law Commission on which Chadwick served as secretary, created union workhouses and applied the principle of "less eligibility" to deter dependence on relief.

Sanitary reform

Chadwick's most influential work was his Report on the Sanitary Condition of the Labouring Population of Great Britain (1842), compiled largely at his own expense. Drawing on evidence from across the country, it linked disease and early death to filthy living conditions — contaminated water, inadequate drainage, and overcrowding — and argued that investment in sewers, clean water, and refuse removal would save both lives and money. Though not a physician and a believer in the (incorrect) miasma theory, his administrative conclusions pointed in the right direction.

His agitation contributed to the Public Health Act 1848 and the creation of the General Board of Health, on which he served until political opposition forced his removal in 1854. Chadwick's abrasive, centralising style made him many enemies, but historians credit him with founding the English public-health movement and the modern idea of preventive, state-organised sanitation.
