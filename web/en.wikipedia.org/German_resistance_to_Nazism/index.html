The German resistance to Nazism comprised the individuals and groups in Germany who opposed the regime of Adolf Hitler between 1933 and 1945. Unlike resistance movements in occupied countries, it operated within the state that had launched the war, against a police apparatus of exceptional reach, and it never became a mass movement — yet it ranged from quiet non-cooperation to repeated attempts to assassinate Hitler.

Early opposition

The first resistance came from the banned political parties — Social Democrats, Communists, and trade unions — whose clandestine networks were largely smashed by the Gestapo in the mid-1930s. Churches produced individual critics such as Dietrich Bonhoeffer and Martin Niemöller, while conservative elites around the Abwehr, including Admiral Canaris and Hans Oster, began plotting a coup as early as 1938.

Civil and youth resistance

The White Rose group of Munich students, led by Hans and Sophie Scholl, distributed anti-Nazi leaflets in 1942–43 before its members were executed. The intelligence network known to the Gestapo as the Red Orchestra passed information to the Soviet Union. The Kreisau Circle, centred on Helmuth James von Moltke, met to plan a democratic post-Nazi Germany.

The 20 July plot

The most serious attempt came on July 20, 1944, when Colonel Claus von Stauffenberg planted a bomb at Hitler's Wolf's Lair headquarters as the trigger for a coup codenamed Operation Valkyrie. Hitler survived, the coup collapsed, and thousands of suspects were arrested; many were executed after show trials. In post-war Germany the resisters, long viewed ambivalently, came to be honoured as proof of "the other Germany."
