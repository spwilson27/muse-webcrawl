JPEG (pronounced "jay-peg") is a commonly used method of lossy compression for digital images, particularly for photographs. The name is an acronym for the Joint Photographic Experts Group, the committee that created the standard in 1992. JPEG files usually use the filename extensions .jpg or .jpeg, and the format has become the most widely used image format on the World Wide Web and in digital cameras.

Compression method

JPEG compression is designed to exploit limitations of human vision, discarding information the eye is least likely to notice. The typical (baseline DCT-based) encoding pipeline converts the image from RGB to the YCbCr colour space, separates brightness (luma) from colour (chroma), and often downsamples the chroma channels (chroma subsampling, commonly 4:2:0), since the eye is more sensitive to brightness than to colour detail. Each channel is divided into 8×8 blocks, which are transformed with the discrete cosine transform (DCT); the resulting frequency coefficients are quantised — the step in which most information is lost — and then entropy-coded using run-length and Huffman coding. The degree of compression can be adjusted, trading file size against visible quality; compression ratios of 10:1 are common with little perceptible loss.

Characteristics

JPEG supports 24-bit colour (about 16.7 million colours) and produces relatively small files for continuous-tone images, which made it ideal for the early web and for storing photographs. It does not support transparency, and it performs poorly on line art, text, and images with sharp edges, where ringing and block artefacts appear; formats such as PNG are preferred for those. Repeatedly re-saving a JPEG degrades quality because each save applies the lossy process again ("generation loss").

Standards and file formats

The JPEG standard itself (ISO/IEC 10918) specifies the compression method; the most common file formats wrapping it are JFIF (JPEG File Interchange Format) and Exif, used by digital cameras and storing metadata such as camera settings and thumbnails. Related standards developed by the same group include JPEG 2000 (wavelet-based, intended as a successor but never widely adopted on the web), JPEG XR, JPEG XS, and JPEG XL, finalised in 2021–2022, which aims to replace legacy JPEG with better compression, lossless recompression of existing JPEGs, and modern features.

Patent history

Although intended as a royalty-free standard, JPEG was the subject of patent claims in the 2000s, notably by Forgent Networks asserting a patent it acquired through Compression Labs; most claims were settled or invalidated, and the patents involved have since expired.
