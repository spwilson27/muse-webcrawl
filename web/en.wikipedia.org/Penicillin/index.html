Penicillins are a group of β-lactam antibiotics originally obtained from Penicillium moulds, principally Penicillium rubens. Penicillin was the first widely used antibiotic and its discovery is credited with revolutionising medicine, saving hundreds of millions of lives since the 1940s.

Discovery and development

In 1928 the Scottish scientist Alexander Fleming observed that a mould contaminating one of his staphylococcal culture plates had killed the bacteria around it; he named the active substance penicillin. A team at Oxford led by Howard Florey and Ernst Chain developed methods to produce and test it, demonstrating dramatic curative effects in 1941. Large-scale production in the United States during the Second World War — using deep-tank fermentation and a high-yielding strain found on a mouldy cantaloupe in Peoria, Illinois — made penicillin widely available by 1944. Fleming, Florey and Chain shared the 1945 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

Mechanism and use

Penicillins work by inhibiting the transpeptidase enzymes (penicillin-binding proteins) that cross-link peptidoglycan in bacterial cell walls, causing the walls to weaken and the bacteria to lyse. They are effective against many Gram-positive and some Gram-negative bacteria and are used to treat conditions including streptococcal infections, syphilis, meningitis and pneumonia. Dorothy Hodgkin determined the molecule's structure by X-ray crystallography in 1945, enabling later development of semi-synthetic penicillins such as amoxicillin and flucloxacillin.

Resistance and allergy

Bacterial resistance, often via β-lactamase enzymes that break the β-lactam ring, was noted even before mass use and has steadily limited penicillin's effectiveness. Penicillin allergy is one of the most commonly reported drug allergies, though true anaphylaxis is rare.
