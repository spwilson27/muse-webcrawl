Helium (from Greek: ἥλιος, helios, "sun") is a chemical element with the symbol He and atomic number 2. It is a colourless, odourless, tasteless, non-toxic, inert monatomic gas and the first in the noble gas group in the periodic table. Its boiling point is the lowest of any element, and it is the second lightest and second most abundant element in the observable universe, after hydrogen.

Discovery

Helium was first detected in 1868 by the French astronomer Jules Janssen and the English astronomer Norman Lockyer, who independently observed an unidentified yellow spectral line in sunlight during a solar eclipse and named the new element after the Greek word for the Sun. It was not isolated on Earth until 1895, when William Ramsay obtained it from the uranium mineral cleveite; near-identical work was done independently by Per Teodor Cleve and Nils Abraham Langlet in Uppsala.

Properties

Helium has the smallest atomic radius of any element and the highest ionisation energy. With a complete outer electron shell, it is chemically inert under all standard conditions. Liquid helium exists in two forms: helium I, a normal liquid, and below the lambda point of 2.17 K, helium II, a superfluid with zero viscosity and extraordinarily high thermal conductivity. Solid helium forms only under pressures above about 25 atmospheres. The most common isotope, helium-4, has a nucleus identical to an alpha particle; the rare isotope helium-3 is prized for cryogenics and neutron detection.

Sources and uses

On Earth, helium is generated by the alpha decay of heavy elements in the crust and accumulates in some natural gas fields at concentrations up to 7 percent, from which nearly all commercial helium is extracted by fractional distillation. Major uses include cryogenics — notably cooling superconducting magnets in MRI scanners and particle accelerators — pressurising and purging rocket fuel systems, shielding gas for arc welding, deep-sea breathing mixtures, leak detection, and lifting gas for balloons and airships. Because terrestrial helium that escapes to the atmosphere is lost to space and reserves are finite, supply shortages have periodically raised conservation concerns.
