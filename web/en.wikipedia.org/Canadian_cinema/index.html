Cinema of Canada

The cinema of Canada dates back to the earliest years of film. Canadian identity in cinema has been shaped by two parallel traditions — English-language film and French-language (largely Québécois) film — and by the dominance of the neighbouring United States industry, which has led Canada to rely heavily on public institutions such as the National Film Board of Canada (NFB) and Telefilm Canada to support domestic production.

History

The NFB, founded in 1939 under John Grierson, became world-renowned for documentary and animation. Feature filmmaking grew slowly; a breakthrough came in the 1970s with directors such as Claude Jutra (Mon oncle Antoine, 1971, often cited as the greatest Canadian film) and in Quebec with the work of Denys Arcand, whose The Decline of the American Empire (1986) and The Barbarian Invasions (2003, Academy Award for Best Foreign Language Film) brought international attention. English Canada produced internationally acclaimed auteurs including David Cronenberg, Atom Egoyan, and later Denis Villeneuve and Sarah Polley.

Industry

Toronto, Vancouver ("Hollywood North"), and Montreal are major production centres, both for Canadian films and for American productions attracted by crews, studios, and tax incentives. The Toronto International Film Festival (TIFF), held each September, is one of the world's most influential film festivals. Canadian productions receive only a small share of domestic box office, however, as exhibition has historically been dominated by American distributors and studios.
