Denisova Cave (Russian: Денисова пещера) is a cave in the Bashelaksky Range of the Altai Mountains in Siberia, Russia. It is one of the most important Palaeolithic sites in the world: the cave gave its name to the Denisovans, an extinct group of archaic humans first identified from DNA extracted from a finger bone found there, and it is the only known site where Denisovans, Neanderthals, and modern humans all lived.

Archaeology

The cave, about 28 metres long with several chambers, has been excavated since the 1970s. Its stratified deposits span the Middle and Upper Palaeolithic, with occupation beginning at least 200,000 years ago. The layers contain stone tools, animal bones, ornaments of bone, ivory, and ostrich eggshell, and a famous bracelet of green chloritolite; some of the sophisticated Upper Palaeolithic objects are difficult to assign confidently to Denisovans or to modern humans.

The Denisovans

In 2010 a team led by Svante Pääbo published the genome sequenced from "Denisova 3", a fragment of a girl's finger bone found in 2008, showing that it belonged to a previously unknown lineage, sister to the Neanderthals. Subsequent finds include teeth and a parietal bone fragment. Most remarkably, "Denisova 11" (nicknamed Denny), a bone fragment from an individual who died about 90,000 years ago, was shown in 2018 to be the first-generation offspring of a Neanderthal mother and a Denisovan father — direct evidence of interbreeding. Denisovan ancestry persists today, especially in Melanesian, Aboriginal Australian, and East and South Asian populations. Sediment DNA studies show the cave was occupied successively — and sometimes perhaps alternately — by Denisovans and Neanderthals over tens of thousands of years, with modern humans arriving by roughly 45,000 years ago.

Name

Local tradition associates the cave with Denis (Dionysius), an Old Believer hermit who is said to have lived there in the 18th century; in the Altai language the cave is called Ayu-Tash, "Bear Rock".
