Rib vault

A rib vault (or ribbed vault) is an architectural structure in which a vaulted ceiling is supported by a framework of arched ribs, with the spaces between the ribs filled by lighter masonry webs. It is one of the defining features of Gothic architecture, allowing taller, lighter and more richly lit interiors than earlier Romanesque vaults.

Development

Precursors appear in Islamic and Romanesque building, notably at Durham Cathedral (begun 1093), which has some of the earliest surviving rib vaults over a large church. Gothic builders from the 12th century, beginning around Saint-Denis and Sens, combined rib vaults with pointed arches and flying buttresses to concentrate loads on piers and open walls for stained glass.

Types

The simplest form, the quadripartite vault, divides a bay with diagonal ribs into four cells. Sexpartite vaults add a transverse rib, dividing the bay into six. Later Gothic produced decorative forms: tierceron vaults with intermediate ribs, lierne (stellar) vaults with short linking ribs forming star patterns, and fan vaults, a specialty of English Perpendicular Gothic seen at King's College Chapel, Cambridge.
