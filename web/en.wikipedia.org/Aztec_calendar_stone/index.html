The Aztec calendar stone or Sun Stone (Spanish: Piedra del Sol) is a late Mexica sculpture housed in the National Museum of Anthropology in Mexico City, and is perhaps the most famous work of Aztec sculpture. It measures 3.58 metres (11.7 ft) in diameter and 98 centimetres (3.22 ft) thick, and weighs about 24–25 long tons. Shortly after the Spanish conquest, the monolithic sculpture was buried in the Zócalo, the main square of Mexico City. It was rediscovered on 17 December 1790 during repairs on Mexico City Cathedral.

Iconography

Despite its popular name, the stone is not a working calendar; it is a richly carved cosmological monument. The central face is usually identified as the sun god Tonatiuh, or alternatively as the earth monster Tlaltecuhtli. Around the centre are the signs of the four previous world ages or "suns", each destroyed by a different catastrophe. The surrounding rings contain the twenty day signs of the sacred tonalpohualli calendar, calendrical and solar symbols, and two great fire serpents (xiuhcoatl) forming the outer border.

History

The stone was probably carved in the early 16th century, during the reigns of Moctezuma II or his predecessors, and may have served as a sacrificial platform (temalacatl) or as an altar associated with the Templo Mayor precinct of Tenochtitlan. After its rediscovery it was mounted on the wall of the cathedral, transferred to the National Museum in 1885, and moved to the new National Museum of Anthropology in Chapultepec in 1964, where it is the centrepiece of the Mexica hall.
