The Vilnius Ghetto was a World War II Jewish ghetto established by Nazi Germany in the Old Town of Vilnius, then in the Reichskommissariat Ostland. Between September 1941 and its liquidation in September 1943, it confined much of the city's Jewish population — Vilnius had been a major centre of Jewish culture known as the "Jerusalem of Lithuania." Most of its inhabitants were murdered, above all in the mass shootings at Ponary (Paneriai) outside the city.

History

Germany occupied Vilnius in June 1941. Mass killings at Ponary began within weeks, carried out by SS Einsatzgruppen and Lithuanian auxiliaries. In September 1941 the surviving Jews were forced into two ghettos; the smaller "Ghetto No. 2" was liquidated within weeks, its inhabitants murdered at Ponary. The larger ghetto, headed by Jacob Gens, pursued a strategy of survival through labour ("work for life"), and developed a remarkable cultural life — a theatre, library, schools, and an orchestra — even as successive "Aktionen" deported thousands to their deaths.

Resistance and liquidation

In January 1942, underground groups united as the Fareynikte Partizaner Organizatsye (FPO), whose manifesto by Abba Kovner was among the first to declare that Hitler intended to murder all of Europe's Jews. When the Germans demanded FPO commander Yitzhak Wittenberg in July 1943, the Judenrat surrendered him under threat of the ghetto's destruction. The ghetto was liquidated on 23–24 September 1943: several thousand were sent to labour camps in Estonia and Latvia, others to Sobibor and Ponary. A few hundred survived in hiding, camps, or with partisans in the forests.
