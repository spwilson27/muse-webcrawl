Ultra was the designation adopted by British military intelligence during the Second World War for wartime signals intelligence obtained by breaking high-level encrypted enemy radio and teleprinter communications at the Government Code and Cypher School at Bletchley Park. Ultra eventually became the standard designation among the Western Allies for all such intelligence. The name arose because the intelligence obtained was considered more sensitive than that designated by the highest British security classification then in use (Most Secret) and so was regarded as being Ultra Secret.

Much of the German cipher traffic was encrypted on the Enigma machine. Used properly, the plugboard-equipped version was virtually unbreakable; in practice, however, operator shortcomings, procedural flaws, capture of key tables, and other errors allowed Bletchley's cryptanalysts to exploit weaknesses. Similar success was achieved against the more advanced Lorenz cipher teleprinter traffic, code-named Fish by the British, which was attacked with the Colossus computer, the world's first programmable electronic digital computer.

Sources

Most Ultra intelligence came from reading German signals traffic, including:

  • Enigma-encrypted radio messages of the Wehrmacht, Luftwaffe, and Kriegsmarine
  • Lorenz-encrypted teleprinter traffic between the German High Command and army groups
  • Italian and Japanese ciphers, shared through cooperation with the United States

The foundations of the work were laid by the Polish Cipher Bureau, whose mathematicians — Marian Rejewski, Jerzy Różycki, and Henryk Zygalski — first broke Enigma in the early 1930s and shared their methods with Britain and France in 1939.

Use and impact

Ultra intelligence was distributed under extraordinarily tight secrecy to a small number of commanders. It played a role in the Battle of the Atlantic, the North African campaign, the Battle of Britain, and the Normandy landings, where deception operations such as Operation Fortitude used Ultra to verify that German intelligence had been misled. Historians have argued that Ultra shortened the war, possibly by as much as two years, though the precise extent of its effect remains debated. F. W. Winterbotham's 1974 book The Ultra Secret first publicly revealed the programme's existence.

Secrecy

To protect the source, the Allies took elaborate precautions: intelligence was never acted upon without a plausible alternative explanation for how the information could have been obtained, reconnaissance flights were staged to "discover" targets Ultra had already located, and distribution was restricted through Special Liaison Units. The secrecy held for nearly three decades after the war ended.
