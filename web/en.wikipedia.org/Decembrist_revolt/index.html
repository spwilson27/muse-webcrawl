The Decembrist revolt (Russian: Восстание декабристов) was an attempted military uprising in Imperial Russia on 26 December 1825 (14 December in the Julian calendar then in use), from which its participants took the name Decembrists. Some 3,000 soldiers led by army officers refused to swear allegiance to the new tsar, Nicholas I, and instead proclaimed their loyalty to his elder brother Constantine and to a constitution. The revolt was put down by loyal artillery fire the same day, and its leaders were executed, imprisoned, or exiled to Siberia.

Background

Many Russian officers who had campaigned across Europe during the Napoleonic Wars returned home with liberal ideas about constitutions, parliaments, and the abolition of serfdom. Secret societies — the Union of Salvation (1816), the Union of Welfare, and later the Northern Society in Saint Petersburg and the Southern Society in Ukraine — debated reform and, among some members, revolution. The Southern Society's Pavel Pestel drafted a republican programme, Russkaya Pravda, while the Northern Society's Nikita Muravyov favoured a constitutional monarchy.

The succession crisis

When Tsar Alexander I died unexpectedly at Taganrog in November 1825, a succession crisis followed: Grand Duke Constantine Pavlovich had secretly renounced his rights in 1822, and Alexander's manifesto naming Nicholas as heir had never been published. Troops initially swore allegiance to Constantine; when Constantine confirmed his renunciation, a second oath to Nicholas was scheduled for 14 December — the day the conspirators chose to act.

The uprising

On the morning of 14 December, officers led about 3,000 men of several Guards regiments to Senate Square, where they formed a square formation and refused the oath, shouting for "Constantine and Constitution" (Konstantin i Konstitutsiya). The designated leader, Prince Sergey Trubetskoy, failed to appear. Count Mikhail Miloradovich, the military governor who tried to talk the troops down, was shot by Pyotr Kakhovsky. Nicholas, who had been proclaimed emperor that morning, gathered loyal forces; after cavalry charges failed, artillery firing grapeshot across the frozen square broke the revolt by evening. A related rising of the Chernigov regiment in Ukraine, led by Sergey Muravyov-Apostol, was defeated in early January 1826.

Aftermath

An investigative commission interrogated 579 people. Five leaders — Pavel Pestel, Pyotr Kakhovsky, Sergey Muravyov-Apostol, Mikhail Bestuzhev-Ryumin, and the poet Kondraty Ryleyev — were hanged in July 1826; more than a hundred others were sentenced to hard labour and exile in Siberia, many followed voluntarily by their wives, the celebrated "Decembrist wives". Nicholas I's reign opened with intensified censorship and the creation of the Third Section political police, though the Decembrists became enduring heroes of the Russian revolutionary tradition, later praised by Alexander Herzen and Lenin.
