The Latin League (c. 7th century BC – 338 BC) was an ancient confederation of about 30 villages and city-states of the Latin peoples in Latium, the region around Rome in central Italy. Originally a religious association gathered around the sanctuary of Jupiter Latiaris on the Alban Mount and the cult festival of the Feriae Latinae, it developed into a military alliance for mutual defence against common enemies such as the Etruscans, Aequi, and Volscians.

Relations with Rome

After the overthrow of the Roman monarchy, Rome concluded the Foedus Cassianum (493 BC) with the League as a treaty between equals, providing for joint military command and an equal division of spoils. Over the following century Rome steadily grew in dominance; resentment at Roman hegemony led to the Latin War (340–338 BC), in which Rome and its Samnite allies defeated the League. In the settlement of 338 BC Rome dissolved the Latin League, annexing some cities outright and binding others individually to Rome with graded rights — the famous "Latin rights" (ius Latii) — a system that became a template for Rome's organisation of Italy and, later, the empire.
