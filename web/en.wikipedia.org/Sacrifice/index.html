Sacrifice

Offerings on altars were central to the sacrificial cults of the ancient world.

Sacrifice is the offering of material possessions or the lives of animals or humans to a deity as an act of propitiation or worship. The term derives from the Latin sacrificium, "to make sacred." Sacrificial rites have been documented in religions across the world, from the Vedic yajna of ancient India and the animal offerings of ancient Israel to the state cults of Greece and Rome.

Types and purposes

Sacrifices may consist of animals, foodstuffs, drink (libations), incense, precious objects, or, in some societies, human beings. Scholars have proposed varied functions: gifts to establish reciprocity with the gods, communion meals shared between worshippers and deity, expiation of sin, thanksgiving, and the maintenance of cosmic order. In ancient Judaism, sacrifices offered in the Temple in Jerusalem included burnt offerings, peace offerings, and sin offerings, ceasing with the Temple's destruction in 70 CE. Christianity reinterpreted sacrifice through the crucifixion of Jesus, and the Eucharist is understood by many traditions as a sacramental participation in that one offering.

Human sacrifice

Human sacrifice was practiced in a number of cultures, including those of Mesoamerica — the Aztecs conducted large-scale sacrifices to sustain the sun god Huitzilopochtli — as well as in ancient China, among the Carthaginians (a debated practice), and in parts of Polynesia and West Africa. Nearly all traditions eventually abandoned the practice. René Girard's mimetic theory famously argued that sacrifice originated in the scapegoating of a victim to end communal violence.

Modern usage

The word is also used figuratively for any costly surrender of something valued for a greater good, and technically in games such as chess, where a sacrifice is the deliberate loss of material for positional or tactical compensation.
