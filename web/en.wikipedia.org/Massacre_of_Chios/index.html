Massacre of Chios

The Massacre of Chios was the killing of tens of thousands of Greeks on the island of Chios by Ottoman troops during the Greek War of Independence in 1822. Chios, a prosperous Aegean island famous for its mastic trade and wealthy merchant families, had a population of roughly 100,000–120,000 before the massacre; afterwards only a fraction remained, with many killed, enslaved, or fled into exile.

Background

In March 1822, Greek revolutionaries from Samos landed on Chios and encouraged revolt against Ottoman rule. The island's notables had been reluctant, fearing reprisals, but Ottoman authorities in Constantinople treated the landing as rebellion and dispatched a fleet and troops under Kapudan Pasha Nasuhzade Ali Pasha.

The massacre

Ottoman forces landed in April 1822. Over the following weeks, troops and irregulars burned villages and the capital town, killed men in large numbers, and enslaved women and children, many of whom were sold in markets in Smyrna and Constantinople. Contemporary estimates commonly cite around 25,000 killed and 45,000 enslaved, though figures vary. In June, Greek admiral Constantine Kanaris retaliated with a fireship attack that destroyed the Ottoman flagship and killed the Kapudan Pasha.

International reaction

News of the massacre shocked Europe and strengthened philhellenic sentiment. The French painter Eugène Delacroix exhibited his large canvas The Massacre at Chios (1824), now in the Louvre, which became an icon of Romantic painting and of the Greek cause. Chian diaspora communities formed in Syros, London, and elsewhere, and survivors were gradually ransomed or returned under later amnesties.
