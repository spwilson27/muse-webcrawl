Suspension of disbelief

Suspension of disbelief is the avoidance — consciously or otherwise — of critical thinking and logic in examining something surreal, such as a work of fiction, for the sake of enjoyment. The term was coined by the poet and philosopher Samuel Taylor Coleridge, who suggested that if a writer could infuse a "human interest and a semblance of truth" into a fantastic tale, readers would suspend judgement about its implausibility.

Origin

In chapter XIV of the Biographia Literaria (1817), Coleridge described the plan of the Lyrical Ballads: Wordsworth would write of ordinary life so as to awaken attention to its wonder, while Coleridge's own poems would treat "persons and characters supernatural" in such a way "as to transfer from our inward nature a human interest and a semblance of truth sufficient to procure for these shadows of imagination that willing suspension of disbelief for the moment".

Use of the concept

The idea underlies the audience's acceptance of fantastic premises in literature, film, theatre and games: dragons, faster-than-light travel or talking animals are accepted as ground rules while consistency within the work's own logic is demanded. J. R. R. Tolkien, in his essay "On Fairy-Stories", offered a related but distinct account in terms of "secondary belief" created by a consistent secondary world, arguing that when the inner consistency fails, the spell is broken.

Criticism

Philosophers of fiction debate whether audiences ever really "believe" or need to suspend anything, proposing instead make-believe and pretence as better explanations. Critics also note the phrase is often used loosely to excuse plot holes, a use Coleridge's formulation — which still requires a "semblance of truth" — does not support.
