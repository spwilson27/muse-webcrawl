Moraceae

The Moraceae — often called the mulberry family or fig family — are a family of flowering plants comprising about 38 genera and over 1,100 species, widespread in tropical and subtropical regions with a few temperate representatives. Most are trees and shrubs, though the family also includes lianas and herbs, and most produce a milky latex.

Moraceae
Kingdom: Plantae
Order: Rosales
Genera: c. 38
Species: c. 1,100

Characteristics

Members of Moraceae typically have alternate leaves with stipules, small unisexual flowers, and compound or multiple fruits. The family's most spectacular genus is Ficus, the figs, whose tiny flowers are enclosed within a hollow receptacle (the syconium) and are pollinated, in one of nature's most studied mutualisms, by species-specific fig wasps.

Important genera

  • Morus — the mulberries, whose leaves feed silkworms and whose fruits are eaten fresh and dried.
  • Ficus — figs, including the common fig, banyan, and many rainforest keystone species whose year-round fruit sustains frugivores.
  • Artocarpus — breadfruit and jackfruit, staple starch crops of the tropics.
  • Maclura — including the Osage orange; Broussonetia — the paper mulberry used for tapa cloth and papermaking.
  • Cannabis and Humulus (hemp and hops) were historically placed in Moraceae but are now assigned to Cannabaceae.

Ecology and uses

Figs are keystone resources in tropical forests, fruiting when little else is available. Family members provide food crops, timber, rubber (from Ficus elastica), bark cloth, and ornamentals; mulberry underpins the silk industry, and upas and other genera yield potent latex poisons.
