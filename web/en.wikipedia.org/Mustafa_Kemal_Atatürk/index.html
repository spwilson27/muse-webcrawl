Mustafa Kemal Atatürk (c. 1881 – 10 November 1938) was a Turkish field marshal, revolutionary statesman, author, and the founding father of the Republic of Turkey, serving as its first president from 1923 until his death in 1938. He undertook sweeping reforms, which modernized Turkey into a secular, industrializing nation-state.

Military career

An Ottoman army officer, Mustafa Kemal distinguished himself at the Battle of Gallipoli (1915) during the First World War, where he commanded the division that repelled the Allied landing at Anzac Cove. After the Ottoman defeat and the Allied occupation, he organised a national resistance movement based in Ankara, leading the Turkish War of Independence (1919–1923). His forces defeated Greek armies in the west and secured international recognition of Turkey's borders in the Treaty of Lausanne (1923).

Presidency and reforms

On 29 October 1923 the Republic of Turkey was proclaimed with Mustafa Kemal as president. His reforms — collectively known as Atatürk's Reforms — included the abolition of the sultanate and caliphate, the adoption of European legal codes, the Latin-based Turkish alphabet (1928), secular education, the emancipation of women (including suffrage in 1934), and state-led industrialisation. In 1934 the Grand National Assembly granted him the surname Atatürk, meaning "Father of the Turks". His political ideology, Kemalism, rests on six principles ("the Six Arrows"), including republicanism, nationalism, secularism and statism.

Death and legacy

Atatürk died of cirrhosis at Dolmabahçe Palace in Istanbul on 10 November 1938 and was succeeded by İsmet İnönü. His mausoleum, Anıtkabir in Ankara, was completed in 1953. He remains a revered figure in Turkey, where his image appears on all Turkish lira banknotes and in public buildings, though his legacy is also debated in connection with the treatment of minorities and the one-party era.
