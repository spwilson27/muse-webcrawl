RepRap (short for replicating rapid prototyper) is an open-source project to build a desktop 3D printer that can print most of its own plastic components, so that owners can make further machines for others. Initiated in 2005 by Adrian Bowyer at the University of Bath, RepRap released its designs under the GNU General Public License and is widely credited with sparking the low-cost 3D printing revolution and the desktop maker movement.

History

Bowyer conceived RepRap as a self-replicating machine in the tradition of von Neumann's theoretical universal constructors. The first printer, Darwin, was released in 2007 and printed its first child part in 2008: a Darwin built by a Darwin printed components for a second machine, assembled at the University of Bath. The improved Mendel design followed in 2009, and Josef Průša's simplified Prusa Mendel (2010) made the design dramatically easier to build; its descendant, the Prusa i3, became one of the most cloned printer designs in the world.

Design and technology

RepRap printers use fused filament fabrication (FFF): a heated extruder melts plastic filament — typically PLA or ABS — and deposits it layer by layer following toolpaths generated from a digital model. The machines combine printed plastic parts with "vitamins": off-the-shelf components they cannot (yet) print, such as stepper motors, threaded rods, belts and electronics (commonly Arduino-based boards such as RAMPS). Designs, firmware (such as Marlin) and software (slicers such as Slic3r) are all open source.

Impact

By making capable 3D printers available for a few hundred dollars, RepRap seeded an ecosystem of hobbyist and commercial derivatives, including early MakerBot machines. The project's philosophy of self-replication and free distribution influenced open hardware broadly, and its community wiki became the principal repository of printer designs and improvements.
