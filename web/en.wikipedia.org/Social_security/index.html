Social security

Old-age pensions are the largest component of most social security systems

Social security is a system of public programmes that provide income and support to individuals and families facing contingencies such as old age, unemployment, illness, disability, and the death of a breadwinner. It is a central component of the modern welfare state and is recognised as a human right in Article 22 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights.

Forms

Social security typically combines several mechanisms: contributory social insurance, in which workers and employers pay contributions that fund pensions, unemployment benefit, and sickness benefit; tax-funded social assistance providing a means-tested safety net; and universal or categorical benefits such as child allowances. Systems are financed either on a pay-as-you-go basis, where current contributors fund current beneficiaries, or through accumulated funds.

History

Germany under Chancellor Otto von Bismarck introduced the first modern social insurance system in the 1880s, with health insurance (1883), accident insurance (1884), and old-age and disability insurance (1889). The United States enacted its Social Security Act in 1935 during the Great Depression, creating old-age pensions and unemployment insurance. After the Second World War, the 1942 Beveridge Report in Britain led to a comprehensive welfare state, and generous systems expanded across Western Europe.

Challenges

Ageing populations, declining ratios of workers to pensioners, changing labour markets, and the growth of informal and gig employment place financial pressure on many systems, prompting reforms such as raising retirement ages and encouraging supplementary private saving. Internationally, the International Labour Organization promotes minimum standards through its Social Security (Minimum Standards) Convention of 1952.
