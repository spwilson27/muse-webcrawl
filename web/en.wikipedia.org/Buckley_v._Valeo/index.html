Buckley v. Valeo

Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1 (1976), is a landmark decision of the U.S. Supreme Court on campaign finance. A majority of justices held that limits on election spending are unconstitutional under the First Amendment, while upholding limits on contributions to candidates and disclosure requirements.

Background

After the Watergate scandal, Congress enacted the Federal Election Campaign Act Amendments of 1974, imposing limits on contributions and expenditures, creating public financing for presidential elections, and establishing the Federal Election Commission (FEC). Senator James L. Buckley, former Senator Eugene McCarthy and others challenged nearly every provision of the Act.

Holdings

  • Contribution limits — upheld as a permissible means of preventing corruption and its appearance.
  • Expenditure limits — struck down; the Court reasoned that restricting spending restricts the quantity of political speech.
  • Personal spending by candidates and independent expenditures — limits struck down.
  • Public financing and disclosure/reporting rules — upheld.
  • The FEC's structure was held unconstitutional because Congress had appointed some of its commissioners, an Appointments Clause violation, though Congress promptly restructured the agency.

Legacy

The per curiam opinion drew the enduring distinction between contributions, which may be limited, and expenditures, which generally may not. Its reasoning that spending money to amplify speech is itself protected speech shaped later decisions, including Citizens United v. FEC (2010), and remains central to debates over money in American politics.
