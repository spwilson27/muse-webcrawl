Palestinians (Arabic: al-Filasṭīniyyūn) are an Arab people native to the region of Palestine in the Levant. They live principally in the Palestinian territories (the West Bank and Gaza Strip), in Israel, and in a large diaspora — notably in Jordan, Syria and Lebanon, as well as in the Americas and Europe — many of them refugees from the wars of 1948 and 1967 and their descendants.

History

Palestinian identity developed in the late Ottoman and British Mandate periods, as the Arabic-speaking population of Palestine — Muslim, Christian and Jewish communities among them, though the modern term generally denotes the Arab population — articulated a distinct national consciousness. The 1948 Arab–Israeli War and the establishment of Israel brought the Nakba ("catastrophe"), in which more than 700,000 Palestinian Arabs fled or were expelled from their homes. The Palestine Liberation Organization (PLO), founded in 1964, became the umbrella of the national movement; the First Intifada (1987–1993) led to the Oslo Accords and limited self-government under the Palestinian Authority.

Culture

Palestinian culture is part of the wider Levantine Arab world, expressed in the Palestinian dialect of Arabic, in cuisine (including musakhan, often considered the national dish), in dabke folk dance, and in tatreez embroidery, inscribed by UNESCO on its list of intangible cultural heritage in 2021. Olive trees and olive oil are potent national symbols, and poets such as Mahmoud Darwish occupy a central place in modern Palestinian identity.

Demographics

Estimates place the worldwide Palestinian population at around 14 million, roughly half living in historic Palestine (the West Bank, Gaza and Israel) and half in the diaspora. A large proportion of those in the territories and neighbouring states are registered refugees with UNRWA.
