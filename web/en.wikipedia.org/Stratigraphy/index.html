Stratigraphy

Stratigraphy is the branch of geology concerned with the description, ordering and correlation of rock layers (strata) and their interpretation in terms of Earth history. By combining the physical, fossil and chemical records of layered rocks, stratigraphers construct the geologic time scale and reconstruct past environments, climates and life. The discipline is also borrowed by archaeology for the analysis of layered deposits.

Principles

Stratigraphy rests on principles formulated in the seventeenth to nineteenth centuries: Nicolas Steno's law of superposition (in an undisturbed sequence, lower layers are older), original horizontality and lateral continuity; William Smith's principle of faunal succession, which lets strata be correlated by their fossils; and the recognition of unconformities — gaps in the record identified by James Hutton. Modern practice distinguishes lithostratigraphy (rock units), biostratigraphy (fossil zones), chronostratigraphy (time-rock units) and chemo-, magneto- and sequence stratigraphy.

The geologic time scale

International chronostratigraphic units — eons, eras, periods, epochs and ages — are defined by Global Boundary Stratotype Sections and Points ("golden spikes") in rock sections worldwide, coordinated by the International Commission on Stratigraphy. Radiometric dating supplies numerical ages for the relative framework.

Applications

Stratigraphy underpins petroleum and groundwater exploration, coal and mineral assessment, engineering geology, and the correlation of ice cores and ocean sediments that document climate change. In archaeology, stratigraphic excavation and the Harris matrix apply the same logic of superposition to human-made layers.
