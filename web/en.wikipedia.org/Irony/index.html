Irony

TypesVerbal, dramatic, situational
EtymologyGreek eirōneia (dissimulation)
RelatedSarcasm, paradox, satire
Notable theoristsSøren Kierkegaard, Wayne Booth

Irony, in its broadest sense, is a rhetorical device and literary technique in which an expression gains a meaning that is incongruous with, or opposed to, its intended or literal meaning, or in which events turn out contrary to expectation. The term derives from the Greek eirōneia, the feigned ignorance Socrates adopted in dialogue.

Types

Verbal irony occurs when a speaker says one thing while meaning another, often the opposite — as when bitter weather is called "lovely". Dramatic irony arises in narrative when the audience knows something a character does not, as in Sophocles' Oedipus Rex, where Oedipus hunts the murderer he himself is. Situational irony occurs when the outcome of events reverses reasonable expectation — a fire station burning down, or a marriage counsellor filing for divorce.

Irony and sarcasm

Sarcasm is sometimes treated as a species of verbal irony, but the two differ in intent: irony need not mock, whereas sarcasm is typically directed at a target with contempt. Literary theorists from Kierkegaard, whose 1841 dissertation treated irony as a stance toward existence, to Wayne C. Booth's A Rhetoric of Irony (1974) have debated how readers recognize ironic meaning.

Other forms

Cosmic irony, or irony of fate, attributes the reversal to gods or fate mocking human plans — a hallmark of Thomas Hardy's novels. Romantic irony, developed by Friedrich Schlegel, involves an author conspicuously breaking the illusion of the work. Socratic irony names Socrates' professed ignorance used to draw out and test the claims of others. In everyday usage the word is often stretched to cover mere coincidence or bad luck, a looseness frequently debated by commentators.
