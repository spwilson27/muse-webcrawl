Sound barrier

The sound barrier or sonic barrier is the large increase in aerodynamic drag and other undesirable effects experienced by an aircraft or other object when it approaches the speed of sound. When aircraft first approached the speed of sound, these effects were seen as constituting a barrier, making faster speeds very difficult or impossible; the term "sound barrier" thus took hold in popular usage, though aerodynamicists regard it as a set of engineering challenges rather than a physical barrier.

In dry air at 20 °C (68 °F), the speed of sound is 343 metres per second (about 1,235 km/h or 767 mph). As an aircraft accelerates toward this speed, airflow over parts of the wing and fuselage becomes locally supersonic, forming shock waves that greatly increase drag (the transonic drag rise) and can cause buffeting, loss of control effectiveness ("Mach tuck"), and violent pitching. Many World War II-era fighters were lost in high-speed dives from these effects.

Breaking the barrier

The first pilot to exceed the speed of sound in controlled level flight was U.S. Air Force Captain Chuck Yeager, who flew the rocket-powered Bell X-1 to Mach 1.06 on 14 October 1947 over the Mojave Desert. Swept wings, thin airfoils, all-moving tailplanes, and the area rule subsequently made supersonic flight practical, leading to supersonic fighters and airliners such as Concorde.
