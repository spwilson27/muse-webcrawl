Lucifer

Lucifer is the Latin name for the morning star, the planet Venus in its morning aspect. The word derives from the Latin lucifer, "light-bringer", a translation of the Greek Phosphoros. As a proper name, "Lucifer" came in Christian tradition to denote Satan before his fall from heaven.

Origins

In the Hebrew Bible, Isaiah 14:12 addresses the king of Babylon with the phrase hêlēl ben šāḥar, "shining one, son of the dawn", referring to the morning star. The Septuagint translated it as heōsphoros, and the Latin Vulgate as lucifer. Early Church Fathers such as Tertullian and Origen interpreted the passage as an allegory of the fall of Satan, and by the medieval period "Lucifer" had become a standard name for the Devil as a fallen angel, as in Dante's Inferno and Milton's Paradise Lost.

In literature

Lucifer is a central figure in John Milton's epic poem Paradise Lost (1667), where Satan's rebellion and fall are depicted in heroic verse. Dante places Lucifer at the frozen centre of Hell in the Divine Comedy. The figure has been reimagined in Romantic literature, in occult traditions, and in modern popular culture, including the television series Lucifer.

Astronomical usage

In classical Latin, lucifer named the morning star as distinct from vesper, the evening star; both refer to the planet Venus. The term survives in various scientific names.
