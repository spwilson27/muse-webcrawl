The Bronze Horseman (poem)

The Bronze Horseman: A Petersburg Tale (Russian: Медный всадник, Mednyy vsadnik) is a narrative poem written in 1833 by Alexander Pushkin, widely considered one of the greatest works of Russian literature. The poem takes its name from the famous equestrian statue of Peter the Great in Saint Petersburg, created by Étienne Maurice Falconet, and it confronts the glory of Peter's imperial city with the suffering of an ordinary clerk destroyed by it.

Plot

The poem opens with a prologue in which Peter stands on the Baltic shore in 1703 and resolves to found a city, followed by the narrator's hymn of praise to Petersburg. The tale then turns to Evgenii, a poor clerk descended from an old noble family, who dreams of marrying his beloved Parasha. In November 1824 the Neva floods catastrophically; Evgenii survives, stranded astride a marble lion, but Parasha and her mother perish. Driven mad by grief, he wanders the city until, a year later, he shakes his fist at the bronze statue and curses it — whereupon the Horseman seems to come alive and gallop after him through the streets. Evgenii dies shortly after, and is buried on a small island.

Themes and interpretation

The poem's central tension — between the majestic state, symbolised by Peter and his city, and the humble individual crushed by the forces of history and nature — has generated endless debate about whether Pushkin celebrates or condemns Peter's achievement. The flood is read both as nature's revenge on Peter's unnatural city and as a symbol of rebellion.

Publication and legacy

Tsar Nicholas I personally censored the poem, objecting to its treatment of the statue; it was published only after Pushkin's death, in 1837, in a version modified by Vasily Zhukovsky, and the full text appeared only in the early 20th century. The poem gave the statue its now-universal nickname and inspired Reinhold Glière's ballet The Bronze Horseman (1949), whose "Hymn to the Great City" became an anthem of Saint Petersburg.
