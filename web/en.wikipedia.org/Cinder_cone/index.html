Cinder cone

A cinder cone, also called a scoria cone, is a steep conical hill of loose pyroclastic fragments, such as volcanic cinders and scoria, that accumulates around a volcanic vent. Cinder cones are among the most common types of volcano and typically grow rapidly to heights of tens to hundreds of metres.

Formation

Cinder cones form during Strombolian eruptions, when gas-rich magma is ejected in fountains of glowing clots that cool and solidify in flight, falling back around the vent as angular fragments. The cone grows at the angle of repose, about 30–40 degrees, and often has a bowl-shaped crater at the summit. Eruptions are usually short-lived, lasting months to a few years, and most cinder cones erupt only once (monogenetic).

Examples

Famous examples include Parícutin in Mexico, which grew in a farmer's field beginning in 1943; Sunset Crater in Arizona; and the many cones of the San Francisco volcanic field. Clusters of cinder cones also flank larger shield and stratovolcanoes.
