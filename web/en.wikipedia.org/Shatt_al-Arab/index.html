Shatt al-Arab

The Shatt al-Arab, formed by the confluence of the Tigris and Euphrates at Al-Qurnah.

The Shatt al-Arab (Arabic: شط العرب, "River of the Arabs"; Persian: اروندرود, Arvand Rūd) is a river in southwestern Asia, formed by the confluence of the Tigris and Euphrates rivers at the town of Al-Qurnah in southern Iraq. It flows southeast for about 200 kilometres (120 mi) to the Persian Gulf, forming the border between Iraq and Iran for much of its lower course, and receives the Karun River from Iran near Khorramshahr.

The waterway is of great economic importance: Iraq's principal port of Basra lies on it, as does Iran's port of Khorramshahr, and it provides access to the sea for Iraq's oil exports from the southern fields. Its banks historically supported vast date-palm groves, many of which were destroyed during the Iran–Iraq War.

Boundary disputes

The location of the Iraq–Iran border in the river was a recurring source of conflict between the Ottoman Empire and Persia and later between Iraq and Iran. The 1937 treaty placed the border at the low-water mark on the Iranian side for most of the river, while the 1975 Algiers Agreement moved it to the thalweg (the deepest channel) in exchange for Iranian concessions elsewhere; Iraq abrogated the agreement in 1980 shortly before invading Iran, and the status quo ante bellum was restored by the ceasefire of 1988. Periodic dredging disputes and reduced freshwater flows from upstream dams in Turkey and Iran have since increased the river's salinity, harming agriculture around Basra.
