Restoration comedy

Restoration comedy is English comedy written and performed in the Restoration period, from 1660 to about 1710. After public stage performances had been banned by the Puritan government for 18 years, the reopening of the theatres in 1660 on the restoration of Charles II signalled a return of a drama noted — and sometimes deplored — for its sexual explicitness, witty dialogue, and satire of fashionable society.

Characteristics

The plays are known as comedies of manners, populated by stock figures such as the witty rake, the fop, the witty heroine, and the country innocent adrift in London. Dialogue prized verbal sparring and repartee, and plots revolved around intrigue, courtship, and cuckoldry. For the first time in England, women appeared on the public stage as professional actresses, and breeches roles — women playing male parts — were a popular attraction.

Playwrights

Leading writers included William Wycherley (The Country Wife, The Plain Dealer), William Congreve (The Way of the World, 1700, often considered the finest of the genre), George Etherege, John Vanbrugh, George Farquhar, and Aphra Behn, the first professional English woman playwright. John Dryden and Thomas Shadwell also wrote notable comedies of the period.

Reaction and legacy

By the 1690s a moral backlash, led by Jeremy Collier's Short View of the Immorality and Profaneness of the English Stage (1698), pushed drama toward the more sentimental comedy that dominated the 18th century. Restoration comedies fell from favour in the Victorian era for their perceived immorality but were revived in the 20th century and are now regularly performed.
