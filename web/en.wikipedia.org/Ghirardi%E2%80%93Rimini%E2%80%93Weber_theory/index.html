The Ghirardi–Rimini–Weber theory (GRW) is a collapse theory in quantum mechanics proposed in 1986 by Gian Carlo Ghirardi, Alberto Rimini and Tullio Weber. It was the first of the spontaneous (or objective) collapse models, which modify the Schrödinger equation by adding a mechanism that causes the wavefunction to collapse randomly and spontaneously, without any role for observers or measurement.

The model

In the GRW theory, each elementary particle has a small probability per unit time — characterised by a collapse rate λ of about 10−16 per second — of undergoing a spontaneous localisation, in which its wavefunction is multiplied by a narrow Gaussian of width about 10−7 metres. For an isolated microscopic particle such events are so rare that ordinary quantum behaviour is essentially unaffected. However, in a macroscopic object containing vast numbers of particles, at least one particle localises almost immediately, and entanglement between the particles then localises the whole object, explaining why macroscopic superpositions are never observed.

Relation to experiment

Unlike the standard interpretation of quantum mechanics, GRW is an empirically testable theory: it predicts tiny deviations from quantum predictions, such as a faint spontaneous heating of matter and the eventual failure of interference experiments with sufficiently massive objects. Experiments involving matter-wave interferometry, optomechanics and searches for anomalous X-ray emission have placed increasingly tight bounds on the model's parameters, though a region of parameter space remains untested. A related continuous version of the model is known as continuous spontaneous localisation (CSL).

Philosophical aspects

GRW addresses the measurement problem by giving a single dynamical law for both microscopic and macroscopic systems. Its ontology has been interpreted in terms of a mass-density field in spacetime or of discrete "flashes" — the spacetime events corresponding to localisations. The theory faces challenges including the "tails problem" (Gaussian localisations never completely eliminate the other branches of the wavefunction) and compatibility with relativistic invariance, which later relativistic collapse models have attempted to address.
