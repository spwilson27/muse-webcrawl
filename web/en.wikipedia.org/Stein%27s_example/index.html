Stein's example

Stein's example (or Stein's phenomenon) is a phenomenon in decision theory and estimation theory in statistics which states that, when three or more parameters are estimated simultaneously, there exist combined estimators (such as the James–Stein estimator) that are more accurate on average than any method that handles the parameters separately. The result, proved by Charles Stein in 1956, was initially surprising because the parameters need not be related in any way.

The James–Stein estimator

Given independent normal observations with unknown means and known variance, the usual estimator (the sample means themselves) is inadmissible in dimensions three or higher under squared-error loss: shrinking the estimates toward a common point — for example toward zero or toward their overall mean — reduces the total expected loss for every possible true value of the means. The James–Stein estimator (1961) exhibits this shrinkage explicitly.

Interpretation and impact

Stein's paradox is not a mathematical contradiction but a challenge to intuition about estimating several quantities at once; unrelated estimation problems can be pooled for mutual benefit. It motivated empirical Bayes methods and shrinkage techniques, including ridge regression and modern hierarchical modelling, and remains a foundational result of multivariate statistics.
