The Mongolic languages are a language family of about a dozen languages spoken in Central and East Asia, principally in Mongolia, the Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region of China, and the Russian republics of Buryatia and Kalmykia. By far the most widely spoken member is Mongolian, the national language of Mongolia, based on the Khalkha dialect.

Members and classification

Besides Mongolian proper — which includes dialects such as Khalkha, Chakhar, and Khorchin — the family includes Buryat in Siberia, Kalmyk (Oirat) on the Caspian steppe, and smaller or endangered languages such as Dagur in Manchuria, Dongxiang (Santa), Bonan, Tu (Monguor), and the isolate-like Moghol of Afghanistan, which is probably extinct. The extinct Khitan language, attested in two scripts from the Liao dynasty, is often grouped with Mongolic in a wider "Para-Mongolic" grouping.

History

The languages descend from Middle Mongol, the language of the Mongol Empire founded by Genghis Khan in 1206, documented in works such as The Secret History of the Mongols. The traditional Mongolian script, adapted from the Old Uyghur alphabet, is written vertically and is still official in Inner Mongolia; in Mongolia itself it was displaced by Cyrillic in the 1940s, though revival efforts continue. Whether Mongolic is related to the Turkic and Tungusic families in a larger "Altaic" grouping is a long-running and contested question; most specialists today treat the similarities as the result of prolonged contact.
