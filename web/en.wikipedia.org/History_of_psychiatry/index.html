History of psychiatry

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Bethlem Royal Hospital in London, one of the oldest institutions for the care of the mentally ill.

The history of psychiatry traces the understanding and treatment of mental disorders from ancient supernatural explanations through the asylum era to modern medicine. In prehistoric and ancient cultures, mental illness was often attributed to demonic possession or divine punishment, and treatments included trepanation, exorcism and prayer.

Greek physicians such as Hippocrates rejected supernatural explanations and proposed that mental disorders had natural causes arising from the brain and from imbalances of the four humours. The Roman physician Galen developed these ideas, and Islamic physicians of the medieval period, including Avicenna, established some of the first hospitals with wards for the mentally ill.

The asylum era

In Europe, institutions such as Bethlem Royal Hospital ("Bedlam") in London housed people with mental illness, often in harsh conditions. From the late eighteenth century, reformers including Philippe Pinel in Paris and William Tuke at the York Retreat promoted "moral treatment" — removing chains, providing humane surroundings and structured daily life. In the nineteenth century, asylums multiplied across Europe and North America, growing large and often overcrowded.

Scientific psychiatry

The German psychiatrist Emil Kraepelin (1856–1926) founded modern scientific psychiatry through his classification of mental disorders, distinguishing dementia praecox (later schizophrenia) from manic-depressive illness. Eugen Bleuler coined the term "schizophrenia" in 1911. Around the same period, Sigmund Freud developed psychoanalysis, which dominated much psychiatric thought in the first half of the twentieth century.

The mid-twentieth century saw a range of somatic treatments, including insulin coma therapy, electroconvulsive therapy and lobotomy, followed by the psychopharmacology revolution: chlorpromazine, introduced in the 1950s, was the first effective antipsychotic, followed by antidepressants and lithium. These drugs, together with social criticism of asylums, drove deinstitutionalization from the 1960s onward.

Modern era

The publication of the DSM-III in 1980 reoriented diagnosis around descriptive, criteria-based categories. Advances in neuroscience, genetics and neuroimaging have deepened understanding of mental illness, while community care, cognitive behavioural therapy and recovery-oriented approaches have reshaped treatment. Debates continue over classification, the medicalization of distress and the balance between biological and psychosocial explanations.
