Scholasticism

Scholasticism was the dominant system of learning in the universities of medieval Europe from about 1100 to 1700. It sought to reconcile Christian theology with classical philosophy — above all the recovered works of Aristotle — using a rigorous dialectical method. Scholastic thinkers, the Schoolmen, aimed to resolve apparent contradictions among authoritative texts (auctoritates) by careful distinctions and logical analysis.

Method

The characteristic exercise was the quaestio: a question was posed, arguments for opposed answers were marshalled from Scripture, the Church Fathers and philosophers, and the master gave a determination, answering each objection in turn. Teaching revolved around the lectio (commentary on a set text, such as Peter Lombard's Sentences) and the disputatio, a formal public debate. Scholastic Latin developed a precise technical vocabulary for this work.

Major figures and stages

Early scholasticism is associated with Anselm of Canterbury, whose motto "faith seeking understanding" set its programme, and with Peter Abelard, whose Sic et Non juxtaposed conflicting authorities. The 12th–13th-century translation movements in Toledo and Sicily brought the full Aristotelian corpus, together with Arabic and Jewish commentaries (Avicenna, Averroes, Maimonides), into Latin. The high scholastic synthesis followed: Albertus Magnus and his pupil Thomas Aquinas, whose Summa Theologiae integrated Aristotelian philosophy with Christian doctrine; the Franciscans Bonaventure, Duns Scotus and William of Ockham developed rival systems, including voluntarism and nominalism.

Later history

Humanists of the Renaissance mocked scholastic style as barbarous pedantry, and the new science and Reformation eroded its monopoly; nevertheless a "Second Scholasticism" flourished in 16th–17th-century Spain (the School of Salamanca, Francisco Suárez), influencing international law and economics, and neo-scholasticism was revived in the 19th–20th centuries. Historians now credit scholasticism with foundational work in logic, semantics, economics and natural philosophy, and many of its questions remain live in analytic philosophy.
