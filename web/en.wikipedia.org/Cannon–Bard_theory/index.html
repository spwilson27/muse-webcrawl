Cannon–Bard theory

Proposed byWalter Bradford Cannon; Philip Bard
Year proposed1927 (Cannon); expanded 1928–1934 (Bard)
FieldPsychology, neuroscience
Challenge toJames–Lange theory of emotion

The Cannon–Bard theory of emotion states that physiological arousal and the subjective experience of emotion occur simultaneously and independently. According to the theory, an emotion-provoking stimulus is relayed by the thalamus both to the cerebral cortex, producing the conscious feeling of emotion, and to the sympathetic nervous system, producing bodily arousal. Neither process causes the other; they happen at the same time.

Schematic of the Cannon–Bard theory: one signal, two simultaneous responses.

Background

The theory was developed by the Harvard physiologist Walter Bradford Cannon in the 1920s and elaborated by his doctoral student Philip Bard in the following decade. It was formulated as a direct alternative to the James–Lange theory, which held that people first experience physiological changes and only then interpret them as emotion — that we feel afraid because we tremble, rather than trembling because we feel afraid.

Cannon's criticisms of James–Lange

Cannon raised several objections: total separation of the viscera from the central nervous system does not alter emotional behavior in experimental animals; the same visceral changes occur in very different emotional states and in non-emotional states such as fever or exercise; the viscera are relatively insensitive structures; visceral changes are too slow to be the source of feelings that seem instantaneous; and artificially inducing visceral changes (for example, by injecting adrenaline) does not produce genuine emotion.

Bard's experiments

Bard's work with cats showed that emotional responses such as "sham rage" persisted after removal of the cerebral cortex but disappeared when the hypothalamus was also removed. This localized the coordination of emotional expression in the diencephalon and supported the view that subcortical structures relay emotional information simultaneously to the cortex and the body.

Legacy

The Cannon–Bard theory influenced later models of emotion, including the Papez circuit and MacLean's limbic system concept, and remains a standard textbook account alongside the James–Lange and Schachter–Singer two-factor theories.
