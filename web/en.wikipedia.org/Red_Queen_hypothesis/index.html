Red Queen hypothesis

The hypothesis takes its name from the Red Queen's race in Lewis Carroll's Through the Looking-Glass.

The Red Queen hypothesis is a hypothesis in evolutionary biology proposed by Leigh Van Valen in 1973, which states that organisms must constantly adapt, evolve, and proliferate in order to survive while pitted against ever-evolving opposing organisms in a constantly changing environment. The hypothesis is named after the Red Queen in Lewis Carroll's Through the Looking-Glass, who tells Alice: "Now, here, you see, it takes all the running you can do, to keep in the same place."

Origin

Van Valen derived the hypothesis from his "law of constant extinction", which he observed from fossil data: within a group of related species, the probability of extinction appeared roughly constant over time, regardless of how long the group had existed. He explained this by arguing that a species' environment deteriorates at a stochastically constant rate because the other species in its ecosystem are continually evolving against it — a zero-sum evolutionary arms race.

Applications

The Red Queen hypothesis has been applied to host–parasite coevolution, where parasites adapt to infect common host genotypes, giving an advantage to rare genotypes and thereby favouring the maintenance of sexual reproduction, which generates genetic diversity. It has also been used to explain the evolution of ageing, arms races between predators and prey, and male ornamentation under sexual selection.

"It takes all the running you can do, to keep in the same place. If you want to get somewhere else, you must run at least twice as fast as that!" — the Red Queen in Through the Looking-Glass (1871)

Evidence

Experimental studies with snails and their trematode parasites, and with bacteria and phages in laboratory cultures, have supported the prediction that parasites adapt most strongly to locally common host genotypes. Long-term experiments with Caenorhabditis elegans and the pathogen Serratia marcescens showed that obligately outcrossing populations persisted against coevolving parasites, while self-fertilising populations were driven extinct.
