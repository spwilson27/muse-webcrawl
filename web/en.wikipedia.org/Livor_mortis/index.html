Livor mortis

Livor mortis (Latin: livor — bluish colour, mortis — of death), also called post-mortem lividity or hypostasis, is the settling of blood in the lower, dependent parts of the body after death, producing a purplish-red discoloration of the skin. It is one of the classic early post-mortem changes, alongside algor mortis (cooling) and rigor mortis (stiffening).

Forensic significance

Lividity begins within about 20 minutes to 3 hours of death as capillaries fill under gravity; areas pressed against a surface remain pale where vessels are compressed. Early lividity blanches under pressure and will shift if the body is moved; after roughly 8–12 hours it becomes fixed as blood haemolyses into surrounding tissues. Forensic pathologists use its pattern and degree of fixation to judge whether a body has been moved and to estimate the post-mortem interval, though colour can mislead — cherry-red lividity suggests carbon monoxide or cyanide poisoning or cold exposure.
