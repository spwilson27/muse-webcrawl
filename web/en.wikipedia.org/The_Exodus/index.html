The Exodus is the founding myth of the Israelites, recounting their departure from slavery in ancient Egypt under the leadership of Moses. The story is told principally in the Book of Exodus, the second book of the Hebrew Bible, and is retold and referenced throughout the Tanakh, the New Testament, and the Quran. It is commemorated annually in Judaism by the festival of Passover.

The biblical narrative

According to the narrative, the descendants of Jacob (Israel) had multiplied in Egypt, where a new pharaoh, fearing their numbers, enslaved them and ordered the killing of their male infants. Moses, spared as a baby and raised in the pharaoh's household, fled to Midian after killing an Egyptian taskmaster. There God revealed himself in the burning bush and commissioned Moses to lead the Israelites out of Egypt.

When Pharaoh refused to release them, God sent the Ten Plagues upon Egypt, culminating in the death of the firstborn, from which the Israelite households were spared by the blood of a lamb marked on their doorposts — the origin of Passover. Pharaoh relented, then pursued the fugitives to the sea, which God parted (the Crossing of the Red Sea), allowing the Israelites to escape while the Egyptian army was drowned.

The wilderness and Sinai

In the wilderness the Israelites received manna and water, and at Mount Sinai Moses received the Ten Commandments and the laws of the covenant. After forty years of wandering, the generation of the Exodus died out, and their children, led by Joshua, entered the land of Canaan.

Historicity

The consensus of modern biblical scholars and archaeologists is that the Exodus as described did not happen on the biblical scale: there is no archaeological evidence of a mass migration of Israelites through the Sinai, and the Israelites appear to have emerged largely indigenously within Canaan. Many scholars nonetheless allow that a smaller group may have experienced an exodus-like event that later became the collective memory of Israel. The story's central themes — liberation from oppression and covenant with God — have made it one of the most influential narratives in Western culture.
