Golden rice is a variety of rice (Oryza sativa) produced through genetic engineering to biosynthesize beta-carotene, a precursor of vitamin A, in the edible parts of the rice. It is intended to address vitamin A deficiency, which the World Health Organization estimates affects millions of children in Africa and Southeast Asia and causes blindness and increased mortality. The rice is called "golden" because of the yellow colour imparted by beta-carotene.

Development

Golden rice was created by Ingo Potrykus of ETH Zurich and Peter Beyer of the University of Freiburg, using genes from daffodil (later replaced in Golden Rice 2 by a maize gene, giving up to 23 times more beta-carotene) and a soil bacterium. Results were published in Science in 2000 and the project was featured on the cover of Time magazine. Rights were arranged so that subsistence farmers could use the seed free of charge through humanitarian licensing.

Regulation and controversy

Golden rice has been approved for food use by regulators in the United States, Canada, Australia/New Zealand and the Philippines, which in 2021 became the first country to approve it for commercial propagation; a Philippine court revoked the permit in 2024. The crop has been a focus of the debate over genetically modified organisms: supporters argue it can prevent blindness and death, while critics, including Greenpeace, question its effectiveness, safety and the wisdom of technological fixes for malnutrition — a controversy that also saw the destruction of a Philippine field trial in 2013. Over 100 Nobel laureates signed a 2016 letter supporting golden rice and criticising its opponents.
