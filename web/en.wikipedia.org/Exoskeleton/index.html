Exoskeleton

Present inArthropods, molluscs, brachiopods, some other invertebrates
Composition (arthropods)Chitin and proteins, often mineralized with calcium carbonate
GrowthBy moulting (ecdysis) in arthropods
The rigid external skeleton of a crab protects its soft tissues.

An exoskeleton (from Greek exo, "outside", and skeletos, "skeleton") is a skeleton that is on the exterior of an animal, in the form of a rigid external covering, as opposed to the internal skeleton (endoskeleton) of, for example, a human. Exoskeletons provide support, protection against predators and desiccation, a surface for muscle attachment, and a barrier against the environment.

Types

The arthropod exoskeleton, or cuticle, is a multilayered structure secreted by the epidermis, composed largely of chitin; in crustaceans it is hardened with calcium salts. Mollusc shells, secreted by the mantle, are exoskeletons made mainly of calcium carbonate. Brachiopods, bryozoans, and some polychaete worms also possess external skeletons or tubes.

Growth and moulting

Because a rigid exoskeleton cannot expand, arthropods must periodically shed it — a process called moulting or ecdysis — and secrete a larger one. During the soft phase after moulting the animal is highly vulnerable. The size limits imposed by the exoskeleton, together with respiratory constraints, are among the reasons land arthropods remain comparatively small, though aquatic species such as the Japanese spider crab reach leg spans of several metres. Powered exoskeletons for humans, developed for medical, industrial, and military purposes, borrow the term by analogy. See also endoskeleton, chitin, and arthropod.
