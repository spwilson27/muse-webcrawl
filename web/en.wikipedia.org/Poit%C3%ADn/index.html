Poitín

TypeDistilled spirit
Country of originIreland
Alcohol by volume40–90%
IngredientsMalted barley, grain, potatoes, whey or sugar beet

Poitín (Irish pronunciation: [ˈpˠɛtʲiːnʲ]), anglicized as poteen or potcheen, is a traditional Irish distilled beverage. It was traditionally distilled in a small pot still and the term is a diminutive of the Irish word pota, meaning "pot".

History

Poitín was traditionally produced in remote rural areas, away from the interference of the law. In 1661 the Irish Parliament made it illegal to distil spirits without a licence, and illicit production flourished for centuries, becoming deeply embedded in Irish folk culture, songs, and literature. The ban on poitín was lifted for licensed producers in 1997, and it has since gained protected geographical status under EU law.

Production

Traditional poitín was made from malted barley, though potatoes, whey, and sugar beet have also been used. It is typically a very strong, clear spirit, traditionally unaged. Modern legal versions are often produced to high standards and sold at 40–50% ABV.

In culture

Poitín features prominently in Irish folk songs such as "The Hills of Connemara" and "The Rare Old Mountain Dew", and in the works of writers including Brian Friel.
