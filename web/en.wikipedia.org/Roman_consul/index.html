A consul was the highest elected political office of the Roman Republic, and a prestigious office under the Empire. Two consuls were elected each year by the Centuriate Assembly to serve together for a one-year term, each holding a veto over the other. Consuls held imperium — supreme executive and military authority — and the year was named after them, making the office central to the Roman dating system (see Roman calendar).

The Republic

According to tradition, the consulship replaced the monarchy in 509 BC, with Lucius Junius Brutus and Lucius Tarquinius Collatinus as the first consuls. Originally restricted to patricians, the office was opened to plebeians in 367 BC by the Licinian-Sextian laws; by convention one consul was often a plebeian. In Rome the consuls presided over the Senate and assemblies, administered justice and commanded armies in the field, attended by twelve lictors each.

In emergencies the consuls could appoint a dictator for six months. A former consul (consularis) retained high rank in the Senate, and the consulship was the summit of the cursus honorum, the ladder of offices, normally reached at age 42 or later under Sulla's regulations.

Under the Empire

Under Augustus and his successors the consulship lost real power but retained immense prestige. Emperors frequently held the office themselves, and the two ordinary consuls (consules ordinarii) who began the year still gave it its name. Additional "suffect" consuls served for part of the year. The office survived in the Eastern Empire until Emperor Justinian allowed it to lapse after 541 AD; thereafter the emperor was regarded as permanent consul.
