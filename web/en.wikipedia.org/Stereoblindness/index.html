Stereoblindness

Specialty: Ophthalmology
Definition: Inability to perceive depth through binocular disparity
Causes: Strabismus, amblyopia, vision loss in one eye
Prevalence: Estimated 5–10% of the population

Stereoblindness (also stereo blindness) is the inability to see in three dimensions using stereopsis — the perception of depth arising from the slightly different images received by the two eyes. People with stereoblindness perceive the world as flat in this binocular sense, although they typically compensate using monocular cues such as motion parallax, relative size, occlusion and shading.

Causes

The condition commonly results from childhood strabismus (misaligned eyes) or amblyopia ("lazy eye"), in which the visual cortex fails to develop the binocular neurons needed to process retinal disparity during the critical period of development. It can also follow loss of vision in one eye. Estimates suggest roughly 5 to 10 per cent of people lack stereoscopic depth perception.

Notable cases and treatment

Neuroscientist Susan R. Barry famously documented gaining stereo vision in adulthood through vision therapy, as recounted in her book Fixing My Gaze and in Oliver Sacks's writing — challenging the belief that stereopsis could never be acquired after childhood. Stereoblind individuals cannot perceive random-dot stereograms or the hidden images in autostereograms ("Magic Eye" pictures), and may be excluded from occupations requiring fine depth judgement, such as certain pilot roles.
