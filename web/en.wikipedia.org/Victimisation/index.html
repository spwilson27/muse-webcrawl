Victimisation

Victimisation (or victimization) is the process of being made a victim — that is, of suffering harm through crime, abuse, exploitation, discrimination, or other wrongful acts. The concept is central to victimology, the study of victims and of the relationship between victims and offenders, and to criminological research that measures crime through victim surveys rather than police records alone.

Forms

Primary victimisation is the direct harm suffered by the target of an offence. Secondary victimisation refers to additional harm arising from the response of institutions and individuals — for example, insensitive treatment of victims by police, courts, medical services, or media, sometimes called "the second wound". In psychology and social work, the literature also discusses self-victimisation and a victim mentality, in which a person habitually perceives themselves as a victim, a pattern distinct from actual victimisation events.

Patterns and risk

Victimisation is unevenly distributed. Surveys consistently find higher risks among young people, residents of deprived urban areas, and those whose lifestyles increase exposure to offenders. A striking regularity is repeat victimisation: a relatively small share of victims accounts for a large share of incidents, so prevention focused on recent victims is an efficient crime-reduction strategy. Lifestyle-exposure and routine activity theories explain victimisation risk in terms of the convergence in time and space of motivated offenders, suitable targets, and the absence of capable guardians.

Measurement

Because much crime is never reported to police, large national victimisation surveys — the National Crime Victimization Survey in the United States (begun 1973) and the Crime Survey for England and Wales (begun 1982) — are used to estimate the "dark figure" of crime, track trends, and compare risk across groups and places.
