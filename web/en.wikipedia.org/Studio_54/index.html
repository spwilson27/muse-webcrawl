Studio 54 is a Broadway theatre and former nightclub located at 254 West 54th Street in Midtown Manhattan, New York City. Opened in 1977 by entrepreneurs Steve Rubell and Ian Schrager, it became the most famous nightclub of the disco era, renowned for its celebrity guest list, restrictive door policy, elaborate theatrical set designs, and open atmosphere of glamour and excess.

History

The building began life in 1927 as the Gallo Opera House and later served as a CBS television studio — the source of its name — hosting shows such as What's My Line? and The $64,000 Question. Rubell and Schrager converted the space in only six weeks, retaining the theatrical rigging and balconies, which allowed the club's scenery and lighting to be transformed nightly.

Studio 54 opened on April 26, 1977, just as disco was reaching mainstream peak. Its door policy, controlled personally by Rubell, mixed celebrities such as Andy Warhol, Liza Minnelli, Mick Jagger, and Bianca Jagger — who famously rode a white horse into the club on her birthday — with stylish unknowns chosen for the "right mix." The club's regulars and imagery came to symbolize both the liberation and the hedonism of late-1970s New York nightlife.

Downfall and legacy

In December 1978 the club was raided by federal agents; Rubell and Schrager were convicted of tax evasion after a second raid found skimmed cash and records. The club's original era ended with a farewell party, "The End of Modern-day Gomorrah," in February 1980, and the founders served prison sentences. The venue reopened under new ownership in 1981 but never recaptured its original aura.

Since 1998 the building has operated as a Broadway theatre under the Roundabout Theatre Company. Studio 54 remains a cultural shorthand for the disco era, and its story has been retold in the 1998 film 54, documentaries, and exhibitions.
