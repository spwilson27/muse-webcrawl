Legal history of cannabis in Canada

The legal history of cannabis in Canada spans a century of prohibition followed by full legalisation for recreational use in 2018, when Canada became the first G7 country — and the second country in the world after Uruguay — to legalise cannabis nationally.

Prohibition

Cannabis was first made illegal in Canada in 1923, when it was added to the schedule of prohibited drugs under the Opium and Drug Act — notably, years before similar federal prohibition in the United States, and with little parliamentary debate. The first seizures and arrests followed in the 1930s. Penalties stiffened under subsequent legislation, including the Narcotic Control Act of 1961. In the early 1970s the Le Dain Commission recommended decriminalisation, but its proposals were not enacted, and enforcement expanded; by the 2010s tens of thousands of Canadians were charged annually, mostly for simple possession.

Medical access

Following court decisions, notably R v Parker (2000), Canada introduced the Marihuana Medical Access Regulations in 2001, allowing patients authorised access. The regulatory regime evolved through the MMPR (2013) and ACMPR (2016), establishing a licensed commercial production sector and, later, restoring patients' right to grow their own supply.

Legalisation

The Liberal government of Justin Trudeau, elected in 2015 partly on a legalisation pledge, introduced the Cannabis Act (Bill C-45). The Act received royal assent in June 2018, and recreational cannabis sales became legal on 17 October 2018, with provinces and territories responsible for retail distribution rules. Edibles, extracts and topicals followed in late 2019 ("Cannabis 2.0"). Legalisation included record-suspension (pardon) provisions for simple possession convictions.
