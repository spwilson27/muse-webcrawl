A ballpoint pen, also known as a biro in many parts of the world, is a pen that dispenses ink over a metal ball at its point — that is, over a "ball point". The metal commonly used is steel, brass, or tungsten carbide. The design was conceived and developed as a cleaner and more reliable alternative to dip pens and fountain pens, and it is now the world's most-used writing instrument.

How it works

The ball, typically 0.5 to 1.2 mm in diameter, sits in a socket at the tip of the pen. As the pen moves across paper, the ball rotates, picking up a thin film of thick, oil-based ink from the reservoir behind it and depositing that ink on the page. Because the ink is a fast-drying paste rather than a watery liquid, ballpoint lines dry almost instantly and rarely smear or leak through paper.

History

Early patents for ball-tipped pens date to the 19th century — John J. Loud received a US patent in 1888 for a pen intended to mark leather — but these designs could not handle ordinary writing smoothly. The breakthrough came from Hungarian-Argentine journalist László Bíró, who, frustrated by fountain-pen ink smudging on newsprint, adapted the quick-drying inks used in newspaper presses and patented a workable ballpoint in 1938. Bíró pens were manufactured in Argentina and supplied to the Royal Air Force during the Second World War, because they performed well at high altitude. After the war, Marcel Bich's BIC company in France mass-produced an inexpensive, reliable version — the Bic Cristal (1950) — which made the ballpoint a ubiquitous disposable item.

Types

Ballpoint variants include retractable and capped disposable pens, pressurised "space pens" that write at any angle, erasable ballpoints using thermosensitive ink, and rollerball pens, which use a similar ball mechanism with water-based or gel ink for a smoother line.
