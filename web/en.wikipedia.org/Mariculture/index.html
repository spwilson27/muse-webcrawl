Mariculture

Mariculture, sometimes called marine farming or marine aquaculture, is a branch of aquaculture involving the cultivation of marine organisms for food and other animal products in seawater, in the open ocean or in enclosed sections of the ocean, or in tanks, ponds, or raceways filled with seawater. It is distinguished from freshwater aquaculture, which takes place in ponds, rivers, and lakes. Non-food products produced by mariculture include fish meal, nutrient agar, jewellery (such as cultured pearls), and cosmetics.

Species farmed

Major categories include finfish such as Atlantic salmon, sea bass, sea bream, cobia, and yellowtail; molluscs such as oysters, mussels, clams, scallops, and abalone; crustaceans such as marine shrimp; and marine plants, especially seaweeds such as nori, kelp, and wakame. Salmon farming is among the largest sectors, concentrated in Norway, Chile, Scotland, and Canada. Seaweed farming, by weight, is the single largest mariculture output worldwide, dominated by China, Indonesia, South Korea, and the Philippines.

Methods

Common methods include net pens and cages moored in coastal waters, longlines and rafts for shellfish and seaweed, bottom culture, and land-based recirculating systems. Offshore mariculture moves cages into deeper, more exposed waters to improve water quality and reduce coastal conflicts, using submersible or semi-submersible cage designs able to withstand storms. Integrated multi-trophic aquaculture (IMTA) combines fed species such as fish with extractive species such as shellfish and seaweed that absorb waste nutrients.

Environmental issues

Mariculture can relieve pressure on wild fish stocks but raises concerns: nutrient and chemical pollution from uneaten feed and faeces, spread of sea lice and disease to wild populations, escapes of farmed fish that interbreed with wild stock, and demand for wild-caught fish to produce fishmeal and fish oil feed. Shellfish and seaweed culture are generally regarded as low-impact or even restorative, since bivalves filter water and seaweeds absorb nutrients and carbon.

Scale

Global aquaculture production surpassed capture fisheries in the early 2020s, and mariculture accounts for a substantial share of that output, particularly in Asia, which produces the overwhelming majority of the world's farmed seafood.
