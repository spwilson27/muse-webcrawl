Gdańsk Agreement

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Gdańsk Agreement (Polish: Porozumienie gdańskie) was an accord signed on 31 August 1980 at the Lenin Shipyard in Gdańsk, Poland, between the striking workers' Interfactory Strike Committee and the government of the Polish People's Republic. Lech Wałęsa signed for the strikers, using a giant novelty pen bearing the image of Pope John Paul II, while Deputy Prime Minister Mieczysław Jagielski signed for the government.

Background

In the summer of 1980, following government-announced increases in food prices, strikes broke out across Poland. The strike at the Gdańsk Lenin Shipyard, which began on 14 August 1980 after the dismissal of crane operator and activist Anna Walentynowicz, spread to factories and shipyards across the coast. The Interfactory Strike Committee (MKS) grouped together representatives of hundreds of workplaces and formulated 21 demands.

The 21 demands

The demands were written on two wooden boards and posted at the shipyard gate; they are now preserved in the European Solidarity Centre and inscribed in UNESCO's Memory of the World Register. The first and most far-reaching demand was the right to form free trade unions independent of the Communist Party and of enterprises. Other demands included the right to strike, freedom of speech and of the press, release of political prisoners, reinstatement of workers dismissed for striking, improvements in food supply and health services, and raising the minimum wage.

Significance

The agreement was the first time a communist government in the Eastern Bloc formally conceded the right to independent trade unions. It directly led to the registration of Solidarity (NSZZ Solidarność), the first independent trade union in the Soviet sphere, which within a year had around ten million members. The accord is regarded as a pivotal moment in the decline of communist rule in Central and Eastern Europe, although martial law imposed in December 1981 temporarily drove Solidarity underground.
