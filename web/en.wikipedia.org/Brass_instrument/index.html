A brass instrument is a musical instrument that produces sound by sympathetic vibration of air in a tubular resonator in sympathy with the vibration of the player's lips. Brass instruments are also called labrosones or labrophones ("lip-vibrated instruments"), referring to the way the player creates vibration with their lips against a mouthpiece.

How brass instruments work

The player's buzzing lips act as a vibrating valve that sets the air column inside the instrument resonating. The pitch is governed by the length of the tube and by the harmonic series; players select different harmonics by changing lip tension and airflow, and alter the tube length with valves, slides or keys to fill in the intervening notes. Most brass instruments have a predominantly conical or cylindrical bore ending in a flared bell.

Valves

Modern brass instruments typically use three (sometimes four) piston or rotary valves. Pressing a valve diverts the air through extra tubing, lowering the pitch by a whole step (second valve, half step; third valve, minor third). The invention of reliable valves in the early 19th century by makers such as Heinrich Stölzel and Frédéric Blühmel made brass instruments fully chromatic and transformed their role in the orchestra.

Families and instruments

Common brass instruments include the trumpet, cornet, flugelhorn, French horn, trombone, bass trombone, baritone horn, euphonium and tuba. Historical instruments include the natural trumpet, natural horn, sackbut, serpent and ophicleide. Although traditionally made of brass, instruments of the family can be made of other materials — the determining feature is the lip-vibrated sound production, not the metal: the wooden alphorn and the conch shell are related labrosones.

Use

Brass instruments form a section of the symphony orchestra, the backbone of the brass band and concert band, and are prominent in jazz, ceremonial and military music, and many popular genres.
