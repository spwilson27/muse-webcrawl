Semiconductor

A semiconductor is a material with electrical conductivity between that of a conductor (such as copper) and an insulator (such as glass). Its conductivity can be modified over a wide range by doping — the introduction of impurities — and by temperature, illumination, or applied fields. Semiconductors are the foundation of modern electronics, including transistors, diodes, integrated circuits, and photovoltaic cells.

Properties

In a pure (intrinsic) semiconductor such as silicon, electrons can be thermally excited across the band gap from the valence band to the conduction band, leaving behind positively charged "holes." Adding pentavalent impurities (e.g., phosphorus) creates an n-type material with excess electrons; trivalent impurities (e.g., boron) create a p-type material with excess holes. The junction between p-type and n-type regions forms the basis of diodes and transistors.

Materials

Silicon dominates the industry because of its suitable band gap, abundance, and excellent native oxide. Other materials include germanium, gallium arsenide, silicon carbide, gallium nitride, and compound and organic semiconductors used in LEDs, lasers, and high-power or high-frequency devices.

Industry

Semiconductor devices are fabricated in highly specialised plants ("fabs") using photolithography and hundreds of process steps. The global semiconductor industry is one of the world's largest manufacturing sectors and a focus of industrial and geopolitical competition.
