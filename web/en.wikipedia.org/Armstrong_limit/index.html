Armstrong limit

The Armstrong limit (also called the Armstrong line) is a measure of altitude above which atmospheric pressure is sufficiently low that water boils at the normal temperature of the human body. Exposure to pressure below this limit results in a rapid loss of consciousness and, shortly thereafter, death. The limit is named after Harry Armstrong, a United States Air Force general who described the phenomenon in the 1930s.

Physiology

At the Armstrong limit, atmospheric pressure falls to about 6.3 kilopascals, equal to the vapour pressure of water at 37 °C. Above this altitude, exposed body fluids such as tears, saliva and the moisture in the lungs boil away — a process called ebullism — and a person cannot survive for more than a minute or two without a pressure suit or a pressurised environment, regardless of how much oxygen is supplied. The limit lies at roughly 18 to 19 kilometres above sea level, varying with local atmospheric conditions.

Significance

The Armstrong limit defines the altitude beyond which full pressure suits are required for pilots and astronauts rather than simple oxygen masks. High-altitude aircraft such as the U-2 and SR-71 were operated by crews wearing pressure suits partly for this reason, and the limit is a standard reference point in aerospace medicine and the design of life-support systems.
