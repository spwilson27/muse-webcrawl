Kanban (development)

Kanban is a method for managing and improving work across human systems, originally developed for software development by David J. Anderson in the mid-2000s. It adapts the kanban scheduling system of lean manufacturing — developed by Taiichi Ohno at Toyota — in which visual cards (kanban means "signboard" in Japanese) signal and limit work in progress.

Principles and practices

The Kanban Method is built on principles such as "start with what you do now", "agree to pursue incremental, evolutionary change" and "encourage acts of leadership at all levels". Its core practices are: visualising the workflow on a board divided into stages; limiting work in progress (WIP) at each stage to expose bottlenecks and smooth flow; managing and measuring flow using metrics such as lead time and throughput; making process policies explicit; implementing feedback loops (cadences); and improving collaboratively using models and experimentation.

The kanban board

A kanban board displays cards representing work items moving through columns such as "To Do", "In Progress" and "Done". A column's WIP limit caps the number of cards it may hold; when a column is full, upstream work stops and the team "pulls" new work only when capacity exists. This pull system contrasts with push systems in which work is assigned regardless of current load.

Relation to other methods

Kanban is often combined with Scrum (as "Scrumban") or used as an alternative agile approach for teams whose work arrives unpredictably, such as support and maintenance teams. Unlike Scrum it prescribes no fixed iterations, roles or ceremonies, instead evolving the team's existing process.
