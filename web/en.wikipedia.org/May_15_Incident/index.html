May 15 Incident

The May 15 Incident (五・一五事件, Goichigo Jiken) was an attempted coup d'état in the Empire of Japan, on May 15, 1932, launched by radical elements of the Imperial Japanese Navy, aided by cadets in the Imperial Japanese Army and civilian remnants of the ultranationalist League of Blood. Prime Minister Inukai Tsuyoshi was assassinated by 11 young naval officers. The attack on the Prime Minister undermined the rule of law, as the lenient sentencing of the assassins set a precedent, and is often identified as a key turning point in Japan's slide toward militarism and the collapse of party government.

Background

Discontent with the London Naval Treaty of 1930, which limited Japanese naval tonnage, and anger at the government's handling of the Manchurian Incident and the economic hardships of the Great Depression fuelled radical nationalism among young officers. The League of Blood had already assassinated former finance minister Inoue Junnosuke and industrialist Dan Takuma earlier in 1932.

The incident

On the evening of May 15, the conspirators attacked the Prime Minister's residence, the headquarters of the Seiyūkai party, the Bank of Japan, and police and transformer stations in Tokyo. Inukai was shot at his official residence after reportedly offering to talk with the attackers ("if we talk, you will understand"). Actor Charlie Chaplin, visiting Japan, may also have been a target but was attending a sumo match with the Prime Minister's son.

Aftermath

The assassins received extraordinarily light sentences after a public petition campaign — reportedly including severed fingers sent as tokens of support — and mass public sympathy. Inukai's death ended party-based cabinets in pre-war Japan; his successor was an admiral, and the military's political ascendancy deepened, paving the way for the February 26 Incident of 1936 and ultimately the Pacific War.
