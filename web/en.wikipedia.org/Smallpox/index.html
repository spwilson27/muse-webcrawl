Smallpox was an infectious disease caused by two virus variants, Variola major and Variola minor. It was characterised by fever and a distinctive progressive skin rash of raised fluid-filled blisters that scabbed over and often left deep pitted scars ("pockmarks"). The last naturally occurring case was in Somalia in 1977, and the World Health Organization declared smallpox globally eradicated in 1980 — the only human disease, alongside the animal disease rinderpest, to have been eliminated worldwide.

Course of disease

After an incubation period of about two weeks, the illness began abruptly with high fever, malaise, headache and backache, followed after two to four days by a rash that started in the mouth and on the face and spread over the body, evolving in unison through macules, papules, vesicles and pustules before crusting. Variola major killed roughly 30% of those infected and often blinded survivors; the much milder Variola minor (alastrim) had a fatality rate near 1%.

History

Smallpox is thought to have afflicted humanity for thousands of years, possibly scarring Egyptian mummies including that of Ramesses V. It devastated populations worldwide, killing an estimated 300–500 million people in the 20th century alone, and was catastrophic for Indigenous peoples of the Americas and Australia, who had no prior exposure.

Inoculation (variolation) — deliberate infection with material from smallpox sores — was practised in China and India and popularised in Britain in the 1720s by Lady Mary Wortley Montagu. In 1796 Edward Jenner demonstrated that inoculation with cowpox protected against smallpox, creating the first vaccine. The WHO's Intensified Eradication Programme, launched in 1967 under D. A. Henderson and using the heat-stable vaccine and surveillance-containment strategy, eliminated the disease within a decade.

After eradication

Known stocks of the virus are officially retained only at the CDC in the United States and the VECTOR institute in Russia, and the possible existence of forgotten or weaponised samples remains a biodefence concern. Routine vaccination ceased after eradication because the vaccine itself carried rare but serious risks.
