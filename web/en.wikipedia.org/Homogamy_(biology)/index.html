Homogamy (biology)

Homogamy in biology has two main usages. In botany, it refers to the condition of a flower in which the male (stamens) and female (pistil) reproductive organs mature at the same time, facilitating self-pollination; the opposite condition, in which the organs mature at different times, is called dichogamy. In zoology and evolutionary biology, homogamy refers to mating between similar individuals — also called assortative mating — as opposed to heterogamy, mating between dissimilar individuals.

Botany

Homogamous flowers can self-pollinate if pollinators fail, providing reproductive assurance, though at the cost of reduced genetic diversity through inbreeding. Dichogamy (protandry, where pollen matures first, or protogyny, where the stigma is receptive first) is a common outcrossing mechanism; homogamy represents the loss or absence of that temporal separation.

Assortative mating

In the mating sense, homogamy describes pairing by similarity in traits such as size, coloration, or genotype. Positive assortative mating increases homozygosity in a population and can contribute to speciation, notably in sympatric scenarios.
