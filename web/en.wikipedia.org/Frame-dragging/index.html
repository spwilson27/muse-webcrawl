Frame-dragging

A rotating mass drags the surrounding spacetime around with it

Frame-dragging is an effect on spacetime predicted by Albert Einstein's theory of general relativity. It is due to a non-static (rotating) mass distribution: a rotating massive object "drags" the spacetime around it, so that inertial frames near the object are pulled in the direction of its rotation. The effect was first derived in 1918 by Josef Lense and Hans Thirring and is also known as the Lense–Thirring effect.

Theory

In general relativity, mass-energy and its currents curve spacetime. Just as a moving electric charge produces a magnetic field, a rotating mass produces a "gravitomagnetic" field. Consequences include precession of gyroscopes and of orbital planes near a rotating body, effects that become extreme near rotating black holes: inside the ergosphere of a Kerr black hole, frame-dragging is so strong that no object can remain stationary relative to a distant observer.

Experimental confirmation

Frame-dragging around Earth is extremely small but has been measured. NASA's Gravity Probe B satellite (launched 2004) measured the precession of ultra-precise gyroscopes, reporting results in 2011 consistent with general relativity, including a frame-dragging drift of about 37 milliarcseconds per year. Laser-ranging to the LAGEOS satellites has also been used to detect the Lense–Thirring precession of their orbits.
