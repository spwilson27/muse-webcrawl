Apsidal precession

In celestial mechanics, apsidal precession (or apsidal advance) is the gradual rotation of the line connecting the apsides (line of apsides) of an astronomical body's orbit. The apsides are the orbit's two points of closest and farthest distance from the body being orbited: the periapsis and the apoapsis. The apsidal precession is the first time derivative of the argument of periapsis, one of the six main orbital elements of an orbit.

Causes

For a two-body system obeying Newton's law of universal gravitation with an exact inverse-square force, orbits are closed ellipses (a consequence of Bertrand's theorem) and there is no apsidal precession. Precession arises when the force deviates from a pure inverse-square law. Sources of such deviation include the gravitational perturbations of other bodies, the oblateness of the central body, tidal effects, and corrections from general relativity.

Examples

The apsidal precession of the planets of the Solar System is dominated by planet–planet interactions. The most famous case is the anomalous advance of the perihelion of Mercury: after accounting for the precession of the equinoxes and the perturbations of the other planets, a residual of about 43 arcseconds per century remained unexplained by Newtonian mechanics. Einstein showed in 1915 that general relativity predicts exactly this amount, one of the first confirmations of the theory.

Apsidal precession is also important in close binary star systems and in the orbits of stars around the supermassive black hole at the centre of the Milky Way, where the star S2 has been observed to undergo relativistic (Schwarzschild) precession.
