The Defenestrations of Prague were three incidents in the history of Bohemia in which people were defenestrated (thrown out of a window). Though already in existence in Middle French, the word "defenestrate" is believed to have first appeared in English in reference to the incidents in Prague.

First Defenestration (1419)

The First Defenestration of Prague involved the killing of several members of the city council by a crowd of radical Czech Hussites on 30 July 1419. Jan Želivský, a Hussite priest, led his congregation on a procession through Prague to the New Town Hall. The town councilors refused to release several Hussite prisoners, and stones were thrown at the procession; the mob stormed the hall and threw the judge, the burgomaster, and several councilors out of the windows onto spears below. The incident precipitated the Hussite Wars.

Second Defenestration (1618)

The Second Defenestration of Prague took place on 23 May 1618 and is considered the event that triggered the Thirty Years' War. Protestant Bohemian nobles, angered by perceived violations of the religious freedoms guaranteed by the Letter of Majesty, confronted the Catholic imperial regents Jaroslav Bořita of Martinice and Vilém Slavata of Chlum, along with their secretary Philip Fabricius, in Prague Castle and threw them from a third-floor window. Remarkably, all three survived the roughly 21-metre fall — Catholics attributed this to angels; Protestants to a conveniently placed dung heap. Fabricius was later ennobled with the title "von Hohenfall" (literally "of the high fall").

"Third Defenestration" (1948)

The term is also applied to the death in 1948 of Czechoslovak foreign minister Jan Masaryk, found dead below a bathroom window of the Foreign Ministry shortly after the Communist coup; the circumstances remain disputed.
