Effusion

In physics and chemistry, effusion is the process in which a gas escapes from a container through a hole of diameter considerably smaller than the mean free path of the molecules. Such a hole is often described as a pinhole, and the escape of the gas is due to the pressure difference between the container and the exterior.

Graham's law

Under these conditions, the rate at which the gas escapes is described by Graham's law: the rate of effusion of a gas is inversely proportional to the square root of its molar mass (or density). Lighter gases therefore effuse faster than heavier ones. The law was formulated by Scottish chemist Thomas Graham in the 1840s.

Applications

Effusion is exploited in effusion cells for molecular-beam epitaxy and in the gaseous diffusion/effusion enrichment of uranium, in which uranium hexafluoride gas passes through porous barriers, slightly enriching the lighter isotope uranium-235. It is distinguished from diffusion, which involves the gradual mixing of gases through collisions in bulk.
