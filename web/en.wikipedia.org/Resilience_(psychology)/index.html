Resilience (psychology)

Psychological resilience is the ability to cope mentally and emotionally with a crisis or to return to a pre-crisis state quickly. It refers to the process of adapting well in the face of adversity, trauma, tragedy, threats, or significant sources of stress—such as family and relationship problems, serious health problems, or workplace and financial stressors. Resilience is not a fixed trait possessed by a few, but a set of behaviours, thoughts, and actions that can be learned and developed.

History of research

Resilience research emerged in the 1970s from studies of children who thrived despite high-risk environments, notably Emmy Werner's longitudinal study of children on the Hawaiian island of Kauai and Norman Garmezy's work on children of parents with schizophrenia. Ann Masten later called resilience "ordinary magic"—arising from ordinary adaptive systems such as secure attachment, cognitive skills, and supportive relationships, rather than rare qualities.

Factors

Factors associated with resilience include:

  • Supportive relationships and social support
  • Realistic optimism and positive emotions
  • Cognitive flexibility and problem-solving skills
  • Sense of purpose and self-efficacy
  • Emotion regulation and impulse control
  • Physical health, sleep, and exercise
  • Cultural and community resources

Development and intervention

Resilience can be strengthened across the lifespan. School-based programmes, cognitive-behavioural approaches, and organisational training—such as the US Army's Comprehensive Soldier Fitness programme—aim to build coping skills before adversity strikes. Researchers distinguish resilience from related outcomes such as recovery (returning to baseline after a period of dysfunction) and post-traumatic growth (positive change beyond the pre-trauma baseline). Critics note that emphasising individual resilience can underplay the role of structural conditions and the resources communities need.
