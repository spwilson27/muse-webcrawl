Second Temple

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Second Temple was the Jewish temple in Jerusalem that stood between c. 516 BC and AD 70, replacing Solomon's Temple (the First Temple), which had been destroyed by the Neo-Babylonian Empire in 586 BC. It was built by the Jewish exiles returning from Babylon under Zerubbabel and vastly enlarged by Herod the Great from around 19 BC, becoming one of the largest sacred complexes of the ancient world.

History

Cyrus the Great of Persia authorised the exiles' return and the Temple's rebuilding, completed under Darius I. The sanctuary was desecrated by Antiochus IV Epiphanes in 167 BC, provoking the Maccabean revolt, and rededicated in 164 BC — the origin of Hanukkah. Herod's rebuilding created an immense artificial platform (the Temple Mount), supported by retaining walls including the Western Wall, and a splendid sanctuary of white stone and gold described by Josephus.

Destruction and legacy

During the First Jewish–Roman War, Roman legions under Titus besieged Jerusalem and burned the Temple in AD 70. The Arch of Titus in Rome depicts the spoils, including the great menorah. The destruction ended the sacrificial cult, redirected Judaism toward synagogue, prayer, and rabbinic study, and remains mourned on Tisha B'Av. The Temple Mount is today occupied by the Dome of the Rock and Al-Aqsa Mosque and is one of the most contested religious sites in the world.
