Soda–lime glass

Soda–lime glass, also called soda–lime–silica glass, is the most prevalent type of glass, used for windowpanes and glass containers such as bottles and jars. It accounts for about 90% of manufactured glass. Its composition is roughly 70% silica (silicon dioxide), 15% soda (sodium oxide), and 9% lime (calcium oxide), with much smaller amounts of other compounds.

Composition and properties

Pure silica has an impractically high melting point and viscosity; adding sodium oxide lowers the working temperature but makes the glass water-soluble, so calcium oxide is added to restore chemical durability. Soda–lime glass is inexpensive, reasonably hard, chemically stable, and readily recycled and reworked. It has a coefficient of thermal expansion that makes it prone to cracking under rapid temperature change, unlike borosilicate glass, and it is typically melted at around 1,300–1,500 °C.

Manufacture and uses

The glass is produced in large continuous furnaces using the float process for flat glass — molten glass floating on a bath of molten tin — and by moulding and blowing for containers. It can be thermally tempered or chemically strengthened for safety glazing and is the standard material for microscope slides, light bulbs, and food jars.
