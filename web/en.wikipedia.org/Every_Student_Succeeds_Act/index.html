The Every Student Succeeds Act (ESSA) is a United States federal law passed in December 2015 that governs the nation's K-12 public education policy. It replaced the No Child Left Behind Act (NCLB) of 2001 and modified — but did not eliminate — provisions relating to the periodic standardized tests given to students. Like the No Child Left Behind Act, ESSA is a reauthorization of the Elementary and Secondary Education Act of 1965.

History

ESSA was passed by Congress with strong bipartisan support. The House of Representatives approved the conference report on December 2, 2015, by a vote of 359–64, and the Senate passed it on December 9, 2015, by a vote of 85–12. President Barack Obama signed the act into law on December 10, 2015. The law marked a significant shift of authority from the federal government back to the states and local school districts.

Provisions

Under ESSA, states retained the requirement to test students annually in reading and mathematics in grades 3–8 and once in high school, and to report results disaggregated by student subgroups. However, states gained flexibility to design their own accountability systems, set their own goals for student achievement, and decide how to intervene in low-performing schools. The law maintained the requirement that states identify their lowest-performing five percent of schools for comprehensive support and improvement.

The act also included provisions supporting early learning, prohibiting the federal government from mandating specific academic standards such as the Common Core, and requiring states to adopt "challenging" academic standards. It replaced the "highly qualified teacher" requirement of NCLB and ended the federal teacher evaluation mandates.

Implementation

States were required to submit accountability plans to the U.S. Department of Education, with most plans approved during 2017 and 2018. ESSA took full effect in the 2017–18 school year. The act's emphasis on state flexibility and its focus on school quality indicators beyond test scores — such as chronic absenteeism — have shaped American education policy since its enactment.
