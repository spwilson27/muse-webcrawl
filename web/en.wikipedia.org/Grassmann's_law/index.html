Grassmann's law

Examples of aspirate dissimilation in Greek and Sanskrit

Grassmann's law, named after the German linguist Hermann Grassmann, who formulated it in 1863, is a dissimilatory sound change in Ancient Greek and Sanskrit. It states that when an aspirated consonant is followed by another aspirated consonant in the next syllable, the first one loses its aspiration (becomes a plain voiceless or voiced stop).

Examples

In Greek, the root meaning "hair," nominative thríks (θρίξ) with initial aspirate, has the genitive trikhós (τριχός), where the first consonant is unaspirated because the following syllable contains an aspirate. Similarly, the verb "to sacrifice," thýō (θύω), forms the future thýsō but the aorist passive etýphthēn, patterns that reveal underlying alternations explained by the law. In Sanskrit, the root budh- "awaken" forms the present bodhati rather than *bhodhati, and reduplicated forms such as babhūva (from bhū-) show the same dissimilation in reduplication syllables.

Significance

Grassmann's law is a classic example of a phonological rule that must be ordered relative to other changes: in Sanskrit it applies after the devoicing processes associated with Bartholomae's law in certain derivations. Because Greek and Sanskrit underwent the change independently, the law is dated after the breakup of Proto-Indo-European. Together with Grimm's law and Verner's law, it forms part of the foundational evidence for the comparative method in historical linguistics.
