Macromolecule

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A macromolecule is a very large molecule, commonly composed of thousands of atoms, of importance in biochemistry and polymer chemistry. The term was coined by the German chemist Hermann Staudinger in the 1920s, when the existence of such giant molecules was still controversial. Most macromolecules are polymers — chains of repeating smaller units called monomers — although some large molecules, such as fullerenes and certain lipids assemblies, do not fit the simple polymer model.

Types

Biological macromolecules fall into four principal classes: proteins (polymers of amino acids), nucleic acids such as DNA and RNA (polymers of nucleotides), polysaccharides such as starch, cellulose and glycogen (polymers of sugars), and, in a looser sense, lipids, which assemble into large structures although they are not true covalent polymers. Each class is built by dehydration-synthesis reactions and broken down by hydrolysis. Synthetic macromolecules include plastics such as polyethylene, nylon and polystyrene, synthetic rubbers, and fibres.

Properties

Because of their size, macromolecules behave differently from small molecules: their solutions are viscous, they may fold into elaborate three-dimensional conformations (as in protein folding), and their properties depend strongly on chain length, branching and cross-linking. Molecular masses range from tens of thousands to millions of daltons; a chromosome's DNA is a single macromolecule containing billions of atoms.

Study

Techniques for characterising macromolecules include X-ray crystallography, cryo-electron microscopy, NMR spectroscopy, gel electrophoresis and size-exclusion chromatography. Staudinger received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1953 for his work establishing macromolecular chemistry.
