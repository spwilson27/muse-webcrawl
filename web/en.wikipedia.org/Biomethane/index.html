Biomethane

Biomethane is a renewable form of methane produced from biogas by removing carbon dioxide and impurities, a process called upgrading. The resulting gas is typically more than 95% methane and is chemically nearly identical to fossil natural gas, so it can be injected into existing gas grids, used in standard gas appliances and vehicles, or traded as a substitute for natural gas. In North America it is often called renewable natural gas (RNG).

Production

The feedstock is raw biogas, usually 50–65% methane and 35–50% carbon dioxide, produced by anaerobic digestion of manure, sewage sludge, food waste, energy crops, and landfill material. Upgrading technologies include water scrubbing, pressure swing adsorption, chemical (amine) scrubbing, membrane separation, and cryogenic separation. The upgrading step also removes hydrogen sulfide, water vapour, siloxanes, and other contaminants that would damage engines or pipelines. The captured CO₂ by-product can sometimes be sold for industrial or greenhouse use.

Uses

Because it is a drop-in replacement for natural gas, biomethane is used for heating, electricity generation, and as a transport fuel (bio-CNG and bio-LNG) for trucks and buses. It commands premium prices in markets with renewable fuel credits, such as California's Low Carbon Fuel Standard and the US federal Renewable Fuel Standard, where biomethane from dairy manure can register very low or even negative carbon-intensity scores because it captures methane that would otherwise escape from manure lagoons.

Climate considerations

Combusting biomethane releases CO₂, but the carbon is biogenic — recently drawn from the atmosphere by plants — so its net climate effect mainly depends on avoiding methane leakage. Methane is a potent greenhouse gas, so capturing it from wastes delivers real benefits; conversely, leakage during production, upgrading, or distribution erodes those benefits. Feedstock choice matters: waste-based biomethane is generally rated highly sustainable, while purpose-grown energy crops involve land-use trade-offs.

Market

Europe, led by Germany, Denmark, France, and Italy, has the most developed biomethane sector, with hundreds of upgrading plants feeding national grids; Denmark has at times met a large share of its gas demand with biomethane. Production in the United States has grown rapidly under credit programmes. Even so, biomethane supplies remain a small fraction of total natural gas consumption worldwide.
