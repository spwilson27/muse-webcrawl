Wilhelm Carl Grimm (24 February 1786 – 16 December 1859) was a German author and anthropologist, and the younger brother of Jacob Grimm, of the literary duo known as the Brothers Grimm. Together they collected and published folklore in Kinder- und Hausmärchen ("Children's and Household Tales", 1812), worked on German legends, grammar and history, and began the monumental Deutsches Wörterbuch.

Life and career

Wilhelm Grimm was born in Hanau and studied law at Marburg, like his brother. Suffering from asthma and heart problems, he held posts as librarian in Kassel, where the brothers gathered tales from storytellers including Dorothea Viehmann. Wilhelm was chiefly responsible for editing the fairy tales through their successive editions between 1812 and 1857, smoothing their style, adding detail and Christian moral colouring, and shaping them for a family readership — the form in which tales such as "Cinderella", "Snow White" and "Hansel and Gretel" became world famous.

In 1825 Wilhelm married Henriette Dorothea Wild. Both brothers moved to Göttingen in 1829, where they served as professors and librarians; in 1837 they were dismissed as two of the "Göttingen Seven" who protested King Ernest Augustus's revocation of the Hanoverian constitution. The brothers later accepted chairs at the University of Berlin.

Scholarship

Wilhelm's independent work included editions of medieval German literature and studies of German heroic saga, notably Die deutsche Heldensage (1829). He contributed extensively to the Deutsches Wörterbuch until his death in Berlin in 1859; the dictionary was completed by later scholars in 1961.
