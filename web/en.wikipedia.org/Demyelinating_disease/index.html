Demyelinating disease

A demyelinating disease is any disease of the nervous system in which the myelin sheath of neurons is damaged. This damage impairs the conduction of signals in the affected nerves, causing impairment in sensation, movement, cognition, or other functions depending on which nerves are involved.

Causes and types

Demyelination can result from autoimmune attack, infection, genetic defects of myelin maintenance (leukodystrophies), metabolic disorders such as vitamin B12 deficiency, toxins, or compression and injury. The best-known demyelinating disease is multiple sclerosis, an autoimmune condition affecting the central nervous system. Other examples include neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder, acute disseminated encephalomyelitis, transverse myelitis, Guillain–Barré syndrome (affecting peripheral nerves), central pontine myelinolysis, and Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease variants.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms vary with the location and extent of demyelination and may include weakness, numbness or tingling, vision problems such as optic neuritis, loss of coordination and balance, bladder and bowel dysfunction, spasticity, and cognitive changes. In peripheral demyelination, slowed nerve conduction can be measured with nerve conduction studies.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis typically combines clinical examination, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to reveal lesions, cerebrospinal fluid analysis, and evoked potential tests. Treatment depends on the cause: autoimmune demyelination may be treated with corticosteroids, plasma exchange, and disease-modifying immunotherapies, while deficiency states are corrected by replacing the missing nutrient. Some remyelination can occur naturally, particularly early in disease, and remyelination therapies are an active research area.
