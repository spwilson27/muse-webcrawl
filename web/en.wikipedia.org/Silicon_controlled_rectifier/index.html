Silicon controlled rectifier

A silicon controlled rectifier or semiconductor controlled rectifier is a four-layer solid-state current-controlling device. The name "silicon controlled rectifier" is General Electric's trade name for a type of thyristor. The principle of four-layer p–n–p–n switching was developed by Moll, Tanenbaum, Goldey, and Holonyak of Bell Laboratories in 1956; the practical device was developed at General Electric and commercialised in 1957.

Operation

The SCR has three terminals: an anode, a cathode, and a gate. It normally blocks current in both directions. Applying a small current to the gate while the anode is positive relative to the cathode switches the device into conduction, and it continues to conduct — "latching" — even after the gate signal is removed, until the anode current falls below the holding current. In alternating-current circuits this happens naturally at each zero crossing, which makes SCRs suitable for phase control of power.

Applications

SCRs are used in lamp dimmers, motor speed controllers, high-voltage direct current power transmission, and industrial power supplies. Large devices can control thousands of amperes and tens of kilovolts. Related devices include the TRIAC, which conducts in both directions, and the gate turn-off thyristor (GTO), which can be switched off from the gate.
