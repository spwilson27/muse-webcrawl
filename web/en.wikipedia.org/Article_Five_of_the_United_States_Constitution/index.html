Article Five of the United States Constitution

Article Five of the United States Constitution describes the process for amending the Constitution. Amendments may be proposed either by a two-thirds vote in both houses of Congress or by a convention called by Congress at the request of two-thirds of the state legislatures, and must be ratified by three-fourths of the states.

Text

The article provides that Congress, "whenever two thirds of both Houses shall deem it necessary, shall propose Amendments to this Constitution", or, on the application of the legislatures of two thirds of the several states, shall call a convention for proposing amendments. In either case, proposed amendments become valid "when ratified by the Legislatures of three fourths of the several States, or by Conventions in three fourths thereof", as Congress chooses.

Provisions and limits

Article Five originally shielded two clauses of Article I, Section 9 — concerning the slave trade and direct taxes — from amendment before 1808. It further provides that no state, without its consent, shall be deprived of its equal suffrage in the Senate, the only explicitly entrenched provision remaining.

Use

Thousands of amendments have been proposed in Congress, but only 33 have been sent to the states and 27 ratified, the first ten constituting the Bill of Rights. No amendment has ever been proposed by a national convention, though applications for one have been filed by states on many subjects.
