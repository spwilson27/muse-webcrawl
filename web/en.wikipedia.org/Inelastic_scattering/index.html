Inelastic scattering

FieldParticle physics, condensed matter physics
Related processesElastic scattering, deep inelastic scattering
Energy transferNonzero (kinetic energy not conserved)

Inelastic scattering is a fundamental scattering process in which the kinetic energy of an incident particle is not conserved, because some of the energy is transferred to internal excitations of the target, such as electronic, vibrational, or rotational states. It is contrasted with elastic scattering, in which the total kinetic energy of the system is conserved.

Principles

In an inelastic collision, the incident particle loses (or occasionally gains) energy, and the internal structure of the target changes. In quantum mechanics the process is described by scattering amplitudes in which the outgoing particle emerges with a different energy and momentum, subject to overall conservation of total energy and momentum. The energy spectrum of scattered particles carries information about the excitation spectrum of the target.

Examples

Familiar examples include the Compton effect, in which a photon scatters from an electron and loses energy; Raman scattering, in which light is scattered with shifts corresponding to molecular vibrations; and neutron inelastic scattering, widely used to measure phonon and magnon dispersions in solids. Deep inelastic scattering of electrons from protons at high energy revealed the point-like quark constituents of nucleons and was historically crucial to the development of the parton model.

Applications

Inelastic scattering techniques are central tools in materials science and chemistry. Inelastic neutron scattering and inelastic X-ray scattering probe lattice dynamics and electronic excitations, while electron energy-loss spectroscopy (EELS) in the transmission electron microscope measures plasmons, band gaps, and chemical bonding at nanometre resolution.
