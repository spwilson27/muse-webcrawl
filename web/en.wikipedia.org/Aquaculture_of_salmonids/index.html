Aquaculture of salmonids

The aquaculture of salmonids is the farming and harvesting of salmonid fishes under controlled conditions. The dominant species is the Atlantic salmon (Salmo salar), alongside rainbow trout and, to a lesser extent, coho and chinook salmon. Farmed salmon production grew from a niche industry in the 1970s to over 2.8 million tonnes annually, supplying roughly 70 percent of the world's salmon market. Norway and Chile are the leading producers, followed by Scotland, Canada and the Faroe Islands.

Production cycle

Farming mirrors the salmon life cycle. Broodstock eggs are fertilised in freshwater hatcheries; the young fish (parr, then smolts) are raised in tanks or raceways until they undergo smoltification, adapting to seawater. They are then transferred to large floating net pens — sea cages up to 200 metres across — where they grow for 14–24 months on pelleted feed made from fishmeal, fish oil and increasingly plant ingredients. Harvest-size fish weigh 4–6 kg.

Issues

The industry faces criticism over sea lice infestations and their treatment, escapes that interbreed with wild salmon, local pollution from waste and feed, and reliance on wild-caught forage fish for feed. Infectious salmon anaemia and other diseases have caused mass mortality events, notably in Chile in 2007–2010. Responses include selective breeding, vaccines, cleaner fish such as lumpfish, closed-containment and land-based recirculating aquaculture systems (RAS), and certification schemes such as the Aquaculture Stewardship Council (ASC).

Economics

Salmon farming is dominated by a handful of companies, including Mowi, SalMar, Lerøy and Cermaq. Futures contracts for salmon trade on the Fish Pool exchange in Oslo, and the sector is a major export earner for Norway and Chile.
