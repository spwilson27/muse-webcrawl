Seahorse

Seahorses are small marine fish of the genus Hippocampus in the family Syngnathidae, which also includes pipefish and sea dragons. About 46 species are recognised. They are named for their horse-like head, held at right angles to an upright, S-curved body armoured in bony rings. Seahorses live in shallow tropical and temperate seas worldwide, among seagrass beds, mangroves, estuaries and coral reefs.

Anatomy and locomotion

Seahorses are poor swimmers. They swim upright, propelled by fluttering the dorsal fin up to dozens of times per second, steering with the pectoral fins behind the head. A prehensile tail anchors them to vegetation in currents. They lack scales and a stomach; food — tiny crustaceans and other plankton — is sucked through a tubular snout in rapid pipette-like strikes and passes quickly through the gut, so seahorses feed almost constantly. Their eyes move independently, like a chameleon's. Sizes range from pygmy seahorses barely 1.5 cm long to the big-belly seahorse at over 30 cm.

Reproduction

Seahorses are famous for male pregnancy. Courtship pairs may bond for a season or for life, greeting each other with daily dances and colour changes. The female deposits her eggs into a brood pouch on the male's abdomen, where he fertilises and carries them, supplying oxygen and nutrients through a placenta-like structure, before giving birth to as many as 2,000 tiny fry after two to four weeks. Few survive to adulthood.

Conservation

Many species are threatened by habitat loss, bycatch in trawl fisheries, and collection for traditional medicine, the aquarium trade and curios. All seahorses were listed on CITES Appendix II in 2004, the first fully marine fish group so regulated, and most assessed species are considered Vulnerable or worse. Their dependence on threatened habitats such as seagrass meadows makes them flagship species for coastal conservation.
