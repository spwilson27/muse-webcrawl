Molecular assembler

A molecular assembler, as defined by K. Eric Drexler, is a "proposed device able to guide chemical reactions by positioning reactive molecules with atomic precision". A molecular assembler is a kind of molecular machine. The concept is central to molecular nanotechnology, in which programmable, positionally controlled synthesis would allow the construction of complex structures atom by atom, analogous to how a ribosome assembles proteins from amino acids according to messenger RNA instructions.

Concept

Drexler introduced the idea in his 1986 book Engines of Creation and developed it technically in Nanosystems (1992). A general-purpose assembler, working with a library of molecular tools and feedstock, could in principle build a wide range of products — including copies of itself — enabling cheap, exponentially scalable manufacturing. Such self-replication also underlies the "grey goo" scenario of uncontrolled replication, which Drexler and others later emphasised is neither necessary for, nor an inevitable consequence of, molecular manufacturing; physically contained "nanofactories" were proposed instead.

Debate

The feasibility of molecular assemblers was famously debated between Drexler and Nobel laureate Richard Smalley in the early 2000s. Smalley's "fat fingers" and "sticky fingers" objections argued that manipulator arms could not fit in the crowded reaction space and could not pick and place individual atoms cleanly in arbitrary chemistry. Drexler and supporters replied that mechanosynthesis proposals use controlled environments, specific feedstock molecules, and enzyme-like precision rather than free-ranging atom grabbers.

Progress

No general-purpose artificial molecular assembler has been built, but related achievements include scanning probe microscope manipulation of single atoms, DNA nanotechnology and DNA origami, molecular machines recognised by the 2016 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, and experimental peptide and small-molecule synthesis machines inspired by the ribosome.
