Taishō era

The Taishō era (Japanese: 大正時代, Taishō jidai, "period of great righteousness") is the period of Japanese history corresponding to the reign of Emperor Taishō (Yoshihito), from 30 July 1912 to 25 December 1926. Though short, it is remembered for the flowering of liberal and democratic movements known as Taishō democracy, sandwiched between the rapid modernisation of the Meiji era and the militarism of early Shōwa.

Reign and politics

Emperor Taishō suffered chronic ill health, and from 1921 his son Hirohito served as regent. Political power shifted toward party cabinets: Hara Takashi, Japan's first commoner prime minister, took office in 1918 after the Rice Riots swept the country. The era saw the rise of party government, expansion of the electorate — culminating in universal manhood suffrage in 1925 — and vigorous debate about democracy, even as the same year brought the repressive Peace Preservation Law.

Foreign affairs and society

Japan fought on the Allied side in the First World War, seizing German possessions in Shandong and the Pacific, and emerged as a major power at Versailles, though its racial equality proposal was rejected. The 1923 Great Kantō earthquake devastated Tokyo and Yokohama and was followed by violence against Koreans and leftists. Culturally, the era produced urban mass culture, cafés, cinema, "modern girls" (moga), and writers such as Tanizaki and Akutagawa, alongside growing labour and tenant-farmer movements.

End of the era

Emperor Taishō died on 25 December 1926; Hirohito succeeded him, beginning the Shōwa era. Later historians often treat "Taishō democracy" ambivalently, as both a genuine liberalisation and a fragile interlude before the authoritarian turn of the 1930s.
