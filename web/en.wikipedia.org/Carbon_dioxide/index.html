Carbon dioxide

Carbon dioxide is a chemical compound with the chemical formula CO₂. It is made up of molecules that each have one carbon atom covalently double bonded to two oxygen atoms. It is found in the gas state at room temperature and is a trace gas in Earth's atmosphere at about 425 parts per million (as of the mid-2020s), having risen from roughly 280 ppm since the Industrial Revolution. Carbon dioxide is a greenhouse gas, absorbing and emitting infrared radiation, and its increase is the principal driver of observed global warming; dissolved in the oceans it forms carbonic acid, causing ocean acidification.

Role in biology

CO₂ is the primary carbon source for life on Earth: photosynthetic organisms, including plants and cyanobacteria, fix it into organic matter, while aerobic organisms produce it as a waste product of respiration and exhale it. In human physiology, CO₂ is carried in the blood mostly as bicarbonate, is the main regulator of blood pH, and drives the urge to breathe. Normal indoor and outdoor air poses no toxicity risk, but high concentrations are dangerous: levels above ~5% cause dizziness, confusion and unconsciousness, and because CO₂ is denser than air it can accumulate fatally in cellars, silos and volcanic crater lakes (as in the 1986 Lake Nyos disaster, which killed about 1,700 people).

Greenhouse effect and climate

CO₂ molecules absorb infrared radiation emitted by Earth's surface. Continuous measurements at Mauna Loa since 1958 (the Keeling Curve) document a steady rise, with fossil-fuel combustion (about 37 billion tonnes of CO₂ per year in recent years), cement production and deforestation as the sources; isotopic analysis confirms the fossil origin of the increase. CO₂ persists for centuries to millennia, making cumulative emissions the key determinant of peak warming; mitigation efforts include decarbonisation, carbon capture and storage, and direct air capture.

Production and uses

Industrially, CO₂ is produced by burning carbonaceous fuels, as a by-product of ammonia and hydrogen production and fermentation, and is recovered for use in carbonated drinks, food processing and refrigeration (as dry ice, solid CO₂), fire extinguishers, greenhouses, welding (as a shielding gas, and in supercritical form for decaffeination and as a solvent). At high pressure and temperature CO₂ is also used in enhanced oil recovery. Liquid and solid CO₂ have distinctive phase behaviour: at atmospheric pressure the solid sublimes directly to gas at −78.5 °C.

History

Carbon dioxide was the first gas distinguished from ordinary air: Jan Baptist van Helmont recognised it around 1625, and Joseph Black characterised it as "fixed air" in the 1750s. Joseph Priestley artificially carbonated water in 1767, and John Tyndall demonstrated its infrared absorption in 1859; Svante Arrhenius calculated in 1896 that doubling it would warm the Earth.
