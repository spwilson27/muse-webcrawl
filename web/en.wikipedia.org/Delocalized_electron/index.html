Delocalized electron

In chemistry, delocalized electrons are electrons in a molecule, ion or solid metal that are not associated with a single atom or a covalent bond. The term delocalization is general and can have slightly different meanings in different fields. In organic chemistry, it refers to resonance in conjugated systems and aromatic compounds. In solid-state physics, it refers to free electrons that facilitate electrical conduction. In quantum chemistry, it refers to molecular orbital electrons that extend over several adjacent atoms.

Resonance

In the prototypical example of benzene, a ring of six carbon atoms, the six pi electrons are delocalized around the entire ring. This is often indicated graphically by a circle inside the hexagon. The delocalization gives benzene equal carbon–carbon bond lengths intermediate between single and double bonds, and greater stability than would be expected for a hypothetical molecule with three isolated double bonds (the resonance energy).

Electrical conduction

In metallic solids, electrons in the outermost shells become delocalized over the whole lattice of positive ions — the so-called "sea of electrons". This delocalization explains the high electrical and thermal conductivity of metals, as the electrons can move freely in response to an applied potential.

Molecular orbitals

In molecular orbital theory, delocalization arises naturally because molecular orbitals are constructed over the whole molecule: an electron occupying a bonding orbital spread over several atoms is delocalized by definition. Extended conjugated pi systems, graphite, and graphene are further examples where delocalization over many atoms confers distinctive electronic and optical properties.
