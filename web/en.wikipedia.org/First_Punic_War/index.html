First Punic War

The First Punic War (264–241 BC) was the first of three wars fought between Rome and Carthage, the two main powers of the western Mediterranean in the early 3rd century BC. For 23 years, the two powers struggled for supremacy, primarily over the island of Sicily. It was the longest and largest war of the ancient world up to that time.

Origins

The war began when the Mamertines, Italian mercenaries occupying Messana (modern Messina), appealed for help against Syracuse and Carthage. Rome intervened in 264 BC, allying with the Mamertines, and fighting spread across Sicily. Carthage, a great naval power, initially dominated at sea, while Rome's strength lay in its legions.

Course of the war

Rome built a navy from scratch, adopting the corvus, a boarding device that turned sea battles into infantry engagements. Roman fleets won at Mylae (260 BC) and Ecnomus (256 BC), enabling an invasion of Africa under Regulus, which ended in disaster at Tunis in 255 BC. Thereafter the war became a grinding struggle for Sicily's western strongholds of Lilybaeum and Drepanum. Carthage's general Hamilcar Barca conducted an effective guerrilla campaign from mountain positions on the island.

Conclusion

In 241 BC a newly built Roman fleet defeated the Carthaginians at the Battle of the Aegates Islands, cutting off Hamilcar's army. Carthage sued for peace: by the Treaty of Lutatius, Carthage evacuated Sicily and paid a large indemnity of 3,200 talents. Sicily became Rome's first overseas province. Carthage's weakness after the war led to the Mercenary War and, eventually, renewed conflict in the Second Punic War.
