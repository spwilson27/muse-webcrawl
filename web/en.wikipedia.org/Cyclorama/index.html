Cyclorama

Viewers stand on a central platform surrounded by the continuous 360° painting.

A cyclorama is a panoramic painting exhibited inside a cylindrical space so that viewers standing on a central platform are surrounded by a continuous 360-degree image, giving the illusion of standing within the depicted scene. The foreground typically blends three-dimensional props, terrain and figures into the painted background to erase the boundary between the viewer and the picture. The term is also used in theatre for a curved backdrop (the "cyc") used for lighting effects, and the exhibition buildings themselves are often called cycloramas.

History

The panorama was patented by the Irish painter Robert Barker in 1787 and became a mass entertainment sensation in London, Paris and later the United States. Cycloramas reached their peak popularity in the 1880s and 1890s, when purpose-built rotundas in major American and European cities displayed enormous battle scenes — often hundreds of feet in circumference. Teams of artists, frequently led by European specialists such as Paul Philippoteaux, painted the canvases; Philippoteaux's depiction of the Battle of Gettysburg (1883–84) is among the most famous.

Surviving examples

Few of the hundreds of cycloramas survive. The Gettysburg Cyclorama, restored in 2008, is displayed at Gettysburg National Military Park. The Atlanta Cyclorama, depicting the Battle of Atlanta, moved in 2019 to the Atlanta History Center after nearly a century at Grant Park. Europe preserves notable examples including the Waterloo panorama in Belgium, the Bourbaki Panorama in Lucerne, and the Mesdag Panorama in The Hague. Australia's early example depicting Old Melbourne is held by the State Library Victoria.

Cinema and theatre

Cinema displaced the cyclorama as immersive entertainment after 1900. In theatre and film production, however, the word survives: a cyclorama is a large curtain or wall, often curved at the floor junction (an "infinity cove"), at the back of a stage or studio, lit to suggest sky or used for projection and chroma-key work.
