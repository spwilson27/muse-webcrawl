Berry–Esseen theorem

In probability theory, the Berry–Esseen theorem quantifies the rate at which the distribution of a normalized sum of independent random variables converges to the normal distribution, as asserted by the central limit theorem. It is named after Andrew C. Berry (1941) and Carl-Gustav Esseen (1942), who proved it independently.

Statement

Let X1, …, Xn be independent identically distributed random variables with mean zero, variance σ² > 0, and finite third absolute moment ρ = E|X1|³. Let Fn be the cumulative distribution function of the normalized sum, and Φ the distribution function of the standard normal. Then there exists a constant C such that, for all x and all n,

|Fn(x) − Φ(x)| ≤ Cρ / (σ³√n).

The theorem therefore bounds the worst-case error of the normal approximation by a quantity of order 1/√n, showing both the rate of convergence and its dependence on the skewness-like ratio ρ/σ³.

The constant

Much work has gone into narrowing the admissible value of C. Esseen showed in 1956 that C cannot be less than (√10 + 3)/(6√(2π)) ≈ 0.4097. Successive improvements by later authors have reduced the best known upper bound to below 0.48, so the optimal constant lies in a narrow interval around 0.41–0.48.

Extensions

Versions of the theorem exist for non-identically distributed summands (with the bound expressed through a Lyapunov-type ratio), for multivariate random vectors, and for dependent sequences. The result is a standard tool for assessing the accuracy of normal approximations in statistics and is among the earliest results in the quantitative theory of the central limit theorem.
