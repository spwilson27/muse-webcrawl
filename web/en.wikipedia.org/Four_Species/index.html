Four Species

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Four Species (Hebrew: אַרְבַּעַת הַמִּינִים, arba'at ha-minim) are four plant species named in the Torah and used ritually during the Jewish festival of Sukkot. They are the etrog (citron), lulav (palm frond), hadass (myrtle) and aravah (willow).

Biblical source

Leviticus 23:40 commands: "And you shall take on the first day the fruit of a goodly tree, branches of palm trees, and the bough of leafy trees, and willows of the brook; and you shall rejoice before the Lord your God seven days." Rabbinic tradition identifies the four items as the citron, a closed palm frond, three myrtle twigs and two willow branches.

The species

  • Etrog — the fruit of the citron tree (Citrus medica), a yellow citrus fruit held in the left hand.
  • Lulav — a ripe, green, unopened frond of the date palm tree; the bundle of the other species is also called by this name.
  • Hadass — three twigs of myrtle, whose leaves grow in groups of three.
  • Aravah — two branches of the willow tree, bound with the lulav and hadass.

The ritual

The lulav, hadass and aravah are bound together and held in the right hand, the etrog in the left; a blessing is recited and the species are waved in six directions (or shaken) during the Hallel prayers on each day of Sukkot except the Sabbath. The ceremony is also performed in procession around the synagogue during Hoshanot. The Talmud and later tradition assign the species symbolic meanings — representing parts of the body, types of Jews, or patriarchs and matriarchs — united in the ritual.

Laws and selection

Extensive rules in the Mishnah and Talmud (tractate Sukkah) govern the fitness of each species: the etrog must be unblemished and complete, the lulav straight and of minimum length, and so forth. Specialized markets sell graded etrogim, including the prized Calabrian ("Yanover") citron, before the festival each autumn.
