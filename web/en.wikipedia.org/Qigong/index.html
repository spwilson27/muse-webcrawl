Qigong

Qigong (Chinese: 氣功; pinyin: qìgōng) is a system of coordinated body posture and movement, breathing, and meditation rooted in Chinese medicine, philosophy, and martial arts. It is traditionally viewed as a practice to cultivate and balance qi (氣), understood as life energy, and is widely practiced in China and around the world for health, relaxation, and exercise.

History

Related health practices date back more than two thousand years: the Mawangdui Silk Texts (2nd century BCE) depict gymnastic postures, and the physician Hua Tuo developed the "frolics of the five animals" in the Han dynasty. Breathing and guiding exercises were cultivated in Daoist, Buddhist, Confucian, medical, and martial traditions. The term "qigong" in its modern sense was popularized in the 1950s, when the Chinese government promoted systematized, secularized forms as health therapy in sanatoria. A mass "qigong boom" followed in the 1980s and early 1990s, during which movements such as Falun Gong (Falun Dafa) attracted tens of millions of practitioners before being banned by the Chinese government in 1999.

Practice

Qigong forms range from slow, flowing standing movements, as in the widely taught Eight Pieces of Brocade (Baduanjin), to seated meditation and static postures (zhan zhuang). Practice emphasizes relaxed alignment, deep regulated breathing, and mental focus. Styles are commonly grouped as medical, martial, spiritual, and scholarly qigong.

Health claims and evidence

Clinical research suggests qigong practice may produce modest benefits for balance, quality of life, and some chronic conditions, but systematic reviews generally find the evidence limited by small samples and methodological flaws; claims of effects beyond those of gentle exercise and relaxation remain unproven. Qigong is generally regarded as safe, though rare adverse psychological effects ("qigong deviation") have been reported.
