The lungs are the primary organs of the respiratory system in many animals, including humans. In mammals and most other tetrapods, two lungs are located near the backbone on either side of the heart. Their function in the respiratory system is to extract oxygen from the atmosphere and transfer it into the bloodstream, and to release carbon dioxide from the bloodstream into the atmosphere, in a process called gas exchange.

The lungs are situated within the thoracic cavity of the chest, enclosed by the rib cage, which protects them, and separated from the abdominal cavity below by the muscular diaphragm. Each lung is surrounded by a double-layered serous membrane called the pleura; the space between the layers, the pleural cavity, contains a thin film of fluid that allows the lungs to slide smoothly as they expand and contract during breathing.

Structure

Gross anatomy

The right lung is larger and heavier than the left, and is divided into three lobes — upper, middle, and lower — by the horizontal and oblique fissures. The left lung has two lobes, upper and lower, separated by the oblique fissure, and has a concave region on its medial surface called the cardiac notch, which accommodates the heart. An average lung can hold around 6 litres of air, although the volume exchanged in quiet breathing is only a fraction of this, the tidal volume being roughly 500 millilitres.

Air enters the lungs through the trachea (windpipe), which divides at the carina into the two main bronchi. Each bronchus enters a lung at the hilum, a medial indentation through which the pulmonary vessels and nerves also pass. Within the lung, the bronchi branch repeatedly into smaller bronchioles, forming the bronchial tree, and ultimately terminate in clusters of tiny air sacs called alveoli, of which there are several hundred million in the adult human lungs, providing an enormous surface area — roughly 70 square metres — for gas exchange.

Microanatomy

The alveoli are lined by a single layer of squamous epithelial cells (type I pneumocytes) interspersed with cuboidal type II pneumocytes, which secrete pulmonary surfactant, a phospholipid mixture that reduces surface tension and prevents the alveoli from collapsing at the end of expiration. Each alveolus is wrapped in a dense network of capillaries. Oxygen diffuses across the thin blood–air barrier from the alveolar air into the blood, while carbon dioxide diffuses in the opposite direction.

Function

The principal function of the lungs is the exchange of gases between the air and the blood. Ventilation — the movement of air into and out of the lungs — is driven by the diaphragm and the intercostal muscles. On inhalation, the diaphragm contracts and flattens, increasing the volume of the thoracic cavity and drawing air in; on exhalation, the muscles relax and the elastic recoil of the lungs expels air.

Beyond gas exchange, the lungs perform several other roles. They help regulate the pH of the blood by controlling the amount of carbon dioxide exhaled. They filter small blood clots formed in veins, trap and dissolve them, and act as a physical and immunological barrier to inhaled pathogens and particles, aided by mucus produced in the airways and the mucociliary escalator that sweeps trapped material upward. The lungs also play a part in metabolism: the enzyme angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE), located on pulmonary capillary endothelium, converts angiotensin I to the potent vasoconstrictor angiotensin II, and the lungs inactivate several vasoactive substances.

Development

The lungs develop from the respiratory diverticulum (lung bud), an outgrowth of the ventral wall of the embryonic foregut that appears around the fourth week of gestation. Branching morphogenesis generates the bronchial tree, and maturation proceeds through pseudoglandular, canalicular, saccular, and alveolar stages. Alveoli continue to form after birth and into childhood. Premature infants may lack sufficient surfactant, causing infant respiratory distress syndrome, which can be treated with exogenous surfactant and respiratory support.

Clinical significance

Diseases of the lung are among the most common medical conditions worldwide. They are broadly classified as obstructive diseases, in which airflow is limited — including asthma, chronic bronchitis, and emphysema (the latter two grouped as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, COPD) — and restrictive diseases, in which lung expansion is reduced, including pulmonary fibrosis and sarcoidosis. Infections such as pneumonia and tuberculosis remain major causes of death globally. Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer death in both men and women, strongly associated with tobacco smoking. Other important conditions include pulmonary embolism, pneumothorax (collapsed lung), and pleural effusion.

Function of the lungs can be assessed with spirometry, which measures volumes such as forced vital capacity (FVC) and forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1). Imaging includes chest radiography and computed tomography. Surgical procedures on the lung range from wedge resection and lobectomy to pneumonectomy (removal of an entire lung), and end-stage lung disease may be treated with lung transplantation.

In other animals

Lungs evolved in early bony fishes as outpocketings of the gut, and lungfish retain functional lungs alongside gills. In amphibians the lungs are simple sacs and much gas exchange occurs across the skin. Reptilian lungs are more internally divided. Birds have a radically different arrangement: rigid parabronchial lungs ventilated by air sacs produce a unidirectional flow of air and exceptionally efficient gas exchange. Invertebrates such as snails and spiders have independently evolved lung-like structures (the mantle cavity of pulmonate snails and the book lungs of arachnids).
