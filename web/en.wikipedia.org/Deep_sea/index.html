Deep sea

The deep sea is broadly defined as the ocean at depths below 200 metres, where sunlight fades and photosynthesis is no longer possible. It is the largest habitat on Earth, characterised by near-freezing temperatures, immense pressure, complete darkness and food supplied largely by organic material sinking from the surface.

Zones

Oceanographers divide the water column into the mesopelagic or twilight zone (200–1,000 m), the bathypelagic or midnight zone (1,000–4,000 m), the abyssopelagic zone (4,000–6,000 m) and the hadalpelagic zone of the ocean trenches below 6,000 m. Each zone supports communities adapted to its conditions.

Life

Deep-sea organisms show adaptations including bioluminescence, gigantism, slow metabolism and pressure-resistant biochemistry. Around hydrothermal vents and cold seeps, entire ecosystems run on chemosynthesis rather than sunlight. Food falls such as marine snow and whale falls sustain scavengers and specialist communities.

Exploration

Exploration began with nineteenth-century dredging expeditions such as HMS Challenger and advanced with bathyscaphes, submersibles such as Alvin, and robotic vehicles. The deep seabed is now of interest for polymetallic nodule mining, a prospect that has raised environmental concern.
