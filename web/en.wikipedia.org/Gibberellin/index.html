Gibberellin


Gibberellic acid (GA3), the best-known gibberellin.

Gibberellins (GAs) are plant hormones that regulate various developmental processes, including stem elongation, germination, dormancy, flowering, flower development, and leaf and fruit senescence. GAs are one of the longest-known classes of plant hormone. It is now known that the dwarf and semi-dwarf varieties central to the Green Revolution of the 1960s owe their reduced stature to altered gibberellin signalling or biosynthesis.

More than 130 gibberellins have been identified; they are tetracyclic diterpenoid acids, designated GA1, GA2, GA3 and so on in order of discovery. Only a few, principally GA1 and GA4 (and GA3 and GA7 in some species), are themselves bioactive hormones; the rest are precursors or deactivated forms.

Discovery

Gibberellins were first recognised in Japan in studies of bakanae ("foolish seedling") disease of rice, in which infected plants grew excessively tall and spindly. In 1926 Eiichi Kurosawa showed that a cell-free extract of the fungus Gibberella fujikuroi (now Fusarium fujikuroi) caused the same symptoms, and by the late 1930s Teijiro Yabuta and colleagues had isolated the active substance, named gibberellin. Western scientists isolated gibberellic acid (GA3) in the 1950s, and the hormone's effects on dwarf pea and maize mutants established it as an endogenous plant growth regulator.

Function

Gibberellins promote stem elongation by stimulating cell division and elongation; they underlie the dramatic elongation (bolting) of rosette plants before flowering. In seeds, GA released by the embryo induces the aleurone layer to produce α-amylase and other hydrolytic enzymes that mobilise stored food reserves during germination — the classic barley aleurone assay demonstrates this. GAs also break seed and bud dormancy, influence the juvenile-to-adult phase change, promote flowering in many long-day and biennial plants, and stimulate fruit set and growth, being used commercially to produce seedless grapes and to increase fruit size.

Signalling

Gibberellin is perceived by the soluble receptor GID1. Binding of GA to GID1 allows the receptor to bind DELLA proteins, growth-repressing transcriptional regulators, which are then targeted for degradation by the proteasome via an F-box protein (SLY1/GID2). Destruction of DELLAs releases their repression of growth. The semi-dwarf wheat varieties of the Green Revolution carry mutant DELLA genes (Rht) whose proteins resist GA-induced degradation, while semi-dwarf rice carries a defective GA biosynthesis gene (sd1, encoding GA 20-oxidase).

Regulation and uses

Plants regulate active GA levels through biosynthesis (via the ent-kaurene pathway in plastids and the endoplasmic reticulum, then soluble 2-oxoglutarate-dependent dioxygenases) and through deactivation, principally by GA 2-oxidases. Synthetic gibberellin (mostly GA3) is applied in agriculture and horticulture, and growth retardants that block GA biosynthesis (such as paclobutrazol) are used to control stature in cereals and ornamentals.
