Human mission to Mars

Human mission to Mars
Type: Proposed crewed exploration
Distance: 56–400 million km (varies)
Typical transit: ~6–9 months each way
Key programmes: NASA Artemis (precursor), SpaceX Starship
First serious study: Von Braun, 1952

A human mission to Mars has been the subject of science fiction, aerospace engineering proposals and space agency planning since the mid-20th century. Sending astronauts to Mars poses extreme challenges: journeys of six to nine months each way, high radiation exposure, entry and landing of heavy payloads through the thin Martian atmosphere, the need for in-situ resource utilisation, and the psychological effects of isolation with communication delays of up to 22 minutes one way.

History of proposals

Wernher von Braun set out the first detailed technical study in Das Marsprojekt (1952), envisaging a flotilla of ten ships. After Apollo, NASA's 1969 Space Task Group proposed a crewed Mars mission for the 1980s, but it was not funded. Later reference designs — including NASA's Design Reference Missions, Robert Zubrin's Mars Direct (1990), which advocatedes making return propellant from Martian resources, and the Mars Society's advocacy — have kept the goal alive. Private efforts, notably SpaceX's Starship programme, aim to lower the cost of transporting large masses to Mars, while NASA pursues the Moon first through Artemis as a proving ground.

Mission architectures

Most studies adopt a conjunction-class profile: crews travel when Earth and Mars align (~every 26 months), stay on the surface for roughly 500 days while the planets realign, and return — a total mission of about 2.5 years. Alternatives include short-stay opposition-class missions and one-way colonisation proposals such as the defunct Mars One. Landing systems must slow payloads far heavier than robotic landers, motivating supersonic retropropulsion and aerocapture research.
