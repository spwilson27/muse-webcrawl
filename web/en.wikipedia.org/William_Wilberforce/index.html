William Wilberforce

William Wilberforce
Born: 24 August 1759, Hull, England
Died: 29 July 1833, London
Known for: Abolition of the British slave trade

William Wilberforce (24 August 1759 – 29 July 1833) was a British politician, philanthropist, and leader of the movement to abolish the Atlantic slave trade. A native of Kingston upon Hull, he entered Parliament in 1780 and, influenced by his evangelical conversion, devoted much of his career to reform causes, most famously the campaign against the slave trade.

Abolition campaign

Persuaded by Thomas Clarkson, Lady Middleton, and others, Wilberforce took up the abolitionist cause in Parliament in 1787, working with Prime Minister William Pitt the Younger. He first spoke against the trade in 1789 in a celebrated three-and-a-half-hour speech, and introduced abolition bills year after year through the 1790s and early 1800s. After two decades of campaigning, the Slave Trade Act of 1807 finally abolished the trade in enslaved people throughout the British Empire.

Wilberforce continued to press for the emancipation of those already enslaved, co-founding the Anti-Slavery Society in 1823. Failing health forced his retirement from Parliament in 1825. He died on 29 July 1833, one month before the Slavery Abolition Act 1833 passed, which freed enslaved people in most of the British Empire. He was buried in Westminster Abbey.

Other work and faith

Wilberforce was a leading figure in the Clapham Sect of evangelical reformers, a co-founder of the Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals (later RSPCA), and an advocate for education, prison reform, and missions. His book A Practical View of Christianity (1797) was an influential statement of evangelical faith.
