Reformism

Reformism is a political philosophy and trend advocating the reform of an existing system and institutions as opposed to its abolition and replacement via revolution. Within the socialist movement, reformism is the view that gradual changes through existing institutions can eventually lead to fundamental changes in a society's political and economic systems.

History

Reformism as a distinct current within socialism is associated with Eduard Bernstein, the German social democrat whose Evolutionary Socialism (1899) argued that capitalism was not collapsing and that the movement's practical work — trade unions, parliamentary representation, social legislation — was itself the path to socialism. His famous formula held that "the movement is everything, the final goal nothing."

Bernstein's revision of orthodox Marxism was condemned by revolutionary Marxists such as Rosa Luxemburg, whose Reform or Revolution (1900) argued that reforms won within capitalism could not substitute for the conquest of political power. The split between reformist and revolutionary currents deepened after the Russian Revolution of 1917 and the founding of communist parties aligned with the Comintern.

Reformism in social democracy

After the Second World War, most Western European socialist and labour parties formally adopted reformist programmes. The German SPD's Bad Godesberg programme of 1959 dropped Marxist references in favour of a managed market economy, while parties in Scandinavia built extensive welfare states through decades of incremental legislation.

Critics from the left have argued that reformist governments administer capitalism rather than transcend it, pointing to episodes such as the French Socialist government's austerity turn of 1983. Defenders reply that reformism delivered rising living standards, universal healthcare, and expanded democratic rights without the authoritarianism associated with revolutionary regimes.
