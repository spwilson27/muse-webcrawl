Flywheel energy storage (FES) works by accelerating a rotor (flywheel) to a very high speed and maintaining the energy in the system as rotational energy. When energy is extracted from the system, the flywheel's rotational speed is reduced; conversely, adding energy accelerates the flywheel. Most FES systems use electricity to accelerate and decelerate the flywheel, but devices that directly use mechanical energy are being developed.

Design

Advanced FES systems have rotors made of high-strength carbon-fibre composites, suspended by magnetic bearings and spinning at speeds of tens of thousands of revolutions per minute in a vacuum enclosure to minimize friction. The stored energy is proportional to the moment of inertia and the square of the rotational speed, so doubling speed quadruples storage — hence the emphasis on high-speed, high-strength rotors rather than massive steel wheels, although traditional steel flywheels remain in use for some applications. A motor-generator on the same shaft converts between electrical and mechanical energy with round-trip efficiencies around 85–95%.

Applications

Flywheels excel at delivering high power for short durations with very fast response and long cycle life, since they do not degrade with charge cycles the way batteries do. Grid-scale plants — such as Beacon Power's 20 MW facilities in New York and Pennsylvania — provide frequency regulation to electricity grids. Flywheel uninterruptible power supplies bridge the seconds between a mains failure and generator start-up in data centres and hospitals. In transport, flywheels have been used for regenerative braking in buses, trains and the KERS systems tested in Formula One. NASA has studied flywheels as combined energy-storage and attitude-control devices for spacecraft.

Safety

The main hazard is catastrophic rotor failure: a high-speed flywheel that disintegrates releases its stored energy suddenly. Rotors are therefore operated within robust containment vessels, and composite rotors are designed to fail by delaminating into fine debris rather than large fragments.
