Xenobiotic

A xenobiotic is a chemical substance found within an organism that is not naturally produced by or expected to be present within it. The term, from Greek xenos ("foreign") and bios ("life"), covers most drugs, industrial chemicals, pesticides, food additives and environmental pollutants insofar as they enter living bodies.

Phases of xenobiotic metabolism

Metabolism

Organisms handle xenobiotics through xenobiotic metabolism, centred in the liver and conventionally divided into phases. Phase I reactions — oxidation, reduction and hydrolysis, often performed by the cytochrome P450 enzyme family — introduce or expose reactive groups. Phase II conjugation reactions attach bulky polar groups (glucuronidation, sulfation, glutathione conjugation), increasing water solubility. Phase III involves further modification and excretion via transporters into bile or urine. The process usually detoxifies, but some compounds are bioactivated into more toxic forms, as with aflatoxin B1 or the paracetamol metabolite NAPQI.

Environmental significance

Xenobiotics such as pharmaceuticals, microplastics additives and persistent organic pollutants enter waterways and food chains; some resist biodegradation and bioaccumulate. Microorganisms in soil and water can, however, degrade many xenobiotics, a capacity exploited in bioremediation. The study of how organisms respond to foreign compounds underpins pharmacology, toxicology and the exposome research field.

Other uses

In transplantation medicine, xenobiotic is occasionally used loosely in connection with xenotransplantation (cross-species grafts), and in astrobiology the term can denote hypothetical biochemicals foreign to terrestrial life.
