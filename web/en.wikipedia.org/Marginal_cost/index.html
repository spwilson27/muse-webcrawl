Marginal cost

The U-shaped marginal cost curve intersects average total cost at its minimum.

In economics, marginal cost is the change in total production cost that arises when the quantity produced is incremented by one unit — that is, the cost of producing one additional unit of a good or service. Mathematically, marginal cost is the derivative of total cost with respect to quantity, MC = dTC/dQ. It is a central concept because rational firms maximise profit by expanding output until marginal cost equals marginal revenue (and, in perfect competition, the market price).

Short run and long run

In the short run, where some inputs such as plant are fixed, marginal cost typically falls at first as specialisation and efficiency gains are exploited, then rises as diminishing marginal returns set in — crowding more variable input onto fixed capacity. This gives the marginal cost curve its characteristic U or check-mark shape. In the long run all inputs are variable, and marginal cost reflects the technology and scale economies of the optimally sized plant.

Because fixed costs do not change with small changes in output, short-run marginal cost equals the change in variable cost. The marginal cost curve intersects both the average variable cost and average total cost curves at their minimum points, a relationship used to locate the break-even and shut-down points of a firm.

Applications

Pricing at marginal cost is efficient in the sense that it equates the value consumers place on the last unit with the resources used to make it, but it may fail to cover fixed costs in industries with decreasing costs, such as utilities — motivating marginal cost pricing debates and two-part tariffs. Public economics uses social marginal cost, adding external costs such as pollution to private marginal cost, as the benchmark for Pigouvian taxes. In service industries with near-zero marginal cost, such as software and digital media, the concept underlies freemium and subscription pricing models.
