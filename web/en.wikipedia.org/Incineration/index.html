Incineration

Incineration is a waste treatment process that involves the combustion of organic substances contained in waste materials. Industrial plants for waste incineration are commonly called waste-to-energy facilities when they recover heat or electricity from the process. Incineration converts waste into ash, flue gas and heat, greatly reducing the volume of the original waste — typically by 85–95 percent.

Process

Waste is burned in a furnace at high temperatures, commonly between 850 and 1,100 °C. The resulting heat may drive a boiler to produce steam for district heating or electricity generation. The solid residue, bottom ash, is largely inert and may be used as aggregate after treatment; fly ash, captured from flue gases, contains concentrated pollutants and is handled as hazardous waste in many jurisdictions.

Emissions and control

Incineration produces carbon dioxide and, depending on the waste feed and controls, pollutants including nitrogen oxides, acid gases, particulates, heavy metals and dioxins. Modern plants employ extensive flue-gas treatment — filters, scrubbers and catalytic systems — and operate under strict emission limits in the European Union, the United States and elsewhere.

Debate

Supporters value incineration for reducing landfill dependence and recovering energy; opponents argue that it discourages recycling, emits greenhouse gases, and raises environmental justice concerns where plants are sited near disadvantaged communities.
