Body art

Body art is art made on, with, or consisting of the human body. The most common forms of body art are tattoos and body piercings, but other types include scarification, branding, subdermal implants, scalpelling, shaping (such as tight-lacing, foot binding and neck stretching), full-body tattoos, and body painting. The term is also used for a form of avant-garde performance art in which the artist's own body serves as the medium or canvas, a movement that flourished in the 1960s and 1970s.

Body art ranges from traditional tattooing to contemporary performance art.

Traditional forms

Body modification is among the oldest of human practices. Ötzi, the natural mummy from the Alps dating to around 3300 BC, bears the oldest preserved tattoos. Tattoo traditions are deeply rooted in cultures worldwide, from the tā moko of New Zealand's Māori people to Japanese irezumi and the ritual tattooing of Polynesian societies—the word "tattoo" itself derives from the Tahitian tatau. Henna (mehndi) body painting is used in South Asian and Middle Eastern celebrations; scarification holds ceremonial significance in parts of Africa and Papua New Guinea; and ear stretching, lip plates, and neck rings represent shaping traditions.

Performance art

In 1960s and 1970s performance art, artists used their bodies to challenge the boundaries of art: Yves Klein used nude models as "living brushes"; Viennese Actionists staged transgressive rituals; Marina Abramović's Rhythm 0 (1974) surrendered her body to the audience; Chris Burden had himself shot in the arm (Shoot, 1971); and the French artist Orlan underwent a series of plastic surgeries as art works. Such work explored pain, endurance, identity, and the relationship between artist and spectator.

Contemporary practice

Tattooing and piercing moved from subcultural margins into the mainstream during the late 20th century, aided by the "modern primitives" movement, improved equipment, and celebrity adoption. Surveys in many Western countries now find that a substantial share of adults—often a quarter to a third—have at least one tattoo. Professional standards, single-use needles, and regulation have improved safety, though risks include infection, allergic reactions, and scarring. Body painting remains popular at festivals and in fine art and advertising.

Social attitudes and law

Attitudes vary widely by culture, religion, and profession; some religious traditions prohibit tattooing, while others regard body markings as sacred. Visible tattoos were long restricted in Japanese bathhouses and in certain workplaces, though such rules are gradually relaxing in many places. Many jurisdictions set minimum ages and licensing requirements for tattooists and piercers.
