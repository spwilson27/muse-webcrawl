Geodesic polyhedron

A geodesic polyhedron is a convex polyhedron made from triangles. They usually have icosahedral symmetry, such that they have 6 triangles at a vertex, except 12 vertices which have 5 triangles. They are the dual of corresponding Goldberg polyhedra, which have mostly hexagonal faces.

Construction

Geodesic polyhedra are constructed by subdividing the triangular faces of an icosahedron (or, less commonly, an octahedron or tetrahedron) into smaller triangles and projecting the new vertices onto the circumscribed sphere. They are classified by two integers (b, c) describing the subdivision grid, following the notation Magnus Wenninger applied to spherical models; the number of triangles is 20 times b² + bc + c² for the icosahedral family.

Relationship to geodesic domes

Buckminster Fuller popularised the geometry in his geodesic domes, in which part of a geodesic polyhedron forms a lightweight, strong shell structure. The same geometry appears in nature in the capsids of many icosahedral viruses and in carbon molecules known as fullerenes, whose dual Goldberg structure combines pentagons and hexagons.

Elements

A geodesic polyhedron has 12 vertices of degree 5 and the remainder of degree 6, a consequence of Euler's formula. Its edges can be of several slightly different lengths depending on the subdivision and projection method, a practical consideration in dome construction.
