Structuralism

Structuralism
FieldsLinguistics, anthropology, literary theory, philosophy
Key figuresFerdinand de Saussure, Claude Lévi-Strauss, Roman Jakobson, Roland Barthes
PeriodEarly to mid-20th century onward

Structuralism is an intellectual current and methodological approach that understands elements of human culture as needing to be understood by way of their relationship to a broader system. It works to uncover the structures that underlie all the things that humans do, think, perceive, and feel. The meaning of an element, in this view, comes not from the element itself but from its differences from and relations to other elements within the system.

Origins in linguistics

Structuralism originated in the linguistics of Ferdinand de Saussure, whose posthumously published Course in General Linguistics (1916) distinguished between langue (the abstract system of a language) and parole (individual speech), and argued that linguistic signs are arbitrary and defined by their differences from other signs. The Prague and Copenhagen schools, and Roman Jakobson, developed these ideas further.

Anthropology and beyond

Claude Lévi-Strauss extended structural analysis to anthropology, studying kinship systems and myth as expressions of underlying mental structures, notably binary oppositions. In the 1950s and 1960s, structuralist approaches flourished in literary criticism (Roland Barthes), psychoanalysis (Jacques Lacan), Marxist theory (Louis Althusser), and the history of ideas (Michel Foucault's early work). From the late 1960s, critics such as Jacques Derrida challenged structuralism's assumptions, giving rise to post-structuralism.
