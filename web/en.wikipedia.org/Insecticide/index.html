Insecticide

DefinitionPesticide formulated to kill, harm, repel, or mitigate insects
Major classesOrganochlorines, organophosphates, carbamates, pyrethroids, neonicotinoids
UsesAgriculture, public health (vector control), households

An insecticide is a substance used to kill insects, including ovicides and larvicides used against insect eggs and larvae respectively. Insecticides are used in agriculture, medicine, industry, and by consumers. They are one class of pesticides, alongside herbicides, fungicides, and others, and are credited with large increases in agricultural productivity in the 20th century, as well as with major public-health gains through control of disease vectors such as malaria-carrying mosquitoes.

History

Botanical and mineral insecticides—sulfur, arsenic compounds, pyrethrum, nicotine, and rotenone—were used for centuries. The modern era began with DDT, whose insecticidal properties were discovered by Paul Hermann Müller in 1939; DDT was used extensively in the Second World War and in malaria eradication campaigns, earning Müller the Nobel Prize in 1948. Rachel Carson's Silent Spring (1962) documented the environmental harm of persistent organochlorines, spurring regulation; DDT was banned for agricultural use in the United States in 1972 and restricted globally under the Stockholm Convention.

Classes and modes of action

Organochlorines (such as DDT and dieldrin) attack the nervous system and persist in the environment and fat tissue. Organophosphates and carbamates inhibit acetylcholinesterase, disrupting nerve transmission; organophosphates include parathion and malathion and are generally less persistent but more acutely toxic to humans. Pyrethroids, synthetic analogues of natural pyrethrins, prolong the opening of sodium channels and are widely used in household sprays and insecticide-treated bed nets. Neonicotinoids (such as imidacloprid) act on nicotinic acetylcholine receptors, are systemic in plants, and became the most widely used class worldwide, though they are implicated in harm to pollinators and have been restricted in the European Union. Other approaches include insect growth regulators, microbial insecticides such as Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt), oils and soaps, and genetically engineered Bt crops.

Environmental and health effects

Insecticides can harm non-target species, including pollinators such as bees, aquatic invertebrates, birds, and natural predators of pests; runoff and drift spread exposure beyond treated fields. Over-reliance selects for insecticide resistance—now documented in hundreds of pest species—which integrated pest management (IPM) seeks to counter by combining biological control, cultural practices, and judicious chemical use. Human health risks range from acute poisoning, a significant problem among agricultural workers in developing countries, to debated chronic effects.
