Intaglio (printmaking)


In intaglio printing, ink held in incised lines is transferred to paper under pressure.

Intaglio (from Italian intagliare, "to carve") is a family of printmaking techniques in which the image is incised into a surface — traditionally a copper or zinc plate — and the incised lines or recessed areas hold the ink. This is the opposite of relief printing, where ink sits on the raised surface. The plate is inked, wiped so that ink remains only in the recesses, covered with dampened paper and run through a high-pressure press, which forces the paper into the lines to pick up the image. Intaglio methods include engraving, etching, drypoint, mezzotint and aquatint.

Techniques

In engraving, lines are cut directly with a burin. In etching, the plate is coated with an acid-resistant ground, drawn through with a needle, and bitten with acid. Drypoint scratches the plate with a hard needle, raising a burr that gives a characteristically soft, velvety line but wears quickly. Mezzotint works from dark to light, roughening the whole plate and smoothing areas for highlights, while aquatint uses a powdered resin ground to create tonal areas, often combined with etched line.

History

Intaglio printing emerged in Germany in the 1430s–1440s, growing out of goldsmiths' engraving, and was brought to perfection by artists such as Martin Schongauer, Albrecht Dürer and, in etching, Rembrandt. Because intaglio plates can reproduce extremely fine detail, the method was long used for banknotes, stamps and security printing, and it remains a major medium of fine-art printmaking.
