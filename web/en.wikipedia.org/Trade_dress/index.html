Trade dress

[Image: Distinctive product packaging as trade dress]
images/tradedress.png

Trade dress is a legal term of art in United States trademark law for the overall visual appearance of a product or its packaging — its design, shape, colour scheme, graphics and packaging — that signifies the source of the product to consumers. Protectable trade dress can include the shape of a Coca-Cola bottle, the layout and décor of a restaurant, or the appearance of a product's packaging, provided it is distinctive and non-functional.

Area of lawTrademark (United States)
StatuteLanham Act §43(a)
RequirementsDistinctiveness, non-functionality, likelihood of confusion

Legal basis

Trade dress protection arises under Section 43(a) of the Lanham Act, which prohibits uses of symbols or devices likely to confuse consumers about the origin of goods. Trade dress is divided into two categories: product packaging, which may be protected if inherently distinctive or if it has acquired secondary meaning, and product design, which the Supreme Court held in Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Samara Bros. (2000) can be protected only upon proof of secondary meaning.

Key cases

  • Two Pesos, Inc. v. Taco Cabana, Inc. (1992) — restaurant décor could be inherently distinctive trade dress protectable without secondary meaning.
  • Qualitex Co. v. Jacobson Products Co. (1995) — colour alone (green-gold dry-cleaning pads) can function as a mark.
  • TrafFix Devices, Inc. v. Marketing Displays, Inc. (2001) — features claimed in a utility patent are strong evidence of functionality and thus unprotectable.

Functionality doctrine

Features that are essential to the use or purpose of a product, or that affect its cost or quality, are functional and cannot be monopolised as trade dress; they belong, if anywhere, to patent law. The burden of proving non-functionality rests on the party claiming protection for unregistered trade dress. Registration on the Principal Register is possible for sufficiently distinctive trade dress.
