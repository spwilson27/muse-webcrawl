Vaquero

The vaquero (Spanish: [baˈkeɾo], "cowboy", from vaca, "cow") is a horse-mounted livestock herder of a tradition that originated in Spain and was transplanted to the Americas, particularly Mexico and the southwestern United States. Vaqueros were the original cattle workers of the ranchos of Spanish and Mexican California, Texas, and the Great Basin.

History and influence

Spanish colonists brought cattle, horses, and roping techniques to New Spain in the 16th century. Indigenous and mestizo workers developed the vaquero tradition, refining horsemanship, the use of the braided rawhide reata (lariat), and centre-fire rigged saddles. After the United States annexed the Southwest, Anglo settlers adopted vaquero techniques, dress, and vocabulary — words such as "buckaroo" (an Anglicisation of vaquero), "rodeo", "chaps" (from chaparreras), and "lariat" entered American English.

The Californio and Great Basin "buckaroo" traditions emphasise slow, refined stock handling and highly trained horses, distinct from the Texas cowboy style. Vaquero horsemanship has experienced a modern revival among stock horse trainers.
