Alpha–beta pruning

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Alpha–beta pruning is a search algorithm used in minimax decision-making, most famously in two-player games such as chess, that reduces the number of nodes evaluated in a game tree. It keeps track of two bounds, alpha and beta, representing the minimum score the maximizing player is assured of and the maximum score the minimizing player is assured of. When a branch is found that cannot possibly improve on these bounds, the rest of that branch is skipped ("pruned"), which can dramatically cut computation time without changing the final result.

History

The idea was developed in the 1950s by researchers including John McCarthy, and was analyzed and refined by Donald Knuth and Ronald Moore in the 1970s. It became a standard component of game-playing programs, including early chess engines.

Improvement over minimax

With optimal move ordering, alpha–beta pruning can reduce the effective branching factor of a tree to roughly its square root, allowing a search roughly twice as deep in the same amount of time. In the worst case, where moves are ordered poorly, it degenerates to plain minimax.

Further optimizations

Techniques building on alpha–beta pruning include principal variation search, transposition tables, killer moves, history heuristics, iterative deepening, and aspiration windows.
