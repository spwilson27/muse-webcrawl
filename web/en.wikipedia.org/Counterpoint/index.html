Counterpoint

Counterpoint
FieldMusic theory
EtymologyLatin punctus contra punctum, "note against note"
Key textFux, Gradus ad Parnassum (1725)
Related formsFugue, canon, polyphony

In music, counterpoint is the relationship between two or more musical lines (or voices) which are harmonically interdependent yet independent in rhythm and melodic contour. It has been most commonly identified in the European classical tradition, strongly developing during the Renaissance and in much of the common practice period, especially in the Baroque. The term originates in the Latin punctus contra punctum, meaning "point against point".

Species counterpoint

The study of counterpoint is traditionally taught through the five "species" systematised by Johann Joseph Fux in Gradus ad Parnassum (1725), progressing from note-against-note writing to florid counterpoint. The rules govern consonance and dissonance, melodic shape, and the treatment of intervals between voices over a fixed cantus firmus.

Contrapuntal forms

Important contrapuntal devices include imitation, canon, and fugue, developed to a high point by J. S. Bach in works such as The Well-Tempered Clavier and The Art of Fugue. Twentieth-century composers extended counterpoint through techniques such as invertible counterpoint and serialism.
