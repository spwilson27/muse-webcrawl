Hell

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In religion and folklore, hell is a location or state in the afterlife in which evil souls are subjected to punitive suffering, most often through torture, as eternal punishment after death. Religions with a linear divine history often depict hells as eternal destinations, the biggest examples being Christianity and Islam, whereas religions with reincarnation usually depict a hell as an intermediary period between incarnations, as is the case in the dharmic religions. Religions typically locate hell in another dimension or under the Earth's surface.

Etymology and early concepts

The English word derives from Old English hel, a term for the underworld realm of the dead, cognate with the Norse goddess and realm Hel. Ancient religions generally conceived a shadowy underworld for all the dead — Mesopotamian Kur, Greek Hades, Hebrew Sheol — with judgement-based punishment of the wicked becoming more prominent in later traditions, notably in Zoroastrianism, whose eschatology (with its bridge of judgement and molten-metal ordeal) influenced Second Temple Judaism and later faiths.

In Abrahamic religions

In Judaism, Sheol is the abode of the dead; Gehinnom developed into a place of purification usually limited (in rabbinic tradition) to a maximum of twelve months. In Christianity, hell (translating Hades, Gehenna and Tartarus in the New Testament) is the fate of the unrepentant: eternal separation from God, traditionally depicted with fire. Catholic teaching describes it as definitive self-exclusion from communion with God; Orthodox, Protestant and other views vary, including annihilationism and, for a minority, eventual universal restoration. In Islam, Jahannam is a fiery abyss of seven levels beneath a narrow bridge (as-Sirat), described vividly in the Qur'an as punishment for unbelievers and sinners; most Sunni theology holds that Muslim sinners may eventually be released, while unbelief is punished eternally.

In Asian religions

Dharmic religions place multiple hells (Naraka) within cycles of rebirth: Hindu, Buddhist and Jain texts describe numerous hot and cold hells with elaborate punishments fitted to deeds (karma), served for vast but finite periods until the karma is exhausted and rebirth follows. Chinese folk religion merged Buddhist Naraka (Diyu) with native underworld bureaucracy, presided over by Yanluo Wang and the Ten Kings, with punishments and reincarnation administered like a celestial civil service.

Literature and culture

Hell's most influential literary portrayal is Dante Alighieri's Inferno (early 14th century), the first part of the Divine Comedy, which structures hell as nine concentric circles of contrapasso punishments; John Milton's Paradise Lost and Hieronymus Bosch's paintings further shaped Western imagery. Modern theologians and writers have reinterpreted hell metaphorically — as absence of God, as self-chosen separation, or as a symbol of injustice — while the concept continues to figure prominently in preaching, philosophy of religion and debates about eternal punishment.
