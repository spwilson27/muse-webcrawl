Runge's theorem

In complex analysis, Runge's theorem (also known as the Runge approximation theorem) states, roughly, that every holomorphic function on an open set can be approximated uniformly on compact subsets by rational functions whose only poles lie in prescribed sets — one point chosen from each connected component of the complement of the compact set. It was proved by Carl Runge in 1885.

Statement

Let K be a compact subset of the complex plane, and let A be a set containing at least one point from each bounded connected component of the complement of K. If f is holomorphic on an open set containing K, then there exists a sequence of rational functions whose only poles lie in A and which converges to f uniformly on K.

Corollaries

If the complement of K is connected, the poles can all be pushed to infinity, so f can be approximated by polynomials — a result that generalises Taylor series expansions beyond discs. If K has empty interior and connected complement, still stronger statements hold. A related result, the Mergelyan theorem, extends the approximation to functions that are merely continuous on K and holomorphic on its interior.

Significance

Runge's theorem is a cornerstone of one-dimensional complex geometry: it underlies the solution of additive and multiplicative Cousin problems, the theory of Stein manifolds in one variable, and constructions of holomorphic functions with prescribed behaviour.
