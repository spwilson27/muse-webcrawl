The St. Lawrence Seaway (French: Voie maritime du Saint-Laurent) is a system of canals, locks, and channels in Canada and the United States that permits ocean-going vessels to travel from the Atlantic Ocean to the North American Great Lakes. The seaway runs 370 kilometres (230 mi) from Montreal to Lake Erie, bypassing the rapids of the St. Lawrence River and the difference in elevation at Niagara Falls via the Welland Canal. Legally, the system extends from Montreal to Lake Erie, including the Welland Canal.

Construction

The Seaway was a joint project of Canada and the United States, built between 1954 and 1959 at a cost of about C$470 million, alongside the Moses-Saunders Power Dam, which generates hydroelectricity for Ontario and New York. Queen Elizabeth II and President Dwight D. Eisenhower formally opened the Seaway on 26 June 1959 aboard the royal yacht Britannia. Construction flooded a series of villages in Ontario — the "Lost Villages" — whose residents were relocated before inundation.

Locks and navigation

The system contains 15 locks: seven in the Montreal–Lake Ontario section and eight on the Welland Canal. Locks can accommodate "Seawaymax" vessels up to 225.6 m long with a beam of 23.8 m and a draught of 8.1 m. The navigation season generally runs from late March to late December, weather permitting, and the Seaway moves roughly 40 million tonnes of cargo annually, chiefly iron ore, coal, grain, and steel. Ships transiting between the Atlantic and Lake Superior climb approximately 180 m (600 ft) in total.

Impact

The Seaway opened the industrial heartland of North America to ocean shipping, boosting ports such as Toronto, Hamilton, Cleveland, Chicago, and Duluth, but it also bypassed older canal towns and allowed invasive species, including zebra mussels carried in ballast water, to enter the Great Lakes. Some cargo that once used the Seaway has shifted to rail and larger ocean vessels that exceed Seawaymax dimensions.
