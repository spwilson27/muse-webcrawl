Self-deception

Self-deception is the process of misleading oneself — denying or rationalising away evidence and arguments that contradict a belief one is motivated to hold. The self-deceived person acquires or retains a belief that is poorly supported, or contrary to the evidence available to them, because desire, fear or some other motivation biases their handling of that evidence.

The paradoxes

Philosophers have long noted two puzzles. The static paradox: deceiving oneself seems to require simultaneously believing a proposition and disbelieving it. The dynamic paradox: intending to deceive oneself appears self-defeating, since the intention would reveal the trick. Responses divide theorists. Intentionalists such as Donald Davidson partition the mind into semi-independent parts between which deception can occur; deflationists, notably Alfred Mele, argue that no intention to deceive is required — motivationally biased treatment of evidence (selective attention, selective evidence gathering, biased interpretation) suffices to explain the phenomenon.

Psychology and evolution

Related psychological phenomena include wishful thinking, denial, rationalisation and cognitive dissonance reduction. Robert Trivers proposed an evolutionary account: self-deception evolved in the service of deceiving others, because the self-deceived display fewer of the involuntary cues of lying. Self-deception may also support confidence and mental health — mildly positive illusions correlate with well-being — while imposing serious costs when it blocks corrective feedback, as in addiction and failing enterprises.
