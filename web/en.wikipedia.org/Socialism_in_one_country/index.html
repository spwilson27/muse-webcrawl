Socialism in one country was a doctrine advanced by Joseph Stalin in 1924 holding that, despite the failure of socialist revolutions to spread across Europe after 1917, the Soviet Union could build a complete socialist society within its own borders on the basis of its internal resources and the alliance of the working class and peasantry.

Background

Classical Marxism expected socialist revolution to be international; the Bolsheviks had assumed that the survival of the Russian Revolution depended on revolutions in advanced industrial countries. When revolutions in Germany and elsewhere failed, and Lenin died in 1924, the question of the Soviet Union's path became central to the succession struggle.

The dispute with Trotsky

Stalin argued that socialism could be built in the USSR alone, though its final victory against capitalist encirclement required revolution elsewhere. Leon Trotsky's theory of permanent revolution held that an isolated socialist state in a backward country would inevitably degenerate or be overthrown without international revolution. The doctrine was adopted by the party in 1925, Trotsky was defeated politically, exiled in 1929, and the policy underpinned Stalin's turn toward rapid industrialization and collectivization in the Five-Year Plans.

Effects and legacy

The doctrine justified prioritizing Soviet state interests in foreign policy, reshaped the Communist International into an instrument of Soviet diplomacy, and framed Soviet ideological orthodoxy for decades. Critics, including Trotskyists, regarded it as a nationalist departure from Marxist internationalism; defenders saw it as a pragmatic adaptation to reality.
