Water purification

Water purification is the process of removing undesirable chemicals, biological contaminants, suspended solids, and gases from water. The goal is to produce water fit for a specific purpose — most commonly safe drinking water. The methods used include physical processes such as filtration and sedimentation, biological processes such as slow sand filters, and chemical processes such as flocculation, chlorination, and the use of electromagnetic radiation such as ultraviolet light.

History

Attempts to purify water date back to ancient Egypt and India; Sanskrit and Egyptian texts recommended filtering water through sand and charcoal and exposing it to sunlight. Modern municipal purification began in the 19th century with slow sand filtration, which dramatically reduced cholera and typhoid, and chlorination, first used continuously in the early 20th century.

Processes

A typical plant screens raw water, adds coagulants to form flocs that trap impurities, settles and filters the water, and disinfects it before distribution. Advanced treatment may add ozonation, activated carbon adsorption, membrane filtration (micro-, ultra-, and nanofiltration), and reverse osmosis for desalination or ultrapure water.

Importance

Safe purification is one of the most effective public-health interventions: access to treated water cuts diarrhoeal disease sharply. Where supplies are scarce or contaminated, purification also enables reuse of wastewater and brackish sources, relieving pressure on freshwater resources.
