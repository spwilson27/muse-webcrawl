Parent company

A parent company (or parent corporation) is a company that has a controlling interest in another company, the subsidiary. Control is usually exercised by holding more than half of the subsidiary's voting stock directly or indirectly, though effective control can exist with smaller stakes. A parent that owns subsidiaries but conducts little or no business of its own is commonly called a holding company.

Control and consolidation

Under accounting standards such as IFRS 10 and US GAAP, a parent consolidates the financial statements of its subsidiaries, presenting the group as a single economic entity. Subsidiaries nonetheless remain separate legal persons: they hold their own assets, incur their own liabilities, and can sue and be sued in their own names. The parent's liability for a subsidiary's debts is normally limited to its investment, although courts may in exceptional circumstances "pierce the corporate veil", and parents sometimes guarantee subsidiary obligations or face statutory responsibilities, for example in environmental or tax law.

Reasons for group structures

Companies organise as parent–subsidiary groups for many reasons: acquisitions are effected by buying a target's shares, leaving it intact as a subsidiary; groups isolate risky activities in separate entities; separate subsidiaries may be needed for regulated industries, foreign operations, joint ventures, or tax planning. Multinational groups often interpose intermediate parents in various jurisdictions, and an ultimate parent at the top consolidates the whole group.

Related concepts

A sister company is another subsidiary of the same parent. An affiliate or associate is a company over which the investor has significant influence but not control, typically a 20–50% holding. Wholly owned subsidiaries are 100% held by the parent, in contrast to partly owned subsidiaries, whose other shareholders hold a non-controlling (minority) interest.
