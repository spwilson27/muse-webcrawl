Legal evidence

The law of evidence, also known as the rules of evidence, encompasses the rules and legal principles that govern the proof of facts in a legal proceeding. These rules determine what evidence must or must not be considered by the trier of fact in reaching its decision.

Types of evidence

Evidence may be testimonial (witness statements), documentary (documents and records), real (physical objects), or demonstrative (charts and models). It is also classified as direct evidence, which directly proves a fact, and circumstantial evidence, from which a fact may be inferred.

Admissibility

Evidence must be relevant and material to be admissible, and must satisfy rules concerning authenticity, hearsay, privilege, and character. Famous exclusionary doctrines include the hearsay rule and its many exceptions, the exclusion of illegally obtained evidence in criminal cases, and rules against unfairly prejudicial material. In the United States, the Federal Rules of Evidence (enacted 1975) govern proceedings in federal courts, and most states have adopted similar codes. Expert testimony is governed by standards such as the Daubert standard.

Burdens of proof

Evidence law interacts with burdens of proof: "beyond a reasonable doubt" in criminal cases, and "preponderance of the evidence" or "clear and convincing evidence" in civil matters.
