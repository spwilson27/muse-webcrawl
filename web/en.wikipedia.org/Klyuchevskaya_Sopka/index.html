Klyuchevskaya Sopka (Russian: Ключевская Сопка) is the highest mountain on the Kamchatka Peninsula in the Russian Far East and the highest active volcano in Eurasia. Its steep, symmetrical cone rises to about 4,750 metres (15,584 ft) above sea level, though its exact height varies with eruptions. It forms part of the Klyuchevskaya volcanic group, near the twin cones of Kamen and Bezymianny, and lies within the Volcanoes of Kamchatka UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Geology and eruptions

Klyuchevskaya Sopka is a young stratovolcano, formed roughly 6,000–7,000 years ago, built of alternating layers of lava flows and pyroclastic deposits of basaltic to basaltic-andesite composition. It is one of the most active volcanoes in the world, having erupted more than a hundred times since the first recorded eruption in 1697, typically every few years. Eruptions range from Strombolian activity at the summit crater to effusive flank eruptions from parasitic vents on its slopes, frequently producing lava flows, ash plumes that disrupt North Pacific aviation routes, and lahars. Major modern eruptions occurred in 1994, 2005, 2009–2010, 2013, 2016, 2020–2021, and 2023, when lava flows reached the Bogdanovich Glacier and a large ash column followed an M6.5 earthquake nearby.

Climbing

The first ascent was made in 1788 by Daniel Gauss and companions during the Billings expedition. Although frequently climbed, the volcano is dangerous: climbers have been killed by falling volcanic bombs even during quiescent periods, and eruptions can begin without warning.
