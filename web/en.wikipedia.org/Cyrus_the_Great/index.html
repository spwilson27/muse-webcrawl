Cyrus II of Persia (c. 600 – 530 BCE), commonly known as Cyrus the Great, was the founder of the Achaemenid Empire, the first and largest Persian empire. Under his rule the empire embraced the territories of the previous Near Eastern powers — Media, Lydia and the Neo-Babylonian Empire — stretching at his death from the Balkans and Egypt in the west to the Indus Valley in the east, the largest empire the world had yet seen.

Rise and conquests

Cyrus succeeded his father Cambyses I as king of Anshan, then a vassal of Media. In 550 BCE he defeated his grandfather, the Median king Astyages, and united Persians and Medes. He then conquered Croesus of Lydia (546 BCE), bringing the Greek cities of Ionia under Persian rule, and in 539 BCE took Babylon from Nabonidus, presenting himself as the chosen restorer of the god Marduk in the Cyrus Cylinder.

Policies

Cyrus was noted for respecting the customs and religions of conquered peoples. He permitted exiled peoples, including the Jews deported to Babylon, to return to their homelands and rebuild their temples — a policy for which the Hebrew Bible praises him as an anointed of God. The Cyrus Cylinder, a royal inscription from Babylon, has been popularly described as an early charter of human rights, though historians debate that characterisation.

Death and legacy

Cyrus died in battle in 530 BCE campaigning against the Massagetae on the empire's north-eastern frontier, according to Herodotus, and was buried in a limestone tomb at Pasargadae. He was succeeded by his son Cambyses II. Cyrus's combination of military genius and tolerant administration made him a model ruler for later ages, celebrated by Xenophon in the Cyropaedia.
