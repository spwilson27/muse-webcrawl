The Charge of the Light Brigade was a failed military action involving the British light cavalry led by Lord Cardigan against Russian forces during the Battle of Balaclava on 25 October 1854, in the Crimean War. Lord Raglan, overall commander of the British forces, had intended to send the Light Brigade to prevent the Russians from removing captured guns from a redoubt on the Causeway Heights. However, due to miscommunication in the chain of command, the Light Brigade was instead sent on a frontal assault against a different artillery battery — one well prepared with excellent fields of defensive fire — at the far end of the North Valley.

The brigade reached the battery under withering fire from three sides and scattered some of the gunners, but was forced to retreat immediately, suffering heavy casualties and accomplishing no decisive military objective. The events were recorded in Alfred, Lord Tennyson's poem "The Charge of the Light Brigade" (1854), which honored the bravery of the men while decrying the blunder that sent them there: "Theirs not to make reply, / Theirs not to reason why, / Theirs but to do and die."

Background

The Light Brigade, commanded by Major-General the Earl of Cardigan, comprised the 4th and 13th Light Dragoons, the 17th Lancers, and the 8th and 11th Hussars. The Heavy Brigade under Major-General Scarlett had earlier that day driven back a Russian cavalry force. Raglan's order, delivered by Captain Nolan, referred to guns the Russians were carrying away from captured redoubts; from the valley floor, Cardigan and his superior, Lord Lucan, could see only the Russian battery at the valley's end, and the order was interpreted against it.

The charge

About 670 troopers rode roughly a mile and a quarter down the valley, exposed to artillery and rifle fire from the heights on both flanks and from the battery ahead — Tennyson's "valley of Death." The brigade broke through to the guns, engaged the Russian cavalry beyond them, and then had to withdraw back along the same ground. Of those who rode, about 110 were killed and some 160 wounded; a high proportion of the horses were lost.

Legacy

The charge became a celebrated example of military incompetence paired with conspicuous bravery. Contemporary observer French Marshal Bosquet remarked, "C'est magnifique, mais ce n'est pas la guerre" ("It is magnificent, but it is not war"). The centenary in 1954 and the 140th anniversary, marked by a commemorative ride in 1994, kept the event in public memory. The last survivor, Private Edwin Hughes of the 13th Light Dragoons, died in 1927.
