The modern Japanese writing system uses a combination of logographic characters borrowed from Chinese — kanji — and two syllabaries, hiragana and katakana, collectively called kana. Japanese texts can combine all three scripts in a single sentence: kanji for word roots, hiragana for grammatical endings and native words, and katakana for foreign loanwords, onomatopoeia, and emphasis. Written Japanese is often considered one of the most complex writing systems in use today.

Kanji

Chinese characters reached Japan by about the 5th century AD via Korea. Kanji usually carry two kinds of reading: on'yomi, derived from historical Chinese pronunciations, and kun'yomi, native Japanese readings. Schoolchildren learn the 1,026 kyōiku kanji in primary school, and official use is guided by the government's jōyō kanji list of 2,136 characters, though literate adults recognise several thousand.

Kana

Hiragana and katakana each comprise 46 basic characters representing syllables (more precisely, morae). Hiragana developed from cursive abbreviations of kanji and was historically associated with women's writing — Murasaki Shikibu wrote The Tale of Genji in it. Katakana's angular forms derive from fragments of kanji used by Buddhist monks for annotations. Diacritics (dakuten) mark voicing, and small ゃ, ゅ, ょ form palatalised sounds.

Usage rules

In standard modern orthography, kanji write nouns and the stems of verbs and adjectives; hiragana writes inflections (okurigana), particles, and words without kanji; katakana writes foreign borrowings (gairaigo), foreign names, scientific terms, and emphasis, functioning somewhat like italics. Small hiragana glosses called furigana may be printed above kanji to aid reading.

Romanisation and punctuation

The Latin alphabet (rōmaji) appears in signage, branding, passports, and for computer input, with the Hepburn system the most common romanisation. Japanese punctuation includes the 、and 。marks, and text may run horizontally (left-to-right) in most modern contexts or vertically in literature and newspapers. Post-war reforms (1946) simplified character forms (shinjitai) and regularised kana usage to reflect modern pronunciation.
