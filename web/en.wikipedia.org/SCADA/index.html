SCADA

Stands forSupervisory control and data acquisition
TypeIndustrial control system architecture
Field devicesPLCs, RTUs, sensors, actuators
Typical usesPower grids, water treatment, pipelines, manufacturing

SCADA (supervisory control and data acquisition) is a control system architecture comprising computers, networked data communications and graphical user interfaces for high-level supervision of machines and processes. It also covers sensors and other devices, such as programmable logic controllers (PLCs) and discrete proportional-integral-derivative (PID) controllers that interface with process plant or machinery. SCADA systems are used in power generation and distribution, water and wastewater treatment, oil and gas pipelines, railways, building management and manufacturing.

Architecture

A typical SCADA system combines: remote terminal units (RTUs) and PLCs at field sites that gather sensor data and drive actuators; a communications network (historically leased lines and radio, today often IP-based); one or more master stations or control centres running supervisory software; and human–machine interfaces (HMIs) through which operators monitor alarms, trends and schematics and issue control commands. Data historians log measurements for analysis and reporting. Common protocols include Modbus, DNP3, IEC 60870-5-104 and OPC UA.

Generations and security

SCADA has evolved through generations — from monolithic mainframe-based systems (1960s–70s), to distributed systems on local networks (1980s–90s), to networked and web/IoT-connected architectures. This increasing connectivity has exposed critical infrastructure to cyber threats: the Stuxnet worm (discovered 2010), which sabotaged Iranian centrifuges, is the best-known attack on SCADA-controlled equipment, and attacks on Ukrainian power grids in 2015 and 2016 demonstrated the risk to utilities. Security standards such as IEC 62443 and practices like network segmentation, unidirectional gateways and strict access control are now central to SCADA deployment.
