Principal–agent problem

The principal–agent problem (also known as agency dilemma or the agency problem) occurs when one person or entity (the "agent") is able to make decisions or take actions on behalf of, or that impact, another person or entity (the "principal"). This dilemma exists in circumstances where agents are motivated to act in their own best interests, which are contrary to those of their principals, and is an example of moral hazard.

Description

Common examples include shareholders (principals) and corporate executives (agents), voters (principals) and politicians (agents), and patients (principals) and physicians (agents). The problem arises because the agent typically has more information about their actions and intentions than the principal does (asymmetric information), and the principal cannot directly ensure the agent always acts in its interest.

Agency costs

Following Jensen and Meckling (1976), agency costs consist of monitoring expenditures by the principal, bonding expenditures by the agent, and the residual welfare loss from divergent decisions. Proposed remedies include incentive contracts linking pay to performance, monitoring and reporting requirements, bonding, and market disciplines such as the market for corporate control.

Applications

Agency theory has been applied to executive compensation, corporate governance, insurance (where it overlaps with moral hazard), bureaucracy and public administration, and international relations. Critics note that the theory assumes self-interested, opportunistic behaviour and may neglect trust, stewardship, and intrinsic motivation.
