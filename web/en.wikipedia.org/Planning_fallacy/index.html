Planning fallacy

The planning fallacy is a phenomenon in which predictions about how much time will be needed to complete a future task display an optimism bias, underestimating the time actually taken. It applies even when people know that similar tasks in the past have taken longer than planned.

History

The term was proposed by Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky in 1979. In classic experiments, students asked to estimate when they would finish academic projects gave estimates far shorter than their actual completion times, and shorter than the times they predicted when asked for realistic or worst-case scenarios.

Explanations and remedies

The fallacy is attributed to taking an "inside view" focused on the plan itself rather than an "outside view" based on the distributional record of similar projects. Reference class forecasting — basing estimates on the outcomes of a reference class of comparable projects — is a widely recommended corrective, used in planning major infrastructure.
