Timber framing

Timber framing (German: Fachwerkbauweise) and post-and-beam construction are traditional methods of building with heavy timbers, creating structures using squared-off and carefully fitted and joined timbers held together with joints secured by large wooden pegs. If the structural frame of load-bearing timber is left exposed on the exterior of the building, it may be referred to as half-timbered, and in many cases the infill between timbers will be used for decorative effect. The country most known for this kind of architecture is Germany, where timber-framed houses are spread all over the country.

History

Timber framing has been used for thousands of years. Neolithic long houses, Roman carpentry described by Vitruvius, and medieval building across northern Europe all employed heavy timber frames. Timber-framed buildings survive from as early as the 12th century in England and Germany, and the technique is also deeply embedded in Japanese temple and castle architecture, where great timber frames have withstood earthquakes for centuries.

In Britain and continental Europe, the frame was traditionally infilled with wattle and daub, brick nogging, or plaster. In Scandinavia, stave churches represent a related monumental tradition. Settlers carried timber framing to North America, where it was the dominant heavy-building method until the spread of lighter balloon framing in the 19th century.

Joinery

Timber frames are made by joinery: mortise and tenon joints, lap joints, and scarf joints for splicing long members, all pinned with hardwood pegs (treenails) rather than metal fasteners. Frames are typically laid out and assembled as "bents" on the ground, then raised into place, historically by communal barn-raising.

Modern revival

Since the 1970s a revival of timber framing has taken place in North America and elsewhere, using both traditional hand joinery and modern power tools, CNC cutting, and structural insulated panels (SIPs) as enclosure. Timber framers' guilds teach the craft, and the method is prized for durability, exposed craftsmanship, and use of renewable material.
