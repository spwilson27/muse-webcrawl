X-ray crystallography

X-ray crystallography is an experimental science determining the atomic and molecular structure of a crystal, in which the crystalline structure causes a beam of incident X-rays to diffract in specific directions.

X-ray crystallography

Field: structural biology, chemistry, materials science

Principle

By measuring angles and intensities of diffracted beams, crystallographers produce a three-dimensional picture of electron density. Bragg's law, nλ = 2d sin θ, relates wavelength, lattice spacing and diffraction angle. Fourier transforms convert diffraction patterns to electron-density maps, though the phase problem must be solved by methods such as molecular replacement, isomorphous replacement or anomalous dispersion.

History

Max von Laue discovered X-ray diffraction by crystals in 1912; William Henry Bragg and William Lawrence Bragg formalised its analysis. The method revealed structures of salts, minerals, DNA (Photo 51), myoglobin and haemoglobin.

Applications

Structures are deposited in the Protein Data Bank. Complementary methods include protein NMR and cryo-electron microscopy. Many results won the Nobel Prize in Physics and Chemistry prizes.
