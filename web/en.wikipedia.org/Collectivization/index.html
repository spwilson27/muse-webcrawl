Collectivization

Collectivization is the process of combining individual farms, land and means of production into collectively or state-controlled units, typically carried out by communist governments in the twentieth century. The best-known example is the Soviet Union under Joseph Stalin, but analogous campaigns took place in China, Eastern Europe and elsewhere.

Soviet collectivization

Launched with the first Five-Year Plan in 1928–29, Soviet collectivization forced peasants into collective farms (kolkhozy) and state farms (sovkhozy). The campaign was accompanied by "dekulakization" — the deportation, imprisonment or execution of wealthier peasants labelled kulaks — and by compulsory grain procurements. Resistance included slaughtering livestock on a massive scale. The disruption contributed to catastrophic famines in 1932–33, including the Holodomor in Ukraine and famine in Kazakhstan, in which millions died. By the late 1930s nearly all Soviet farmland had been collectivised.

Elsewhere

In China, land reform in the early 1950s was followed by mutual-aid teams, cooperatives and, during the Great Leap Forward (1958), vast people's communes; the resulting famine was among the deadliest in history. After 1978 the household responsibility system effectively decollectivised Chinese agriculture. Eastern European states collectivised after 1945 with varying completeness; Poland and Yugoslavia largely retained private farming. Most post-communist states decollectivised or privatised collective farms after 1989.
