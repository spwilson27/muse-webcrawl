Fellwalking

Fellwalking is a form of hillwalking in the fells — the hills and mountains of northern England, particularly the Lake District, Pennines, and Yorkshire Dales. The word "fell" derives from the Old Norse fjall, meaning mountain, a legacy of Norse settlement in the region.

Background

Fellwalking became popular as a recreational pursuit in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, as improved rail access brought industrial workers from Lancashire and Yorkshire towns into the countryside. The activity is closely associated with Alfred Wainwright, whose hand-drawn Pictorial Guides to the Lakeland Fells (1955–66) described 214 fells — now known as "Wainwrights" — and remain beloved by walkers.

Activities

Fellwalkers commonly aim to climb lists of summits such as the Wainwrights, the Birketts, or the Marilyns. Routes range from gentle valley walks to exposed ridge scrambles such as Striding Edge on Helvellyn and Sharp Edge on Blencathra. Long-distance challenges include the Bob Graham Round, a 66-mile circuit of 42 Lakeland peaks to be completed within 24 hours.

Access

Much fellwalking takes place on open access land under the Countryside and Rights of Way Act 2000 ("right to roam"), supplemented by an extensive network of public footpaths and bridleways. The mass trespass of Kinder Scout in 1932 was a landmark in the campaign for access to upland areas.
