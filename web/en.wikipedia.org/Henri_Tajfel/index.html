Henri Tajfel (born Hersz Mordche Tajfel; 22 June 1919 – 3 May 1982) was a Polish social psychologist, best known for his pioneering work on the cognitive aspects of prejudice and for social identity theory, which he developed together with John Turner and others at the University of Bristol. His minimal group experiments showed that merely categorising people into arbitrary groups is sufficient to produce in-group favouritism and discrimination against an out-group.

Life

Tajfel was born in Włocławek, Poland, into a Jewish family. Unable to study in Poland because of anti-Jewish quotas, he went to France to study chemistry at the Sorbonne. When Germany invaded, he served in the French army and spent the rest of the war as a prisoner of war in Germany, surviving under a concealed identity; on returning to Poland he learned that none of his immediate family had survived the Holocaust. He worked with displaced persons and refugees for relief organisations, emigrated to Britain in 1946, and naturalised as a British citizen.

Tajfel studied psychology at Birkbeck College and Durham University, where he received his PhD in 1954. He taught at Durham and then at the University of Oxford, and in 1967 became professor of social psychology at the University of Bristol, where he built one of Europe's strongest social psychology departments and founded the European Journal of Social Psychology.

Research

His early work on perception and judgement produced accentuation theory: categorising stimuli exaggerates differences between categories and similarities within them. In the minimal group paradigm (1970–71), schoolboys assigned to groups on trivial or random grounds nevertheless awarded more points (convertible to money) to anonymous members of their own group. With John Turner, Tajfel formulated social identity theory (1979), proposing that part of a person's self-concept derives from membership in social groups, and that people strive for a positive social identity through favourable comparison of their in-group with out-groups. The theory became foundational to research on intergroup relations, organisations, and identity.
