Sequoyah

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sequoyah (c. 1770–1843), also known as George Gist, was a Cherokee silversmith, blacksmith, and polymath who in 1821 completed the Cherokee syllabary, a writing system of 85 characters, each representing a syllable of the Cherokee language. His achievement made the Cherokee the first Native American people to develop a written language created by a single individual and led to mass Cherokee literacy within a few years.

The syllabary

Sequoyah, who spoke only Cherokee, became convinced of the power of the settlers' "talking leaves" (writing). After experiments with logographic symbols, he devised a syllabary, teaching it first to his daughter Ayokeh. Initially ridiculed and even accused of witchcraft, he proved the system in public tests before the Cherokee leadership, who adopted it in 1825. Within months, thousands of Cherokee could read and write; in 1828 the bilingual newspaper Cherokee Phoenix began publication using syllabary type.

Later life

Sequoyah served as a diplomat for the Western Cherokee, signing treaties in Washington, and later sought to reunite Cherokee factions and to find a Cherokee group said to have migrated to Mexico, where he died in 1843. The giant sequoia trees and Sequoia National Park are generally believed to be named in his honour.
