Freedman

Freedpeople in the post-Civil War American South

A freedman or freedwoman is a formerly enslaved person who has been released from slavery, usually by legal means. Historically, enslaved people were freed by manumission (a grant by their owner), by emancipation decrees, by self-purchase, or by military action. The term is used across many slaveholding societies, from ancient Rome to the Americas.

Ancient Rome

In Roman society, freedmen (liberti) formed a substantial class. Manumitted slaves typically became clients of their former owners (patroni) and owed them services, but their freeborn children could become full citizens; some freedmen, such as the emperor Claudius's secretaries Narcissus and Pallas, amassed great wealth and influence.

United States

In the United States, "freedmen" most often refers to the roughly four million African Americans emancipated during and after the Civil War, culminating in the Thirteenth Amendment (1865). During Reconstruction, the Freedmen's Bureau (1865–1872) provided food, schooling, legal aid, and land assistance, while freedpeople built schools, churches, and political organizations; Black men voted and held office across the South before Jim Crow disenfranchisement. The status of the "Freedmen" of the Five Civilized Tribes — people of African descent held in slavery by Native nations and freed under the treaties of 1866 — has remained a live question of tribal citizenship into the twenty-first century.

Other contexts

Freedpeople appear in many settings: the alforriados of Brazil, freed by gradual laws culminating in the Golden Law of 1888; the maroon communities of the Caribbean; and the freed slaves who founded Liberia and Freetown, Sierra Leone.
