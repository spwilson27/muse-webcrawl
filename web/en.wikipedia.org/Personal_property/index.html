Personal property

Personal property is property that is movable, in contrast to real property (land and buildings), which is immovable. In common law systems, personal property is roughly equivalent to the civil-law concept of movables, and is sometimes called personalty or, for tangible items, chattels.

Classification

Personal property is divided into two main categories:

  • Chattels real — leasehold interests in land, treated as personal property despite concerning realty.
  • Chattels personal — all other personal property, subdivided into choses in possession (tangible things that can be physically held, such as furniture, vehicles and jewellery) and choses in action (intangible rights enforceable by legal action, such as debts, shares, patents and copyrights).

Legal significance

The distinction matters for taxation (for example, personal property taxes on business equipment in some jurisdictions), for secured transactions (in the United States, Article 9 of the Uniform Commercial Code governs security interests in personal property), for inheritance, and for the rules governing transfer: personal property generally passes by delivery or by a bill of sale, whereas real property requires a deed and registration.

Property that begins as personal property can become part of real property when permanently affixed to land, becoming a fixture; conversely, severing part of land (such as harvested crops or cut timber) converts it into personal property.

Historical development

The common law originally regarded land as the principal form of wealth, and remedies for interference with chattels were comparatively limited; actions such as trover and detinue developed to protect ownership and possession of goods. As commerce grew, intangible personal property — shares, bonds and intellectual property — came to dominate in value.
