Network hub

A network hub is a basic networking device that connects multiple Ethernet devices together, making them act as a single network segment. A hub operates at the physical layer (Layer 1) of the OSI model: it simply receives electrical signals on one port and rebroadcasts them, amplified or regenerated, to all other ports, without examining addresses or filtering traffic.

Operation

Because a hub forwards every frame to every port, all devices attached to a hub (or to interconnected hubs) share the same collision domain and the same bandwidth. Devices must use half-duplex communication with CSMA/CD to arbitrate access: only one device can transmit successfully at a time, and simultaneous transmissions produce collisions that all devices must detect and retry from. Hubs were typically built for 10BASE-T (10 Mbit/s) and 100BASE-TX (Fast Ethernet, 100 Mbit/s) networks; dual-speed hubs contained an internal two-port switch between their 10 and 100 Mbit/s segments.

Replacement by switches

Hubs have been almost entirely superseded by network switches, which operate at Layer 2, learn the MAC addresses on each port, and forward frames only to the intended recipient, giving each port its own collision domain and allowing full-duplex links. As switch prices fell to hub levels in the early 2000s, hubs became obsolete for new installations; they also pose a security disadvantage, since every station receives all traffic, making sniffing trivial.
