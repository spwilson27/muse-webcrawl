Tensile testing

Tensile testing, also known as tension testing, is a fundamental materials science and engineering test in which a sample is subjected to a controlled tension until failure. The results — including ultimate tensile strength, yield strength, elongation, and reduction of area — are used in engineering and materials selection, quality control, and predicting how a material will behave under other forms of loading.

Process

A specimen of standardised dimensions, often with a reduced parallel section and enlarged ends ("dog-bone" or dumbbell shape for flat specimens), is mounted in a tensile testing machine (universal testing machine) and pulled at a constant rate of extension. Load cells record the applied force, and extensometers measure the change in gauge length. The measured force and elongation are converted into engineering stress and strain, plotted as a stress–strain curve.

Properties measured

  • Young's modulus — the slope of the initial, elastic portion of the curve.
  • Yield strength — the stress at which permanent (plastic) deformation begins, often taken as the 0.2% proof stress.
  • Ultimate tensile strength (UTS) — the maximum stress on the engineering stress–strain curve.
  • Elongation at break and reduction of area — measures of ductility.
  • Toughness — approximated by the area under the curve.

After the UTS, ductile specimens form a localised neck, in which deformation concentrates until fracture. True stress and true strain, calculated from instantaneous cross-sectional area and length, correct for this effect.

Standards

Tensile testing of metallic materials is governed by standards such as ASTM E8/E8M and ISO 6892-1; plastics are tested under ASTM D638 and ISO 527, and elastomers under ASTM D412. Standards specify specimen geometry, gripping, test speed, and reporting requirements so that results are comparable between laboratories.
