Grand Staircase

The Grand Staircase is a vast sequence of sedimentary rock layers and cliff escarpments in the Colorado Plateau region of southern Utah and northern Arizona in the United States. It rises in a series of giant steps from the floor of the Grand Canyon northward to the high plateaus around Bryce Canyon, exposing rock layers that span hundreds of millions of years of geologic history.

Geology

The staircase consists of five great cliffs, named — from south to north, and oldest to youngest — the Chocolate, Vermilion, White, Gray and Pink Cliffs. Each cliff is formed by a particularly erosion-resistant rock formation: the Chocolate Cliffs by the Moenkopi and Chinle formations, the Vermilion Cliffs by the Wingate and Kayenta formations, the White Cliffs by the Navajo Sandstone, the Gray Cliffs by the Dakota and Tropic formations, and the Pink Cliffs by the Claron Formation, whose colourful hoodoos are displayed at Bryce Canyon National Park.

Exploration

The concept was described by geologist Clarence Dutton in the 1870s, who called the succession a "grand staircase" of cliffs ascending from the Grand Canyon to the High Plateaus of Utah. The region includes Zion National Park, Bryce Canyon National Park and large parts of the Grand Staircase–Escalante National Monument, proclaimed in 1996.

Significance

The exposed strata record environments from ancient seas and coastal plains to great sand deserts, and preserve rich fossil assemblages, including dinosaurs of the Kaiparowits Formation. The remoteness of the region — parts of it were among the last areas in the contiguous United States to be mapped — gives it exceptional wilderness value.
