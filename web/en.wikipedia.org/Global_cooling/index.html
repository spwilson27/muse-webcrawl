Global cooling

Global cooling was a conjecture, popular in some scientific discussion and much amplified in the popular press during the 1970s, that the Earth would experience a sustained cooling trend, potentially leading toward renewed glaciation. The conjecture rested on the observed slight decline in global average temperatures from the 1940s to the early 1970s and on the theory of ice ages driven by orbital (Milankovitch) cycles, together with the cooling effect of aerosol pollution.

Historical context

A survey of the peer-reviewed literature of 1965–1979 found that papers projecting warming from greenhouse gases already outnumbered those projecting cooling; nevertheless, media coverage in the mid-1970s — including prominent magazine articles about a coming ice age — created a lasting public impression of a scientific consensus on cooling that did not in fact exist. Some scientists did seriously discuss the risk that aerosol emissions could cool the climate, and a 1975 National Academy of Sciences report emphasised large uncertainties in climate understanding.

Explanation of the mid-century cooling

The slight cooling of roughly 1940–1970 is now attributed mainly to anthropogenic sulfate aerosols, which reflect sunlight, combined with natural variability — powerful enough to temporarily mask greenhouse warming. As air pollution controls reduced aerosols while greenhouse gas concentrations rose, rapid warming followed from the 1970s onward. Modern usage of "global cooling" sometimes refers to this aerosol masking effect, or to proposed solar geoengineering methods (stratospheric aerosol injection) intended to offset warming.
