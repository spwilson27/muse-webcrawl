Curing (food preservation)

Curing is any of various food preservation and flavouring processes of foods such as meat, fish and vegetables, by the addition of salt, with the aim of drawing moisture out of the food by the process of osmosis. Because curing increases the solute concentration in the food and hence decreases its water potential, the food becomes inhospitable for the bacteria that cause food spoilage.

Methods

In dry curing, meat is rubbed or packed with salt (often mixed with sugar, spices and curing salts) and left for days to weeks, then frequently air-dried or smoked, as in prosciutto and many salamis. In wet curing or brining, food is submerged in a salt solution. Modern meat curing usually adds nitrite or nitrate salts, which inhibit Clostridium botulinum, fix the pink colour of cured meats through reactions with myoglobin, and contribute characteristic flavour. Curing is often combined with smoking and fermentation in products such as bacon, ham, pastrami, corned beef and gravlax.

History

Salt curing is ancient: Mesopotamian, Egyptian, Chinese and Roman sources all describe preserving meat and fish with salt. Saltpetre (potassium nitrate) was noticed by the early modern period to redden and protect meat, an effect traced to nitrite in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, leading to regulated curing salts such as Prague powder.

Health considerations

Nitrites can react to form nitrosamines, some of which are carcinogenic, and processed meats are classified by health agencies as carcinogenic to humans, so nitrite levels in cured meats are legally limited and alternatives (including celery-derived nitrite) are widely used. Curing nevertheless remains essential to the safety of many traditional products, above all in preventing botulism.
