Alpha compositing

In computer graphics, alpha compositing or alpha blending is the process of combining one image with a background to create the appearance of partial or full transparency. It is often useful to render picture elements (pixels) in separate images or "passes" and then combine the resulting images into a single final image, called a composite.

A semi-transparent foreground composited over a chequered background.

The alpha channel and the "over" operator

The technique stores, alongside each pixel's red, green, and blue values, an additional alpha value (α) representing coverage or opacity: α = 1 is fully opaque, α = 0 fully transparent. The algebra of compositing was formalized by Thomas Porter and Tom Duff in their influential 1984 SIGGRAPH paper "Compositing Digital Images," which defined the over operator: placing element A over B yields a colour computed as A plus B attenuated by A's transparency. In premultiplied form the resulting colour is Co = CA + CB(1 − αA), with combined alpha αo = αA + αB(1 − αA).

Premultiplied alpha

Most professional systems store colour values premultiplied by alpha, which simplifies compositing arithmetic and makes filtering and interpolation behave correctly. Confusing straight and premultiplied data is a classic source of dark or bright fringes ("halos") at image edges.

Applications

Alpha compositing underlies virtually all modern image editing, motion-picture visual effects, video-game sprites, and graphical user interfaces, and is supported by file formats such as PNG and TIFF with an alpha channel and by window systems that composite layered windows in real time.
