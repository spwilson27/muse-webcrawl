Cree (nēhiyawēwin) is a dialect continuum of Algonquian languages spoken by approximately 96,000 people across Canada, from the Northwest Territories to Alberta to Labrador, making it the most widely spoken Indigenous language in Canada by number of speakers. Varieties include Plains Cree, Woods Cree, Swampy Cree, Moose Cree and East Cree (James Bay Cree); together with Innu-aimun (Montagnais) and Naskapi they form the Cree–Montagnais–Naskapi grouping.

Linguistic features

Like other Algonquian languages, Cree is polysynthetic: complex verbs incorporate pronominal, directional and adverbial elements, and a single verb-word can express a whole sentence. Nouns are classed as animate or inanimate, a grammatical gender distinction, and the language distinguishes proximate and obviative third persons. Word order is pragmatically driven rather than fixed.

Writing

Cree is famous for its syllabic writing system, devised in the 1840s by Methodist missionary James Evans while working at Norway House, Manitoba. In Canadian Aboriginal syllabics, each character encodes a syllable, with orientation indicating the vowel — a system so learnable it spread rapidly among Cree and was adapted for Inuktitut and other languages. In the south, a standard Roman orthography is also widely used.

Status and revitalisation

Though comparatively robust, Cree suffered severe intergenerational disruption through the residential school system, in which Indigenous children were punished for speaking their languages. Today Cree is taught in schools across the Prairies and James Bay, broadcast on Indigenous radio, and used in modern media and literature; East Cree and Plains Cree have official recognition in their territories.
