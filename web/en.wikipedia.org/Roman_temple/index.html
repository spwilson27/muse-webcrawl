Roman temple

Ancient Roman temples were among the most important buildings in Roman culture, and some of the richest buildings in Roman architecture, though only a few survive in any sort of complete state. Their construction and maintenance was a major part of ancient Roman religion, and all towns of any importance had at least one main temple, as well as smaller shrines. The main room (cella) housed the cult image of the deity to whom the temple was dedicated, and often a small altar for incense or libations.

Design

Roman temples combined Etruscan and Greek influences. From Etruscan models they took the high podium, a deep front porch (portico) reached by a frontal staircase, and an emphasis on the facade; from Greece they took the classical orders and peripteral colonnades, which in Roman practice often became pseudo-peripteral, with engaged columns along the sides of the cella. The Maison Carrée at Nîmes is the best-preserved example of the typical rectangular podium temple.

The most famous Roman temple is the Pantheon in Rome — a circular temple (rotonda) with a vast concrete dome and oculus, rebuilt under Hadrian around 118–125 AD and surviving almost intact because it was converted into a church. Circular temples in the Greek tholos tradition were also built, such as the Temple of Hercules Victor in the Forum Boarium.

Function

Worship was largely performed outside: sacrifices took place at an altar in front of the temple, with the doors open so the cult statue could "witness" the rite, while crowds gathered in the forum or precinct. Temples also served civic purposes — the Senate met in certain temples, treasuries and archives were stored in others (notably the Temple of Saturn), and triumphal spoils were displayed in them.

Decline and survival

With the Christianisation of the empire in the 4th century, temples were closed by imperial edicts of the 390s and gradually abandoned, quarried, or converted into churches — the reason the Pantheon and a few others survive largely complete.
