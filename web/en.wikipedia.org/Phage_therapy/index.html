Phage therapy

ApproachUse of bacteriophage viruses against bacterial infection
PioneersFélix d'Hérelle; Frederick Twort
Key centresEliava Institute, Tbilisi, Georgia

Phage therapy, viral phage therapy or phagotherapy is the therapeutic use of bacteriophages — viruses that infect and kill bacteria — to treat pathogenic bacterial infections. Although the approach was explored from the 1910s, it was largely eclipsed in the West by antibiotics after the Second World War, while continuing in the Soviet Union and Eastern Europe.

Bacteriophages attaching to and infecting a bacterial cell

Félix d'Hérelle, who co-discovered bacteriophages, used phage preparations against dysentery and cholera in the 1920s and founded institutes to produce them. The Eliava Institute in Tbilisi, Georgia, became the world centre of phage research and production, and commercial phage cocktails remain available in Georgia, Poland and Russia for infections of wounds, the gut and the urinary tract. Because phages are highly specific to their bacterial hosts, therapy requires matching a phage — or a cocktail of phages — to the patient's infecting strain.

Renewed interest

The rise of antibiotic resistance has revived Western interest. Well-publicised compassionate-use cases, such as the 2016 treatment of a life-threatening Acinetobacter baumannii infection in San Diego, and the 2019 use of genetically engineered phages against Mycobacterium infection in London, have led to dedicated centres and clinical trials. Challenges remain in regulation, manufacturing standardisation, bacterial resistance to phages and the economics of highly personalised treatments.
