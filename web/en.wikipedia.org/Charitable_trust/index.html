Charitable trust

Legal fieldTrust law; charity law
BeneficiariesThe public or a section of it (no private beneficiaries)
Key featuresCharitable purposes; public benefit; tax privileges; cy-près doctrine
RegulatorsCharity Commission (England and Wales); IRS (United States)

A charitable trust is a trust established for charitable purposes, in which the benefit is directed to the public or a section of the public rather than to identified private individuals. In common law jurisdictions charitable trusts enjoy special treatment: they are exempt from the beneficiary principle that invalidates most non-charitable purpose trusts, may last indefinitely despite the rule against perpetuities, and generally receive favourable tax status.


Charitable trusts fund purposes from poverty relief to education and health.

Charitable purposes

The classic classification comes from Commissioners for Special Purposes of Income Tax v Pemsel (1891), in which Lord Macnaghten grouped charitable purposes under four heads: relief of poverty, advancement of education, advancement of religion, and other purposes beneficial to the community. Modern statutes, such as the Charities Act 2011 in England and Wales, expand the list to include health, citizenship, the arts and heritage, amateur sport, human rights, environmental protection, animal welfare, and the efficiency of the armed forces, among others — each subject to a public benefit requirement.

Public benefit

A purpose is charitable only if it benefits the public or a sufficient section of it, and any private benefit must be incidental. The beneficiaries must not be defined by a personal nexus (such as family or employment by a single firm) unless the purpose falls within the poverty exception recognised by case law.

Cy-près

If a charitable purpose fails, becomes impossible, or leaves a surplus, the court or charity regulator may apply the property cy-près — "as near as possible" — to a similar charitable purpose, rather than the gift failing. The doctrine preserves charitable funds for public purposes consistent with the donor's general charitable intention.

Charitable trusts in practice

Many major foundations are charitable trusts, and the form is also used for giving vehicles such as charitable remainder trusts and charitable lead trusts in the United States, which split benefits between charitable and private recipients. Trustees are fiduciaries subject to charity law regulation, reporting duties, and restrictions on political and trading activities.
