Dziga Vertov (Russian: Дзига Вертов, born David Abelevich Kaufman, 2 January 1896 – 12 February 1954) was a Soviet pioneer documentary film and newsreel director, as well as a cinema theorist. His filming practices and theories influenced the cinéma vérité style of documentary moviemaking and the Dziga Vertov Group, a radical cooperative of filmmakers active in the 1960s. His younger brothers Boris Kaufman and Mikhail Kaufman were also notable cinematographers.

Early life

Kaufman was born in Białystok, then part of the Russian Empire, to a Jewish family of booksellers. He studied music and wrote poetry and science fiction before turning to film after the October Revolution. He adopted the pseudonym "Dziga Vertov"—dziga suggesting a whirring spinning top (often glossed as a Ukrainian word for a spinning sound) and vertov from the Russian verb vertet, "to spin" or "turn". In 1918 he joined the Moscow Cinema Committee and edited the newsreel Kino-Nedelya ("Cine-Week").

Kino-Eye

Vertov developed the theory of the kino-glaz ("cine-eye" or "kino-eye"): the camera, perfected by montage, could perceive and organise reality better than the human eye. With his wife Elizaveta Svilova (editor) and brother Mikhail Kaufman (cameraman) he formed the "Council of Three" and issued manifestos such as "WE: Variant of a Manifesto" (1922) attacking theatrical, acted cinema. From 1922 to 1925 he produced the Kino-Pravda ("Film Truth") newsreel series, whose playful editing and graphics influenced later documentary and even the name of Jean Rouch's cinéma vérité.

Man with a Movie Camera

His masterpiece, Man with a Movie Camera (1929), filmed by Mikhail Kaufman and edited by Svilova, portrays a day in Soviet cities (Kyiv, Moscow, and Odesa) without actors, sets, or intertitles, using split screens, freeze frames, slow and fast motion, superimpositions, and self-reflexive shots of the cameraman at work. Voted the greatest documentary of all time in the British Film Institute's 2014 Sight and Sound poll, it remains a landmark of experimental cinema.

Later work

Vertov embraced sound with Enthusiasm: Symphony of the Donbass (1931), an industrial symphony of recorded noise, and made Three Songs About Lenin (1934). Under the pressures of Socialist Realism his opportunities shrank; he spent his later years editing the newsreel Novosti Dnya ("News of the Day"). He died of cancer in Moscow in 1954, largely forgotten in the USSR, before an international revival from the 1960s onward.

Legacy

Vertov's ideas shaped cinéma vérité, direct cinema, and filmmakers from Jean-Luc Godard (whose Dziga Vertov Group took his name) to contemporary documentarists. His diaries and writings, published posthumously, established him as one of cinema's foundational theorists.
