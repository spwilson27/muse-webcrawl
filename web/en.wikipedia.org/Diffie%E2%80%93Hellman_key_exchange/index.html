Diffie–Hellman key exchange

DesignersWhitfield Diffie, Martin Hellman (concept also by Ralph Merkle)
Published1976
BasisDiscrete logarithm problem

Diffie–Hellman (DH) key exchange is a mathematical method of securely exchanging cryptographic keys over a public channel. It was one of the first public-key protocols, published by Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman in 1976, and it allows two parties with no prior shared secret to jointly establish one that eavesdroppers cannot feasibly compute.

Protocol

The parties agree on a large prime p and a generator g. Each chooses a secret exponent (a and b), exchanges ga mod p and gb mod p, and raises the received value to their own secret exponent, arriving at the same shared secret gab mod p. Security rests on the difficulty of the discrete logarithm problem.

Use and variants

Diffie–Hellman underlies protocols such as TLS, SSH, and IPsec. Because the basic exchange is unauthenticated, it is usually combined with signatures to prevent man-in-the-middle attacks. Ephemeral variants (DHE and ECDHE) provide forward secrecy; elliptic-curve Diffie–Hellman achieves the same security with much smaller keys.
