An intentional community is a voluntary residential community of people who share a common social, political, religious or spiritual vision, and who typically share responsibilities and resources. Intentional communities include collective households, cohousing communities, ecovillages, communes, kibbutzim, ashrams and housing cooperatives. Members often hold common values relating to sustainability, simplicity, spirituality or social justice, and organise governance, work and income according to those values.

History

Planned communal living has ancient roots in monastic orders and religious sects. The 19th century saw secular experiments such as the Owenite community at New Harmony, Indiana (1825) and Fourierist phalanxes, as well as enduring religious communities like the Shakers and Hutterites. Israeli kibbutzim, founded from 1909, combined communal ownership with agricultural settlement. The counterculture of the 1960s and 1970s produced thousands of communes, most short-lived, and the ecovillage and cohousing movements expanded from the 1990s.

Characteristics

Communities vary in how much they share: from cohousing groups with private homes and shared common facilities, to income-sharing communes such as Twin Oaks in Virginia, founded in 1967 and inspired by B. F. Skinner's novel Walden Two. Governance may be by consensus, majority vote, or a charismatic or religious leadership. Common motivations include ecological sustainability, self-sufficiency, spiritual practice, and mutual aid.

Examples

Well-known intentional communities include the Findhorn Foundation in Scotland, Auroville in India, Freetown Christiania in Copenhagen, the Bruderhof communities, and the Farm in Tennessee. The Fellowship for Intentional Community maintains directories listing thousands of communities worldwide.
