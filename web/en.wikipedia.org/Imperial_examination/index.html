Imperial examination

Rows of examination cells at Nanjing's Jiangnan Examination Hall
Chinese科舉 (kējǔ)
Period605 – 1905 (with earlier antecedents)
CountryImperial China (also adopted in Vietnam and, briefly, Korea)
PurposeSelection of officials for the state bureaucracy

The imperial examination (Chinese: 科舉; pinyin: kējǔ) was a civil-service examination system in Imperial China administered for the selection of officials for the state bureaucracy. The system, formalised under the Sui dynasty in 605 and abolished in 1905 in the late Qing dynasty, lasted for roughly 1,300 years and shaped Chinese society, education and governance, as well as the bureaucracies of neighbouring states.

History

Although recommendation-based systems existed earlier, the Sui and Tang dynasties made written examinations the principal route to office, testing candidates on the Confucian classics. The system matured under the Song dynasty, which expanded quotas, introduced palace examinations presided over by the emperor, and used anonymised scripts and recopying to limit favouritism. Under the Ming and Qing, examinations proceeded through local, provincial and metropolitan levels, culminating in the palace examination.

Degrees and content

Candidates who passed county/prefectural tests earned the xiucai (licentiate) degree; provincial examinations, held every three years, conferred the juren degree; and the metropolitan and palace examinations in the capital produced jinshi, the highest degree, whose top three graduates received special honours. From the Ming dynasty the core format included the "eight-legged essay" (baguwen), a rigidly structured composition on passages from the Four Books, interpreted according to Zhu Xi's commentaries. Candidates wrote in individual cells in vast examination compounds for sessions lasting days.

Effects and abolition

The examinations embodied an ideology of meritocracy — in principle open to most men regardless of birth — and created a scholar-official class (the "mandarins") that integrated local elites with the imperial state. Critics, both contemporary and modern, argued the curriculum rewarded rote memorisation and literary formalism over technical skill. Facing the challenges of modernisation and defeat in wars, the Qing court abolished the system in 1905. Its legacy persists in modern China's civil-service examinations and university entrance examination (gaokao), and examination systems in Vietnam (until 1919) and Korea were modelled on it.
