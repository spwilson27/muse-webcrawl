Metal detector

A metal detector is an instrument that detects the nearby presence of metal. Metal detectors are useful for finding metal objects hidden underground or included within other objects. They typically consist of a hand-held unit with a sensor probe which can be swept over the ground; when the sensor comes near a piece of metal, the device indicates it, commonly with an audible tone whose pitch or volume changes with proximity, or a needle or readout on a display.

History

The first industrial metal detectors were developed in the 1960s for mining and industrial applications. Earlier, Alexander Graham Bell devised a crude electromagnetic device in 1881 in an unsuccessful attempt to locate a bullet lodged in President James Garfield. Modern development followed the work of Gerhard Fischer and others in the 1920s-30s, and wartime research produced mine detectors used extensively in the Second World War.

Technology

Most detectors use one of three technologies: very low frequency (VLF) phase-shift detection, pulse induction (PI), or beat-frequency oscillation (BFO). A VLF detector has two coils: a transmitter generating a rapidly alternating magnetic field and a receiver; metal in the field induces eddy currents and its own magnetic field, which the receiver picks up. The phase shift between the signals helps discriminate between metals such as iron, aluminium, gold and silver.

Applications

Uses include demining, weapons screening at airports and public buildings, construction and rebar location, archaeology, prospecting for gold and other metals, and the hobby of treasure hunting and coin shooting on beaches and in parks. Food processing and industry use fixed detectors to find metal contaminants.
