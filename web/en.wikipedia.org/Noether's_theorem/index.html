StatementEvery differentiable symmetry of the action corresponds to a conservation law

Noether's theorem states that every differentiable symmetry of the action of a physical system has a corresponding conservation law. Proved by the mathematician Emmy Noether in 1915 and published in 1918, the theorem has become a cornerstone of theoretical physics and the calculus of variations. In simple terms: if a system's Lagrangian does not change under some continuous transformation, then a certain quantity is conserved as the system evolves.

Symmetries and their corresponding conserved quantities

Examples

Invariance of the laws of physics under translation in time implies conservation of energy. Invariance under translation in space implies conservation of linear momentum. Invariance under rotation implies conservation of angular momentum. In field theory, global gauge (phase) invariance implies conservation of electric charge. These correspondences explain why conservation laws hold, grounding them in the symmetries of space, time, and the fields themselves.

Historical background

Noether, working at Göttingen at the invitation of David Hilbert and Felix Klein, developed the theorem while addressing questions about energy conservation in general relativity. Her result appeared in the paper "Invariante Variationsprobleme" (1918). The theorem initially received little attention but was gradually recognized as one of the most important results in mathematical physics; Albert Einstein praised Noether's insight, and the theorem became central to particle physics, where symmetries dictate the interactions of the Standard Model.

Statement and scope

For a system described by an action integral, Noether's (first) theorem asserts that to each one-parameter group of transformations leaving the action invariant (up to a boundary term) there corresponds a current whose divergence vanishes on solutions of the equations of motion — a conserved current, whose spatial integral is a conserved charge. A second theorem concerns infinite-dimensional (local gauge) symmetries and underlies the structure of general relativity and gauge theories. The theorem assumes continuous, differentiable symmetries; discrete symmetries such as parity do not yield conservation laws of this type.
