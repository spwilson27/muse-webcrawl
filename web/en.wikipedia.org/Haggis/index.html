Haggis

Haggis is a savoury pudding considered the national dish of Scotland.

Haggis (Scottish Gaelic: taigeis) is a savoury pudding containing sheep's pluck (heart, liver, and lungs), minced with onion, oatmeal, suet, spices, and salt, mixed with stock, and cooked while traditionally encased in the animal's stomach, though now an artificial casing is often used instead. Although its description is not immediately appealing, haggis has an excellent nutty texture and a delicious savoury flavour.

The Oxford English Dictionary records that the dish, with essentially the same recipe, has been eaten in England as well as Scotland, and similar offal-based puddings exist in many culinary traditions. Haggis is traditionally served with "neeps and tatties" (boiled and mashed turnip (swede) and potatoes), and a dram of Scotch whisky. It is considered the national dish of Scotland as a result of Robert Burns' poem "Address to a Haggis" of 1786.

History

Food writer Alan Davidson suggests that the ancient Romans were the first generally to make products of the haggis type, as similar recipes can be found in ancient sources. A similar dish of minced entrails appears in Homer's Odyssey. The first known written recipe for a dish of the name comes from the cookbook Liber Cure Cocorum, dating from around 1430 in Lancashire, North West England, for a dish called "hagese". Haggis is popularly assumed to be of Scottish origin, but many countries have produced similar dishes with different names. The haggis may have been brought to Scotland by Scandinavian settlers, in the way that words such as kirk and gimmer came into Scots; or it may have developed locally as a way of using offal and preserving it in the stomach of the slaughtered animal.

Burns Supper

Haggis remains popular with expatriate Scots in Canada, the United States, and Australia. It is the main dish at a Burns Supper, held on or around 25 January, the birthday of Robert Burns. During Burns's lifetime, haggis was a common dish of the poor as it made use of cheap ingredients left over after the better cuts of meat were sold. The haggis is traditionally piped in by a bagpiper and ceremonially addressed with Burns's poem before being cut open and served.

Legality in the United States

In 1971, the United States Department of Agriculture banned the importation of livestock lungs, effectively prohibiting authentic haggis, because animal lungs may contain fluids such as stomach acid and phlegm that could present a food safety risk. Haggis made in the United States substitutes other offal for the lungs. The ban remains the subject of periodic lobbying by Scottish producers and members of the Scottish diaspora.
