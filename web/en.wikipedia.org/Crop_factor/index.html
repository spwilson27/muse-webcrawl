Crop factor

In digital photography, the crop factor, sometimes called the focal length multiplier, is the ratio of the dimensions of a camera's image sensor format to a reference format; most commonly, this term refers to the ratio relative to the 35 mm film format (43.3 mm diagonal). If a sensor is smaller than the reference, the image is cropped: a lens produces images with the same focal length, but the field of view is narrower, as if only the central portion of the image circle were used.

Sensor sizes relative to the 35 mm full-frame format.

Definition

The crop factor is defined as the ratio of the diagonal of the reference format to the diagonal of the sensor in question. For the common APS-C format (about 23.6 × 15.7 mm on Nikon and Sony cameras), the crop factor is approximately 1.5; Canon's APS-C sensors give about 1.6. Micro Four Thirds sensors have a crop factor of 2.0.

Equivalent focal length

Multiplying a lens's focal length by the crop factor gives the 35 mm-equivalent focal length, which describes the field of view a full-frame camera would need to match. A 50 mm lens on a 1.5× crop camera frames like a 75 mm lens on full frame. Crop factor also affects depth of field and the effective brightness of the image: for equivalence in field of view, depth of field, and total light, the f-number must be multiplied by the crop factor as well.

Uses and criticism

Crop factor became a ubiquitous marketing and discussion term with the spread of DSLRs using APS-C sensors. Critics note that it can mislead, because focal length itself does not change; only the recorded angle of view does. The term also persists in discussions of smartphone cameras, whose small sensors have large crop factors relative to full frame.
