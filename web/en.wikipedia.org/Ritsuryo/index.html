Ritsuryō

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Ritsuryō (Japanese: 律令) was the system of law and government established in Japan during the late Asuka and Nara periods, modelled on the legal codes of Tang-dynasty China. The name combines ritsu (律), the penal code, and ryō (令), the administrative code.

Development

The system was introduced through a series of reforms beginning with the Taika Reform of 645 and codified in the Taihō Code of 701 and the Yōrō Code, compiled in 718 and implemented in 757. Under ritsuryō, all land and people were in principle registered to the emperor, land was redistributed periodically to cultivators under the handen shūju system, and taxes were levied in rice, cloth, and labour or military service.

Government structure

The central government was headed by the Daijō-kan (Council of State), beneath the emperor, with ministries handling ceremonies, civil affairs, war, justice, the treasury, and the imperial household, alongside the Jingikan which supervised Shinto rites. Officials were ranked in a hierarchy of court ranks and appointed — at least in theory — by merit through examination influences borrowed from China. The country was divided into provinces (kuni) administered by appointed governors (kokushi).

Decline

From the ninth century the system's fiscal foundations eroded as tax-exempt private estates (shōen) spread and the land redistribution system lapsed. Political power shifted first to regents of the Fujiwara clan and retired emperors, and eventually to the warrior government of the Kamakura shogunate, although the ritsuryō framework was never formally abolished and traces of it survived until the Meiji Restoration.
