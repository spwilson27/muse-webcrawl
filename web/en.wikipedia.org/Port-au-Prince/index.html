Port-au-Prince

Port-au-Prince (French: Port-au-Prince; Haitian Creole: Pòtoprens) is the capital and most populous city of Haiti. Founded in 1749 under French colonial rule, it sits on the Gulf of Gonâve in the Ouest department and anchors a metropolitan area — including Pétion-Ville, Delmas, and Carrefour — of roughly three million people, about a third of Haiti's population.

History

Named for a French ship (Le Prince) anchored in the bay, the city became capital of the colony of Saint-Domingue in 1770. It was a center of the Haitian Revolution and of the independent state proclaimed in 1804. Landmarks include the National Palace (1918), the Cathedral of Our Lady of the Assumption, the Iron Market (Marché de Fer), and the gingerbread houses of the Bois Verna and Pacot districts.

2010 earthquake and aftermath

On 12 January 2010 a magnitude 7.0 earthquake struck just west of the city, killing by various estimates between 100,000 and 316,000 people and destroying the National Palace, the cathedral, ministries, and much of the urban fabric; over a million people were displaced into camps. Reconstruction has been slow, and since 2021 the capital has been gripped by escalating gang violence following the assassination of President Jovenel Moïse, displacing hundreds of thousands and severely disrupting the port, hospitals, and schools, prompting international security missions.

Geography

The city spreads from the shore up into the hills of the Massif de la Selle foothills, with a tropical savanna climate and a hurricane season from June to November.
