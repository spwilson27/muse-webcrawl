Colectomy

Colectomy (from Greek kolon, "colon", and -ectomy, "removal") is the surgical resection of all or part of the large intestine (the colon). A partial colectomy removes one segment of the colon, while a total colectomy removes the entire colon. When combined with removal of the rectum it is called a proctocolectomy.

Indications

Colectomy may be performed for colorectal cancer, inflammatory bowel disease such as ulcerative colitis or Crohn's disease that does not respond to medical treatment, diverticulitis with complications, bowel obstruction, ischaemia, trauma, or hereditary conditions such as familial adenomatous polyposis, where prophylactic removal greatly reduces cancer risk.

Procedure

The operation may be performed by open surgery or laparoscopically. After the diseased segment is removed, the remaining ends of bowel may be joined together (anastomosis), or the end of the remaining bowel may be brought out through the abdominal wall as a stoma — a colostomy if colon, or an ileostomy if the whole colon is removed and the ileum is used. In some patients an ileal pouch (such as a J-pouch) is constructed to restore continuity after proctocolectomy.

Types

Named partial procedures correspond to the segment removed: right hemicolectomy, left hemicolectomy, sigmoid colectomy, transverse colectomy, and anterior resection. Risks include anastomotic leak, infection, bleeding and adhesions.
