Hemochromatosis

Hemochromatosis (also spelled haemochromatosis) is a condition in which the body absorbs and stores too much iron, leading to progressive iron overload and damage to organs including the liver, heart, pancreas, joints and skin. The most common form, hereditary hemochromatosis, is one of the most frequent genetic disorders in people of Northern European ancestry; it is most often caused by mutations in the HFE gene, particularly the C282Y mutation.

Cause and mechanism

Iron absorption is regulated by the liver hormone hepcidin, which normally limits uptake of dietary iron when stores are sufficient. In HFE-related hemochromatosis, hepcidin production is inappropriately low, so iron continues to be absorbed and deposited in tissues, where its excess generates free radicals and causes fibrosis. Rarer genetic forms involve mutations in genes for hemojuvelin, hepcidin itself, transferrin receptor 2 or ferroportin; secondary iron overload can also result from repeated blood transfusions, for example in thalassaemia major.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms usually appear in middle age — later in women, who lose iron through menstruation and pregnancy — and are often nonspecific at first: fatigue, joint pain (especially in the knuckles of the index and middle fingers), and abdominal discomfort. Advanced disease classically produces the triad of liver cirrhosis, diabetes mellitus (from pancreatic damage) and bronze skin pigmentation, sometimes summarised as "bronze diabetes". Other complications include heart failure and arrhythmias, hypogonadism, arthritis and an increased risk of liver cancer.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on iron studies — raised transferrin saturation and serum ferritin — confirmed by genetic testing for HFE mutations and, where needed, imaging or liver biopsy to assess iron burden and fibrosis. Treatment is straightforward and highly effective if begun before irreversible organ damage: regular therapeutic phlebotomy (blood removal) to bring ferritin down to target levels, followed by maintenance bloodletting; patients are also advised to avoid iron supplements and excess alcohol. Iron chelation therapy is used when phlebotomy is not possible, chiefly in secondary overload. With early treatment, life expectancy is normal.
