Dacia

RegionNorth of the lower Danube, largely modern Romania and Moldova
PeopleDacians (Getae)
Kingdomc. 168 BC – AD 106
CapitalSarmizegetusa Regia

Dacia was the land inhabited by the Dacians, an ancient people related to the Thracians, located north of the lower Danube in the area of the Carpathian Mountains, corresponding broadly to modern Romania and Moldova, with parts of neighbouring countries. The Dacians spoke the Dacian language, now largely lost. Under King Burebista (82–44 BC), the Dacian kingdom became a powerful state that alarmed Rome.

History

Greek sources called the people Getae, Roman sources Daci. Burebista unified the tribes, extended Dacian power from the Tisza to the Black Sea, and intervened in Roman politics, siding with Pompey against Caesar. After Burebista's assassination in 44 BC the kingdom fragmented. It was reunified under Decebalus (r. 87–106), who defeated a Roman army under Domitian and forced a negotiated peace in AD 89.

Dacian Wars and Roman province

The emperor Trajan resolved to eliminate the Dacian threat and exploit Dacia's gold mines. In the Dacian Wars (101–102 and 105–106), Roman forces defeated Decebalus, who committed suicide; his capital Sarmizegetusa was destroyed. Dacia became a Roman province, colonized by settlers from across the empire, commemorated in Rome by Trajan's Column. Roman Dacia was abandoned by Aurelian around AD 271–275 in the face of Gothic pressure, and its population was resettled south of the Danube.

Culture and legacy

Dacian religion centred on the god Zalmoxis and prized immortality of the soul. The Dacians built fortified mountain sanctuaries in the Orăștie Mountains, now UNESCO World Heritage Sites. The origins of the Romanian people and language — formed through the Romanization of Dacia and later history — remain a central theme in Romanian national historiography, and the name "Dacia" was revived by Romania's national car manufacturer.
