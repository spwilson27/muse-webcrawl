Anonymity

An anonymous person is one whose identity is unknown or concealed.

Anonymity describes situations in which the identity of a person is unknown or untraceable. The term derives from the Greek word ἀνωνυμία (anonymia), meaning "without a name" or "namelessness". Anonymity is distinct from pseudonymity, in which a person acts under a consistent assumed name that can accumulate reputation or history while remaining unlinkable, in principle, to a legal identity.

Concepts

In information science, anonymity is often formalised through the idea of an anonymity set: the set of all possible subjects who could have performed an action. A larger anonymity set generally means stronger anonymity. Related concepts include unlinkability, unobservability, and k-anonymity in statistical databases, in which each record is indistinguishable from at least k−1 others.

Anonymity online

Internet anonymity is pursued through technologies such as virtual private networks, the Tor network (which routes traffic through multiple relays using onion routing), anonymous remailers, and privacy-focused cryptocurrencies. Research has shown that supposedly anonymous data can often be de-anonymised by cross-referencing datasets—famously, Latanya Sweeney showed that most Americans could be uniquely identified by ZIP code, birth date, and sex alone.

Social and legal aspects

Anonymous speech has a long political history, including the pseudonymous Federalist Papers. The United States Supreme Court has recognised a right to anonymous speech under the First Amendment, notably in McIntyre v. Ohio Elections Commission (1995). Anonymity also underlies anonymous peer review, anonymous voting, whistleblowing, and twelve-step programmes such as Alcoholics Anonymous. Critics associate anonymity with disinhibition and antisocial behaviour online, sometimes described through the "online disinhibition effect".
