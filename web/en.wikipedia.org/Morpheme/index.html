Morpheme

A morpheme is the smallest meaningful unit of a language. Morphemes are the building blocks of words: the word unbreakable, for example, consists of three morphemes — un-, break and -able. The study of morphemes and how they combine is called morphology. The term was coined in analogy with phoneme, deriving from the Greek morphē ("form").

Classification

Free morphemes can stand alone as words (such as book or run), while bound morphemes must attach to others, like the plural -s or the prefix re-. Roots carry the core meaning of a word; affixes modify it. Affixes are further divided into derivational morphemes, which create new words or change word class (teach → teacher), and inflectional morphemes, which mark grammatical features such as tense, number and case (walk → walked, walks).

Allomorphs and irregular forms

A single morpheme may have several pronunciations or spellings, called allomorphs: the English plural appears as /s/ in cats, /z/ in dogs and /ɪz/ in horses. Some morphemes have zero realisation (the plural of sheep), and others are fused or suppletive, as in the past tense went of go. Morphemes like the cran- of cranberry, which occur in only one word, are sometimes called cranberry morphemes or unique morphemes.

In different languages

Languages differ greatly in how much meaning they pack into words. Isolating languages such as Vietnamese use mostly one morpheme per word; agglutinative languages such as Turkish string many morphemes together in regular chains; fusional languages such as Latin fuse several grammatical meanings into single endings. Identifying morphemes in a language is a foundational task of linguistic analysis.
