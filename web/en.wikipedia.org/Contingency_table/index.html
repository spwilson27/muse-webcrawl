In statistics, a contingency table (also known as a cross tabulation or crosstab) is a table that displays the frequency distribution of two or more categorical variables. Each cell shows the number of observations falling into the combination of categories defined by its row and column, allowing the relationship between the variables to be examined at a glance.

Structure

A simple 2 × 2 table cross-classifies observations by two binary variables; tables may be extended to any number of rows and columns and to several variables at once (multiway tables). Row and column totals are called marginal distributions. For a 2 × 2 table, the four cells are often labelled a, b, c and d, and summary measures such as the odds ratio, relative risk and the phi coefficient are computed directly from them.

Tests of association

Contingency tables are the data layout for classical tests of independence. Pearson's chi-squared test compares observed counts with the counts expected if the variables were independent; for small samples Fisher's exact test gives an exact p-value for 2 × 2 tables. Measures of association derived from tables include Cramér's V and the contingency coefficient, while log-linear models generalise the analysis to multiway tables.

History and use

The term was introduced by Karl Pearson in 1904. Contingency tables are used throughout the social sciences, epidemiology (for example in cohort and case-control studies), market research, and quality control, wherever survey or count data on categories must be summarised.
