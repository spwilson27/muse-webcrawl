Śvētāmbara

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Śvētāmbara (Sanskrit: श्वेताम्बर, "white-clad") is one of the two main traditions of Jainism, the other being Digambara ("sky-clad"). The names refer to the dress of their ascetics: Śvētāmbara monks and nuns wear simple white garments, whereas Digambara monks renounce clothing altogether.

The schism

Tradition dates the division to the centuries after Mahāvīra (6th–5th century BCE), the twenty-fourth tīrthaṅkara. According to Śvētāmbara accounts, a twelve-year famine in Magadha drove a group of monks south under Bhadrabāhu; those who remained in the north, led by Sthūlabhadra, relaxed the rule of nudity during hardship and compiled the scriptures at councils in Pāṭaliputra and later Valabhī. When the migrants returned, differences in practice and scripture had hardened into two sects, a split conventionally dated by about the 4th–5th century CE.

Beliefs and practices

Śvētāmbaras accept a canon of scriptures (the Āgamas) written in Ardhamāgadhī Prakrit, which Digambaras reject as lost. They hold that women can attain liberation (mokṣa) in a female body, and that the nineteenth tīrthaṅkara, Mallinātha, was a woman; Digambaras dispute both points. Śvētāmbara ascetics carry a small broom (rajoharaṇa) to sweep insects from their path, an alms bowl, and often wear a cloth over the mouth (mukhavastrikā) to avoid inhaling small creatures, in keeping with the central vow of ahiṃsā (non-violence).

Sub-traditions

The tradition divides between image-worshipping Mūrtipūjaka communities and the aniconic Sthānakavāsī and Terāpanthī orders, which arose from reform movements in the fifteenth to eighteenth centuries. Śvētāmbaras today form the majority of Jains in Gujarat, Rajasthan, and Mumbai, and the tradition's merchant communities have built celebrated temples such as those at Palitana and Mount Abu.
