Engel's law

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Engel's law is an economic relationship proposed in 1857 by the German statistician Ernst Engel (1821–1896) stating that, as income rises, the proportion of income spent on food falls, even though absolute expenditure on food may rise. Engel formulated the law from studies of household budgets, first of Saxon and then of Belgian working families collected by Édouard Ducpétiaux.

Statement and evidence

The law implies that food is a necessity good with an income elasticity of demand below one. It is among the best-established empirical regularities in economics: across countries and over time, richer households and richer nations devote smaller shares of expenditure to food — from over half of household budgets in poor economies to around a tenth in the richest. The food share, sometimes called the Engel coefficient, is used as an indicator of welfare and of poverty lines.

Implications

Engel's law underlies the long-run decline in agriculture's share of employment and output as economies develop, and it qualifies sweeping claims about consumption: growth in food demand grows more slowly than income, a fact of central importance for agricultural policy, development economics and the design of consumption taxes. Engel curves — relating expenditure on a good to total income — remain a standard tool of applied microeconomics.
