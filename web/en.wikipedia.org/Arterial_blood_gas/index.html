Arterial blood gas test

An arterial blood gas test, commonly called an ABG test or an arterial blood gas analysis, is a test that measures the amounts of arterial gases, such as oxygen and carbon dioxide, in the blood. An ABG test also measures the acidity (pH) and the levels of oxygen and carbon dioxide in the blood from an artery. This test is used to check how well the lungs are able to move oxygen into the blood and remove carbon dioxide from the blood.

As blood passes through your lungs, oxygen moves into the blood while carbon dioxide moves out of the blood into the lungs. An ABG test uses arterial blood — often drawn from the radial artery at the wrist — because arterial blood has not yet delivered its oxygen to the tissues, unlike venous blood.

Interpretation

ABG results are used to diagnose and classify acid–base disturbances: respiratory acidosis and alkalosis (driven by PaCO2) and metabolic acidosis and alkalosis (driven by bicarbonate), as well as to assess oxygenation in critically ill patients and in conditions such as COPD, pneumonia, and sepsis. Interpretation typically proceeds by examining pH first, then the respiratory (PaCO2) and metabolic (HCO3−) components, and finally the degree of compensation.

Procedure

A small sample of arterial blood is drawn with a heparinised syringe, usually from the radial artery after an Allen test to confirm collateral circulation, although the brachial or femoral arteries are also used. The sample is analysed promptly on a blood gas analyser, which uses electrodes to measure pH, PaCO2, and PaO2, and calculates derived values.
