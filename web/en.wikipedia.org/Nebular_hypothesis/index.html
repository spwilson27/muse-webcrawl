The nebular hypothesis is the most widely accepted model in cosmogony explaining the formation and evolution of the Solar System (as well as other planetary systems). It suggests that the Solar System formed from a vast cloud of gas and dust — a nebula — which collapsed under its own gravity, flattening into a rotating protoplanetary disk from which the Sun and planets grew.

History

The idea was first sketched by Emanuel Swedenborg in 1734 and developed by Immanuel Kant in his Universal Natural History and Theory of the Heavens (1755); Pierre-Simon Laplace gave a mathematical version in 1796, hence the alternative name Kant–Laplace hypothesis. After periods of competition from catastrophic theories (such as passing-star and tidal models), the nebular model was revived in the twentieth century in the form of the solar nebular disk model, supported by observations of disks around young stars.

Process of formation

Some 4.6 billion years ago, a region of a giant molecular cloud — possibly disturbed by a nearby supernova — began to collapse. As it contracted, conservation of angular momentum spun it faster and flattened it into a disk. Most material fell to the centre, heating until hydrogen fusion ignited the Sun. Within the disk, dust grains stuck together and grew through planetesimals into protoplanets by accretion; inside the frost line, where it was too warm for volatile ices to condense, rocky planets formed, while beyond it the giant planets grew cores large enough to capture hydrogen and helium gas.

Evidence and developments

The model naturally explains why all planets orbit in the same direction and nearly the same plane, and why rocky and gaseous planets are sorted by distance from the Sun. Modern refinements — including pebble accretion and planetary migration models such as the Nice model — address the formation timescales and the present orbits of the giant planets, and thousands of exoplanetary systems now let astronomers test the theory comparatively.
