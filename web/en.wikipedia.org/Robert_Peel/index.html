Sir Robert Peel, 2nd Baronet (1788–1850) was a British Conservative statesman who served twice as prime minister and is regarded as the founder of the modern Conservative Party and of modern policing in Britain.

[Image placeholder: Portrait of Sir Robert Peel]

Early career

Son of a wealthy cotton manufacturer, Peel entered Parliament in 1809 and rose rapidly: as Chief Secretary for Ireland (1812–18) he established the Royal Irish Constabulary, and as Home Secretary (1822–27, 1828–30) he consolidated and humanised the criminal law and in 1829 created the Metropolitan Police of London — whose officers were nicknamed "bobbies" (and earlier "peelers") after him.

First ministry and the Conservative Party

As prime minister from December 1834 Peel issued the Tamworth Manifesto, accepting the Reform Act 1832 and pledging measured reform — the founding document of modern Conservatism. Defeated in 1835, he returned to power in 1841 with a reforming government that cut tariffs, introduced an income tax (1842), and regulated banks (Bank Charter Act 1844) and factories.

Repeal of the Corn Laws

Convinced by the Irish Famine and by free-trade arguments, Peel repealed the Corn Laws protecting British agriculture in 1846, carrying the measure with Whig votes against most of his own party. The split shattered the Conservatives for a generation; Peel resigned days later. His followers, the Peelites — including Gladstone and Aberdeen — later merged into the Liberal Party. Peel died in 1850 after a riding accident on Constitution Hill.
