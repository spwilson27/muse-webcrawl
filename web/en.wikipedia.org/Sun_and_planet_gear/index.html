Sun and planet gear

The sun and planet gear is a method of converting reciprocal motion to rotary motion, invented by the Scottish engineer William Murdoch, an employee of Boulton and Watt, for use in a steam engine. It was patented by James Watt in October 1781. The gear existed as a workaround to the patent then held by James Pickard on the crank, which Watt could not legally use.

Mechanism

The sun and planet gear consists of a gear (the "planet") fixed to the end of the connecting rod, meshing with a gear (the "sun") on the main shaft. The planet does not rotate about its own axis; instead, the connecting rod constrains it to orbit the sun, and since the two gears have equal numbers of teeth, the sun gear makes two revolutions for each reciprocating cycle of the engine. The planet gear is held in its orbital path by the radius rod linking its centre to the crank arm.

History

The mechanism was fitted to Boulton and Watt rotative engines from the 1780s and remained in use for about twenty years, until Pickard's crank patent expired in 1794, after which the simpler crank and flywheel were generally adopted. Surviving examples include the Lap Engine of 1788 and engines preserved at the Science Museum, London.

Relation to epicyclic gearing

The sun and planet gear is an ancestor of epicyclic (planetary) gear trains, in which planet gears rotate on their own axes while carried around a central sun gear — a configuration now ubiquitous in automatic transmissions, though in modern planetary gears the planets do spin on their own spindles, unlike Watt's arrangement.
