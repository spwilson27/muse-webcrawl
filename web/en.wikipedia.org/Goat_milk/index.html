Goat milk

Goat milk is the milk of domestic goats. Goats produce about 2 percent of the world's total annual milk supply, but their milk is disproportionately important in regions such as the Mediterranean, the Middle East, South Asia and parts of Africa, where goats are easier to keep than cattle. Most goat milk is consumed locally or made into cheese, yogurt and other products rather than traded globally.

Composition and properties

Goat milk is similar in gross composition to cow's milk but differs in detail: its fat globules are smaller and its curd is softer and more fragile, which is often cited as making it easier to digest. It contains somewhat less alpha-s1 casein, different proportions of medium-chain fatty acids (caproic, caprylic and capric acids — named after capra, the goat — which contribute to its characteristic tangy flavour when lipolysed), and slightly more calcium, potassium and vitamin A per unit than cow's milk, but less folate and vitamin B12. It contains lactose in amounts only slightly below cow's milk, so it is not suitable for people with lactose intolerance, and its proteins cross-react in most people with cow's milk allergy.

Products

Goat milk is used to make a wide range of cheeses, from French chèvre varieties such as Sainte-Maure de Touraine and Crottin de Chavignol to Greek feta-style cheeses (often mixed with sheep's milk), Spanish Manchego-style cheeses, and Norwegian whey cheese geitost. It is also made into yogurt, kefir, butter (naturally white, since goats convert beta-carotene fully to vitamin A), cajeta (a Mexican caramel), and infant formula in some countries.

Production

Major producers include India, Bangladesh, Sudan and France. Dairy goat breeds such as the Saanen, Alpine, Nubian and Toggenburg can yield 3–4 litres per day over a lactation of about ten months. Goat milk must be handled carefully and cooled quickly to avoid the development of strong "goaty" flavours from free fatty acids.
