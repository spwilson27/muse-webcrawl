Government of Canada


Parliament Hill, Ottawa.

The Government of Canada (French: gouvernement du Canada) is the federal administration of Canada, a constitutional monarchy and parliamentary democracy. Formally headed by the monarch — the King of Canada, represented by the Governor General — executive authority is exercised in practice by the prime minister and the Cabinet, who are responsible to the elected House of Commons.

Structure

Canada's government has three branches. The legislature, Parliament, consists of the Crown, the appointed Senate and the elected House of Commons. The executive comprises the Privy Council in form, and in practice the Cabinet, supported by federal departments and agencies, Crown corporations and the public service. The judiciary, headed by the Supreme Court of Canada, is independent of the other branches. Powers are divided between the federal government and the ten provinces under the Constitution Act, 1867; three territories exercise delegated authority.

Operation

Following each election, the governor general invites the leader able to command the confidence of the Commons to form a government. Ministers head departments such as Finance, Foreign Affairs (Global Affairs Canada), National Defence and Health. Federal spending, taxation — including the goods and services tax — and programmes such as Old Age Security and Employment Insurance are administered from Ottawa, with services delivered in both official languages, English and French, under the Official Languages Act.
