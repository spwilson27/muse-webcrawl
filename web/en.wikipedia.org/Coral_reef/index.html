A coral reef is an underwater ecosystem characterised by reef-building corals. Reefs are formed of colonies of coral polyps held together by calcium carbonate. Most coral reefs are built from stony corals, whose polyps cluster in groups. Coral belongs to the class Anthozoa in the animal phylum Cnidaria, which includes sea anemones and jellyfish. Unlike sea anemones, corals secrete hard carbonate exoskeletons that support and protect the body. Most reefs grow best in warm, shallow, clear, sunny, and agitated water.

Coral reefs first appeared in the Cambrian period and have been built by successive groups of organisms; modern reefs are dominated by scleractinian corals that arose in the Triassic. Sometimes called "rainforests of the sea", shallow coral reefs form some of Earth's most diverse ecosystems. They occupy less than 0.1% of the world's ocean area, about half the area of France, yet they provide a home for at least 25% of all marine species, including fish, molluscs, worms, crustaceans, echinoderms, sponges, tunicates, and other cnidarians.

Formation and types

Charles Darwin identified three major reef types — fringing reefs, barrier reefs, and atolls — as stages in a sequence driven by the subsidence of volcanic islands. A fringing reef grows directly from an island shore; as the island slowly sinks, a lagoon opens between reef and shore, producing a barrier reef; when the island disappears entirely, the reef ring remains as an atoll enclosing a lagoon. Darwin's subsidence theory, developed during the voyage of HMS Beagle, was confirmed by drilling at Enewetak Atoll in the twentieth century.

Biology

Reef-building corals live in symbiosis with photosynthetic dinoflagellate algae called zooxanthellae, which live in the coral's tissues and supply much of its energy. This partnership constrains most reef corals to sunlit, nutrient-poor tropical waters. Corals reproduce both sexually — often in spectacular synchronised mass-spawning events — and asexually by budding and fragmentation.

Threats

Coral reefs are fragile ecosystems sensitive to water temperature. Rising ocean temperatures cause coral bleaching, in which stressed corals expel their zooxanthellae and may starve. Mass bleaching events on the Great Barrier Reef in 2016 and 2017 killed large areas of shallow-water coral. Ocean acidification, caused by absorption of anthropogenic carbon dioxide, reduces the ability of corals to build their skeletons. Local threats include overfishing, destructive fishing practices, pollution, sedimentation from coastal development, and outbreaks of crown-of-thorns starfish. Projections suggest that even with strong mitigation of climate change, most warm-water reefs will suffer severe declines this century.

Value to humans

Reefs support fisheries that feed hundreds of millions of people, protect coastlines from storm surge and erosion, and underpin major tourism economies. The global economic value of coral reefs has been estimated in the tens of billions of dollars per year. Many reef-derived compounds are used in medicine or are under investigation, including treatments for cancer and viral infections.
