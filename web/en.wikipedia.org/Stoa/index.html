Stoa

A stoa (plural: stoas or stoae), in ancient Greek architecture, is a covered walkway or portico, commonly for public use. Early stoas were open at the entrance with columns, usually of the Doric order, lining the side of the building; they created a safe, enveloping, protective atmosphere. Later stoas were often two-storeyed and housed shops or offices.

History

Stoas surrounded the marketplaces (agoras) of Greek cities and served as places of business, promenading, and philosophical discussion. The most famous is the Stoa Poikile ("Painted Porch") in Athens, decorated with paintings of battles, where Zeno of Citium taught around 300 BC — giving the philosophical school of Stoicism its name.

Notable examples

The Stoa of Attalos in the Agora of Athens, originally built by King Attalos II of Pergamon in the 2nd century BC, was reconstructed in the 1950s and now houses a museum. Other examples include the stoas at Olympia, Delphi, and the South Stoa at Corinth.

Stoicism

The philosophical school founded by Zeno took its name from the Stoa Poikile. See also Stoicism, agora, and Greek architecture.
