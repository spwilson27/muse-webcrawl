Satellite internet constellation

A satellite internet constellation is a network of artificial satellites working together as a system to provide internet access, typically from low Earth orbit (LEO). Unlike traditional geostationary communications satellites, which orbit at about 35,786 km and introduce noticeable latency, constellation satellites orbit at altitudes of roughly 340–2,000 km, reducing signal delay to levels comparable with terrestrial broadband.

History

The concept dates to the 1990s, when ventures such as Iridium, Globalstar and Teledesic proposed large LEO networks, initially for satellite telephony. The bankruptcy of several early ventures around 2000 discouraged investment until the 2010s, when SpaceX announced Starlink in 2015. OneWeb, Amazon's Project Kuiper and Telesat Lightspeed followed, alongside Chinese state-backed systems such as Guowang.

Operation

Constellations require dozens to thousands of satellites so that at least one is always above the horizon for any user. Satellites communicate with ground stations and user terminals, and newer designs use inter-satellite laser links to route traffic in space. Because LEO satellites move rapidly relative to the ground, terminals must hand over connections between satellites every few minutes.

Criticism

Astronomers have raised concerns about light pollution affecting observations, and regulators worry about orbital debris and collision risk as the number of objects in LEO grows. Operators respond with darker satellite coatings, automated collision avoidance and controlled de-orbiting at end of life.
