Duty of care

In tort law, a duty of care is a legal obligation imposed on an individual to exercise reasonable care while performing acts that could foreseeably harm others. It is the first element that must be established in an action for negligence: the claimant must show that the defendant owed them a duty of care, that the duty was breached, and that the breach caused compensable damage.

Development in English law

The modern doctrine derives from the landmark House of Lords decision in Donoghue v Stevenson [1932], in which Lord Atkin formulated the neighbour principle: one must take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions which one can reasonably foresee would be likely to injure persons so closely and directly affected that one ought reasonably to have them in contemplation. The case arose from a decomposed snail allegedly found in a bottle of ginger beer and established a manufacturer's duty to the ultimate consumer.

Later English law organised the inquiry around the three-stage test of Caparo Industries plc v Dickman [1990]: (1) the harm must be reasonably foreseeable; (2) there must be a relationship of proximity between claimant and defendant; and (3) it must be fair, just and reasonable to impose a duty. More recent decisions, such as Robinson v Chief Constable of West Yorkshire [2018], have emphasised applying established categories incrementally and by analogy rather than treating Caparo as a universal test.

Scope

Duties of care arise in well-recognised relationships — doctor and patient, employer and employee, driver and other road users, occupier and visitor — and may be extended by analogy. Pure omissions generally attract no duty absent a special relationship, and public authorities benefit from particular restrictions. In the United States, the analysis was famously reframed by Judge Cardozo in Palsgraf v. Long Island Railroad Co. (1928) around the zone of foreseeable danger, and many jurisdictions use multi-factor tests such as that of Rowland v. Christian in California.
