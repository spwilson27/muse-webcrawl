Qumran

Qumran (Hebrew: קומראן; Arabic: خربة قمران, Khirbet Qumran) is an archaeological site in the West Bank managed by Israel's Qumran National Park. It lies on a dry marl plateau about 1.5 km from the northwestern shore of the Dead Sea. The site was occupied most notably during the Hellenistic and early Roman periods and is best known as the settlement nearest to the Qumran Caves, where the Dead Sea Scrolls were discovered.

Excavations and settlement

Excavations by Roland de Vaux in the 1950s revealed a communal settlement with ritual baths (mikva'ot), a large dining room, a scriptorium with inkwells and plastered tables, a pottery workshop, and a cemetery of over a thousand graves. The settlement was destroyed by the Romans during the First Jewish–Roman War, around AD 68.

The scrolls

Between 1947 and 1956, Bedouin shepherds and archaeologists found some 900 manuscripts in eleven caves near the site — biblical books, sectarian rules such as the Community Rule, commentaries (pesharim), and apocryphal works, dating from roughly the 3rd century BC to the 1st century AD. The scrolls are the oldest surviving group of biblical manuscripts known.

The community

The dominant scholarly view identifies the inhabitants with the Essenes, a Jewish sect described by Pliny the Elder, Josephus, and Philo; some scholars have proposed other interpretations, including a villa or fort. Debate continues over the exact relationship between the settlement and the scrolls.
