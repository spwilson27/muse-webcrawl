Fuging tune

Form of Anglo-American sacred choral music

A fuging tune (or fuguing tune) is a form of sacred choral music, principally of English and early American origin, in which the voices begin together in homophony and then enter imitatively, one part after another, in a brief fugal section before a final chordal conclusion. Despite the name, fuging tunes are not strict fugues; the imitative passage is short and free.

History

The form appeared in English parish church music ("West Gallery music") in the mid-eighteenth century, in tunebooks by composers such as William Tans'ur. It crossed the Atlantic and flourished in New England in the 1770s and 1780s, where William Billings — whose "Chester" and "Jordan" are classic examples — made it the centrepiece of the first American school of composition. Singing masters carried the style south and west, and fuging tunes became a standard component of shape-note tunebooks.

Today

Fuging tunes survive in the living Sacred Harp singing tradition: The Sacred Harp (1844 and later editions) contains many, including "Idumea" and "Wondrous Love"-era pieces, sung by unaccompanied voices in four parts at all-day singings. Composers such as Charles Ives later quoted the idiom as a musical emblem of early America.
