James Scott Skinner (5 August 1843 – 17 March 1927) was a Scottish fiddler, composer and dancing master, known as "The Strathspey King". He was one of the most influential figures in Scottish traditional music, publishing some 600 compositions and making some of the earliest recordings of Scottish fiddle playing.

Early life

Skinner was born in Banchory, Aberdeenshire, the son of a dancing master and fiddler. After his father's death, the family fell on hard times, and at eight James joined Dr. Mark's "Little Men", a troupe of child musicians who toured Britain. He later studied in Aberdeen with the virtuoso Peter Milne, "the Strathspey King", a title he inherited. He also studied cello and classical music and toured as a young man with the orchestra of a music hall.

Career

From the 1870s Skinner worked as an itinerant dancing master across the north-east of Scotland, teaching the quadrilles and country dances then in fashion. His first collection of tunes appeared in 1868; later collections included The Miller o' Hirn Collection (1881) and The Harp and Claymore (1903–04). His best-known compositions include "The Bonnie Lass o' Bon-Accord", "Cradle Song", "The President", "The Miller o' Hirn", "Hector the Hero" (a lament for Sir Hector MacDonald) and "The Rosebud of Allenvale". His playing combined classical technique with the traditional Northeast strathspey style.

Skinner performed before Queen Victoria at Balmoral in 1868 and toured the United States in 1893. In 1899 he made some of the first recordings ever of Scottish traditional music, and later recorded extensively for Columbia in the 1910s and 1920s.

Death and legacy

Skinner died in Aberdeen in 1927; his funeral drew thousands of mourners, and a monument was erected to him at Allenvale Cemetery. His manuscripts and recordings remain central to the repertoire of Scottish fiddlers.
