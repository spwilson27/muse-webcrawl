Transuranium element

The transuranium elements (also known as transuranic elements) are the chemical elements with atomic numbers greater than 92, which is the atomic number of uranium. All are unstable and decay radioactively into other elements; none occur naturally on Earth in significant quantities (trace amounts of neptunium and plutonium occur from neutron capture in uranium ores), so all were first produced artificially in laboratories and nuclear reactors.

List and synthesis

The series begins with neptunium (93) and plutonium (94), identified in 1940–41 by Edwin McMillan, Glenn T. Seaborg and co-workers at Berkeley. Elements through fermium (100) can be produced by neutron capture in reactors or nuclear explosions; heavier elements are synthesised atom-by-atom by bombarding targets with accelerated ions. The heaviest recognised element is oganesson (118), completing the seventh period of the periodic table; its synthesis was reported by the Joint Institute for Nuclear Research in Dubna and Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory.

Properties and applications

The early transuranium elements up to californium (98) have practical uses: plutonium-239 is a fissile fuel and weapons material; americium-241 is used in smoke detectors; californium-252 is a strong neutron source. Heavier elements are produced only in atom-at-a-time quantities and are of purely scientific interest, used to test theories of nuclear structure, including the predicted "island of stability" of relatively long-lived superheavy nuclei.

Nomenclature

Several discoveries were contested during the Cold War — the so-called "Transfermium Wars" — between groups in Berkeley, Dubna and Darmstadt; the disputes were resolved by IUPAC/IUPAP joint committees in the 1990s, with elements 104–109 receiving agreed names such as rutherfordium, dubnium, seaborgium and hassium.
