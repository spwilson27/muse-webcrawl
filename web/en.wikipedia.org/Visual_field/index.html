Visual field

The visual field is the "spatial array of visual sensations available to observation" in a person's vision — in practical terms, the entire area that can be seen when the eye is directed forward, including peripheral vision. For a single human eye the normal field extends roughly 100 degrees temporally (toward the ear), 60 degrees nasally, 60 degrees upward and 75 degrees downward. Together the two eyes cover about 200 degrees horizontally, with a central region of binocular overlap where depth perception is possible.

Measurement

Visual fields are measured clinically by perimetry: the patient fixates centrally while targets are presented at various positions and brightnesses, mapping the sensitivity of each point in the field. The physiological blind spot, where the optic nerve leaves the retina, lies about 15 degrees on the temporal side of fixation. Central acuity is sharpest at the fovea and falls off steeply with eccentricity.

Visual field defects

Disease at different points along the visual pathway produces characteristic patterns of field loss, which clinicians use for localization. Retinal and optic nerve disorders cause monocular defects; glaucoma classically produces arcuate scotomas and nasal steps. Damage at the optic chiasm, often from a pituitary tumour, causes bitemporal hemianopia — loss of the outer half of each eye's field. Lesions behind the chiasm, in the optic radiations or visual cortex, cause homonymous defects affecting the same side of the field in both eyes.
