Liquorice (British English) or licorice (American English) is the common name of Glycyrrhiza glabra, a flowering plant in the bean family Fabaceae, and of the sweet, aromatic flavouring extracted from its roots. The root contains glycyrrhizin, a compound some 30–50 times sweeter than sugar. Liquorice is used in confectionery (often combined with aniseed flavour), tobacco, drinks, herbal teas and traditional medicine.

The plant

Glycyrrhiza glabra is a herbaceous perennial up to about 1 metre tall, native to Western Asia, North Africa and Southern Europe. It spreads by long stolons and deep roots, which are harvested, dried, and boiled down to produce a concentrated extract that solidifies into sticks or blocks. Other species, including Chinese liquorice (G. uralensis), are used similarly; in traditional Chinese medicine liquorice root (gāncǎo) is among the most common ingredients.

Uses

In the Netherlands, Scandinavia and Finland, salty liquorice (salmiakki) flavoured with ammonium chloride is especially popular. Pontefract in Yorkshire became an English centre of liquorice cultivation and of the "Pomfret cakes" stamped with the castle. Liquorice flavours liqueurs such as sambuca and pastis (alongside anise), soft drinks, and chewing tobacco. In herbal medicine it has been used for coughs, sore throats and digestive complaints since antiquity — it was found in Tutankhamun's tomb.

Health effects

Excessive consumption of glycyrrhizin can cause pseudoaldosteronism: it inhibits the enzyme that protects mineralocorticoid receptors from cortisol, leading to sodium retention, potassium loss, high blood pressure and oedema. Health authorities advise moderation, particularly for people with hypertension. Deglycyrrhizinated liquorice (DGL) preparations remove most of the glycyrrhizin.
