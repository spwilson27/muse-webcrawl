Biblical apocrypha

The biblical apocrypha (from Greek apokrypha, "hidden things") denotes the books found in the Greek Septuagint and Latin Vulgate but absent from the Hebrew Bible, whose canonical status differs among Christian traditions. Roman Catholic and Eastern Orthodox churches regard them as scripture — Catholics call them deuterocanonical — while most Protestant traditions treat them as useful but non-canonical writings.

The books

The apocrypha typically includes Tobit, Judith, the Wisdom of Solomon, Sirach (Ecclesiasticus), Baruch, and 1 and 2 Maccabees, together with Greek additions to Esther and Daniel (the Prayer of Azariah, Susanna, and Bel and the Dragon), and in Orthodox canons additional works such as 3 Maccabees, 1 Esdras, and Psalm 151. These works were mostly composed in the last two centuries BCE and the first century CE.

History of the term and canon

Jerome, translating the Vulgate, distinguished the apocrypha from the Hebrew canon, though the books continued to be included in Latin Bibles. The Council of Trent (1546) affirmed the deuterocanonical books as Catholic scripture. Martin Luther placed them in a separate section of his 1534 Bible as books "not held equal to the Holy Scriptures, but useful and good to read"; the King James Version of 1611 included them between the Testaments, a practice largely dropped by 19th-century Bible societies.

Other uses of "apocrypha"

The word also covers a far wider penumbra of writings — New Testament apocrypha such as the Gospel of Thomas and the Infancy Gospels — which no major church includes in its canon. Scholars increasingly prefer "deuterocanon" and "pseudepigrapha" to avoid the pejorative tone of "apocryphal".
