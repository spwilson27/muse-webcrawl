The Apprentice Boys of Derry is a Protestant fraternal society based in the city of Derry in Northern Ireland, founded in 1814. It commemorates the actions of thirteen young apprentices who, in December 1688, shut the gates of the city against the Jacobite army of James II, and the subsequent Siege of Derry of 1689, during which the Williamite garrison and citizens held out until relieved.

History

The society takes its name from the thirteen apprentices who seized the keys of the city gates and locked them against a regiment sent by the Earl of Tyrconnell. The siege that followed lasted 105 days, with thousands inside the walls dying of hunger and disease before relief ships broke the boom across the River Foyle in July 1689. The first parent club, the Apprentice Boys of Derry Club, was established in 1814, and the organisation grew into a network of parent clubs and branch clubs across Northern Ireland, the Republic of Ireland, Scotland, England, Canada and Australia.

Parades

The society's two principal commemorations are the "Shutting of the Gates" parade on the first Saturday in December and the "Relief of Derry" parade on the second Saturday in August, the latter being one of the largest loyalist parades in Northern Ireland, drawing dozens of bands and thousands of participants. During the Troubles the August parade was a frequent flashpoint for disorder; since the late 1990s, negotiation and the Maiden City Festival have considerably reduced tensions surrounding the events.

Organisation

The society is centred on the Apprentice Boys Memorial Hall on Society Street, within the city walls, which opened in 1877 and houses a museum dedicated to the siege and the organisation's history. Membership is open to Protestant men, and the society is one of the principal loyal orders alongside the Orange Order and the Royal Black Institution.
