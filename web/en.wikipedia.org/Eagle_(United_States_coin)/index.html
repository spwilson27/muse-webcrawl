The eagle was a United States gold coin with a face value of ten dollars, authorised by the Coinage Act of 1792 and minted from 1795 until 1933. It was the largest of the regularly issued US denominations until the double eagle ($20) appeared in 1850, and its name — derived from the heraldic eagle on its reverse — became the base unit for a family of coins: the quarter eagle ($2.50), half eagle ($5) and double eagle ($20).

History and designs

The first eagles, struck from 1795, bore Robert Scot's Turban Head (Capped Bust) design. Christian Gobrecht's Liberty Head (Coronet) type followed from 1838 and was produced, with modifications, for nearly seventy years — the longest-running design in the series. In 1907, as part of President Theodore Roosevelt's redesign of American coinage, Augustus Saint-Gaudens's Indian Head eagle replaced it and was minted until regular gold coinage ended in 1933, when the United States abandoned the gold standard and recalled its gold coins under President Franklin D. Roosevelt.

Eagles contained 0.4837 troy ounces of gold at the standard prevailing for most of their later history (16.718 g at 90% fineness after 1837). Because so many were melted in 1933–34, surviving high-grade examples of certain dates are major rarities.

Modern usage

The name was revived in 1986 for the American Gold Eagle bullion coin programme, in which "eagle" denotes the one-ounce coin and fractional pieces descend from the old denomination names; American Eagle coins are also struck in silver, platinum and palladium. The Saint-Gaudens double eagle design, regarded as among the most beautiful of US coins, was adapted for the modern bullion series.
