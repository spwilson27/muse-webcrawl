Ten Plagues

The Ten Plagues (Hebrew: עשר המכות, Eser HaMakkot), also known as the Plagues of Egypt, are disasters inflicted on Egypt by the God of Israel in the Book of Exodus, chapters 7–12, in order to convince the Pharaoh to allow the Israelites to depart from slavery in Egypt. They are recited annually at the Passover Seder.

The plagues

The plagues, in order, are: (1) water turning to blood; (2) frogs; (3) lice (or gnats); (4) flies (or wild animals); (5) pestilence on livestock; (6) boils; (7) a thunderstorm of hail and fire; (8) locusts; (9) darkness for three days; and (10) the death of the firstborn. After each of the first nine plagues Pharaoh relents and then hardens his heart, or has his heart hardened; only after the tenth plague does he release the Israelites, leading to the Exodus and the crossing of the Red Sea.

Interpretations

Scholars debate the historicity of the account and the composition of the Exodus narrative; proposed natural explanations link sequences of the plagues to ecological events such as red algal blooms in the Nile, volcanic fallout from the eruption of Thera, or climate anomalies. Within Jewish and Christian tradition the plagues are central to the theology of liberation celebrated at Passover.
