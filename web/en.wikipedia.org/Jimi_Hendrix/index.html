Jimi Hendrix

James Marshall "Jimi" Hendrix (born Johnny Allen Hendrix; November 27, 1942 – September 18, 1970) was an American guitarist, songwriter and singer. He is widely regarded as the greatest guitarist in the history of popular music and one of the most influential musicians of the 20th century. The Rock and Roll Hall of Fame describes him as "arguably the greatest instrumentalist in the history of rock music."

Early life and career

Born in Seattle, Washington, Hendrix began playing guitar at the age of 15. After a brief stint in the U.S. Army as a paratrooper in the 101st Airborne Division, he worked as a sideman for acts including the Isley Brothers, Little Richard and Curtis Knight. In 1966, animal manager Chas Chandler brought him to London, where he formed the Jimi Hendrix Experience with bassist Noel Redding and drummer Mitch Mitchell.

Success

The Experience released three studio albums — Are You Experienced (1967), Axis: Bold as Love (1967) and Electric Ladyland (1968) — containing songs such as "Purple Haze", "The Wind Cries Mary", "Foxy Lady", "Fire" and his celebrated cover of Bob Dylan's "All Along the Watchtower". At the Monterey Pop Festival in 1967 he famously set his guitar on fire, and in 1969 he headlined Woodstock, where his rendition of "The Star-Spangled Banner" became an iconic moment of the era.

Death and legacy

Hendrix died in London in 1970 at the age of 27, from asphyxia related to barbiturate intoxication. Despite a mainstream career of only four years, his innovative use of feedback, distortion, and the wah-wah pedal, and his virtuoso technique, profoundly shaped rock, blues, funk and heavy metal guitar playing.
