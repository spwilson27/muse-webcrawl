Oasis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In ecology, an oasis is a fertile area in a desert or semi-desert environment, usually where water is present at or near the surface — from springs, seasonal watercourses, or groundwater in aquifers — allowing plants and animals to thrive amid otherwise arid surroundings. Oases range from small palm-fringed pools to large agricultural regions such as the Nile Valley margins and the Al-Hasa oasis in Saudi Arabia, one of the largest in the world.

Formation

Oases form where wind deflation lowers the land surface to the water table, where faults or impermeable rock layers force groundwater upward, or where runoff from distant mountains collects. Humans have long enlarged and maintained oases through wells, qanats, and irrigation, planting date palms above crops to shade them.

Importance

Historically, oases were vital staging points on desert trade routes, including the trans-Saharan caravans and the Silk Road, providing water, food and rest. They remain crucial for desert agriculture and settlement, though over-extraction of groundwater threatens many of them today.
