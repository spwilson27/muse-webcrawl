Willem Barentsz (c. 1550 – 20 June 1597), anglicized as William Barents or Barentz, was a Dutch navigator, cartographer and Arctic explorer. He led three expeditions in search of a Northeast Passage to Asia and is regarded as one of the greatest early polar explorers. The Barents Sea, Barents Island and the settlement of Barentsburg on Svalbard are named after him.

Expeditions

Barentsz, a skilled cartographer who had sailed the Mediterranean with Petrus Plancius, was appointed chief pilot and navigator of the Amsterdam expeditions. On the first voyage (1594) he reached Novaya Zemlya; on the second (1595) the fleet was blocked by ice in the Kara Sea. On the third voyage (1596), sailing with Jacob van Heemskerck and Jan Rijp, the expedition discovered Bear Island and Spitsbergen before Barentsz's ship rounded the northern tip of Novaya Zemlya and became trapped in the ice.

Wintering and death

The seventeen men built a wooden lodge, Het Behouden Huys ("The Saved House"), and endured the polar winter of 1596–97 — the first recorded European wintering in the high Arctic. Gerrit de Veer's diary describes the cold, scurvy and polar bear attacks. When the ship remained icebound, the crew escaped in open boats in June 1597; Barentsz died at sea on 20 June 1597. Twelve survivors eventually reached the Kola Peninsula and were taken home by Rijp's ship.

Legacy

Het Behouden Huys was rediscovered in 1871, its relics — including a clock, books and navigational instruments — now held chiefly by the Rijksmuseum in Amsterdam. Barentsz's voyages provided the first reliable charts of the Novaya Zemlya region.
