The Ancient Greek language distinguishes several senses in which the word "love" can be used. Ancient Greek has the words philia, eros, storge and agape, and a number of other terms, for the variety of relationships and affections that English covers with the single word "love." These distinctions have been widely discussed in philosophy and theology, and were popularised in English by C. S. Lewis in his book The Four Loves (1960).

The principal terms

Agape

Agape (ἀγάπη, agápē) was comparatively rare in classical Greek literature, but became central in the Septuagint and the New Testament, where it denotes selfless, sacrificial, unconditional love — most famously in 1 Corinthians 13 ("love is patient, love is kind"). In Christian theology it is distinguished from eros and philia as the highest form of love, directed even toward enemies. The term also gave its name to the agape feast, the communal meal of early Christians.

Eros

Eros (ἔρως) is passionate desire and longing, often but not exclusively sexual. In Plato's Symposium and Phaedrus, eros is treated philosophically as a yearning for beauty itself which, beginning with attraction to a particular person, can ascend — by the so-called "ladder of love" — to contemplation of the Form of Beauty. From this derives the notion of "Platonic love." The word survives in English in terms such as "erotic."

Philia

Philia (φιλία) is the affection found in friendship, family bonds and civic life: a dispassionate, virtuous love between equals. Aristotle devoted two books of the Nicomachean Ethics to analysing philia, dividing it into friendships of utility, of pleasure, and of virtue. Philia appears in many English compound words, such as philosophy ("love of wisdom"), philanthropy and bibliophile.

Storge

Storge (στοργή) is natural, instinctive affection, paradigmatically the love of parents for their offspring. Unlike eros and philia, it does not depend on the beloved's qualities or on choice; it can also describe the affection that grows between spouses in a long marriage, or the tolerant acceptance of a situation one cannot change.

Other terms

  • Philautia (φιλαυτία): self-love, which Aristotle and later writers divided into a healthy self-regard and a vice of selfishness.
  • Xenia (ξενία): the ritualised guest–friendship of the ancient world, the bond of hospitality between host and stranger, sacred to Zeus Xenios.
  • Pothos (πόθος): longing for what is absent or unattainable.
  • Himeros (ἵμερος): pressing, impulsive desire.
  • Anteros (ἀντέρως): "love returned," requited love, personified as a brother of Eros.

Influence

The Greek distinctions have been used by philosophers, theologians and psychologists to analyse love's varieties. Anders Nygren's Agape and Eros (1930s) contrasted the two as rival conceptions of love. John Alan Lee's "colours of love" theory (1973) adapted Greek and Latin terms — eros, ludus, storge, pragma, mania, agape — into a typology of loving styles used in relationship psychology.
