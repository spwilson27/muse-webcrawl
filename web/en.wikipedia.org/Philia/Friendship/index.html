Friendship is a relationship of mutual affection between people. It is a stronger form of interpersonal bond than an association or acquaintance, characterised by trust, empathy, mutual support, shared interests and the enjoyment of one another's company. The study of friendship is part of sociology, social psychology, anthropology and philosophy.

In the ancient Greek world, friendship was understood through the concept of philia (φιλία), one of the several Greek words for love. Philia denotes a dispassionate, virtuous affection found in friendships, family bonds and civic loyalty, in contrast to eros (passionate desire) and agape (unconditional, selfless love).

Aristotle's account

The most influential early analysis of friendship is found in Books VIII and IX of Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics. Aristotle argued that philia is necessary for the good life and distinguished three kinds of friendship based on their object:

  • Friendships of utility, based on mutual advantage; they last only as long as the benefit does.
  • Friendships of pleasure, based on shared enjoyment or activity; common among the young, they dissolve when tastes change.
  • Friendships of virtue (or of the good), in which each friend loves the other for who they are and wishes them well for their own sake. These require time, trust and equality, and are rare but enduring.

Aristotle held that the friend is "another self," and that complete friendship is possible only between good people who are alike in virtue. He also treated civic friendship — concord, or homonoia — as the bond that holds a political community together.

Later philosophy

Cicero's dialogue De Amicitia (On Friendship, 44 BC) celebrated friendship between virtuous men as one of life's greatest goods. In the Renaissance, Montaigne's essay "Of Friendship" described his bond with Étienne de La Boétie in terms that resisted analysis: "Because it was he, because it was I." Immanuel Kant distinguished friendships of taste, of need and of disposition, and treated friendship as a union of love and respect. In the twentieth century, C. S. Lewis's The Four Loves (1960) revived the Greek distinctions, describing friendship as the least "natural" of the loves because it is chosen rather than instinctive.

Developmental psychology

Friendships develop across the lifespan. Young children's friendships centre on shared play and proximity; adolescents form intense, intimate friendships important to identity formation; adults typically maintain smaller networks of close friends alongside wider circles of acquaintances. Research associated with Dunbar's number suggests humans can maintain roughly 150 stable social relationships, with a much smaller inner circle of close friends. Friendship quality in childhood and adolescence is associated with later well-being, and in older adults friendship is linked to life satisfaction and cognitive health.

Social and cultural aspects

Norms governing friendship vary across cultures: expectations of loyalty, self-disclosure, gift-giving and obligation differ widely, and in many societies kinship-like obligations extend to close friends. In the twenty-first century, social media has reshaped how friendships are formed and maintained, enabling contact across distance while raising questions about depth, comparison and online-only relationships.

Health

Strong friendships are associated with lower rates of depression and anxiety, better cardiovascular outcomes and increased longevity. Conversely, loneliness and social isolation are risk factors comparable in magnitude to smoking and obesity in some analyses, which has led public-health bodies to treat social connection, including friendship, as a health priority.
