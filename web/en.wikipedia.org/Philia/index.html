Philia

Philia denotes affectionate regard and friendship in Greek thought.

Philia (Ancient Greek: φιλία) is one of the four ancient Greek words for love: philia, storge, agape and eros. In Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics, philia is usually translated "friendship" or affection. The complete opposite is called a phobia.

Philia is a dispassionate virtuous love, a concept addressed and developed by Aristotle in Book VIII and IX of the Nicomachean Ethics. It includes loyalty to friends (specifically, "brotherly love"), family, and community, and requires virtue, equality, and familiarity. Philia is motivated by practical reasons; one or both of the parties benefit from the relationship.

Aristotle's three kinds of friendship

Aristotle divides friendship into three types, based on the motive for its formation:

  • Friendships of utility — relationships formed without the foundation of mutual affection, for mutual benefit, as among business partners; they dissolve when the benefit ends.
  • Friendships of pleasure — relationships based on shared enjoyment or activity, common among the young; they last while the pleasure lasts.
  • Friendships of the good — perfect friendship, between virtuous people who wish each other well for the other's own sake; rare, slow to form, and enduring.

For Aristotle, self-love properly understood is the model for philia: a good person is a friend to himself and therefore can extend that goodwill to another "as to another self." Philia also binds together the polis: Aristotle held that friendship is a greater bond of the city than justice alone.

Later usage

The term appears in English as a suffix, -philia, denoting fondness or attraction (as in "bibliophilia"), and as its opposite -phobia. C. S. Lewis discussed philia in The Four Loves (1960) as the least "natural" and most freely chosen of the loves.
