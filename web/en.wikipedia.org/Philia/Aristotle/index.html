Aristotle (384–322 BC) was an Ancient Greek philosopher and polymath. His writings cover a broad range of subjects spanning the natural sciences, philosophy, linguistics, economics, politics, psychology and the arts. As the founder of the Peripatetic school of philosophy in the Lyceum in Athens, he began the wider Aristotelian tradition that followed, which set the groundwork for the development of modern science.

Born in Stagira in northern Greece, Aristotle joined Plato's Academy in Athens at the age of seventeen and remained there for twenty years. After Plato's death he left Athens and, at the request of Philip II of Macedon, tutored his son Alexander the Great. In 335 BC he returned to Athens and founded his own school, the Lyceum, where he taught for twelve years before fleeing to Chalcis, where he died the following year.

Life

Aristotle was born in the city of Stagira in the Chalcidice region of northern Greece. His father, Nicomachus, was the court physician to King Amyntas of Macedon. Orphaned young, Aristotle travelled to Athens in 367 BC and became a student of Plato. After Plato's death in 347 BC, Aristotle travelled with Xenocrates to the court of Hermias of Atarneus in Asia Minor, and later settled in Mytilene on Lesbos. In 343 BC Philip II summoned him to Macedonia to tutor the young prince Alexander.

Returning to Athens in 335 BC, Aristotle established the Lyceum, a school whose members were known as Peripatetics, allegedly because of their habit of walking while discussing philosophy. In 323 BC, following the death of Alexander, anti-Macedonian feeling in Athens led to a charge of impiety against Aristotle; recalling the fate of Socrates, he fled to Chalcis on Euboea, where he died in 322 BC.

Logic

Aristotle is credited with the earliest formal study of logic. The collection of his logical writings, later known as the Organon, introduced the syllogism — a form of deductive reasoning in which a conclusion is drawn from two premises — and dominated logical thought for more than two thousand years. His theory of the syllogism, set out in the Prior Analytics, analysed arguments in terms of propositions connected by terms, and laid the foundations of formal logic later developed by medieval scholastics and modern logicians.

Natural philosophy and biology

Aristotle's works on natural science include the Physics, On the Heavens, Meteorology and an extensive body of biological treatises such as the History of Animals, Parts of Animals and Generation of Animals. He classified more than 500 animal species, dissecting many of them, and made observations — for example on the embryology of chickens and the anatomy of octopuses — that were not bettered for centuries. His explanatory framework rested on four causes: the material, formal, efficient and final causes of a thing.

Metaphysics

In the Metaphysics, Aristotle investigated being as being, substance, form and matter, and the principles of non-contradiction and excluded middle. He argued that change is the actualisation of potentiality, and posited an "unmoved mover" — a first cause of motion in the universe — as pure actuality and thought thinking itself.

Ethics and friendship

Aristotle's ethical writings, principally the Nicomachean Ethics and the Eudaimonian Ethics, present a virtue ethics centred on eudaimonia, usually translated as flourishing or happiness, achieved through the habitual practice of moral and intellectual virtues according to reason. Virtue is described as a mean between two vices, one of excess and one of deficiency.

Books VIII and IX of the Nicomachean Ethics contain an influential analysis of philia (φιλία), often translated as friendship. Aristotle distinguished three kinds of friendship: friendships of utility, friendships of pleasure, and complete friendships of virtue, in which each person wishes good to the other for the other's own sake. Complete friendship, he held, is rare, enduring, and essential to the good life, since "no one would choose to live without friends, even if he had all other goods."

Politics and rhetoric

In the Politics, Aristotle classified constitutions, defended the polis as the natural community in which human beings — "political animals" — realise their nature, and argued for the rule of the middle class as a stabilising force. His Rhetoric analysed persuasion through the three modes of ethos, pathos and logos, while the Poetics examined tragedy, mimesis and catharsis, remaining a foundational text of literary theory.

Legacy

Aristotle's writings were preserved and transmitted through Hellenistic, Roman, Byzantine, Syriac and Arabic scholarship. Commentators such as Alexander of Aphrodisias, Averroes and Thomas Aquinas shaped their reception, and Aristotelian natural philosophy structured university curricula into the early modern period. Although parts of his physics and cosmology were displaced by the Scientific Revolution, his logic, ethics and biology continue to be studied, and modern virtue ethics draws directly on his work.
