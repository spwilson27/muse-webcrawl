The Nicomachean Ethics (Ancient Greek: Ἠθικὰ Νικομάχεια, Ēthika Nikomacheia) is Aristotle's best-known work on ethics. Written in the fourth century BC and comprising ten books, it is a philosophical inquiry into the nature of the good life for human beings. The title is believed to derive from Aristotle's son Nicomachus, to whom the work may have been dedicated or who may have edited it after his father's death.

The work plays a pre-eminent role in defining Aristotelian ethics and virtue ethics generally, and remains one of the most influential texts in moral philosophy. It closely overlaps with parts of the Eudemian Ethics, Aristotle's other surviving ethical treatise.

Book I: the human good

Aristotle begins by observing that every art, inquiry, action and choice aims at some good. Reasoning that chains of ends must terminate in an end desired for its own sake, he identifies the highest human good as eudaimonia — usually translated as "happiness" or "flourishing" — and argues in the famous "function argument" that the human good consists in activity of the soul in accordance with virtue, or reason, over a complete lifetime.

Books II–V: virtue

Virtue of character arises from habit, not nature: "we become just by doing just acts, temperate by doing temperate acts, brave by doing brave acts." Virtue is a settled disposition to choose the mean — the intermediate between excess and deficiency relative to us — as determined by reason. Aristotle then examines individual virtues: courage, temperance, liberality, magnificence, magnanimity, gentleness, truthfulness and wit, and in Book V gives an extended analysis of justice.

Book VI: intellectual virtue

Book VI distinguishes the intellectual virtues, among them techne (craft knowledge), episteme (scientific knowledge) and phronesis — practical wisdom, the capacity to deliberate well about what is good for human beings in particular situations. Phronesis links moral virtue to right action: virtue makes the goal right, while practical wisdom finds the means.

Book VII: akrasia and pleasure

Book VII treats akrasia, incontinence or weakness of will — knowing the good but failing to do it — and gives a first discussion of pleasure, arguing against both hedonism and the view that pleasure is never a good.

Books VIII–IX: friendship

Two books are devoted to philia, friendship, which Aristotle calls "most necessary with a view to living." He distinguishes friendships based on utility, on pleasure, and on virtue. Only the third — between good people who love each other for their character and wish each other well for the other's own sake — is complete friendship. Aristotle discusses why the happy person needs friends, whether one should love oneself, and the role of concord (homonoia) as political friendship binding a city together.

Book X: pleasure and contemplation

The final book returns to pleasure, arguing that pleasure completes virtuous activity "as the bloom of youth completes the young." Aristotle concludes that the highest happiness is the activity of contemplation (theoria), the exercise of the intellect — the most divine element in us — and closes with a transition to politics, noting that ethics requires the support of good laws and education, the subject of his Politics.

Influence

Transmitted through late-antique commentary, Syriac and Arabic philosophy and Latin scholasticism, the Nicomachean Ethics became the core ethics text of medieval universities; Thomas Aquinas wrote a major commentary on it. In the twentieth century, G. E. M. Anscombe's "Modern Moral Philosophy" (1958) and the work of Alasdair MacIntyre helped revive it as the founding text of modern virtue ethics.
