Leonard Hayflick

Leonard Hayflick (born 20 May 1928) is an American anatomist and gerontologist, professor of anatomy at the University of California, San Francisco. He is best known for discovering that normal human cells divide only a limited number of times in culture — a phenomenon known as the Hayflick limit — which overturned the long-standing belief that cultured cells are inherently immortal.

The Hayflick limit

Working at the Wistar Institute in Philadelphia in the early 1960s with Paul Moorhead, Hayflick showed that human fetal fibroblasts divide roughly 40 to 60 times before entering a non-dividing state he called replicative senescence. The finding, published in 1961 and 1965, was initially controversial but became foundational to the biology of ageing. The limit was later explained mechanistically by the progressive shortening of telomeres at each cell division.

Cell lines and controversy

Hayflick also developed the WI-38 human diploid cell strain, used worldwide in the production of vaccines against polio, measles, rubella, rabies and other diseases. His dispute with the National Institutes of Health over ownership of the WI-38 cells became a landmark case in the commercialisation of biological research materials.

Later work

Hayflick served as president of the Gerontological Society of America and wrote extensively on the distinction between ageing and longevity determination, arguing in his book How and Why We Age (1994) that ageing is an inevitable increase in molecular disorder distinct from disease.
