Alfred Stieglitz (1864–1946) was an American photographer and modern-art promoter who was instrumental in establishing photography as a recognised art form and in introducing European avant-garde art to the United States.

Career

Stieglitz studied photography in Berlin in the 1880s and returned to New York, where his urban scenes, such as The Steerage (1907), became landmarks of photographic modernism. He founded the Photo-Secession group and the journal Camera Work (1903–1917). At his "291" gallery (the Little Galleries of the Photo-Secession) he gave American debuts to Matisse, Cézanne, Picasso, and Duchamp, and championed American modernists including Marsden Hartley, John Marin, and Arthur Dove.

Later work

From 1918 he made hundreds of portraits of his wife, the painter Georgia O'Keeffe, and later the celebrated Equivalents series of cloud photographs, meant as equivalents of inner emotional states. He continued to promote modern art at his later galleries, the Intimate Gallery and An American Place, until his death in 1946.
