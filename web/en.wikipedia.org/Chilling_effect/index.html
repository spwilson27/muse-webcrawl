Chilling effect

In a legal context, a chilling effect is the inhibition or discouragement of the legitimate exercise of natural and legal rights by threat of legal sanction. The term is most often applied to freedom of speech: vague or overbroad laws, or the threat of costly litigation, may cause speakers to self-censor even protected expression.

Origins and doctrine

The phrase came into use in United States constitutional law in the 1950s. In Wieman v. Updegraff (1952), Justice Felix Frankfurter referred to the "chilling effect" of loyalty oath requirements. The concept underlies the overbreadth and vagueness doctrines in First Amendment law, and it influenced decisions such as Dombrowski v. Pfister (1965) and Lamont v. Postmaster General (1965).

Applications

Chilling effects are argued in cases involving libel law (where the cost of defending even truthful statements deters publication), anti-SLAPP statutes designed to counter lawsuits meant to silence critics, surveillance programs that discourage association or inquiry, and copyright enforcement practices such as abusive takedown notices.

Criticism

Some scholars argue the concept is invoked too broadly, that any regulation may deter some conduct, and that courts should require concrete evidence of deterrence rather than speculation.
