Atticus Finch is a fictional character in Harper Lee's Pulitzer Prize-winning novel To Kill a Mockingbird (1960). A widowed lawyer in the fictional Alabama town of Maycomb during the Great Depression, he raises his children, Jem and Scout (the novel's narrator), while serving in the state legislature. Lee modelled him partly on her own father, Amasa Coleman Lee, a lawyer and newspaper editor.

Role in the novel

Atticus is appointed to defend Tom Robinson, a Black man falsely accused of raping a white woman, Mayella Ewell. Despite hostility from much of the town and threats against his family, Atticus mounts a principled defence that exposes the accusation's falsehood — yet the all-white jury convicts Robinson, who is later killed. Atticus teaches his children empathy and moral courage, in lines that have become famous: "You never really understand a person until you consider things from his point of view… until you climb into his skin and walk around in it", and his definition of courage as knowing you're "licked before you begin but you begin anyway".

Cultural impact

Gregory Peck's portrayal in the 1962 film adaptation won the Academy Award for Best Actor, and in 2003 the American Film Institute named Atticus Finch the greatest hero in American cinema. He long served as a moral exemplar for the legal profession. The publication of Lee's earlier draft, Go Set a Watchman (2015), which depicts an older Atticus holding segregationist views, sparked widespread debate and complicated his legacy, prompting reassessment of the character as a more flawed, period-bound figure.
