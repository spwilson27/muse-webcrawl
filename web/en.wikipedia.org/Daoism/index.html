Daoism

Daoism (also spelled Taoism) is a Chinese philosophical and religious tradition emphasising living in harmony with the Dao (道, "the Way") — the ineffable source and pattern of the universe. Its roots lie in the Warring States period (4th–3rd centuries BCE), in texts such as the Tao Te Ching, attributed to Laozi, and the Zhuangzi. Alongside Confucianism and Buddhism, it is one of the three great teachings of China.

Core ideas

The Dao cannot be defined: "The Dao that can be told is not the eternal Dao." Human flourishing comes from wu wei — effortless action aligned with the natural order rather than forced striving — and from ziran, "self-so-ness" or spontaneity. The Tao Te Ching praises softness and yielding (water overcoming stone), humility, simplicity (pu, the uncarved block) and the reversal of opposites embodied in yin and yang. Zhuangzi's writings add playful scepticism about language, social convention and the boundary between self and world, as in his famous butterfly dream.

Religious Daoism

From the 2nd century CE, Daoism developed organised religious forms. Zhang Daoling founded the Way of the Celestial Masters, claiming revelation from the deified Laozi. Later schools — Shangqing and Lingbao — elaborated pantheons, rituals, alchemy and longevity practices, including meditation, breath control, dietetics and the search for immortality through internal alchemy (neidan). The Daoist canon, the Daozang, was compiled in successive editions. Quanzhen Daoism, founded by Wang Chongyang in the 12th century, emphasises monastic discipline and still thrives today.

Influence

Daoism shaped Chinese medicine, martial arts (tai chi), feng shui, poetry and landscape painting, and spread to Korea, Japan and Vietnam. In the West, translations of the Tao Te Ching — among the most translated books in the world — have made Daoist ideas influential in philosophy, environmental ethics and popular spirituality.
