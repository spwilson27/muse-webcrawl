Poverty Point

Poverty Point (French: Pointe de Pauvreté) is a prehistoric earthwork complex constructed by the Poverty Point culture, a group of Native Americans living during the Late Archaic period in North America. The site, located in present-day northeastern Louisiana near the village of Epps, consists of concentric earthen ridges and several large mounds built between about 1700 and 1100 BC, around 3,400 years ago.

Description

The core of the site covers about 163 hectares (400 acres) and includes six concentric, C-shaped earthen ridges arranged around a central plaza, opening toward the Mississippi River floodplain to the east, together with several mounds. The largest, Mound A—sometimes called the Bird Mound for its bird-like shape seen from the air—rises about 21 metres (70 feet) high and measures roughly 210 by 195 metres (690 by 640 feet) at its base. Building the earthworks required moving an estimated 750,000 cubic metres of soil, likely basket by basket.

The site's builders were hunter-fisher-gatherers rather than agriculturalists, which makes the scale of the construction remarkable: monumental earthworks are more often associated with farming societies. Artefacts found at the site, including stone sourced from distant regions of eastern North America, indicate a vast trade network. Fired earth objects known as Poverty Point Objects were used as heating elements for cooking.

Preservation

Poverty Point is a Louisiana state park (Poverty Point State Historic Site) and a National Historic Landmark. In 2014 it was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List as "Monumental Earthworks of Poverty Point", recognized as an outstanding example of earthwork construction by a hunter-gatherer society and for its sophisticated engineering and astronomical alignments.
