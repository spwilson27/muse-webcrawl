Ferrite (magnet)

A ferrite magnet is a ceramic compound consisting of iron(III) oxide (Fe2O3) combined with metallic elements such as strontium, barium, or lead, most commonly strontium ferrite (SrFe12O19) or barium ferrite (BaFe12O19). Ferrite magnets were developed in the 1950s by Philips and are the most widely used type of permanent magnet in the world by volume, because of their low cost, although their magnetic strength is lower than that of rare-earth magnets.

Properties

Ferrites are hard, brittle, dark grey or black ceramics. They are ferrimagnetic, electrically insulating (which suppresses eddy currents), chemically stable, and highly resistant to corrosion and demagnetization. Their maximum energy product is roughly one tenth that of neodymium magnets, but they retain their magnetism at higher temperatures and cost far less. "Soft" ferrites — chemically related spinel compounds such as manganese-zinc and nickel-zinc ferrites — are not permanent magnets but are used extensively as cores for transformers, inductors, antennas, and electromagnetic interference suppression (ferrite beads).

Production and uses

Ferrite magnets are made by powder metallurgy: the raw oxides are calcined, milled, pressed (often in a magnetic field to orient the grains), and sintered. Applications include refrigerator seals and novelty magnets, loudspeakers, small electric motors, magnetic separators, holding and lifting assemblies, and microwave devices exploiting ferrite non-reciprocity, such as circulators and isolators.
