Diabetic foot ulcer

A diabetic foot ulcer is an open sore or wound that occurs in people with diabetes mellitus, typically located on the bottom of the foot. It is one of the most common and serious complications of diabetes, arising from a combination of peripheral neuropathy, peripheral artery disease, foot deformity and repetitive mechanical stress. Foot ulcers precede the majority of diabetes-related lower-limb amputations.

A diabetic foot ulcer typically develops at a pressure point on the sole.

Causes and risk factors

Loss of protective sensation from diabetic peripheral neuropathy means minor trauma goes unnoticed; motor neuropathy causes intrinsic foot muscle wasting and deformities such as claw toes, concentrating pressure; and peripheral artery disease impairs the blood supply needed for healing. Previous ulceration, poor glycaemic control, foot deformity and inappropriate footwear all increase risk.

Classification

The Wagner–Meggitt system grades ulcers from grade 0 (pre-ulcerative lesion) through grade 5 (gangrene of the whole foot). The University of Texas system adds depth and the presence of infection and ischaemia, and the more recent WIfI (Wound, Ischaemia, foot Infection) classification guides amputation-risk assessment.

Treatment

Management centres on offloading pressure (the total contact cast is the reference standard), sharp debridement of necrotic tissue, moist wound care, treatment of infection, revascularisation when ischaemia is present, and control of blood glucose. Prevention through regular foot examination, education and therapeutic footwear substantially reduces incidence.
