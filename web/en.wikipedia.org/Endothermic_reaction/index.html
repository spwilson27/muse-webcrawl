Endothermic reaction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An endothermic reaction is a chemical reaction (or physical process) that absorbs energy from its surroundings, usually in the form of heat. The term, coined by the French chemist Marcellin Berthelot, combines the Greek roots endo-, meaning "inside", and thermē, meaning "heat". The opposite of an endothermic process is an exothermic process, one that releases energy.

Details

For a reaction at constant pressure, the change in enthalpy ΔH is positive for an endothermic reaction: the products have a higher internal energy content than the reactants, the difference being supplied from the surroundings. A familiar demonstration is the instantly cold compress, in which dissolving ammonium nitrate in water absorbs heat and the mixture feels cold. Other examples include photosynthesis, the evaporation of liquids, melting ice, and the thermal decomposition of limestone into quicklime and carbon dioxide.

Whether a reaction proceeds spontaneously depends not on enthalpy alone but on the Gibbs free energy change, ΔG = ΔH − TΔS. Endothermic reactions can be spontaneous when accompanied by a sufficient increase in entropy — as with the melting of ice above 0 °C — and many endothermic reactions are driven simply by supplying heat, as in cooking or in the industrial calcination of limestone.

Examples

  • Photosynthesis: plants absorb sunlight to convert carbon dioxide and water into glucose and oxygen.
  • The reaction of barium hydroxide with ammonium thiocyanate, which can freeze a beaker to a wet board.
  • Electrolysis of water into hydrogen and oxygen.
