Combinatorics is an area of mathematics primarily concerned with counting, both as a means and as an end to obtaining results, and certain properties of finite structures. It is closely related to many other areas of mathematics and has many applications ranging from logic to statistical physics and from evolutionary biology to computer science.

The standard starting points of combinatorics are permutations (ordered arrangements) and combinations (unordered selections), counted using factorials and the binomial coefficient, "n choose k". Pascal's triangle organises the binomial coefficients and connects them to the binomial theorem and to probability, where combinatorial counting underlies the classical theory of games of chance developed by Pascal, Fermat, and Bernoulli.

Branches

  • Enumerative combinatorics counts families of structures, often via generating functions, recurrences, and the twelvefold way.
  • Graph theory studies networks of vertices and edges, with applications from chemistry to social networks.
  • Extremal combinatorics asks how large or small a structure can be while satisfying given conditions (e.g. Ramsey theory, Turán-type problems).
  • Probabilistic combinatorics uses random constructions, following Erdős's probabilistic method, to prove existence results.
  • Algebraic combinatorics brings algebra (representation theory, symmetric functions) to combinatorial problems and vice versa.
  • Design theory studies symmetric arrangements such as Latin squares and block designs, with roots in recreational problems like Kirkman's schoolgirl problem.

History

Combinatorial problems appear in ancient Indian, Chinese, and Greek mathematics — Pingala's work on poetic metres involves binomial coefficients. The subject grew through medieval Islamic and European mathematics, the probability correspondence of Pascal and Fermat (1654), and Leibniz, who coined the term ars combinatoria. In the 20th century, figures such as Paul Erdős and Gian-Carlo Rota transformed combinatorics into a central, unified field, driven in part by the rise of computing and discrete mathematics.

Applications

Combinatorial methods underpin the analysis of algorithms, coding theory, cryptography, scheduling, statistical mechanics, and the study of molecular structures. The exponential growth of combinatorial counts ("combinatorial explosion") is a recurring theme in computer science and optimisation.
