Pothole

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
A water-filled pothole in asphalt pavement.

A pothole is a depression in a road surface, usually in asphalt pavement, where traffic has removed broken pieces of the road material. Potholes form when water seeps into cracks in the surface and the soil beneath, weakening the road structure; the repeated passage of vehicles then breaks up the surface over the softened ground. Freeze–thaw cycles accelerate the process: water in cracks expands as it freezes, heaving and cracking the pavement, and the resulting voids collapse under traffic when the ice melts. Potholes are therefore most common in climates with cold winters and in spring, during the thaw.

Pothole

Primary causes: Water infiltration, freeze–thaw cycles, traffic loading

Repairs: Temporary patching, semi-permanent patching, full-depth reconstruction

Repair

Repair methods range from "throw-and-roll" patching — workers shovel cold asphalt mix into the hole and compact it with the truck's wheels — to more durable semi-permanent repairs in which the hole is squared off, cleaned, filled, and compacted in layers. Throw-and-roll is fast and cheap but the patches often fail within months; properly prepared semi-permanent patches can last nearly as long as the surrounding pavement. Deferred maintenance increases costs sharply, because water damage to the road base eventually requires full-depth reconstruction rather than surface repair.

Effects and costs

Potholes damage tyres, wheels, suspension, and steering, and pose a particular hazard to cyclists and motorcyclists. Surveys by motoring organisations regularly estimate the collective cost to drivers in the billions of dollars annually in large countries. In many jurisdictions road authorities are liable for pothole damage only if they knew (or had been notified) of the defect and failed to act within a reasonable time, which is one reason many cities operate pothole-reporting hotlines and apps. The word itself comes from the holes made by potters digging clay — or, by another account, from the resemblance to the deep "giant's kettles" scoured in rock by rivers, which geologists also call potholes.
