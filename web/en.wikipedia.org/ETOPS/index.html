ETOPS

ETOPS
Full name: Extended Operations (formerly Extended-range Twin-engine Operations Performance Standards)
Regulators: FAA, EASA, ICAO
Typical ratings: ETOPS-120, ETOPS-180, ETOPS-370

ETOPS (now formally "Extended Operations") is an aviation rule and certification framework that permits twin-engine aircraft to fly routes that, at some point, are farther from a suitable diversion airport than a specified threshold flying time. It enabled twinjets such as the Boeing 767, 777 and 787 and the Airbus A330 and A350 to operate long-haul overwater routes previously reserved for three- and four-engine aircraft.

ETOPS ratings define how far a twin-engine airliner may fly from a diversion airport.

History

Early regulations required aircraft with fewer than three engines to remain within 60 minutes of an adequate airport, effectively confining early twinjets to overland and coastal routes. With growing engine reliability, the FAA approved 120-minute ETOPS in 1985, enabling transatlantic twin-engine service beginning with TWA's Boeing 767 flights, and extended it to 180 minutes in 1988. ETOPS is jokingly said to stand for "Engines Turn Or Passengers Swim".

Modern framework

Under the FAA's 2007 rule, renamed Extended Operations, approval is granted by diversion time. The Boeing 787 is certified to ETOPS-330, and the Airbus A350 XWB to ETOPS-370, allowing twinjets to operate nearly all routes worldwide, including over the poles and remote Pacific, with only a handful of ultra-remote routes (such as parts of the southern Indian Ocean) remaining impractical.

Requirements

ETOPS certification imposes requirements on aircraft design (engine reliability statistics, electrical and cargo fire suppression systems), airline maintenance programmes, crew training, and dispatch procedures, including equal-time points and alternate airport weather minima.
