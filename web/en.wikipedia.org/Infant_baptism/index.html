Infant baptism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Infant baptism, also called christening or paedobaptism, is the Christian practice of baptising infants or very young children. It is practised by the Roman Catholic Church, the Eastern Orthodox Church, Oriental Orthodox churches, and most historic mainline Protestant denominations, including Lutherans, Anglicans, Methodists, Presbyterians, and many Reformed churches.

Infant baptism stands in contrast to believer's baptism (credobaptism), practised by Baptists, most Pentecostals, and Anabaptist traditions, in which baptism is reserved for those who personally profess faith.

Theological basis

Churches practising infant baptism argue that baptism is a means of grace and a sign of the covenant, analogous to circumcision in the Old Testament, and cite New Testament accounts of whole households being baptised. Catholic theology holds that baptism removes original sin and incorporates the child into the Church; sponsors or godparents profess faith on the child's behalf and undertake to raise the child in the faith, with the baptised later confirming those vows personally (for example at Confirmation).

Practice

The rite is ordinarily performed with water — by immersion in Orthodox practice, or by pouring (affusion) in much of the West — accompanied by the Trinitarian formula "I baptise you in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit." In Catholic practice infant baptism normally takes place within the first weeks of life.

History

The practice is attested from at least the late 2nd century and was defended by Origen and Cyprian; Augustine's teaching on original sin reinforced it in the West. The Anabaptist movement of the 16th century rejected infant baptism as unscriptural, a position that became a defining and often persecuted feature of the Radical Reformation.
