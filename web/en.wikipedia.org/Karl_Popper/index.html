Sir Karl Raimund Popper (28 July 1902 – 17 September 1994) was an Austrian-British philosopher, academic and social commentator. One of the 20th century's most influential philosophers of science, Popper is known for his rejection of the classical inductivist views on the scientific method in favour of empirical falsification.

Falsificationism

In The Logic of Scientific Discovery (1934, expanded English edition 1959), Popper argued that a theory in the empirical sciences can never be proven, but it can be falsified, meaning that it can (and should) be scrutinised by decisive experiments. Falsifiability thus serves as his criterion of demarcation between science and non-science, directed against the verificationism of the Vienna Circle, psychoanalysis and vulgar Marxism. Science progresses, on his account, by conjectures and refutations.

Political philosophy

In exile in New Zealand during the Second World War he wrote The Poverty of Historicism and The Open Society and Its Enemies (1945), defending liberal democracy against Plato, Hegel and Marx, whom he accused of historicism — the belief in inexorable laws of historical destiny. He advocated "piecemeal social engineering" over utopian reform. From 1946 he taught at the London School of Economics, was knighted in 1965, and influenced figures from Friedrich Hayek to George Soros.
