The Migration Period, also called the Barbarian Invasions or, in German historiography, the Völkerwanderung, was a period of large-scale human migration in Europe, roughly from AD 300 to 800, with its decisive phase between about 375 and 568. Germanic, Slavic, Iranian, and Turkic peoples moved across the frontiers of the Roman Empire and within it, contributing to the fall of the Western Roman Empire and laying the foundations of the early medieval kingdoms of Europe.

Overview

The migrations unfolded in two broad waves. In the first (c. 375–455), the arrival of the Huns in Eastern Europe set Gothic groups in motion across the Danube, precipitating the Gothic crossing of 376, the Gothic War, and the Roman disaster at Adrianople in 378, where the emperor Valens was killed. In the following decades Visigoths, Ostrogoths, Vandals, Alans, Suebi, Burgundians, and Franks carved out kingdoms in Gaul, Hispania, Italy, and North Africa; the Vandals sacked Rome in 455. Roman authority in the West collapsed, conventionally dated to the deposition of Romulus Augustulus in 476.

The second wave (c. 550–800) saw the Lombards enter Italy in 568, the settlement of Slavic peoples across the Balkans and Eastern Europe, and Avar and later Bulgar states on the Danube frontier, while Anglo-Saxon settlement reshaped lowland Britain and Frankish expansion under the Merovingians and Carolingians reorganized the West.

Causes

Historians debate the drivers of the migrations: population pressure, climate change (including the cooling associated with the Late Antique Little Ice Age and the extreme weather events of 535–536), the attraction of Roman wealth, the search for land, and the push effect of steppe peoples — above all the Huns — displacing groups westward. Modern scholarship also emphasizes that "peoples" were not fixed ethnic blocs but fluid confederations that formed and reformed around successful war leaders, frequently incorporating Romans and others.

Consequences

The migrations ended Roman political control in the West but preserved much of its cultural inheritance: the successor kingdoms adopted Roman law, Christianity, Latin administration, and coinage to varying degrees, while new vernacular, legal, and political cultures took shape. The demographic and linguistic map of modern Europe — Romance, Germanic, and Slavic language zones — is in large part a product of this period.

Historiography

Older accounts, influenced by German nationalism, treated the Völkerwanderung as the movement of whole, ethnically pure peoples. Twentieth-century scholars such as Reinhard Wenskus developed the concept of ethnogenesis — identities crystallizing around small "tradition-bearing" cores — and more recent work by historians including Peter Heather and Guy Halsall stresses the role of Roman frontier politics, economics, and the agency of the migrants themselves.
