Nuclear physics

Nuclear physics is the field of physics that studies atomic nuclei and their constituents and interactions, in addition to the study of other forms of nuclear matter. It should not be confused with atomic physics, which treats the atom as a whole including its electrons.

History

The field began with the discovery of radioactivity by Henri Becquerel in 1896 and Ernest Rutherford's identification of the nucleus in 1911 from gold-foil scattering experiments. The discovery of the neutron by James Chadwick in 1932 gave nuclei their modern proton–neutron composition. Subsequent decades developed models of nuclear structure, including the liquid-drop model and the nuclear shell model, the latter earning Maria Goeppert Mayer and J. Hans D. Jensen a share of the 1963 Nobel Prize.

Topics

Major topics include nuclear structure and reactions, radioactive decay, nuclear fission and fusion, the properties of hot and dense nuclear matter studied in heavy-ion collisions, and nuclear astrophysics — the reactions that power stars and synthesise the elements. Binding energy per nucleon peaks near iron-56, which explains why fission of heavy nuclei and fusion of light nuclei both release energy.

Applications

Applications include nuclear power, nuclear weapons, nuclear medicine (radiotherapy and medical imaging), radiocarbon dating, and materials analysis. The subfield of particle physics grew out of nuclear physics, though the two are now distinct.
