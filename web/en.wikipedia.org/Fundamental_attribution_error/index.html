Fundamental attribution error

In social psychology, the fundamental attribution error (also known as correspondence bias or the attribution effect) is the tendency for people to underemphasise situational and environmental causes for an individual's observed behaviour, while overemphasising dispositional and personality-based explanations.

The term was coined by psychologist Lee Ross in 1977, building on earlier work by Edward E. Jones and Victor Harris. In the classic Jones and Harris (1967) experiment, participants read essays about Fidel Castro that were either pro- or anti-Castro. Even when told that the essay's position had been assigned by coin toss, participants still inferred that the writer held genuine attitudes matching the essay — underestimating the situational constraint of the assignment.

Explanations

Several mechanisms have been proposed. Behaviour is perceptually salient to observers, whereas situational factors are often invisible; judgments are made quickly by anchoring on a dispositional inference and then adjusting insufficiently for the situation. Cultural factors matter as well: the bias is stronger in individualistic cultures that emphasise personal agency than in collectivist cultures that attend more to context.

Related effects

The fundamental attribution error is related to, but distinct from, the actor–observer asymmetry (we explain our own behaviour more situationally), the self-serving bias (we attribute our successes internally and failures externally), and the ultimate attribution error in intergroup settings. It has practical implications for how managers judge employees, how juries assess defendants, and how observers interpret poverty, illness, and failure.

Criticisms

Critics note that the effect varies in size across paradigms and cultures, that some classic demonstrations confound dispositional inference with reasonable use of base rates, and that "error" presupposes a normative standard of attribution that may itself be debatable. Nevertheless, correspondence bias remains one of the most robustly replicated phenomena in social psychology.
