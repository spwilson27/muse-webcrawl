Oregon boundary dispute

The Oregon boundary dispute or Oregon Question was a nineteenth-century controversy between the United States and the United Kingdom over the Oregon Country in the Pacific Northwest of North America. Both nations claimed the region on the basis of exploration and settlement, and from 1818 they governed it under a convention of joint occupation, repeatedly renewed.


The disputed Oregon Country

The dispute

The United States rested its claim on the explorations of Robert Gray and Lewis and Clark and on the Adams–Onís Treaty with Spain; Britain rested its on the voyages of Cook and Vancouver and the fur trade of the Hudson's Bay Company, whose Columbia District was administered from Fort Vancouver. The core disagreement was the boundary line: Britain proposed the Columbia River, while the United States demanded the parallel of 49°40′ or, in the expansionist slogan of the 1844 election campaign, "Fifty-four Forty or Fight!" — a claim to latitude 54°40′N, the southern limit of Russian Alaska.

Resolution

President James K. Polk, whose party platform had asserted the whole territory, ultimately accepted a compromise. The Oregon Treaty, signed in Washington on 15 June 1846, extended the existing 49th-parallel boundary along the Rockies to the Pacific, leaving Vancouver Island wholly British and securing navigation rights on the Columbia for the Hudson's Bay Company. A remaining ambiguity over the water boundary through the San Juan Islands later produced the bloodless "Pig War" of 1859, resolved by arbitration in Britain's — ultimately America's — favor in 1872 when the German emperor awarded the islands to the United States.
