The Yayoi period (弥生時代, Yayoi jidai) is one of the major divisions of Japanese history, named after the neighbourhood of Tokyo where archaeologists first uncovered its characteristic pottery in 1884. Traditionally dated from about 300 BC to AD 300, the period is now often extended back to around 1000 BC on the basis of radiocarbon dating.

Characteristics

The Yayoi period saw the introduction of wet-rice agriculture in irrigated paddy fields, new styles of pottery, weaving, and bronze and iron metallurgy, brought to Japan by migrants from the Asian continent, probably via the Korean Peninsula. Society became stratified, with chiefdoms and early states emerging; Chinese histories such as the Wei zhi describe the land of Wa and its queen Himiko of Yamatai. Large moated settlements such as Yoshinogari in Kyushu illustrate the period's fortified villages and ceremonial precincts.

Origins debate

Scholars debate the relationship between Yayoi migrants and the earlier Jōmon hunter-gatherer population; genetic and archaeological evidence indicates substantial migration from the continent combined with admixture with Jōmon peoples, producing the population ancestral to most modern Japanese.
