Thebes (Ancient Greek: Θῆβαι, Thēbai) was an ancient Greek city-state in Boeotia, north of the Cithaeron range dividing Boeotia from Attica. In Greek myth it was founded by Cadmus and served as the setting for the cycles of Oedipus, Dionysus, and Heracles. Historically, Thebes was the largest city of Boeotia, leader of the Boeotian confederacy, and a perennial rival of Athens and Sparta; in the 360s BC it became the dominant power in Greece before being destroyed by Alexander the Great in 335 BC.

Ancient history

Bronze Age Thebes was a fortified Mycenaean palace citadel (the Cadmea), attested by Linear B tablets and rich chamber-tomb finds. After a dark age, the historical polis emerged as head of the Boeotian League. Thebes medised during Xerxes' invasion (480–479 BC) and fought Athens in the Peloponnesian War on Sparta's side. Following the King's Peace it broke with Sparta; its generals Epaminondas and Pelopidas reformed its army around the elite Sacred Band and defeated Sparta at Leuctra (371 BC) and Mantinea (362 BC), inaugurating a decade of Theban hegemony. Philip II crushed the Thebans and Athenians at Chaeronea (338 BC). When Thebes revolted on Philip's death, Alexander stormed the city, killed or enslaved its population, and razed it — an act intended as a warning to Greece. Cassander refounded it in 316 BC, and it persisted as a minor city into Roman and Byzantine times.

The city in myth and literature

Ancient tragedy returned obsessively to Theban myth: Sophocles' Oedipus Rex, Antigone, and Oedipus at Colonus; Euripides' Bacchae and Phoenician Women; Aeschylus' Seven Against Thebes; and Statius' Latin epic Thebaid. The lyric poet Pindar, whose odes celebrate victors across the Greek world, was Thebes' most famous citizen and the one whose house Alexander spared.

Distinction from Egyptian Thebes

The Greek Thebes is not to be confused with the Egyptian city of Thebes (Luxor), the "hundred-gated Thebes" of Homer. The Greeks called the Egyptian city Diospolis Magna or simply Thebai as well; sources distinguish the two as Boeotian Thebes ("seven-gated") and Egyptian Thebes.
