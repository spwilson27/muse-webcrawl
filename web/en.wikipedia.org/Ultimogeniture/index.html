Ultimogeniture

Ultimogeniture, also referred to as postremogeniture or junior right, is the tradition of inheritance by the last-born of the entirety of, or a privileged position in, a parent's wealth, estate or office. The younger children are thus favoured, in contrast to primogeniture, in which the eldest child inherits. The custom has appeared in England, Mongolia, and among several peoples in Asia and Africa.

Examples

In medieval England, ultimogeniture was known as "Borough English", a customary tenure in some boroughs and manors under which land descended to the youngest son. It survived in scattered English customs until inheritance law reforms in 1925. Among the Mongols, the youngest son traditionally inherited the father's hearth and household — Genghis Khan's youngest son Tolui received the Mongolian homeland — while elder sons received territories farther afield. Ultimogeniture or youngest-daughter inheritance is also found among some matrilineal societies, and traces appear in folklore, where the youngest of three sons frequently succeeds where his elders fail.

Rationale

Explanations offered for the custom include that elder children have already established themselves or married out by the time of a parent's death, so the youngest, who has cared for ageing parents, remains at home to inherit the house and holding.
