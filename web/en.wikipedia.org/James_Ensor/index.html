James Sidney Edouard, Baron Ensor (13 April 1860 – 19 November 1949) was a Belgian painter and printmaker, an important influence on Expressionism and Surrealism. He lived in Ostend for almost his entire life, and his imagery of masks, skeletons, and grotesque crowds made him one of the most original artists of the late 19th century.

Early life

Ensor was born in the coastal resort town of Ostend to an English father and a Belgian mother whose family ran a souvenir shop selling carnival masks, shells, and curios — the world of objects that would furnish his art. He studied at the Académie Royale des Beaux-Arts in Brussels (1877–1880), where he found academic instruction stifling, and thereafter worked in the attic studio of his parents' house in Ostend.

Work

In the early 1880s Ensor painted dark-toned bourgeois interiors and luminous seascapes, then turned to scenes populated by masked figures, skeletons, and bizarre personages — a satirical mirror of Belgian society and of his own frustrated ambitions. He co-founded the avant-garde circle Les XX in 1883, though his colleagues frequently rejected his most provocative canvases. His masterpiece, the enormous Christ's Entry into Brussels in 1889 (painted in 1888), a teeming, jeering carnival crowd headed by a tiny Christ, was too large and too radical to exhibit and remained rolled up in his studio for decades; it is now in the Getty Museum in Los Angeles.

Recognition and later life

Recognition came slowly. From the 1900s younger Expressionists in Germany and artists such as Paul Klee, Emil Nolde, and later the Surrealists acknowledged his importance. Belgium honoured him late: he was made a baron in 1929 and became a celebrated local figure in Ostend, where he rarely painted after 1900 but remained a personality. He died in Ostend in 1949. His house and studio are preserved as the James Ensor House museum, and the Royal Museum of Fine Arts Antwerp and the Musées royaux in Brussels hold major collections of his work.
