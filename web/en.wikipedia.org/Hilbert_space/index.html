In mathematics, a Hilbert space is a real or complex vector space equipped with an inner product that induces a complete norm: every Cauchy sequence in the space converges to an element of the space. Hilbert spaces generalise the geometry of finite-dimensional Euclidean space — including notions of length, angle, orthogonality and orthogonal projection — to infinite-dimensional settings, and they are a central object of study in functional analysis.

The concept is named after the German mathematician David Hilbert, who studied such spaces in the context of integral equations in the first decade of the 20th century. The abstract axiomatic formulation was given by John von Neumann around 1929–1930, and Hilbert spaces became the standard mathematical framework for quantum mechanics, where the state of a physical system is represented by a vector in a Hilbert space and observables by self-adjoint operators on it.

Definition

A Hilbert space H is a vector space over the real or complex numbers together with an inner product ⟨·, ·⟩ satisfying linearity in one argument, conjugate symmetry, and positive-definiteness. The inner product defines a norm ∥x∥ = √⟨x, x⟩, and the space is required to be complete with respect to the metric induced by this norm. The inner product satisfies the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and the parallelogram law; conversely, a complete normed space whose norm satisfies the parallelogram law admits an inner product (given by the polarisation identity).

Examples

  • Euclidean space ℝn and ℂn with the standard dot product are finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces.
  • Sequence spaces: the space ℓ2 of square-summable sequences, with inner product the sum of coordinatewise products.
  • Lebesgue spaces: L2(X) of square-integrable functions modulo equality almost everywhere.
  • Sobolev spaces Hk, used in the theory of partial differential equations.
  • Hardy spaces and Bergman spaces in complex analysis.

Basic properties

Every Hilbert space has an orthonormal basis in the generalised sense: a maximal orthonormal set whose closed linear span is the whole space. All orthonormal bases of a given space have the same cardinality, called the dimension of the space. Any two Hilbert spaces of the same dimension are isometrically isomorphic. Separable infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces are all isomorphic to ℓ2.

The Riesz representation theorem states that every continuous linear functional on a Hilbert space is given by the inner product with a unique fixed vector. A consequence is that a Hilbert space is canonically identified with its dual space. For every closed convex subset there is a unique element of smallest norm, and every closed subspace admits an orthogonal complement, so the space decomposes as the direct sum of the subspace and its orthogonal complement. This fails in general Banach spaces and is one reason Hilbert spaces are far more tractable.

Operators on Hilbert spaces

Continuous linear operators on Hilbert spaces include bounded operators, compact operators and unbounded operators such as those arising from differential equations. The adjoint of an operator, self-adjoint and unitary operators, and the spectral theorem — which represents self-adjoint operators as multiplication operators and underlies the mathematical formulation of quantum observables — are fundamental topics. Weak convergence, in which xn → x whenever ⟨xn, y⟩ → ⟨x, y⟩ for all y, is an important mode of convergence: the closed unit ball of a Hilbert space is weakly compact in the separable case metrisable sense.

History

Hilbert introduced the space ℓ2 in his work on integral equations (1904–1910). Erhard Schmidt and Frigyes Riesz developed the geometry of function spaces; the name "Hilbert space" and the abstract definition crystallised with von Neumann's work on unbounded operators and quantum mechanics around 1930. Marshall Stone's 1932 monograph Linear Transformations in Hilbert Space consolidated the theory.
