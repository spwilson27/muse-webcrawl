Hydraulis

The hydraulis (Greek: ὕδραυλις) was a type of pipe organ blown by air, which was diverted into the organ through a water-driven mechanism, invented in the third century BC by Ctesibius of Alexandria. It was the world's first keyboard instrument with a significant sound volume, and an important precursor of the modern church organ.

The hydraulis used water pressure to stabilize the supply of wind to the pipes, which were activated by keys. It was widely used in the Roman world in arenas, theatres and public entertainment, and its player was called a hydraularius.

Mechanism

The instrument consisted of a set of pipes of varying lengths mounted above a wind chest. Air was pumped into an inverted funnel (pnigeus) submerged in a tank of water; the water pressure equalized the air pressure, providing a steady wind supply. Assistants pumped air with hand pumps while the organist pressed keys that admitted air to individual pipes.

History

Ctesibius, a Greek engineer working in Alexandria under the Ptolemies, is credited with the invention around 246 BC. The hydraulis remained in use for centuries; depictions survive in mosaics, and a largely intact example was excavated at Dion in Greece in 1992. In later centuries air was supplied directly by bellows, and the water mechanism was abandoned, leading toward the medieval organ.
