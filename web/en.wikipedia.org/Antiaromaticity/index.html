Antiaromaticity

Antiaromaticity is a chemical property of a cyclic molecule with a π electron system that has higher energy and is less stable due to the presence of 4n delocalized (π or lone pair) electrons in the ring, in contrast to aromatic compounds, which follow Hückel's rule with 4n + 2 π electrons and are unusually stable. Antiaromatic compounds are typically highly reactive and difficult to isolate.

Criteria

For a molecule to be antiaromatic it must be cyclic, planar (or nearly so), fully conjugated around the ring, and possess 4n π electrons. Because antiaromatic systems are destabilized, molecules often distort from planarity or localize their double bonds to escape antiaromatic character.

Examples

The classic example is cyclobutadiene, a square 4 π electron system that is extremely unstable and distorts to a rectangular geometry with alternating bond lengths. Pentalene and the cyclopentadienyl cation are also antiaromatic, whereas the corresponding cyclopentadienyl anion with 6 π electrons is aromatic. The concept was developed by Ronald Breslow in the 1960s and is supported by experimental measures such as bond-length patterns, NMR ring-current shifts, and computational indices like NICS (nucleus-independent chemical shift).
