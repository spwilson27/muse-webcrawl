Victory gardens
Also called: war gardens
Periods: First and Second World Wars
Purpose: supplement rations, boost morale, ease pressure on food supply

Victory gardens, also called war gardens or food gardens for defense, were vegetable, fruit and herb gardens planted at private residences and public parks in the United States, United Kingdom, Canada, Australia and Germany during the First and Second World Wars. They were intended to supplement rationed food supplies and to lift civilian morale by letting non-combatants contribute to the war effort.

Second World War

In the United States the Department of Agriculture promoted victory gardens from 1942; by 1944 some 20 million gardens produced roughly 8 million tons of food — over 40 percent of the fresh vegetables consumed domestically. In Britain the "Dig for Victory" campaign turned parks, railway sidings and even the moat of the Tower of London over to allotments.

Legacy

The idea has been revived in modern community-gardening and local-food movements, and a demonstration victory garden has been maintained on the grounds of the Smithsonian in Washington, D.C.
