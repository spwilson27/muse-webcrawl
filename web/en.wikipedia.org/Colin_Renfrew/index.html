Andrew Colin Renfrew, Baron Renfrew of Kaimsthorn (25 July 1937 – 24 November 2024), was a British archaeologist, palaeolinguist and Conservative peer. A leading figure of processual archaeology, he reshaped the study of European prehistory through his advocacy of scientific methods — above all radiocarbon dating — and proposed the Anatolian hypothesis for the origin and spread of Indo-European languages.

Career

Renfrew read Natural Sciences and then Archaeology and Anthropology at St John's College, Cambridge. He undertook fieldwork in the Cyclades and mainland Greece and completed his PhD on the Neolithic and Bronze Age cultures of the Cyclades. After lecturing at the University of Sheffield (1965–1972), he became Professor of Archaeology at the University of Southampton (1972–1981), then returned to Cambridge as Disney Professor of Archaeology (1981–2004) and Master of Jesus College (1986–1997). He founded and directed the McDonald Institute for Archaeological Research in Cambridge. In 1991 he was created a life peer.

Contributions

  • The radiocarbon revolution: Renfrew was among the first to grasp that calibrated radiocarbon dates destroyed diffusionist explanations of European prehistory — megalithic monuments in western Europe predated their supposed Near Eastern prototypes — forcing a rethinking of independent invention.
  • Anatolian hypothesis (1987): in Archaeology and Language he proposed that Proto-Indo-European was spoken in Anatolia around 7000 BCE and spread into Europe with the advance of farming, a model later partly tested by archaeogenetics.
  • Cognitive archaeology: he pioneered the study of ancient minds — symbolism, measurement, religion — within a scientific archaeology, including excavations at Phylakopi on Melos.
  • Heritage protection: he campaigned prominently against the illicit antiquities trade and looting, notably in Loot, Legitimacy and Ownership (2000).
