The GNU Project is a free-software, mass-collaboration project announced by Richard Stallman on 27 September 1983. Its goal was to develop a complete Unix-like operating system composed entirely of free software — software respecting users' freedom to run, study, share and modify it. The name is a recursive acronym for "GNU's Not Unix", chosen because the system's design would be Unix-like but contain no AT&T Unix code.

History

Stallman, then working at the MIT Artificial Intelligence Laboratory, began writing GNU software in January 1984. He published the GNU Manifesto in 1985 explaining the project's philosophy, and founded the non-profit Free Software Foundation (FSF) the same year to employ programmers and provide legal infrastructure. To guarantee that GNU software would remain free, Stallman devised "copyleft" and wrote the GNU General Public License, first released in 1989.

Software

By the early 1990s the GNU system was nearly complete — including the Emacs text editor, the GNU Compiler Collection (GCC), the Bash shell, core utilities, the C library (glibc) and many other components — except for the operating-system kernel. The project's own kernel, GNU Hurd, based on the Mach microkernel, proved slow to develop. In 1991 Linus Torvalds released the Linux kernel, which was adopted under the GPL from 1992 and combined with GNU components to form the widely used GNU/Linux systems. Stallman and the FSF continue to argue for the name "GNU/Linux" to acknowledge the project's contribution.

Philosophy and influence

The GNU Project launched the free-software movement and established the legal and cultural framework — copyleft licensing, the four freedoms, and collaborative development — on which free and open-source software was built. GNU packages remain foundational components of most Linux distributions, servers, embedded systems and supercomputers.
