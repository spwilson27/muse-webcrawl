Obduction

[Image placeholder: Obduction]

Obduction is a geological process in which oceanic lithosphere — crust and upper mantle material from an ocean basin — is thrust over the edge of a continent or island arc, rather than being subducted beneath it. The emplaced slices of oceanic rock are known as ophiolites when they preserve a recognisable sequence of mantle peridotite, gabbro, sheeted dykes and pillow lavas.

Mechanisms and examples

Obduction occurs during episodes of plate convergence and collision, often where a young, hot and buoyant slab resists sinking, or where a continent enters a subduction zone. Classic examples include the Semail Ophiolite of Oman and the United Arab Emirates — the largest and best-studied ophiolite in the world — the Troodos Massif of Cyprus, and the Bay of Islands complex in Newfoundland.

Significance

Because drilling cannot reach the oceanic mantle, obducted ophiolites give geologists direct access to samples of ocean-floor and upper-mantle rocks on land.
