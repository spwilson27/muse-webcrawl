Fragrance

A fragrance is a mixture of aromatic compounds designed to produce a pleasant scent.

A fragrance (or perfume) is a mixture of fragrant essential oils and other aroma compounds, fixatives and solvents, usually in liquid form, used to give the human body, animals, food, objects and living spaces a pleasant scent. Fragrances are produced by the fragrance industry for fine perfumery (perfumes and aftershaves), for functional products such as soaps, detergents and cosmetics, and for flavouring food and beverages.

Composition

A finished fragrance is typically described in terms of three layers of notes. Top notes are light, volatile molecules perceived immediately on application; heart (middle) notes form the main character of the scent as the top notes fade; and base notes, heavy and long-lasting, provide depth and fix the composition to the skin. Perfumers combine natural materials (flower absolutes, citrus oils, resins, woods, animal-derived musks) with synthetic aroma chemicals, which since the late 19th century have greatly expanded the available palette.

Concentration

Fragrance products are graded by the concentration of aromatic compounds in solvent: parfum (extrait) is strongest at roughly 20–30%, followed by eau de parfum, eau de toilette and eau de Cologne, the lightest of the traditional categories. Scent families are often organised by the "fragrance wheel" into floral, amber (oriental), woody and fresh groups and their subfamilies.

Industry and regulation

The industry centres historically on Grasse in France and is dominated by large flavour-and-fragrance houses such as Givaudan, Firmenich (now dsm-firmenich), IFF and Symrise, which create scents both for designer brands and household products. The International Fragrance Association (IFRA) sets safety standards restricting or banning ingredients that cause allergies or other harm.
