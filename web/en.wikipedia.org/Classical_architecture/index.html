Classical architecture

Classical architecture is the architecture of ancient Greece and Rome and the styles derived from it in later periods. Its defining vocabulary is the system of the classical orders—Doric, Ionic, and Corinthian, with the Roman Tuscan and Composite—governing the proportions of columns, capitals, and entablatures.

Origins

Greek temple architecture of the 6th–4th centuries BCE, exemplified by the Parthenon in Athens, established canons of proportion, symmetry, and refinement. Roman builders combined the orders with arches, vaults, and concrete, producing structures such as the Pantheon. The Roman writer Vitruvius's De architectura is the only architectural treatise to survive from antiquity.

Revivals

The Renaissance, described by Brunelleschi and codified by Alberti and Palladio, revived classical forms after a millennium; Palladio's villas and churches spread the style across Europe and later America. Baroque, Neoclassical, Greek Revival, and Beaux-Arts architecture successively reinterpreted the classical language, which became the default for banks, courthouses, museums, and government buildings into the 20th century.

Contemporary practice

Though modernism displaced it, classical design persists in the New Classical movement, with practitioners arguing for its durability, legibility, and civic resonance; the University of Notre Dame's architecture school and the Institute of Classical Architecture & Art are prominent centres of teaching.
