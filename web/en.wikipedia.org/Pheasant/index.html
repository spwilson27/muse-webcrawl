Pheasant

Pheasants are birds of several genera within the family Phasianidae in the order Galliformes. They are native mainly to Asia, though some species range into Europe, and many have been widely introduced elsewhere as game birds. Pheasants are characterized by strong sexual dimorphism: males are strikingly coloured, often with iridescent plumage and long tails, while females are mottled brown and cryptic — an adaptation for camouflage on the nest.

Species and distribution

The best-known species is the common pheasant (Phasianus colchicus), native from the Caucasus across Asia to China and introduced into Europe, North America, Australia, and New Zealand; it is the state bird of South Dakota. Other notable pheasants include the golden pheasant, Lady Amherst's pheasant, the green pheasant of Japan, Reeves's pheasant (with the longest natural tail feathers of any bird), the tragopans, monals, and the great argus. Peafowl and many partridges and junglefowl belong to the same subfamily, Phasianinae.

Behaviour and ecology

Pheasants are ground-dwelling birds that prefer grassland, farmland, and scrub edges. They feed on seeds, grain, shoots, and invertebrates, fly only reluctantly in short bursts, and roost in trees at night. Males are typically polygynous, gathering small harems in the breeding season.

Hunting and husbandry

Pheasants are among the world's most popular game birds; millions of common pheasants are reared and released annually for shooting in Britain alone. They are also raised for meat and kept as ornamental birds.
