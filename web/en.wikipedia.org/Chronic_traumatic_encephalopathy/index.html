Chronic traumatic encephalopathy

Chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE) is a neurodegenerative disease linked to repeated head impacts. Formerly called dementia pugilistica or "punch-drunk syndrome" in boxers, it became widely known through research on former American football players, though it has also been identified in ice hockey, soccer, rugby players, military veterans and others exposed to repetitive head trauma.

Pathology

CTE is a tauopathy: abnormal deposits of hyperphosphorylated tau protein accumulate in neurons, initially around small blood vessels at the depths of cortical sulci, and spread through the brain in advanced stages. Diagnosis can at present be confirmed only by post-mortem examination of brain tissue, though criteria for a clinical syndrome called traumatic encephalopathy syndrome are used in research.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms typically appear years after the exposure ends and may include memory loss, impaired judgement, impulsivity, aggression, depression, apathy and progressive dementia. Motor symptoms such as tremor and gait disturbance occur in some cases.

Research and impact

Research led by neuropathologist Bennet Omalu, who identified CTE in former NFL player Mike Webster in 2002, and by the Boston University CTE Center, brought the condition to public attention. A widely reported 2017 study found CTE in 110 of 111 donated brains of former NFL players, though brain-bank studies are subject to selection bias. The findings prompted rule changes, concussion protocols and litigation in contact sports, and broader debate about youth participation in tackle football.
