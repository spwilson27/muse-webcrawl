Domestic goose

Domestic goose
Scientific namesAnser anser domesticus; Anser cygnoides domesticus
AncestorsGreylag goose; swan goose
UsesMeat, eggs, foie gras, feathers, guarding, weeding

A domestic goose is a goose that humans have domesticated and kept for their meat, eggs, or down feathers, or as a pet. Two lineages exist: European domestic geese descend from the greylag goose (Anser anser), while Asian domestic geese — including the knobbed Chinese goose — descend from the swan goose (Anser cygnoides). Geese were domesticated at least 3,000 years ago in Egypt (greylag lineage), and genomic evidence suggests the swan goose may have been domesticated in China even earlier, making geese among the first domesticated poultry.

Characteristics

Domestic geese are larger and heavier than their wild ancestors, with upright posture and fat deposits that mostly preclude sustained flight. They are long-lived — often 20 years or more — form strong pair and family bonds, and are vigorous grazers able to subsist largely on grass. Their loud honking and territorial aggression have made them valued guard animals since antiquity; Roman tradition credited sacred geese with warning the Capitol of a Gallic night attack in 390 BC.

Uses

Geese are raised for roast meat (traditional at Michaelmas and Christmas in Europe), for large rich eggs, and for foie gras, produced mainly from geese and ducks. Goose down is a premium insulator, quills served as pens for centuries, and "weeder geese" are used to control weeds in crops such as cotton and strawberries. Production of goose meat is concentrated in China, which accounts for the overwhelming majority of world output.

Breeds

Breeds include the Embden, Toulouse, Pilgrim, Chinese, African, Sebastopol (with curled feathers), and the auto-sexing West of England. Many are listed as heritage breeds of conservation concern.
