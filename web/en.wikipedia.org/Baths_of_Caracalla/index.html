Baths of Caracalla

Baths of Caracalla
Location: Rome, Italy
Built: c. 212–216 AD
Commissioned by: Emperor Caracalla
Type: Roman thermae

The Baths of Caracalla (Italian: Terme di Caracalla), in Rome, were the city's second-largest Roman public baths, or thermae. Built between roughly AD 212 and 216/217 under the emperor Caracalla, they remained in use until the 530s, when the Ostrogoths cut the aqueducts during the Gothic War. The vast, richly decorated ruins are among the best-preserved examples of an imperial bath complex.

The towering ruins of the frigidarium and caldarium.

Description

The complex covered about 25 hectares (62 acres) within a walled precinct containing gardens, libraries (in Greek and Latin), lecture halls, and an open-air stadium. The main bath block, roughly 214 by 110 metres, followed the standard sequence: a cold frigidarium with vaults rising nearly 33 metres, a warm tepidarium, and a hot circular caldarium, flanked by exercise yards (palaestrae) and an open-air swimming pool (natatio). Water came via a branch of the Aqua Marcia. The baths could accommodate an estimated 1,600 bathers at a time and were free and open to all citizens.

Famous artworks recovered from the ruins include the Farnese Hercules and the Farnese Bull. Since 1937 the ruins have hosted Rome Opera's summer season; the 1990 Three Tenors concert took place there. The baths are a UNESCO World Heritage Site as part of the historic centre of Rome.
