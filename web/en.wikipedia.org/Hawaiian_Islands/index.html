The Hawaiian Islands (Hawaiian: Mokupuni Hawai'i) are an archipelago of 137 volcanic islands, atolls, and islets in the North Pacific Ocean, forming most of the U.S. state of Hawaii. The eight main islands, from southeast to northwest, are Hawai'i (the "Big Island"), Maui, Kaho'olawe, Lana'i, Moloka'i, O'ahu, Kaua'i, and Ni'ihau. The chain continues northwest as the Northwestern Hawaiian Islands, protected within Papahānaumokuākea Marine National Monument, as far as Kure Atoll.

Geology

The islands were formed as the Pacific Plate moved northwestward over a stationary hotspot in the Earth's mantle. The youngest and volcanically active islands are in the southeast — the Big Island's Kīlauea and Mauna Loa are among the world's most active volcanoes — while islands grow older, more eroded, and eventually subside into atolls and seamounts toward the northwest, continuing as the Emperor Seamount chain. Mauna Kea, a dormant shield volcano on the Big Island, is the tallest mountain on Earth measured from its base on the ocean floor.

History

Polynesian voyagers from the Marquesas and later Tahiti settled the islands between roughly 1000 and 1200 CE, developing a sophisticated chiefdom society. Kamehameha I unified most of the islands into the Kingdom of Hawaii by 1795–1810. The kingdom was overthrown in 1893, followed by a Republic, U.S. annexation in 1898 as the Territory of Hawaii, and statehood in 1959 as the 50th state.

Ecology and climate

Isolated for millions of years, the islands evolved extraordinary endemic biodiversity — including honeycreepers, silverswords, and the nēnē goose — much of it now threatened by introduced species and habitat loss. The climate is tropical but moderated by trade winds, with striking rainfall contrasts between windward and leeward coasts.
