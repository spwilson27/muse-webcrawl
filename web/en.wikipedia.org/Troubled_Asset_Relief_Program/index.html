Troubled Asset Relief Program


The U.S. Treasury administered TARP

The Troubled Asset Relief Program (TARP) was a United States government program created by the Emergency Economic Stabilization Act of 2008 to purchase toxic assets and equity from financial institutions and stabilize the financial system during the 2008 financial crisis. Signed into law by President George W. Bush on 3 October 2008, it authorized expenditures of $700 billion, later reduced to $475 billion by the Dodd–Frank Act.

Programs

Although initially intended to buy mortgage-backed securities, TARP was quickly redirected to inject capital directly into banks through the Capital Purchase Program. Other components supported the automotive industry (General Motors and Chrysler), insurer AIG, credit markets, and foreclosure-mitigation efforts such as the Home Affordable Modification Program.

Outcome and legacy

The program was administered by the Treasury's Office of Financial Stability and overseen by the Special Inspector General for TARP and the Congressional Oversight Panel. Most funds were repaid with interest; the Congressional Budget Office ultimately estimated a net cost of roughly $30 billion, concentrated in the auto rescue and housing programs. TARP remains politically controversial, criticized both as a bailout of Wall Street and defended as having averted a collapse of the financial system.
