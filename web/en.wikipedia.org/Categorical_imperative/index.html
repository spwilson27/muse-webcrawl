The categorical imperative (German: kategorischer Imperativ) is the central philosophical concept in the deontological moral philosophy of Immanuel Kant. Introduced in Kant's 1785 Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals, it is a way of evaluating motivations for action. An imperative is categorical when it applies unconditionally — to all rational agents, regardless of their desires — in contrast to hypothetical imperatives, which command actions as means to some desired end ("if you want X, do Y").

Formulations

Kant presented several formulations which he held to be equivalent:

Universal law

"Act only according to that maxim whereby you can at the same time will that it become a universal law."

One must be able to will that the rule (maxim) of one's action become a universal law without contradiction. A maxim that cannot be universalized — such as making false promises, which would undermine the practice of promising itself — is forbidden.

Humanity as an end in itself

"Act in such a way that you treat humanity, whether in your own person or in the person of any other, never merely as a means to an end, but always at the same time as an end."

Rational beings have dignity — an intrinsic, unconditional worth — and must never be treated as mere instruments.

Autonomy and the kingdom of ends

The third formulation holds that one should act as a legislating member of a rational "kingdom of ends," a systematic union of rational agents under common moral laws, where each agent is both author and subject of the law (autonomy of the will).

Contrasts and influence

Kant's ethics is a leading example of deontology, standing in contrast to consequentialism (e.g., utilitarianism), which judges actions by outcomes. The categorical imperative has been immensely influential and also criticized — for alleged emptiness or rigorism (Hegel), for conflicts between duties, and in Schopenhauer's and later critiques. It remains a touchstone in moral and political philosophy, informing ideas about human rights and the dignity of persons.
