Bhavacakra

The bhavacakra (Sanskrit: भवचक्र, "wheel of existence"; Tibetan: srid pa'i 'khor lo) is a symbolic representation of saṃsāra, the cycle of birth, death, and rebirth, found in Tibetan Buddhist art. Such wheels of life are painted on the walls of temples and monasteries throughout Tibet, Bhutan, and Nepal, and tradition holds that the design originated with the Buddha himself.

Structure

The great wheel is gripped by Yama, the wrathful lord of death, symbolizing impermanence: all beings within the wheel are subject to death. At the hub, three animals — a pig (or boar), a snake, and a bird (rooster) — bite one another's tails, representing the three poisons of ignorance/delusion, attachment, and aversion that drive the cycle.

The realms

The main body of the wheel is divided into six sections depicting the six realms of rebirth: the gods (devas), the demigods (asuras), humans, animals, hungry ghosts (pretas), and the hell realms. A darker lower half in some versions contrasts with the lighter upper realms. Around the rim run twelve panels illustrating the nidānas, the twelve links of dependent origination (pratītyasamutpāda) that explain how beings become trapped in saṃsāra.

Outside the wheel

Outside the wheel, in the upper corners, stand the Buddha — pointing toward liberation — and a representation of the moon or of nirvāṇa, indicating that freedom from the cycle lies beyond it. Verses often inscribed beneath the wheel exhort viewers to strive for awakening.
