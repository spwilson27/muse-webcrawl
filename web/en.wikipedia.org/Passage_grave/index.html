Passage grave

A passage grave (or passage tomb) is a type of Neolithic and Bronze Age megalithic tomb consisting of one or more burial chambers covered by a large mound of earth or stone (a cairn or barrow), and reached by a narrow, roofed passage. Unlike other megalithic tombs, the passage grave is designed so the chamber can be entered repeatedly, and many received successive burials over generations. They are characteristic of Atlantic Europe between roughly 4500 and 2500 BC.

Distribution and examples

Passage graves are found along the Atlantic seaboard from Iberia through Brittany, Britain, Ireland, and into Denmark and southern Sweden. Celebrated examples include Newgrange, Knowth, and Dowth in Ireland's Boyne Valley; Maeshowe in Orkney; the passage graves of the Carnac region in Brittany, such as Gavrinis with its carved stones; and Los Millares and Antequera in Spain. Some, like Newgrange, have roof boxes or passage alignments that admit sunlight at the winter solstice, demonstrating detailed astronomical knowledge among their builders.

Construction and use

Chambers are built of orthostats (upright slabs) or drystone walling, roofed with corbelled vaults or massive capstones. The dead — cremated or inhumed — were placed with grave goods including pottery, polished stone axes, beads, and carved objects. Finds of disarticulated bone suggest the tombs served as communal ossuaries for kin groups. Many passages and chamber walls carry megalithic art: spirals, lozenges, zigzags, and cup marks carved into the stones.

Interpretation

These monuments required large communal labour and are widely read as expressions of ancestral cult and territorial identity among early farming communities. They belong to the same broad megalithic tradition as dolmens, gallery graves, and stone circles, though archaeologists distinguish them by the clear separation of passage and chamber.
