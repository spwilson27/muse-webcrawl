John Dryden

John Dryden (19 August 1631 – 12 May 1700) was an English poet, literary critic, translator and playwright who dominated the literary life of Restoration England so completely that the period came to be known in literary circles as the Age of Dryden. He was appointed England's first Poet Laureate in 1668.


John Dryden

Career

Educated at Westminster School and Trinity College, Cambridge, Dryden first won notice with his Heroic Stanzas on the death of Oliver Cromwell (1659), then welcomed Charles II with Astraea Redux (1660). He became the leading playwright of the Restoration stage, writing heroic dramas such as The Conquest of Granada and comedies including Marriage à la Mode; All for Love (1677), his retelling of Antony and Cleopatra, is considered his finest play.

Poetry and criticism

Dryden's great political satires — Absalom and Achitophel (1681), The Medal and Mac Flecknoe — established the heroic couplet as the dominant verse form of the next century. His Essay of Dramatic Poesy (1668) founded modern English prose criticism. After converting to Catholicism, he wrote The Hind and the Panther (1687); losing his laureateship at the Glorious Revolution for refusing the oath to William and Mary, he turned to translation, producing celebrated versions of Juvenal, Persius and, in 1697, the complete works of Virgil, and the tales of Fables, Ancient and Modern (1700).

Legacy

Samuel Johnson praised Dryden as the writer who "refined the language, improved the sentiments, and tuned the numbers of English poetry." His influence on Pope, Johnson and the Augustan age was immense.
