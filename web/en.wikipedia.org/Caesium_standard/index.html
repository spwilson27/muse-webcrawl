Caesium standard

The caesium standard is the primary standard for the measurement of time and frequency. It defines the SI unit of time, the second, in terms of the properties of the caesium-133 atom: since 1967 the second has been defined as the duration of 9,192,631,770 periods of the radiation corresponding to the transition between the two hyperfine levels of the ground state of the caesium-133 atom. In the 2019 revision of the SI, this is expressed by fixing the caesium hyperfine transition frequency at exactly 9,192,631,770 Hz.

Atomic clocks

A caesium atomic clock uses a beam or fountain of caesium-133 atoms passing through a microwave cavity. Atoms prepared in one hyperfine state are exposed to microwave radiation; only when the frequency exactly matches the transition do atoms change state and get detected. A feedback loop ("servo") locks the oscillator to this resonance, producing an extremely stable 9,192,631,770 Hz reference from which one-second intervals are counted. Modern caesium fountains, such as NIST-F2 in the United States, achieve uncertainties around one second in hundreds of millions of years.

Role in timekeeping

National metrology institutes operate ensembles of caesium clocks and fountains whose data are combined by the Bureau International des Poids et Mesures into International Atomic Time (TAI), the basis of Coordinated Universal Time (UTC). Caesium standards also discipline the clocks aboard GPS satellites, which combine caesium and rubidium oscillators to provide the timing signals on which satellite navigation depends.
