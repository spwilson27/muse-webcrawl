Sir Thomas Stamford Bingley Raffles (5 July 1781 – 5 July 1826) was a British colonial official who served as the lieutenant-governor of the Dutch East Indies (1811–1816) and lieutenant-governor of Bencoolen (1818–1824). He is best known for founding modern Singapore in 1819, establishing it as a free port that transformed regional trade.

Early life and career

Raffles was born at sea off Jamaica, the son of a merchant captain. Financial difficulties forced him to leave school early and take a clerkship with the British East India Company in London at age fourteen. Self-educated, he rose through the Company and in 1805 was posted to Penang as assistant secretary, where his study of the Malay language and culture drew the attention of Lord Minto, the Governor-General of India.

Java

During the Napoleonic Wars, Raffles helped plan the British invasion of Java in 1811 and became its lieutenant-governor. He introduced wide-ranging reforms, abolishing slavery and forced labour in some areas, restoring the ancient temple of Borobudur, and writing The History of Java (1817), a landmark work of scholarship.

Singapore

Seeking a British port in the Strait of Malacca to counter Dutch influence, Raffles landed at Singapore on 28 January 1819 and signed a treaty with the local Temenggong and Sultan Hussein Shah, establishing a trading post. Declared a free port, Singapore grew rapidly into one of the world's great entrepôts. Raffles laid out the town plan of 1822–1823 and founded an educational institution that became Raffles Institution.

Later life and legacy

Raffles returned to England in 1824, helped found the Zoological Society of London and London Zoo, and died in 1826. His name is commemorated across Singapore — in Raffles Place, the Raffles Hotel, and the giant corpse flower Rafflesia, a genus named in his honour from his time in Sumatra. His legacy is debated: admired as a visionary founder and enlightened scholar-administrator, he is also viewed critically as an agent of colonial expansion.
