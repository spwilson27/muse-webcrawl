Negative interest on excess reserves

Negative interest on excess reserves is an unconventional monetary policy instrument in which central banks charge commercial banks interest—that is, pay a negative rate—on reserves held above required amounts. It is intended to encourage banks to lend rather than park funds at the central bank, thereby stimulating credit and inflation.

Adoption

The European Central Bank moved its deposit facility rate below zero in June 2014, and the Bank of Japan adopted a negative rate on a portion of reserves in January 2016. Central banks in Switzerland, Denmark and Sweden also operated negative policy rates for periods.

Effects and debate

Supporters argue the tool eases financial conditions and counters deflation; critics warn it can squeeze bank profitability, distort money markets and encourage excessive risk-taking. Tiered systems were often introduced to shield most reserves from the charge.
