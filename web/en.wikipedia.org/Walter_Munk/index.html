Walter Heinrich Munk (19 October 1917 – 8 February 2019) was an Austrian-born American oceanographer and geophysicist at the Scripps Institution of Oceanography in La Jolla, California. Called "the Einstein of the oceans", he made fundamental contributions to the study of ocean waves, tides, ocean circulation, and the rotation of the Earth.

Career

Munk emigrated to the United States in 1932 and, after an unhappy spell in banking, studied at Caltech before joining Scripps for his doctorate under Harald Sverdrup. During the Second World War he and Sverdrup developed methods of forecasting surf conditions for amphibious landings, used in North Africa and the Pacific. His 1950 paper on wind-driven ocean circulation explained the great subtropical gyres and gave the "Munk layer" and related concepts to the field.

Research

In the 1960s Munk led an experiment tracking waves generated by storms near Antarctica as they crossed the Pacific, confirming theories of wave propagation. He showed that most tidal energy is dissipated in shallow seas and that tidal friction slows Earth's rotation and lengthens the day. With Carl Wunsch he later developed ocean acoustic tomography — measuring ocean temperature over whole basins from the travel time of sound — and the 1991 Heard Island experiment tested the method globally. He remained active in research into his hundredth year, publishing on sea-level rise and tidal dynamics.

Honours

Munk received the National Medal of Science (1983), the Kyoto Prize (1999), and the Crafoord Prize (2010). The Walter Munk Award for distinguished research in ocean acoustics is named for him.
