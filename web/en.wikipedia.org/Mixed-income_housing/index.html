Mixed-income housing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Mixed-income housing is a type of residential development — or a housing-policy strategy — in which dwellings are deliberately provided for households across a range of income levels within the same building, complex or neighbourhood. Units may include market-rate homes alongside housing subsidised for low- and moderate-income residents, rather than concentrating assisted housing in separate, single-income projects.

Rationale

Advocates argue that mixed-income development deconcentrates poverty, gives low-income residents access to better-resourced neighbourhoods and services, broadens social networks, and improves the financial and political viability of affordable housing: revenue from market-rate units can cross-subsidise the affordable ones. The approach became prominent in the United States through the federal HOPE VI programme of the 1990s, which replaced distressed high-rise public housing with lower-density mixed-income communities, and through inclusionary zoning ordinances that require or incentivise a share of affordable units in new private developments.

Models

  • Single-site mixed-income developments — one project combining public, subsidised and market-rate units, often in indistinguishable buildings.
  • Inclusionary zoning — private market developments include a mandatory or bonus-incentivised percentage of affordable homes.
  • Scattered-site and area-based approaches — affordable units distributed through existing mixed neighbourhoods.

Criticism and debate

Researchers debate the strength of the claimed social benefits: studies find that proximity alone does not guarantee interaction across income lines, and that redevelopment programmes have sometimes displaced more low-income households than they rehoused, replacing demolished public housing with far fewer subsidised units. Others respond that mixed-income housing improves safety, maintenance and access to opportunity even where cross-income social ties remain limited, and that outcomes depend heavily on management, design and the share of deeply affordable units retained.
