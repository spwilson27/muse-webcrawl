Vaishnavism (Sanskrit: वैष्णवसम्प्रदायः, romanized: Vaiṣṇavasampradāyaḥ) is one of the major Hindu denominations, characterised by devotion to Vishnu — or to his avatars such as Krishna and Rama — as the supreme God. It is the largest of the Hindu traditions.

Beliefs

Vaishnavas regard Vishnu as the ultimate reality (Brahman) and preserver of the universe, who descends into the world as avatars to restore dharma. Devotion (bhakti) to Vishnu or Krishna, expressed through worship, chanting of the divine names, and surrender (prapatti), is the principal path to liberation (moksha). Traditions differ on the relationship between God and the soul, from Ramanuja's qualified non-dualism (Vishishtadvaita) to Madhva's dualism (Dvaita) and the Gaudiya tradition of Chaitanya Mahaprabhu.

Practices

Worship centres on temples and home shrines, offerings of food (prasada), festivals such as Janmashtami (Krishna's birthday) and Rama Navami, and pilgrimage to sites including Vrindavan, Tirupati, and Puri. Many Vaishnavas follow a vegetarian diet — compare veganism — and wear the U-shaped tilaka mark of Vishnu on the forehead.

History

The tradition has roots in the Vedic god Vishnu and the Bhagavata religion of the late centuries BCE. The Alvar poet-saints of Tamil Nadu (6th–9th centuries CE) spread emotional devotion across South India, paralleling the Buddhist commentarial tradition exemplified by the Visuddhimagga. In the 20th century the Hare Krishna movement (ISKCON), founded by A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada in 1966, carried Gaudiya Vaishnavism worldwide.
