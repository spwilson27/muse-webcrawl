Ocean deoxygenation

Ocean deoxygenation is the global-scale expansion of low-oxygen (hypoxic) areas in the world's oceans, driven principally by anthropogenic climate change and nutrient pollution. The global ocean is estimated to have lost roughly 2% of its dissolved oxygen since 1960, and naturally occurring oxygen minimum zones are expanding.

Causes

  • Warming — warmer water holds less dissolved gas, and increased stratification reduces ventilation of the deep ocean by oxygen-rich surface waters.
  • Eutrophication — nutrient runoff fuels algal blooms whose decomposition consumes oxygen, especially in coastal seas.
  • Circulation changes — slowing or shifts in ocean circulation alter the transport of oxygen into the interior.

Effects

Declining oxygen compresses habitat for fish and invertebrates, alters marine food webs, favours microbes and jellyfish tolerant of low oxygen, and changes the cycling of nutrients and greenhouse gases such as nitrous oxide. Coastal dead zones now number in the hundreds worldwide. Highly productive ecosystems such as upwelling regions and semi-enclosed seas (the Baltic, the Black Sea, the Gulf of Mexico) are particularly affected.

Projections

Climate models project continued oxygen loss through the 21st century under high-emissions scenarios, with recovery, if warming is reversed, taking many centuries because of the slow overturning of the deep ocean.
