The Wen Xuan is the oldest surviving large anthology of Chinese literature.

The Wen Xuan (文選, "Selections of Refined Literature"), often translated as Selections of Literature, is one of the earliest and most important anthologies of Chinese poetry and literature, and is one of the world's oldest literary anthologies to be arranged by topic. It was compiled from the late 520s to 530 by Xiao Tong, the eldest son of Emperor Wu of Liang, and a team of scholars working under him.

Contents

The anthology contains more than 700 pieces by 130 authors, spanning from the late Zhou dynasty and Han dynasty to the compiler's own Liang dynasty, arranged into 37 generic categories. Its largest sections are devoted to fu (rhapsodies) on capitals, hunts, and other subjects, and to shi poetry; it also includes letters, memorials, prefaces, laments, and epitaphs. Conspicuously excluded are the classics, most philosophy, and works Xiao Tong judged merely historical or didactic.

Influence

The Wen Xuan became a central text in the education of literati in China, Japan, Korea, and Vietnam; "read the Wen Xuan to pieces" was a traditional saying about mastering literary composition. The Tang-dynasty commentary by Li Shan and the "Five Officials" made it a focus of scholarly exegesis, and it shaped Chinese ideas of genre for over a millennium.
