Folklore

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Folklore is the body of expressive culture shared by a particular group of people, culture, or subculture. This includes oral traditions such as tales, myths, legends, proverbs, jokes, and nursery rhymes, as well as the material culture and customs, rituals, dances, songs, festivals, folk art, and traditional knowledge of such groups. The term was coined in 1846 by the English antiquarian William John Thoms, who proposed "folk-lore" as a replacement for "popular antiquities".

Genres

Folklore is commonly divided into three broad types: verbal lore (folktales, legends, myths, ballads, proverbs, riddles, and place names), material lore (vernacular architecture, traditional crafts, folk art, foodways, and textiles), and customary lore (festivals, rituals, games, dances, and beliefs surrounding birth, marriage, and death).

Transmission and performance

Folklore is learned informally, usually by imitation and word of mouth, rather than through formal instruction. Because it is transmitted person to person, it varies regionally and over time; variants are a hallmark of genuine folk tradition. Scholars distinguish the bearer of tradition from the audience and study folklore as performance in social context, not merely as collected texts.

Study of folklore

The academic discipline of folklore studies (folkloristics) developed in the 19th century alongside nationalism, when collectors such as the Brothers Grimm in Germany and Elias Lönnrot in Finland gathered tales and epics. Modern folklorists examine how traditions express group identity, values, and worldview, and study emerging forms including urban legends, photocopy lore, and internet memes.

Functions

Folklorist William Bascom identified four functions of folklore: it entertains and amuses; it validates culture and justifies rituals and institutions; it educates, transmitting knowledge and values; and it maintains conformity, enforcing social norms and releasing social pressures.
