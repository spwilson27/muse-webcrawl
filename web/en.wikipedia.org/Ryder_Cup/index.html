The Ryder Cup is a biennial men's golf competition between teams from the United States and Europe. Named after the English businessman Samuel Ryder, who donated the trophy, it was first held in 1927 at Worcester Country Club in Massachusetts and is jointly administered by the PGA of America and Ryder Cup Europe.

Format

The competition is played over three days, usually Friday to Sunday, in match play. The first two days feature four foursomes (alternate shot) and four fourball matches each; the final day has twelve singles matches. Each match is worth one point, with 28 points available in total; the defending champion needs 14 points to retain the cup and the challenger 14½ to win it. Players receive no prize money, competing only for the trophy and national or continental pride.

History

The United States dominated for decades when the opposing team comprised only Great Britain and Ireland, prompting the expansion to all of continental Europe in 1979 — a change associated with Jack Nicklaus and Seve Ballesteros's era of European resurgence. Since then the contest has been closely fought, producing celebrated editions such as the 1999 "Battle of Brookline" and Europe's 2012 comeback at Medinah. It now ranks among the most watched sporting events in the world.
