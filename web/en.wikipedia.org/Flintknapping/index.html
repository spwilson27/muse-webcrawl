Flintknapping

Knapping, also called flintknapping when the material is flint, is the shaping of stone through the controlled removal of flakes by striking or pressure, a process known as lithic reduction. It was the principal technology by which prehistoric peoples made cutting tools — hand axes, scrapers, projectile points and blades — for over three million years, and it survives today as a craft, an experimental archaeological method, and in the manufacture of gunflints and architectural flintwork.

Principles

Suitable stones are fine-grained and brittle, fracturing conchoidally — like glass — when struck. A blow delivered near the edge of a prepared striking platform initiates a Hertzian cone that detaches a flake with a characteristic bulb of percussion. Skilled knappers manage platform angles and force to control flake size and shape, progressively reducing a core or thinning a biface. Techniques include hard-hammer percussion with stones, soft-hammer percussion with antler or wood billets, indirect percussion through a punch, and pressure flaking for fine retouch.

History

The earliest known knapped tools, from Lomekwi in Kenya (about 3.3 million years old) and the Oldowan industry of East Africa, predate the genus Homo itself. Acheulean hand axes, Levallois prepared-core techniques, Upper Paleolithic blade industries and the pressure-flaked points of the Neolithic trace a long refinement of the craft. Flintknapping persisted into the industrial era for gunflints — the town of Brandon in Suffolk, England, supplied them into the twentieth century — and for flint walling in chalk regions.

Modern practice

Experimental archaeologists knap stone to understand prehistoric technology, replicating ancient toolkits to test hypotheses about manufacture and use-wear. Flintknapping is also taught as a primitive-skills craft, with safety precautions against the extremely sharp flakes and the inhalation of silica dust.
