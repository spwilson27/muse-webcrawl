Fructan

Polymer of fructose molecules; storage carbohydrate in many plants

A fructan is a polymer of fructose molecules built on a sucrose primer. Fructans are storage carbohydrates found in more than 45,000 plant species — roughly 15% of flowering plants — including grasses, wheat, barley, onions, garlic, leeks, artichokes, asparagus and bananas, as well as in some bacteria. Humans lack enzymes to digest them, so fructans pass to the colon where they are fermented by gut bacteria, acting as dietary fibre and prebiotics.

Types

Fructans are classified by the linkage between fructose units. Inulin, the best known, consists mainly of β(2→1) linkages and is extracted commercially from chicory root as a food ingredient. Levan has β(2→6) linkages and is produced by bacteria and some grasses. Mixed-linkage and branched forms (graminans and agavins, the latter from agave) also occur. Short-chain fructans are called fructooligosaccharides (FOS).

Role in plants and diet

In plants, fructans accumulate in vacuoles as an energy reserve and contribute to tolerance of drought and cold, since their synthesis can proceed at low temperatures when starch synthesis stalls. In human nutrition, fructans are classed among the FODMAP carbohydrates: their fermentation produces gas, and they can provoke symptoms in people with irritable bowel syndrome, though they also promote beneficial bifidobacteria. In horses, excessive fructan intake from lush pasture is implicated in laminitis.
