Network throughput

Network throughput (or just throughput, when in context) refers to the rate of message delivery over a communication channel, such as Ethernet or packet radio, in a communication network. The data that these messages contain may be delivered over physical or logical links, or through network nodes. Throughput is usually measured in bits per second (bit/s, bps), and sometimes in packets per second (pps) or data packets per time slot.

Throughput versus bandwidth

Throughput is distinct from the theoretical maximum capacity of a link, often called bandwidth. The maximum throughput of a link is limited by its channel capacity, but the throughput actually achieved is reduced by protocol overhead, retransmissions, congestion, latency and the performance of the endpoints. The term goodput is sometimes used for the rate of useful application-level data delivered, excluding retransmitted and overhead data.

Factors affecting throughput

Factors that limit throughput include the properties of the transmission medium, the signal-to-noise ratio, protocol behaviour such as TCP congestion control and window sizes, the load on shared media, and processing limits of routers and hosts. In wireless networks, interference and contention further reduce achievable throughput below nominal rates.

Measurement

Throughput is measured with tools such as iperf, speed tests, and network analysers, typically by transferring data between two endpoints and recording the rate over a defined interval. Average, peak and sustained throughput may all be reported.
