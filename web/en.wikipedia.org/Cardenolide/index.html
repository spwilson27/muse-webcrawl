Cardenolide

ClassCardiac glycosides (steroids)
Distinguishing featureFive-membered lactone ring at C17
ExamplesDigoxin, digitoxin, ouabain, oleandrin
TargetNa+/K+-ATPase

A cardenolide is a type of steroid compound found in many plants and some animals, characterized by a five-membered unsaturated lactone (butenolide) ring attached at C17 of the steroid nucleus. Cardenolides are a subclass of cardiac glycosides when they bear sugar residues, and they act on the heart by inhibiting the sodium–potassium ATPase pump.

Digoxigenin, the aglycone of digoxin, showing the butenolide ring.

Sources

Cardenolides occur in foxgloves (Digitalis purpurea and D. lanata), oleander, lily of the valley, milkweeds (Asclepias), and Strophanthus seeds, among many others. They function in plants as chemical defences against herbivores. Some animals sequester them: monarch butterflies store milkweed cardenolides, and certain toads produce related bufadienolides — similar steroids with a six-membered lactone ring.

Pharmacology and toxicity

By inhibiting Na+/K+-ATPase, cardenolides raise intracellular sodium, which reduces calcium extrusion via the sodium–calcium exchanger; the resulting increase in intracellular calcium strengthens cardiac contraction (positive inotropy). Digoxin remains in clinical use for heart failure and rate control in atrial fibrillation, though its therapeutic window is narrow. Toxicity produces nausea, visual disturbances (such as yellow-tinted vision), and potentially fatal arrhythmias; severe poisoning is treated with digoxin-specific antibody fragments.

Ecology

Specialist insects such as monarchs, milkweed bugs, and some leaf beetles have evolved target-site insensitivity through mutations in their Na+/K+-ATPase, a textbook case of coevolution between plants and herbivores.
