Joseph von Fraunhofer

Born: March 6, 1787, Straubing, Bavaria
Died: June 7, 1826 (aged 39), Munich, Bavaria
Fields: Optics, physics
Known for: Fraunhofer lines; diffraction grating; high-quality telescope lenses

Joseph Ritter von Fraunhofer (March 6, 1787 – June 7, 1826) was a German-Bavarian physicist and optician. He is known for discovering the dark absorption lines in the spectrum of the Sun, now called Fraunhofer lines, and for making optical glass and achromatic telescope lenses of unsurpassed quality. His work laid the foundations of spectroscopy and precision optical instrument making.

Early life

Fraunhofer was born in Straubing, Bavaria, the eleventh child of a glassmaker. Orphaned at eleven, he was apprenticed to a Munich mirror-maker and lens-grinder. In 1801 the workshop building collapsed and buried him; his rescue attracted the attention of the future King Maximilian I Joseph, whose patronage bought him books and release from the harshest apprentice labour. He largely educated himself in mathematics and optics.

Optical Institute at Benediktbeuern

In 1806 Fraunhofer joined the Mathematical-Mechanical Institute of Reichenbach, Utzschneider and Liebherr, and from 1809 worked at its optical institute in the secularized monastery of Benediktbeuern. There he developed methods for melting pure flint glass and grinding lenses with unprecedented precision, becoming a partner and eventually directing the institute. His refracting telescopes, equipped with achromatic objectives and equatorial mountings of his design, were exported across Europe; the great Dorpat refractor supplied to Wilhelm Struve was used to measure stellar parallax.

Fraunhofer lines and the grating

While testing lenses around 1814, Fraunhofer used a prism and a theodolite telescope to examine sunlight and discovered hundreds of sharp dark lines in the solar spectrum, mapping 574 of them and labelling the most prominent A through K — designations still in use. The lines had been noted earlier by William Hyde Wollaston but not systematically studied. Fraunhofer also invented the diffraction grating, using it to measure wavelengths of light precisely. The chemical explanation of the lines came decades later with Kirchhoff and Bunsen, founding astronomical spectroscopy.

Death and legacy

Fraunhofer died in 1826, probably of pulmonary tuberculosis, aged only 39, and was knighted and honoured in his lifetime despite his artisanal origins. His grave in Munich bears the Latin inscription Approximavit sidera — "He brought the stars closer." The Fraunhofer Society, Germany's leading applied-research organization, is named after him.
