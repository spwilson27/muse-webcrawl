Flying buttress

The flying buttress (arc-boutant, arch buttress) is a specific form of buttress composed of an inclined bar of stone carried on an arch, extending from a large pier built some distance from the wall of a building to the upper part of that wall. It is a defining element of Gothic architecture, transferring the lateral thrust of high stone vaults outward so walls could be made thinner and filled with glass.

Structure

The buttress consists of the flyer — the arch that "flies" across the aisle roof — and a massive upright pier, often crowned with a decorative pinnacle whose weight helps keep the forces vertical. The arch meets the nave wall at the level where the vault's thrust is greatest, channeling it down through the pier to the ground.

Development

Precursors appeared in Romanesque construction, but the mature flying buttress emerged in late-12th-century France, notably at the nave of Notre-Dame de Paris (c. 1180). It enabled the soaring interiors of Chartres, Reims, and Amiens cathedrals, where slender walls dissolve into stained-glass windows. In High Gothic and Rayonnant designs, double-tiered flyers spanned double aisles.

Later use

Gothic Revival architects revived the form in the 19th century, and engineers have used analogous external arch struts in masonry and concrete construction. Beyond their structural role, flying buttresses became one of the most recognizable silhouettes in architecture.
