First cause

A first cause is the earliest or ultimate cause in a chain of causes, itself uncaused. The idea is central to the cosmological argument for the existence of God, in which the chain of causes and effects in the universe is held to require an initial uncaused cause, often identified with God.

In philosophy

Aristotle argued for an "unmoved mover" — a first cause of motion that is itself unmoved — in his Physics and Metaphysics. In medieval philosophy, Thomas Aquinas placed the argument from causation among his Five Ways, arguing that an infinite regress of efficient causes is impossible and that there must be a first efficient cause.

Criticisms

Critics, including David Hume and later Bertrand Russell, have questioned why the causal chain cannot be infinite, and why, if everything requires a cause, the first cause should be exempt. Others argue that the concept of causation may not apply to the universe as a whole or to its origin.
