Meteorite classification

Meteorite classification schemes divide meteorites — solid debris from asteroids, comets or planets that survives passage through the atmosphere to reach the ground — into groups with similar mineralogy, chemistry and origin. The broadest traditional division is into chondrites, achondrites and iron and stony-iron meteorites, reflecting whether the parent body remained undifferentiated or melted and separated into core, mantle and crust.

Chondrites

Chondrites, the most common falls, contain chondrules — millimetre-sized molten droplets from the early Solar System — and are subdivided into ordinary, carbonaceous and enstatite chondrites, with petrologic types 1-6 recording degrees of aqueous alteration and thermal metamorphism. Carbonaceous chondrites are rich in water and organic compounds.

Differentiated meteorites

Achondrites lack chondrules and come from melted parent bodies: groups include the HED meteorites (howardites, eucrites, diogenites) from asteroid Vesta, lunar meteorites, and Martian meteorites (shergottites, nakhlites, chassignites). Iron meteorites are fragments of metallic cores, classified by nickel content and trace elements and by structures such as the Widmanstätten pattern; stony-irons include pallasites (olivine crystals in metal, from a core-mantle boundary) and mesosiderites.

Modern schemes

Modern classification uses chemical and oxygen-isotope data to assign meteorites to clans and groups likely derived from a single parent body, and every classified meteorite is recorded in the Meteoritical Bulletin Database.
