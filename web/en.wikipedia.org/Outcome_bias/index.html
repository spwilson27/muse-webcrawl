Outcome bias

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The outcome bias is an error made in evaluating the quality of a decision when the outcome of that decision is already known. Specifically, the outcome effect occurs when the same behaviour produces more ethical condemnation when it happens to produce bad rather than good outcomes, even if the outcome is determined by chance.

While similar to the hindsight bias, the two phenomena are different. Hindsight bias focuses on memory distortion to favour the actor, while the outcome bias focuses exclusively on weighting the outcome heavier than other pieces of information in deciding if a past decision was correct.

Overview

One will often judge a past decision by its ultimate outcome instead of basing the judgment on the quality of the decision at the time it was made, given what was known at that time. This is an error because no decision-maker ever knows in advance whether or not a calculated risk will turn out for the best. The actual outcome of a decision will often be determined by chance; even good decisions can fail and even bad decisions can succeed. Judging decisions solely by their outcomes punishes reasonable risk-taking and rewards luck.

Research

The effect was demonstrated in experiments by Jonathan Baron and John Hershey (1988), in which subjects were presented with hypothetical decisions — for example, whether to undergo a risky operation — and asked to rate the quality of the decision after being told whether the outcome was favourable or unfavourable. Subjects consistently rated identical decisions as better when the outcome was good, even when the outcome was explicitly attributed to chance. The bias has since been replicated in medical, legal, financial, and managerial settings.

Examples

In medicine, a physician who makes a guideline-conforming choice that happens to end in a rare complication may be judged more harshly in peer review or malpractice proceedings than one whose identical choice ended well. In business, managers are often promoted or dismissed based on short-term results that reflect market conditions more than the soundness of their choices. In sport, a coach's gamble that succeeds is called bold, while the same gamble failing is called reckless.

Mitigation

Decision analysts recommend evaluating decisions against the information available at the time they were made, keeping decision journals, and using blinded or outcome-masked review in fields such as medicine and aviation safety. Separating the assessment of decision quality from the assessment of results is a central theme of the literature on decision-making under uncertainty.
