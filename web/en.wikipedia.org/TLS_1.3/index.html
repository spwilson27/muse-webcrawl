TLS 1.3 is the thirteenth version of the Transport Layer Security (TLS) protocol, defined in RFC 8446 and published in August 2018. It is widely used to secure communications over computer networks, including HTTPS web traffic, and improves on TLS 1.2 in both security and speed.

Improvements over TLS 1.2

TLS 1.3 removes support for obsolete and insecure features, including static RSA and Diffie–Hellman key exchange, RC4, MD5 and SHA-1 hashes, and compression. All key exchange mechanisms now provide perfect forward secrecy. The handshake was streamlined from two round trips to one (1-RTT), and an optional zero round-trip (0-RTT) resumption mode allows previously contacted servers to receive encrypted application data immediately, at some replay risk.

Handshake

In the TLS 1.3 handshake the client sends its supported cipher suites and a key share in the initial ClientHello; the server replies with its own key share, an encrypted certificate, and a Finished message. Supported key exchange is based on ephemeral Diffie–Hellman (finite-field or elliptic-curve), and only authenticated encryption with associated data (AEAD) ciphers such as AES-GCM and ChaCha20-Poly1305 are permitted. Much of the handshake after the ServerHello is encrypted, improving privacy.

Adoption

Major browsers and TLS libraries — including OpenSSL, BoringSSL, NSS, and SChannel — added support in 2018, and content delivery networks reported TLS 1.3 carrying a majority of HTTPS connections within a few years of standardization.
