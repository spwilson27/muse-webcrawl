Free float

Free float, also known as public float, is the proportion of a company's shares that are available for trading by the general public on a stock exchange — that is, shares not held by insiders, controlling shareholders, governments, or other strategic holders whose shares are effectively locked up or unlikely to trade. Free float is expressed either as an absolute number of shares or as a percentage of total shares outstanding.

Significance

Free float matters because it determines a stock's liquidity: the larger the float, the easier it is for investors to buy and sell without moving the price. Small floats tend to be more volatile, since modest orders can exhaust available supply. Free float also underpins the construction of most major stock market indices. Index providers such as MSCI and FTSE Russell, and the S&P 500, use float-adjusted market capitalization: a company's index weight equals its share price multiplied only by its free-float shares, so companies whose shares are mostly held by insiders count for less than their full market value would suggest.

Regulation and changes

Many exchanges impose minimum free-float requirements for listing, commonly around 25 percent of shares in public hands. A company's float changes over time as lock-up periods expire after an initial public offering, insiders sell shares, companies buy back stock, or large holders convert restricted holdings into tradable shares. A related concept, the float in project management, is unrelated; in finance the term may also refer to funds in transit in payment systems (see check float).
