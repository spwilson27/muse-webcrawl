Circle of Willis

SystemCirculatory system
SuppliesBrain
Named afterThomas Willis

The circle of Willis (also called cerebral arterial circle) is a circulatory anastomosis that supplies blood to the brain and surrounding structures in reptiles, birds and mammals, including humans. It is named after Thomas Willis (1621–1675), an English physician who described it in his 1664 work Cerebri Anatome.

Structure

The ring lies at the base of the brain around the optic chiasm and pituitary stalk. It is formed by the two internal carotid arteries joining, via the anterior communicating artery, the anterior cerebral arteries, and by the posterior cerebral arteries connected to the internal carotids by the posterior communicating arteries; the posterior circulation is supplied from the basilar artery, formed by the vertebral arteries.

Function

The circular arrangement provides redundant collateral circulation: if one part of the circle becomes blocked or narrowed, blood can flow around the ring to preserve cerebral perfusion. In practice, the circle is anatomically complete in only a minority of people; hypoplasia of one or more segments is common.

Clinical significance

The junctions of the circle are common sites for saccular ("berry") aneurysms, whose rupture causes subarachnoid haemorrhage. The circle is also important in moyamoya disease and in collateral supply in carotid stenosis.
