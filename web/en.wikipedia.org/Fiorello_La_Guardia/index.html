Fiorello Henry La Guardia (born Fiorello Enrico La Guardia; December 11, 1882 – September 20, 1947) was an American attorney and politician who represented New York in the U.S. House of Representatives and served as the 99th mayor of New York City from 1934 to 1945. Known as "the Little Flower" (a translation of his Italian first name, Fiorello), he was a short, irascible, and energetic reformer who attacked corruption, reorganized the city government, and became one of the most celebrated mayors in American history.

Early life

La Guardia was born in Greenwich Village, New York City, to Achille La Guardia, an Italian immigrant from Foggia who became a bandmaster in the U.S. Army, and Irene Luzzato Coen, a Jewish woman from Trieste. He spent much of his childhood at army posts in the Arizona Territory. After his father was discharged, the family lived in Trieste, where the young La Guardia worked as a clerk and interpreter at the American consulate in Budapest and Fiume, becoming fluent in several languages including Italian, German, Hungarian, Croatian, French, and Yiddish.

Congressional career

Returning to New York, La Guardia worked as an interpreter at Ellis Island while attending New York University School of Law. He was elected to Congress in 1916 from a Manhattan district and, after service as a pilot on the Italian-Austrian front in World War I, won again in 1918. Representing East Harlem from 1923 to 1933, he became a leading progressive voice, co-sponsoring the Norris–La Guardia Act of 1932, which restricted the use of injunctions against labor unions.

Mayor of New York City

Elected mayor in 1933 on a Fusion ticket in the wake of the Tammany Hall scandals that had driven Jimmy Walker from office, La Guardia won re-election in 1937 and 1941. His administration restored fiscal integrity, professionalized the civil service, unified the transit system, built public housing, and worked closely with Parks Commissioner Robert Moses to create parks, playgrounds, bridges, and parkways funded by New Deal money. He read the Sunday newspaper comics aloud over the radio during a 1945 newspaper delivery strike, an episode that cemented his folksy public image.

Later life and death

La Guardia declined to seek a fourth term in 1945. He served briefly as director general of the United Nations Relief and Rehabilitation Administration in 1946. He died of pancreatic cancer at his home in the Bronx on September 20, 1947. New York's LaGuardia Airport, opened in 1939, is named in his honor.
