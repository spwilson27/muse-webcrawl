1923 Great Kantō earthquake

The Great Kantō earthquake (関東大地震) struck the Kantō Plain on the Japanese island of Honshū at 11:58 a.m. on Saturday, September 1, 1923. The megathrust earthquake, with a magnitude estimated at 7.9–8.2, devastated Tokyo and the port city of Yokohama, and killed an estimated 105,000–142,000 people, most of them in the firestorms that followed.

The disaster

The quake struck at lunchtime, when many households were cooking over open fires. Fires broke out across the wooden cities and merged into fire whirlwinds; in one notorious incident, some 38,000 people who had taken refuge in an open space at the Honjo Clothing Depot were killed by a single fire whirlwind. Water mains ruptured, hampering firefighting. A tsunami of up to 10 metres struck parts of the coast, and landslides buried villages including Nebukawa.

Kantō Massacre

In the chaos, rumours spread that Koreans were committing arson and poisoning wells. Vigilante groups, police and soldiers massacred an estimated several thousand Koreans, along with Chinese residents and Japanese socialists mistaken for Koreans. The episode remains a painful subject in Japanese–Korean relations and is commemorated annually in Tokyo.

Aftermath

Plans for a radical reconstruction of Tokyo were scaled back, but the rebuilt capital gained modern infrastructure, parks and wider boulevards. September 1 is now Disaster Prevention Day in Japan, with nationwide drills. The earthquake reshaped Japanese society in the years before the rise of militarism in the 1930s.
