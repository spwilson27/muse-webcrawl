The Gothic War of 376–382 was a war between the Eastern Roman Empire and the Goths, precipitated by the mass migration of Gothic peoples across the Danube, fleeing the advance of the Huns. It is famous above all for the catastrophic Roman defeat at the Battle of Adrianople in 378, in which the emperor Valens was killed — an event often treated as a landmark in the decline of the Roman Empire.

Background

In 376, enormous numbers of Thervingian Goths under Fritigern and Alavivus petitioned Emperor Valens for permission to settle south of the Danube. Valens agreed, hoping for recruits and taxpayers. But Roman officials — notably the corrupt commanders Lupicinus and Maximus — profiteered from the refugees' desperation, starving them and trading food at extortionate prices. Mistreatment escalated into violence: after a failed Roman attempt to assassinate the Gothic leaders at a banquet in Marcianople, the Goths rose in revolt and were joined by the Greuthungi and other groups.

Course of the war

The Goths defeated Roman forces at Marcianople and ravaged Thrace. In 377 they were contained at the Battle of the Willows, but on 9 August 378, at Adrianople, the Gothic cavalry destroyed the eastern field army; Valens died on the field, his body never found. Perhaps two-thirds of the army perished. Ammianus Marcellinus compared the disaster to Cannae.

The new emperor, Theodosius I, proved unable to defeat the Goths decisively. After years of raiding and stalemate, he concluded a treaty in October 382: the Goths were settled in Thrace and Moesia as foederati, keeping their own leaders and laws, in exchange for military service — an unprecedented degree of autonomy for a barbarian group inside the empire.

Consequences

The settlement foreshadowed the later fragmentation of the western empire: within a generation, the Goths under Alaric would revolt again and, in 410, sack Rome itself.
