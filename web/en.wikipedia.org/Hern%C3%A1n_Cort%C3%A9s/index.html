Hernán Cortés de Monroy y Pizarro Altamirano (1485 – 2 December 1547) was a Spanish conquistador who led the expedition that caused the fall of the Aztec Empire and brought large portions of what is now mainland Mexico under the rule of the King of Castile in the early 16th century. Cortés was part of the generation of Spanish explorers and conquistadors who began the first phase of the Spanish colonization of the Americas.

Conquest of Mexico

Cortés arrived in the New World in 1504 and took part in the conquest of Cuba. In 1519, defying the cancellation of his commission by Cuba's governor, he sailed to Mexico with about 500 men. Landing near present-day Veracruz, he formed alliances with indigenous peoples hostile to the Aztecs, most importantly the Tlaxcalans, and relied on the interpreter La Malinche. After the massacre at Cholula, the retreat of the Noche Triste (1520), and a prolonged siege aided by a devastating smallpox epidemic, Tenochtitlan fell on 13 August 1521.

Later life

Cortés became the first governor of New Spain but was soon stripped of real power by royal officials and auditors. He explored Honduras and the Baja California peninsula, returned to Spain, and died near Seville in 1547, embittered by disputes with the Crown. His letters to Charles V (Cartas de relación) are a major primary source for the conquest. His legacy remains deeply contested, particularly in Mexico.
