The Mongol Empire of the 13th and 14th centuries was the largest contiguous empire in world history. Originating in the steppes of Central Asia, it emerged from the unification of nomadic tribes under Genghis Khan, who was proclaimed universal ruler of the Mongols in 1206. Through campaigns of extraordinary speed and destructiveness, the empire came to stretch from the Pacific Ocean and the South China Sea to Eastern Europe and the Levant.

Conquests

Genghis Khan first subdued Western Xia and the Jin dynasty of northern China, then annihilated the Khwarazmian Empire of Persia and Central Asia (1219–1221). His successors completed the conquest of the Kievan Rus' principalities (1237–1240), invaded Poland and Hungary (1241), destroyed Baghdad and ended the Abbasid caliphate under Hülegü (1258), and finally extinguished the Song dynasty of southern China, a process completed by Kublai Khan in 1279. Attempts to conquer Mamluk Egypt (checked at Ain Jalut in 1260), Vietnam, and Japan — where typhoons wrecked the invasion fleets of 1274 and 1281 — marked the empire's limits; see also the invasion of Java of 1293.

Pax Mongolica and administration

Within its borders the empire secured the trans-Eurasian trade routes of the Silk Road, operating the yam relay-post system, issuing passports (paiza) to merchants and envoys, and practising broad religious toleration. Mongol rule facilitated the transfer of technologies — gunpowder, printing, astronomy and medicine — between China, the Islamic world and Europe, and enabled travellers such as Marco Polo to cross the continent.

Division and decline

Succession disputes after the death of Möngke Khan in 1259, including the Toluid Civil War between Kublai and Ariq Böke, broke the empire into four largely independent khanates: the Yuan dynasty in China, the Ilkhanate in Persia, the Golden Horde on the Pontic steppe, and the Chagatai Khanate in Central Asia. The Yuan fell to the Ming in 1368; the other khanates endured, in mutated forms, for centuries.
