The Battle of the Little Bighorn, known to the Lakota as the Battle of the Greasy Grass and commonly as Custer's Last Stand, was fought on 25–26 June 1876 along the Little Bighorn River in present-day Montana. An alliance of Lakota, Northern Cheyenne and Arapaho warriors overwhelmed the U.S. 7th Cavalry Regiment; the five companies under Lieutenant Colonel George Armstrong Custer were annihilated.

Background

The Great Sioux War of 1876 followed the discovery of gold in the Black Hills—land guaranteed to the Lakota by the Treaty of Fort Laramie (1868)—and the refusal of "non-treaty" bands under leaders such as Sitting Bull and Crazy Horse to move to reservations. A large encampment, perhaps the biggest gathering of Plains Indians in history, assembled on the Little Bighorn. Custer, under orders from General Terry, located the village and attacked without waiting for reinforcements or confirming its size.

The battle

Custer divided his regiment into three battalions. Major Reno's attack on the south end of the village was repulsed and his men were besieged on the bluffs east of the river, joined by Captain Benteen's battalion. Custer's own five companies, moving north along the bluffs, were enveloped by warriors led in the fighting by Gall, Crazy Horse and others; within about an hour the entire detachment of some 210 men was killed on and around the hill now called Last Stand Hill. Reno and Benteen held out until the village withdrew on 26 June as Terry's column approached.

Aftermath and legacy

News of the disaster reached the East during the nation's Centennial celebrations and hardened federal resolve: the great village dispersed, and within a year most of its people had surrendered or fled to Canada; the Black Hills were seized in 1877. The battle has been retold ever since in books, paintings (including the famous Budweiser-distributed lithograph), and film. The battlefield is preserved as Little Bighorn Battlefield National Monument; an Indian Memorial was dedicated in 2003 near the cavalry monument.
