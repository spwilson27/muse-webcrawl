Point of view (philosophy)

In philosophy, a point of view is a specific attitude or manner through which a person thinks about something — the perspective from which a subject is considered, judged, or experienced. The notion is closely connected to perspectivism, the view associated especially with Friedrich Nietzsche that knowledge and truth are always apprehended from some perspective and that there is no "view from nowhere."

Concepts

A point of view in philosophy is not merely a physical vantage point but a structured outlook: a set of beliefs, concepts, values, experiences, and dispositions that shapes what a person notices and how they interpret it. Philosophers distinguish between the first-person (subjective) point of view, from which one's own experiences are directly known, and third-person (objective) descriptions given in impersonal terms. Thomas Nagel famously argued that subjective experience — "what it is like" to be a creature — cannot be fully captured by objective physical description, yet also defended the human capacity to strive toward greater objectivity by transcending particular viewpoints.

Point of view and truth

Perspectivists hold that the dependence of knowledge on perspective does not reduce to crude relativism: perspectives can be compared, criticized, and combined. In epistemology, "standpoint theory" argues that socially situated perspectives — for example those of marginalized groups — can confer epistemic advantages in certain domains. In ethics and aesthetics, questions about point of view concern how much weight to give impartiality versus partial commitments.

In literature and other fields

The term is also used in narratology for the perspective through which a story is told (first person, third person, omniscient), and in visual art for the position from which a scene is depicted. In each case the core idea is the same: the position from which something is apprehended affects what is apprehended.
