East Anglia

East Anglia is a geographical area in the east of England, generally defined as comprising the counties of Norfolk and Suffolk, and often also Cambridgeshire and Essex (in whole or in part). The name derives from the Anglo-Saxon Kingdom of the East Angles, itself named for the Angles, a Germanic people from Angeln in northern Germany who settled the area in the fifth and sixth centuries. The region forms a distinctive low-lying bulge of coastline between the Wash and the Thames estuary.

Geography

East Anglia is one of the flattest and driest parts of Britain. Its landscapes include the drained marshland of the Fens (partly below sea level), the Breckland heaths, the Broads — a network of shallow lakes and rivers now protected as a national park — and long sandy and shingle coasts facing the North Sea. Principal settlements include Norwich (the historic regional capital), Ipswich, Cambridge, Peterborough, Colchester and Chelmsford. The University of East Anglia, founded in Norwich in 1963, takes its name from the region.

History

The East Anglian kingdom was one of the Anglo-Saxon kingdoms; its kings included Rædwald, often identified with the ship burial at Sutton Hoo. The area was overrun by the Danes in the ninth century and became part of the Danelaw before being reconquered by Edward the Elder. In the Middle Ages Norwich was among England's largest cities, wealthy from the wool trade with Flanders, and the region prospered from agriculture — a specialisation that continues, with East Anglia sometimes called Britain's breadbasket for its arable farming.
