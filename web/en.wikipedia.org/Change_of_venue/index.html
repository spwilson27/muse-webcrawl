Change of venue

Legal areaCriminal procedure, civil procedure
GroundsPrejudicial publicity, local bias, convenience
AlternativeChange of venire (imported jury)

A change of venue is the legal term for moving a trial to a new location. In a change of venue, a different court (often in a different geographic district or county) hears the case. Judges typically order a change of venue when they determine that the original location cannot provide a fair trial, most often because of extensive pre-trial publicity that may have prejudiced the local jury pool.

Grounds

Motions for a change of venue are most common in high-profile criminal cases, where saturation media coverage, community outrage, or the prominence of the parties makes an impartial local jury unlikely. Courts consider factors such as the size and character of the community, the nature and extent of publicity, the time elapsed since the events, and the difficulty experienced in seating jurors during selection. A related but distinct remedy is a change of venire, in which jurors are imported from another district while the trial remains in place.

Notable examples

Famous venue changes in American legal history include the trial of Timothy McVeigh, moved from Oklahoma City to Denver following the Oklahoma City bombing, and the trials arising from the Rodney King beating, moved from Los Angeles to Simi Valley — a relocation whose consequences, acquittals followed by the 1992 Los Angeles riots, remain a standard case study in the doctrine. Civil cases may also be transferred between federal districts for the convenience of parties and witnesses under 28 U.S.C. § 1404.
