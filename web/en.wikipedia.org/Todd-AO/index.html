Todd-AO is an American post-production and exhibition company and the eponymous 70mm widescreen film format it developed in the early 1950s. Created by showman Michael Todd and the American Optical Company, Todd-AO was designed to give audiences an immersive, wraparound image — Todd's famous aim was "Cinerama out of one hole" — using a single camera and projector rather than Cinerama's three-strip system.

Format

Todd-AO photography uses 65mm film running at 30 frames per second (in its first two productions), with a spherical lens and an aspect ratio of about 2.20:1 on 70mm release prints, the extra width of the print carrying six channels of magnetic stereo sound. The very wide "bug-eye" lenses gave a deep, curved-screen image intended to fill the viewer's peripheral vision. Later productions standardised on 24 frames per second for compatibility with other 70mm systems.

History

The format premiered with the film of Rodgers and Hammerstein's Oklahoma! (1955) and Around the World in 80 Days (1956), which won the Academy Award for Best Picture. Major Todd-AO productions followed, including South Pacific (1958), Can-Can (1960), Cleopatra (1963) and The Sound of Music (1965), one of the most commercially successful films of its era. The company diversified into sound post-production, becoming one of Hollywood's major sound houses, and the Todd-AO name long outlived the camera format itself. The brand passed through several owners and its sound facilities were eventually absorbed into larger post-production groups.

Legacy

Todd-AO helped establish 70mm as the prestige large format of the roadshow era. Reduction prints in 35mm CinemaScope-compatible form allowed general release, and the format's cameras and lenses influenced later 65mm systems such as Super Panavision 70.
