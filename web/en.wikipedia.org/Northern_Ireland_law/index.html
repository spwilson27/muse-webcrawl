Northern Ireland law is the legal system of Northern Ireland, one of the three distinct legal jurisdictions of the United Kingdom, alongside England and Wales and Scotland. It is a common law jurisdiction, closely related to the law of England and Wales but with its own courts, statutes, and legal profession, reflecting Northern Ireland's separate existence since the partition of Ireland in 1921.

Sources

The law derives from common law precedent, statutes of the Northern Ireland Assembly on devolved matters, Acts of the UK Parliament, and Orders in Council made during periods of direct rule. Before 1921, the law was that of Ireland as a whole; the Government of Ireland Act 1920 created the Parliament of Northern Ireland, whose legislation remained in force after its abolition in 1972 unless repealed.

Courts

The court structure consists of the Court of Judicature of Northern Ireland, comprising the Court of Appeal and the High Court, together with Crown Courts, county courts, and magistrates' courts. Appeals on points of law of general public importance, and devolution issues, go to the Supreme Court of the United Kingdom in London. The Lord Chief Justice of Northern Ireland heads the judiciary.

Devolution and the peace process

Under the Northern Ireland Act 1998, which implemented the Good Friday Agreement, policing and justice powers were eventually devolved to the Northern Ireland Executive in 2010, the last major policing powers to transfer. Legacy issues arising from the Troubles — including investigations into conflict-era deaths — have continued to shape Northern Ireland law, most controversially through the Northern Ireland Troubles (Legacy and Reconciliation) Act 2023.
