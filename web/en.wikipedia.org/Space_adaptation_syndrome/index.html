Space adaptation syndrome (SAS), also called space motion sickness, is a condition experienced by a majority of astronauts during the first days of a spaceflight as the body adapts to weightlessness. It was first documented during the early crewed missions and publicly acknowledged after Gherman Titov reported disorientation on Vostok 2 in 1961.

Symptoms and cause

Symptoms resemble terrestrial motion sickness: nausea, vomiting, headache, dizziness, lethargy and difficulty concentrating. The leading explanation is sensory conflict — in microgravity the otolith organs of the inner ear no longer signal a consistent "down", while the eyes report normal orientation, and the brain interprets the mismatch as poisoning, triggering nausea. Head movements, especially pitch and roll, provoke symptoms.

Management

The syndrome generally resolves within two to four days as the nervous system reinterprets vestibular signals. Crews schedule light duties during the first days of a mission and avoid spacewalks until symptoms pass. Anti-motion sickness medications such as promethazine and scopolamine are used, though they can cause drowsiness. Susceptibility on Earth-based tests does not reliably predict who will be affected, and prior flight experience reduces but does not eliminate risk.
