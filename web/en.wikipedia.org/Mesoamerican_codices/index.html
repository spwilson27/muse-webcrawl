Mesoamerican codices

Mesoamerican codices are manuscripts written by indigenous peoples of central Mexico and the Maya region before and shortly after the Spanish conquest. They were painted on bark paper (amatl), deerskin, or cloth, folded screen-style, and contain calendars, rituals, histories, genealogies, tribute records, and maps.

Pre-conquest codices

Few pre-Hispanic books survive, as many were destroyed during the conquest and evangelization. The Maya codices — the Dresden, Madrid, Paris, and the disputed Grolier (Maya Codex of Mexico, authenticated in 2018 as the oldest surviving book of the Americas, c. 1119–1121) — deal mainly with astronomy, divination, and ritual. Central Mexican screenfolds such as the Codex Borgia group present divinatory almanacs, while Mixtec codices (Zouche-Nuttall, Bodley, Colombino) record dynastic history.

Colonial codices

After the conquest, indigenous scribes (tlacuiloque) produced thousands of manuscripts combining native pictorial conventions with alphabetic Nahuatl, Mixtec, and Spanish text. Notable examples include the Florentine Codex compiled under Bernardino de Sahagún, the Codex Mendoza (a record of Aztec tribute and history), the Lienzo de Tlaxcala, and the Techialoyan codices.

Materials and format

Most codices are screenfolds: long strips folded accordion-style between wooden covers. Pigments were mineral and organic, applied with brushes; pages were often burnished with a gesso-like coating. Modern scholarship reads them as a mixed pictorial-phonetic system, with Nahua glyphs increasingly shown to carry phonetic values.
