Vitis vinifera

Grapes of Vitis vinifera

Kingdom: Plantae

Order: Vitales

Family: Vitaceae

Genus: Vitis

Species: V. vinifera (L.)

Vitis vinifera, the common grape vine, is a species of flowering plant native to the Mediterranean region, Central Europe, and southwestern Asia, from Morocco and Portugal north to southern Germany and east to northern Iran. It is the principal species used in winemaking worldwide and also supplies most table grapes and raisins.

Description

Vitis vinifera is a liana growing 12–15 metres tall, with flaky bark, palmately lobed leaves, and tendrils opposite the leaves. The fruit is a berry — a grape — varying in colour from green to black depending on variety, and containing seeds. Wild vines are dioecious, while cultivated forms are hermaphroditic and usually propagated clonally by cuttings to preserve varietal character.

Domestication and varieties

The species was domesticated in the South Caucasus and Near East roughly 6,000–8,000 years ago, with the earliest winery evidence from Georgia and Armenia. Thousands of cultivars exist, including Cabernet Sauvignon, Chardonnay, Merlot, Pinot noir, Riesling, and Sangiovese, giving rise to the world's wine industry. Because V. vinifera is highly susceptible to the phylloxera aphid, most vines outside a few refuges are grafted onto rootstocks of North American Vitis species.

Uses

Beyond wine, the grapes are eaten fresh, dried as raisins, sultanas, and currants, and processed into juice, vinegar, and grape seed oil. Leaves are used in cooking, notably for dolma.
