Literary journalism

Literary journalism, also called narrative journalism or (in its best-known 1960s–70s form) New Journalism, is a form of nonfiction writing that combines factual reporting with the narrative techniques of fiction — scene construction, dialogue, point of view, and symbolic detail — while remaining accountable to the facts. Practitioners immerse themselves in their subjects, often over long periods, and write in a distinctive authorial voice.

Characteristics

Tom Wolfe, in his 1973 anthology The New Journalism, identified four devices borrowed from the realist novel: constructing scenes rather than narrating summary, recording dialogue in full, presenting scenes through the eyes of a participant (third-person point of view), and noting everyday status details. Unlike fiction, however, literary journalism claims that everything in the account actually happened and was verified through reporting.

History

Precursors include Daniel Defoe's documentary writing, nineteenth-century magazine sketches, Stephen Crane's reporting, and George Orwell's essays such as Down and Out in Paris and London (1933) and Homage to Catalonia (1938). The form flowered in American magazines of the 1960s — Esquire, New York, Harper's, and The New Yorker — with works such as Truman Capote's In Cold Blood (1966), which he called a "nonfiction novel"; Hunter S. Thompson's "gonzo" reporting; Joan Didion's essays; Norman Mailer's The Armies of the Night (1968); and Gay Talese's profiles.

Notable practitioners

  • John Hersey — Hiroshima (1946)
  • Truman Capote — In Cold Blood
  • Joan Didion — Slouching Towards Bethlehem (1968)
  • Tom Wolfe — The Electric Kool-Aid Acid Test (1968), The Right Stuff (1979)
  • Hunter S. Thompson — Fear and Loathing in Las Vegas (1971)
  • Ryszard Kapuściński — Polish reporter and author of The Emperor
  • Svetlana Alexievich — Belarusian oral historian, Nobel laureate 2015
  • David Grann, Katherine Boo, and Adrian Nicole LeBlanc — contemporary long-form practitioners

Criticism

Critics have questioned the verifiability of reconstructed dialogue and interior thoughts, composite characters, and the temptation to privilege narrative neatness over factual ambiguity. Defenders respond that the form's immersion and verification standards can exceed those of daily news reporting. Undergraduate and graduate programmes in creative nonfiction now teach the form widely.
