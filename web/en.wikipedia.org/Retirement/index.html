Retirement

Retirement is the withdrawal from one's position, occupation or active working life. A person may also semi-retire by reducing work hours or workload. Many countries have national pension systems — public, occupational and private — intended to provide income in retirement.

History

Retirement as a mass institution is relatively modern. Germany under Otto von Bismarck introduced the first old-age social insurance in 1889 (initially from age 70). In the United States, the Social Security Act of 1935 set the framework for public pensions; statutory retirement ages in many developed countries have since clustered around 62–67, and are rising with increased life expectancy.

Financing

Retirement income commonly comes from state pensions financed on a pay-as-you-go basis, employer schemes (defined benefit or defined contribution), and personal savings and investments. The "three-pillar model" promoted by the World Bank combines a public safety net, mandatory funded savings, and voluntary provision.

Early and phased retirement

Early retirement before the statutory age, sometimes with reduced benefits, is offered in many systems; phased retirement allows a gradual reduction of working hours. Trends such as the FIRE movement (Financial Independence, Retire Early) pursue retirement far earlier through high savings rates.
