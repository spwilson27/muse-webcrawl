Elder Futhark

The Elder Futhark (also called the Elder Fuþark or Germanic Futhark) is the oldest form of the runic alphabets. It was used by Germanic peoples for writing Northwest Germanic dialects from roughly the 2nd to the 8th centuries AD, appearing on jewellery, amulets, tools, weapons and runestones across northern Europe. Its 24 runes are conventionally arranged in three groups of eight called ættir.

The runes

The alphabet takes its name from its first six runes: Fehu, Uruz, Thurisaz, Ansuz, Raidho and Kenaz. The full sequence is: ᚠ f, ᚢ u, ᚦ þ (th), ᚨ a, ᚱ r, ᚲ k, ᚷ g, ᚹ w, ᚺ h, ᚾ n, ᛁ i, ᛃ j, ᛇ ï, ᛈ p, ᛉ z, ᛊ s, ᛏ t, ᛒ b, ᛖ e, ᛗ m, ᛚ l, ᛜ ŋ (ng), ᛞ d, ᛟ o. The runic letters were designed with vertical and diagonal strokes, avoiding curves and horizontal lines, so they could be carved easily into wood and stone.

Origins and use

The script probably derives from an Old Italic alphabet (such as Raetic or Etruscan-derived alphabets of northern Italy) adapted around the 1st century AD. The earliest known inscriptions include the Vimose comb (c. AD 160) and the Thorsberg chape. Around 500 inscriptions survive, far fewer than for the later Younger Futhark. As the Germanic languages changed, the Elder Futhark developed into the Anglo-Saxon futhorc in England and Frisia and the reduced Younger Futhark in Scandinavia during the Migration Period and early Viking Age.
