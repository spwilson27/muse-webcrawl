Promissory estoppel

Promissory estoppel is a legal principle in the law of contracts (and more broadly an equitable estoppel) under which a promise may be enforceable by law even though it was made without formal consideration, when the promisor made the promise expecting the promisee to rely on it, the promisee did so rely to their detriment, and injustice can be avoided only by enforcement. The doctrine prevents a promisor from arguing that the underlying promise is unenforceable for lack of consideration after another party has reasonably relied upon it.

Elements

American law, as restated in section 90 of the Restatement (Second) of Contracts, requires: (1) a clear and definite promise; (2) the promisor's reasonable expectation that the promise would induce action or forbearance; (3) actual, reasonable reliance by the promisee; and (4) that enforcement is necessary to avoid injustice. Recovery is typically limited by reliance damages — compensation for what the promisee lost by relying — rather than the full expectation value of the promise, though courts may grant full enforcement as justice requires.

History and development

The doctrine developed from 19th-century equitable cases and Lord Denning's influential judgment in Central London Property Trust v High Trees House (1947), in which a wartime agreement to accept reduced rent was held binding for the war years even without consideration. In England promissory estoppel traditionally operates as a "shield, not a sword" — it can defend against enforcement of strict legal rights but usually cannot found a new cause of action — whereas Australian courts, in Waltons Stores (Interstate) v Maher (1988), allowed it broader offensive use. In the United States, promissory estoppel is routinely pleaded as an alternative to a contract claim.

Applications

Courts apply the doctrine to gratuitous promises relied upon in employment offers (a job candidate who resigns a prior position and relocates on the strength of an offer), charitable subscriptions promised to institutions that build in reliance, and promises made during contract negotiations that fall short of agreement. It does not ordinarily override a formal contract between the parties covering the same subject.
