Lancelot Brown (1716–1783), more commonly known as Capability Brown, was an English gardener and landscape architect, remembered as "the last of the great English 18th-century artists to be accorded his due" and "England's greatest gardener". He designed more than 170 parks, many of which survive today, and his smooth, naturalistic style — sweeping lawns down to the water's edge, serpentine lakes, belts and clumps of trees — came to define the English landscape garden for the world.

He earned his nickname from his habit of telling clients that their estates had fine "capabilities" for improvement.

Early life and Stowe

Brown was born in the village of Kirkharle in Northumberland, the fifth child of a land agent and a chambermaid. Educated at Cambo school until he was sixteen, he worked as an apprentice gardener on the Kirkharle estate of Sir William Loraine, then moved south, working at Wotton House in Buckinghamshire before joining the staff of Lord Cobham at Stowe in 1741. At Stowe, under the head gardener's successors to William Kent, Brown rose to head gardener in 1749, married Bridget Waymark, and began taking independent commissions with Cobham's encouragement, notably at Croome Court for the Earl of Coventry, where he also designed the house and interiors.

Independent practice

Leaving Stowe in 1751, Brown set up in Hammersmith, London, and built the most successful landscape practice of the century. At Blenheim Palace for the Duke of Marlborough he dammed the River Glyme to create the great lake, flooding Vanbrugh's Grand Bridge to its arches. At Chatsworth, Petworth, Burghley, Harewood, Bowood, Longleat, Cliveden and dozens of other seats he swept away formal parterres and avenues near the house, substituting grass, scattered trees, hidden drives, ha-has that concealed the boundary between garden and farmland, and kitchen gardens discreetly screened by shrubberies.

In 1764 Brown was appointed Master Gardener at Hampton Court Palace by George III. He famously declined to alter the palace's Great Vine's surroundings as he found them and, in a well-known anecdote, refused a commission from an Irish peer because he "had not yet finished England". Brown died in London in February 1783 and was buried at Fenstanton in Cambridgeshire, his modest estate.

Style and legacy

Brown's mature style offered a seamless, apparently natural idealised landscape: undulating turf, mirror-like lakes formed by invisible dams, belts of woodland curving around estate boundaries, and individual specimen trees placed for picturesque effect. Critics such as Uvedale Price later attacked the style as bare and monotonous, and many Brownian parks were overlaid with Victorian bedding schemes, but twentieth-century scholarship restored his reputation. The tercentenary of his birth in 2016 was marked by a national festival celebrating over 170 surviving landscapes, and his influence extends through public parks and suburban layouts across the English-speaking world.
