Banana Wars

The Banana Wars were a series of United States military interventions, occupations, and police actions in Central America and the Caribbean between the end of the Spanish–American War in 1898 and the advent of the Good Neighbor Policy in 1934. The interventions were frequently conducted by the United States Marine Corps, which developed the Small Wars Manual from its experience.

Background

The stated motives included protecting American lives and property, enforcing financial stability and debt repayment, and preventing European influence in the region, in line with the Monroe Doctrine and its Roosevelt Corollary. Critics associated the interventions with the interests of American corporations, above all the United Fruit Company, whose extensive banana plantations and political influence in countries such as Honduras and Guatemala gave rise to the term "banana republic".

Interventions

Major episodes included the long occupations of Haiti (1915–1934) and the Dominican Republic (1916–1924), repeated interventions in Nicaragua (culminating in the 1927–1933 occupation and the insurgency of Augusto César Sandino), and landings in Honduras, Panama, Cuba, and Mexico. Marine officer Smedley Butler, twice a Medal of Honor recipient, later became a prominent critic of the interventions.

End

President Franklin D. Roosevelt's Good Neighbor Policy renounced armed intervention in Latin America, and the last Marines withdrew from Haiti in 1934.
