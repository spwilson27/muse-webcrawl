Alexei Abrikosov

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Born: 25 June 1928, Moscow, Russian SFSR, Soviet Union

Died: 29 March 2017 (aged 88), Palo Alto, California, US

Known for: Theory of type-II superconductors; Abrikosov vortices

Awards: Nobel Prize in Physics (2003)

Alexei Alexeyevich Abrikosov (25 June 1928 – 29 March 2017) was a Soviet, Russian and American theoretical physicist whose main contributions are in the field of condensed matter physics. He shared the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2003 with Vitaly Ginzburg and Anthony Leggett for pioneering work on superconductors and superfluids.

Career

Abrikosov studied at Moscow State University and worked at the Institute for Physical Problems under Lev Landau, receiving his doctorate in 1951. He later worked at the Landau Institute for Theoretical Physics, which he helped to found, and taught in Moscow for many years. In 1991 he moved to the United States, joining Argonne National Laboratory in Illinois, and became a US citizen in 1999.

Type-II superconductors

Working within the Ginzburg–Landau theory of superconductivity, Abrikosov showed in the 1950s that a second class of superconductors — now called type-II superconductors — can allow magnetic field to penetrate in the form of a regular lattice of quantised vortices, each carrying one flux quantum. These Abrikosov vortices explain how such materials can remain superconducting in very high magnetic fields, a property exploited in superconducting magnets. With collaborators he also developed influential work on quantum electrodynamics at high energies, the theory of normal metals, and the method of quantum field theory in statistical physics, co-authoring the classic textbook Methods of Quantum Field Theory in Statistical Physics.
