The Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms (French: Charte canadienne des droits et libertés) is a bill of rights entrenched in the Constitution of Canada, forming the first part of the Constitution Act, 1982. The Charter guarantees certain political rights to Canadian citizens and civil rights to everyone in Canada from the policies and actions of all levels of government, and is designed to unify Canadians around a common set of values.

The Charter came into force on April 17, 1982, when the Constitution Act was proclaimed by Queen Elizabeth II in Ottawa, patriating the Canadian constitution from the United Kingdom. The equality rights provision, section 15, was delayed three years to allow governments to review their legislation, taking effect on April 17, 1985.

Contents

The Charter's guarantees include fundamental freedoms (section 2: conscience, religion, expression, press, peaceful assembly, and association); democratic rights (sections 3–5); mobility rights (section 6); legal rights (sections 7–14, including life, liberty and security of the person, protection against unreasonable search and seizure, and the presumption of innocence); equality rights (section 15); and official-language and minority-language education rights (sections 16–23). Section 25 protects Aboriginal and treaty rights from being abrogated by the Charter.

Limitations and the notwithstanding clause

Two features distinguish the Charter from many other rights instruments. Section 1 permits rights to be subject to "such reasonable limits prescribed by law as can be demonstrably justified in a free and democratic society"; the Supreme Court developed the Oakes test for applying this clause in R v Oakes (1986). Section 33, the notwithstanding clause, permits Parliament or a provincial legislature to declare that a law operates notwithstanding certain Charter rights for renewable five-year periods — a controversial power used sparingly, most prominently by Quebec, Alberta, Ontario, and Saskatchewan.

Impact

The Charter transformed Canadian law, enabling courts to strike down legislation on rights grounds and shaping jurisprudence on abortion (R v Morgentaler, 1988), same-sex marriage, freedom of religion, and assisted dying (Carter v Canada, 2015). Before 1982, an implied bill of rights existed in the common law, supplemented by the statutory Canadian Bill of Rights of 1960, which had limited enforceability.
