Gable

The triangular gable end of a pitched-roof house

A gable is the generally triangular portion of a wall at the end of a ridged roof, between the edges of the intersecting roof pitches. The shape of the gable and how it is detailed depends on the structural system used, which in turn is influenced by climate and the availability of materials, as well as by aesthetic concerns.

Forms

The wall beneath a gable may be a continuation of the facade below, and the gable may overhang it, as in the gablet or cross-gable arrangements of vernacular building. Decorative treatments are common: bargeboards along the sloping edges, finials at the apex, and, in historic European towns, elaborate shaped gables — stepped (crow-stepped) gables in Dutch and Flemish architecture, and curvilinear Dutch gables that became a hallmark of Amsterdam canal houses and Cape Dutch buildings in South Africa. A gable that projects forward from a roof slope is related to the dormer; a wall rising above the roof line as a parapet gable is common in Georgian and medieval architecture.

Considerations

Gable ends are structurally vulnerable: the triangular wall section above the last ceiling diaphragm can be weak in high winds and earthquakes if not braced, and gable-ended roofs perform worse in hurricanes than hipped roofs, whose slopes on all sides provide bracing. The orientation of a house so that its gable faces the street is sometimes called "gable-front", a common form in 19th-century Greek Revival housing in North America.
