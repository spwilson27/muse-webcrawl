In mathematics, a partial differential equation (PDE) is an equation which imposes relations between the various partial derivatives of a multivariable function. The function is often thought of as an unknown to be solved for, and the equation describes physical quantities such as temperature, pressure or displacement evolving in space and time. PDEs are the fundamental language of mathematical physics and engineering, underlying fluid dynamics, electromagnetism, heat conduction, elasticity, quantum mechanics and general relativity.

Introduction

The order of a PDE is the order of its highest derivative. A PDE is linear if the unknown and its derivatives appear only to the first power and are not multiplied together; otherwise it is nonlinear. The best-known second-order linear PDEs are the elliptic Laplace equation, the parabolic heat equation, and the hyperbolic wave equation:

Laplace: ∇²u = 0   ·   Heat: ∂u/∂t = α∇²u   ·   Wave: ∂²u/∂t² = c²∇²u

To determine a particular solution, the equation must be supplemented with boundary conditions (Dirichlet, Neumann or mixed) and, for evolution equations, initial conditions. A problem is "well-posed" in Hadamard's sense if a solution exists, is unique, and depends continuously on the data.

Methods of solution

Analytic methods include separation of variables (leading to Fourier series and special functions), integral transforms, the method of characteristics for first-order and hyperbolic equations, and Green's functions. Famous exact solutions are rare for nonlinear equations, so numerical methods dominate applications: finite difference, finite element and finite volume methods discretise the domain and solve large linear systems computationally. Weak (variational) formulations and Sobolev spaces provide the modern rigorous framework in which existence and regularity are proved.

Famous equations and open problems

The Navier–Stokes equations of fluid mechanics are a nonlinear system whose global existence and smoothness in three dimensions is one of the Millennium Prize Problems, worth one million US dollars. Other landmark PDEs include Maxwell's equations, the Schrödinger equation, the Einstein field equations and the Black–Scholes equation of finance. The classification theory of second-order linear PDEs (elliptic/parabolic/hyperbolic) parallels the classification of conic sections, and Schauder theory and De Giorgi–Nash–Moser regularity are among the field's central achievements.
