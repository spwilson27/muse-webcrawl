Fiduciary duty

A fiduciary duty is the legal responsibility to act solely in the best interest of another party (the beneficiary or principal). A person or entity who owes this duty is called a fiduciary. Fiduciary duties arise in relationships of trust and confidence, such as those between a trustee and a beneficiary, a corporate director and shareholders, a lawyer and a client, or an investment adviser and an investor.

Principal duties

The two cardinal duties are the duty of loyalty — the fiduciary must act solely in the principal's interest, avoid conflicts of interest, and not profit secretly from the position — and the duty of care — the fiduciary must act with the prudence and diligence that a reasonable person in that position would exercise. In corporate law, directors' decisions are often protected by the business judgment rule when these duties are met.

Breach and remedies

A breach of fiduciary duty may result in remedies including damages, disgorgement of profits, rescission of transactions, and injunctions. Because the relationship depends on trust, courts apply standards stricter than the ordinary tort duty of care; for example, a fiduciary who profits from an opportunity belonging to the principal may be required to account for the entire profit even if the principal suffered no loss.

Examples

Fiduciary relationships commonly include trustee–beneficiary, guardian–ward, agent–principal, attorney–client, and corporate officer–shareholder relationships. Pension fund managers under laws such as the U.S. Employee Retirement Income Security Act (ERISA) are fiduciaries with respect to plan assets.
