Bernard of Clairvaux

Bernard of Clairvaux (Latin: Bernardus Claraevallensis; 1090 – 20 August 1153), venerated as Saint Bernard, was a Burgundian abbot and a major leader in the revitalization of Benedictine monasticism through the nascent Cistercian Order. He was a towering figure of 12th-century Latin Christianity — theologian, mystic, preacher of the Second Crusade, and adviser to popes and kings.

Life

Born into the Burgundian nobility, Bernard entered the new monastery of Cîteaux in 1113, bringing with him thirty companions including several of his brothers. In 1115 he founded the daughter house of Clairvaux in Champagne, which under his leadership grew into the centre of a network of hundreds of Cistercian monasteries across Europe. His eloquence earned him the title Doctor Mellifluus — the "honey-sweet doctor".

Works and influence

Bernard's writings include the Sermons on the Song of Songs, On Grace and Free Choice, and On Consideration, addressed to his former monk Pope Eugene III. He famously clashed with the philosopher Peter Abelard, securing his condemnation at the Council of Sens (1141), and preached the Second Crusade at Vézelay in 1146. He was canonized in 1174 and declared a Doctor of the Church in 1830. His hymns and his devotion to the Virgin Mary deeply influenced Western spirituality; several hymns attributed to him, such as "O Sacred Head", remain in use.
