Synthetic-aperture radar

Synthetic-aperture radar (SAR) is a form of radar used to create two- or three-dimensional images of objects and landscapes. It exploits the motion of a relatively small antenna over a target region to synthesise a much larger aperture, achieving fine spatial resolution that is, remarkably, independent of the distance to the target. Because radar is an active sensor, SAR works day and night and through clouds, making it invaluable for Earth observation and reconnaissance.

Principle

As the platform — an aircraft or satellite — moves, successive transmitted pulses illuminate the scene from slightly different positions. Recording the echoes coherently (amplitude and phase) and combining them by signal processing mimics a single antenna thousands of wavelengths long. Refinements include stripmap, spotlight, and ScanSAR modes, polarimetric SAR, and interferometric SAR (InSAR), which measures ground deformation at millimetre precision from orbit.

Applications

Civil missions include ESA's Sentinel-1 satellites, Germany's TerraSAR-X, Canada's RADARSAT constellation, and newer commercial constellations from companies such as ICEYE and Capella Space. SAR data support flood and oil-spill mapping, glacier and sea-ice monitoring, subsidence and earthquake measurement, agriculture, archaeology, and ship detection. The technique was pioneered in the 1950s by Carl Wiley at Goodyear Aircraft and first deployed in military reconnaissance systems.
