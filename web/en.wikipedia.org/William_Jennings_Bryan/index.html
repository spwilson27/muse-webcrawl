William Jennings Bryan

William Jennings Bryan (March 19, 1860 – July 26, 1925) was an American lawyer, orator and politician. He was a dominant force in the Democratic Party, running three times as the party's nominee for President of the United States in the 1896, 1900, and 1908 presidential elections. He served in the House of Representatives from 1891 to 1895 and as the 41st United States Secretary of State under President Woodrow Wilson from 1913 to 1915.

Cross of Gold and presidential campaigns

Bryan gained fame at the 1896 Democratic National Convention with his "Cross of Gold" speech, which advocated bimetallism—the "free silver" movement—against the gold standard, and won him the nomination at age 36, making him the youngest major-party presidential nominee in U.S. history. He lost to William McKinley in 1896 and again in 1900, and to William Howard Taft in 1908.

Later career

As Secretary of State, Bryan pursued arbitration treaties and resigned in 1915 over Wilson's handling of the Lusitania crisis, which he feared would lead to war. In his later years he was a leading figure in the fundamentalist movement and in the prohibition movement. In 1925 he assisted the prosecution in the Scopes Trial, which tested the legality of teaching evolution in Tennessee public schools, facing Clarence Darrow for the defence. Bryan died five days after the trial ended.
