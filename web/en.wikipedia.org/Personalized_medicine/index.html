Personalized medicine

Also calledPrecision medicine, stratified medicine
BasisGenomics, biomarkers, pharmacogenomics, clinical data
Key applicationsOncology, pharmacogenetics, rare disease diagnosis

Personalized medicine, also commonly called precision medicine, is a medical model that tailors prevention, diagnosis and treatment to the individual characteristics of each patient — especially their genetic makeup, biomarkers, environment and lifestyle — rather than applying a one-size-fits-all approach. The terms overlap, though "precision medicine" is often preferred because treatment is usually stratified by subgroup characteristics rather than literally unique to each person.

Applications

Oncology has led adoption: tumours are profiled for driver mutations and matched to targeted drugs, such as trastuzumab for HER2-positive breast cancer or EGFR inhibitors for lung cancers bearing those mutations; "basket trials" enrol patients by mutation rather than organ of origin. In pharmacogenomics, genetic testing guides drug choice and dose — for example, testing for HLA-B*57:01 before prescribing abacavir, or TPMT status before thiopurine therapy. Sequencing has transformed the diagnosis of rare genetic diseases, ending "diagnostic odysseys" for many families, and enables individualised therapies such as patient-specific antisense oligonucleotides and autologous CAR-T cell treatments manufactured from a patient's own cells.

History and initiatives

The completion of the Human Genome Project in 2003 and the subsequent fall in sequencing costs — from hundreds of millions of dollars per genome to a few hundred — made routine genomic medicine feasible. Large national programmes followed, including the US Precision Medicine Initiative and its All of Us research programme (2015), Genomics England's 100,000 Genomes Project, and national genomic medicine services in several countries.

Challenges

Critics note that benefits have so far accrued unevenly: genomic reference data skew toward European ancestries, raising equity concerns; many variants found by sequencing are of uncertain significance; and the cost-effectiveness of some personalised interventions remains unproven. Data privacy, incidental findings and integration with electronic health records pose further practical and ethical questions.
