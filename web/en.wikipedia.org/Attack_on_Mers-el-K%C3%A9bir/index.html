Attack on Mers-el-Kébir

Date3 July 1940
LocationMers-el-Kébir, near Oran, French Algeria
British forceForce H under Vice-Admiral Sir James Somerville
French commanderAdmiral Marcel-Bruno Gensoul
ResultFrench battleship Bretagne sunk; Dunkerque and Provence disabled; 1,297 French sailors killed

The attack on Mers-el-Kébir on 3 July 1940, part of Operation Catapult, was a British naval attack on French Navy ships moored at the naval base of Mers-el-Kébir near Oran, on the coast of French Algeria. After France signed an armistice with Nazi Germany in June 1940, the British War Cabinet, led by Winston Churchill, feared that the powerful modern French fleet — the fourth largest in the world — might fall into German hands despite French assurances and armistice terms, upsetting the naval balance in the Mediterranean and Atlantic. Britain therefore resolved to neutralise the French warships, by seizure, negotiated disablement, or force.

The ultimatum and the attack

At Mers-el-Kébir, Vice-Admiral Sir James Somerville's Force H presented Admiral Marcel-Bruno Gensoul with an ultimatum offering several options: join the British and continue the fight; sail to a British port or to the French West Indies or the United States for internment with reduced crews; or scuttle the ships — otherwise the British would use "whatever force may be necessary". A breakdown in communication (the French admiral took offence at negotiating with a junior emissary, and Paris received a garbled version of the terms) and the arrival of French reinforcements forced Somerville's hand. At 17:54 the British opened fire. The old battleship Bretagne blew up and capsized, killing 977 of her crew; the battlecruiser Dunkerque and battleship Provence were run aground disabled; the battlecruiser Strasbourg and several destroyers escaped to Toulon. In total 1,297 French sailors died and some 350 were wounded. A follow-up air attack on 6 July further damaged Dunkerque.

Aftermath

The attack shocked France: the Vichy government broke off diplomatic relations with Britain, and the incident was exploited by German and Vichy propaganda as proof of British perfidy, embittering Anglo-French relations for years. Elsewhere, Operation Catapult saw French ships in British ports seized (with some bloodshed aboard the submarine Surcouf) and the French squadron at Alexandria peacefully interned by agreement with Admiral Cunningham. In the United States, the attack was widely read as evidence that Britain would fight on at all costs, strengthening support for the Churchill government. The surviving capital ships at Toulon were scuttled by their own crews in November 1942, when Germany occupied Vichy France — vindicating French promises that the fleet would never be surrendered intact.
