Arrestin

Arrestins are a family of small proteins that regulate the signalling and trafficking of G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs). By binding to activated, phosphorylated receptors they "arrest" further G-protein signalling (desensitization), target receptors for internalization, and scaffold their own signalling pathways. Mammals have four arrestin subtypes: visual arrestin (arrestin-1), β-arrestin1 (arrestin-2), β-arrestin2 (arrestin-3) and cone arrestin (arrestin-4).

Function

After a GPCR is activated by its ligand, G protein-coupled receptor kinases (GRKs) phosphorylate the receptor's intracellular domains. Arrestin binds the phosphorylated, active receptor with high affinity, sterically blocking further G-protein coupling. β-arrestins also link the receptor to the endocytic machinery (clathrin and AP-2), driving internalization, and act as scaffolds for kinases such as ERK1/2 and Src, producing G-protein-independent signals — a phenomenon exploited in the design of "biased" drugs that favour one pathway over another.

History

The first arrestin was identified in the retina as the 48 kDa "S-antigen" protein that shut off the phototransduction cascade by binding light-activated, phosphorylated rhodopsin; the name arrestin reflects its signal-arresting role. β-arrestin was discovered shortly afterwards as a cofactor needed for desensitization of the β2-adrenergic receptor.

Clinical significance

Arrestin dysfunction is implicated in disease: mutations in visual arrestin cause Oguchi disease, a form of congenital stationary night blindness, and S-antigen is a potent autoantigen in experimental uveitis. β-arrestins are drug targets in heart failure, pain and metabolic disease.
