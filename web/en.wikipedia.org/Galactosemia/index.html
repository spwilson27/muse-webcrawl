Galactosemia

SpecialtyMedical genetics, endocrinology
CauseEnzyme deficiency in galactose metabolism (usually GALT)
InheritanceAutosomal recessive
TreatmentLifelong galactose-restricted diet

Galactosemia is a group of rare inherited metabolic disorders in which the body cannot properly break down the sugar galactose, a component of lactose in milk. In the classic form, deficiency of the enzyme galactose-1-phosphate uridylyltransferase (GALT) causes galactose-1-phosphate and related metabolites to accumulate, damaging the liver, kidneys, brain, and eyes.

Signs and symptoms

Affected newborns typically appear healthy at birth but, after feeding on breast milk or lactose-containing formula, develop poor feeding, vomiting, jaundice, lethargy, liver failure, cataracts, and susceptibility to severe E. coli sepsis, which can be fatal if untreated. Milder variants involve deficiencies of galactokinase (causing mainly cataracts) or UDP-galactose epimerase.

Diagnosis and screening

Galactosemia is included in newborn screening programmes in many countries, measured by enzyme activity or galactose levels in dried blood spots, with confirmatory genetic testing of the GALT gene. The most common severe mutation in European populations is known as Q188R, while the Duarte variant causes milder enzyme reduction.

Treatment and prognosis

Treatment is immediate and lifelong exclusion of lactose and galactose from the diet, which prevents the life-threatening neonatal illness. Despite dietary control, many patients develop long-term complications including speech and cognitive difficulties, tremor and other neurological signs, and premature ovarian insufficiency in women — thought to result from endogenous galactose production and prenatal damage.
