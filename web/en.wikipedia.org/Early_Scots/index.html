Early Scots

Early Scots is the name given to the developing form of the Northumbrian Old English spoken in the lowlands of Scotland and neighbouring parts of northern England from roughly 1100 to 1450. Descended from the Anglian dialect of Old English spoken in the kingdom of Northumbria, it arrived in what is now south-eastern Scotland in the seventh century and spread northwards, especially from the twelfth century with the growth of burghs and royal administration under David I and his successors.

Development

The language of the period was at first nearly identical to the Northern Middle English of northern England; it was generally called "Inglis" by its speakers ("Scottis" then referred to Gaelic). Distinctively Scottish spelling conventions, vocabulary and sound changes gradually accumulated, influenced by Old Norse, Gaelic, Dutch and French contacts. Notable features include distinctive vowel developments (later codified under Aitken's vowel-length rules), the retention of sounds such as /x/ (written ch in loch, licht), and characteristic spellings such as quh- for wh-.

Literature

Although few texts survive from before 1375, the period's culminating work is John Barbour's epic The Brus (c. 1375), the earliest major surviving work of Scottish literature. Around 1450 the language enters its next phase, Middle Scots, the language of the great makars such as Robert Henryson and William Dunbar, by which time it functioned as the state language of the Kingdom of Scotland.
