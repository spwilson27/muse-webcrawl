Quakers are people who belong to the Religious Society of Friends, a historically Protestant Christian set of denominations. Members are known as Friends. The movement arose in England in the mid-17th century from the Seekers and other dissenting groups. George Fox (1624–1691) is generally regarded as its principal founder and early leader.

Quakers are traditionally characterized by their belief in the Inner Light — the capacity of every person to experience the divine directly — their rejection of outward sacraments and a paid clergy, silent worship in many branches, and the peace testimony, a commitment to nonviolence. The nickname "Quaker" is said to derive from Fox's injunction to a judge to "tremble at the word of the Lord," or from early Friends shaking during religious experiences.

History

Origins

The movement coalesced around 1652, following Fox's preaching mission in northern England. Early Friends travelled widely as itinerant preachers, frequently clashing with the authorities of the Commonwealth and, after the Restoration, the Church of England. Thousands of Quakers were imprisoned for refusing to swear oaths, pay tithes, or cease meeting for worship. The Quaker Act 1662 and the Conventicle Act made their worship illegal, though the Toleration Act 1689 eventually granted relief.

Pennsylvania and America

In 1681, William Penn, a prominent Quaker, received a royal charter for a colony in North America. Pennsylvania became a refuge for Friends and other persecuted groups, and Penn's "holy experiment" in government influenced later American democratic ideals. Quakers settled extensively in the Delaware Valley, New England, and the southern colonies.

Beliefs and practices

Central to Quaker faith is the doctrine of the Inner Light, sometimes expressed as "that of God in everyone." Worship in the unprogrammed tradition consists of silent waiting upon God, during which any participant may feel led to speak. Programmed meetings, more common among evangelical Friends, include pastors, hymns, and prepared sermons.

Quakers typically express their faith through the testimonies, often summarized by the acronym SPICES: Simplicity, Peace, Integrity, Community, Equality, and Stewardship. Friends have historically practised plain dress and plain speech, refused oaths, and opposed war and slavery.

Social activism

Quakers played a leading role in the abolition of the slave trade and slavery, in prison reform (notably through Elizabeth Fry), in the peace movement, and in humanitarian relief. The American Friends Service Committee and the British Friends Service Council jointly received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1947. Quakers were also prominent in business and industry, founding firms such as Cadbury, Rowntree, and Barclays Bank, and conscientious objection during both world wars.

Branches

Today the Society of Friends comprises several branches, including liberal unprogrammed Friends (especially in Britain and parts of the United States), conservative Friends maintaining older customs, programmed pastoral Friends, and evangelical Friends, who form the majority of Quakers worldwide, particularly in Kenya and Bolivia.
