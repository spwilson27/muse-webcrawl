Kangaroo

FamilyMacropodidae
Principal speciesRed kangaroo; eastern grey; western grey; antilopine
RangeAustralia (and New Guinea for some macropods)
DietHerbivorous (grasses, leaves)
LocomotionRicochetal (hopping); pentapedal at slow speed

Kangaroos are marsupials of the family Macropodidae, indigenous to Australia. The term is commonly applied to the four largest species — the red kangaroo, eastern grey kangaroo, western grey kangaroo, and antilopine kangaroo — while smaller relatives are called wallabies, wallaroos, and tree-kangaroos. Kangaroos are an unofficial emblem of Australia, appearing on its coat of arms and currency.

Description and locomotion

Kangaroos have large, powerful hind legs adapted for hopping, big hind feet, a muscular tail used for balance and support, and a small head with large ears. Red kangaroo males can exceed 2 metres in total length and 90 kg. At speed, kangaroos move by ricochetal locomotion — hopping on both hind legs — reaching over 70 km/h in short bursts and sustaining about 20–25 km/h; elastic energy storage in their tendons makes faster hopping proportionally cheaper. At slow speeds they walk "pentapedally", planting their forelimbs and tail while swinging the hind legs forward. Kangaroos cannot move their hind legs independently on land (though they can while swimming) and cannot walk backwards.

Reproduction

After a gestation of about a month, a single jellybean-sized joey is born, climbs to the pouch, and nurses for months, continuing to suckle after emerging at around eight months. Females can sustain embryonic diapause, holding a fertilized embryo in reserve, and can produce two kinds of milk simultaneously for young of different ages.

Ecology and behaviour

Kangaroos are herbivorous grazers and browsers, mostly nocturnal and crepuscular, living in groups called mobs. Males compete for mates by "boxing" — balancing on the tail to kick with both hind feet. Their predators include dingoes and, for joeys, wedge-tailed eagles. Populations of the large species number in the tens of millions and fluctuate with rainfall; kangaroos are commercially harvested for meat and leather under government quotas, a practice that remains debated.

Cultural significance

Kangaroos feature in Aboriginal Australian mythology and art over tens of thousands of years. Since European settlement they have become a national symbol — on the coat of arms (with the emu), Qantas aircraft, and the "boxing kangaroo" sporting flag. A popular myth claims the word derives from an Aboriginal phrase meaning "I don't know"; it actually comes from gangurru, the Guugu Yimithirr name for a large kangaroo.
