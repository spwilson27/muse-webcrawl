Superoxide dismutase

Superoxide dismutase (SOD) is an enzyme that catalyzes the dismutation of the superoxide radical (O2•−) into molecular oxygen and hydrogen peroxide. Discovered by Joe McCord and Irwin Fridovich in 1969, SODs form a major antioxidant defense in nearly all living cells exposed to oxygen.

Types

SODs are metalloenzymes classified by their metal cofactor. In humans there are three forms: SOD1 (copper/zinc, in the cytoplasm), SOD2 (manganese, in the mitochondria), and SOD3 (copper/zinc, extracellular). Bacteria also use iron- and nickel-containing forms. The reaction proceeds at near diffusion-limited speed: the metal center alternately reduces and oxidizes successive superoxide molecules, producing H2O2, which is then removed by catalase and glutathione peroxidase.

Medical significance

Mutations in the SOD1 gene cause a subset of familial amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), linked to toxic gain of function of the misfolded protein. SOD2 is considered a tumor suppressor in some contexts, and loss of SOD activity is associated with oxidative stress in aging and inflammation. Bovine SOD (orgotein) has been used as an anti-inflammatory drug.
