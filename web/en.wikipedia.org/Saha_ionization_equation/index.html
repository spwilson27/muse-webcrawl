Saha ionization equation

The Saha ionization equation is an expression that relates the ionization state of a gas in thermal equilibrium to the temperature and pressure. It is a result of a minority of atoms or molecules being ionized in a hot gas, and it was derived by the Indian astrophysicist Meghnad Saha in 1920 and 1921, and later elaborated by Ralph H. Fowler and others. It is a fundamental result in astrophysics, particularly in the interpretation of stellar spectra, and also finds applications in plasma physics.

Formulation

For a gas at temperature T composed of a single species of atom, the Saha equation expresses the ratio of the number densities of atoms in successive ionization states. If ni is the density of atoms in the i-th state of ionization, with i = 0 denoting the neutral atom, then:

ni+1 ne / ni = (2π me kB T / h2)3/2 · (2 gi+1 / gi) · exp(−εi / kB T)

where ne is the electron density, me is the electron mass, kB is the Boltzmann constant, h is the Planck constant, gi is the degeneracy of the i-th state, and εi is the energy required to remove an electron from an atom in the i-th state, creating an atom in the (i+1)-th state.

Derivation

The equation can be derived by minimizing the Helmholtz free energy of a system of atoms, ions, and electrons at fixed temperature and volume, treating the gas as an ideal mixture and the electrons as a classical ideal gas. The treatment relies on the assumption of local thermodynamic equilibrium: collisions must be frequent enough that the velocity distribution of particles is Maxwellian, even though the radiation field need not be that of a black body.

Applications

The Saha equation explains the sequence of stellar spectral types: the temperature of a star's photosphere determines which ionization states, and hence which absorption lines, are present. It also governs the ionization of the solar corona, the formation of the ionosphere, and the behaviour of the early universe at the epoch of recombination, when electrons combined with protons to form neutral hydrogen. In plasma diagnostics it is used to infer electron temperatures from measured line intensities.

Limitations

The Saha equation is valid only for weakly ionized, ideal gases in thermal equilibrium. At high densities, interactions between particles — including the lowering of ionization energies by neighbouring charges — must be taken into account, and the equation ceases to be accurate. It also fails when the gas is far from equilibrium, as in many laboratory and astrophysical plasmas.
