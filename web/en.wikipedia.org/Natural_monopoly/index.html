Natural monopoly

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A natural monopoly is a monopoly in an industry in which high infrastructural costs and other barriers to entry relative to the size of the market give the largest supplier an overwhelming cost advantage over potential competitors. In such a market the total cost of supplying a good or service is lower for a single firm than for two or more firms—a condition called subadditivity of the cost function.

Definition

Natural monopolies typically arise where economies of scale are large relative to market demand and average costs decline over the relevant range of output. Network industries are the classic examples: water supply and sewers, electricity distribution, gas pipelines, railways, and telecommunications networks, where duplicating fixed infrastructure would be wasteful.

Regulation

Because an unregulated monopolist can restrict output and raise price above marginal cost, natural monopolies are commonly subject to price regulation, rate-of-return regulation, price-cap regulation, or public ownership. Regulators aim to approximate the efficiency benefits of competition while preserving the cost advantages of single supply.

Changing technology

Technological change can erode natural monopoly conditions: long-distance telephony, electricity generation, and postal services were once treated as natural monopolies but became competitive as technologies evolved. Modern policy often separates the genuinely monopolistic network element (the "grid") from potentially competitive services delivered over it, a practice known as unbundling.
