Arch-gravity dam

An arch-gravity dam is a dam with the characteristics of both an arch dam and a gravity dam. It is a dam that curves upstream in a narrowing curve that directs most of the water pressure against the canyon rock walls, providing the force to compress the dam. Arch-gravity dams combine the inherent strengths of the two forms: the dam's weight resists part of the water load by gravity, while the arch action transfers much of the remaining load to the abutments.

Characteristics

Arch-gravity dams are generally much thicker than pure arch dams of comparable height and require larger volumes of concrete, but they can be built in wider valleys where the rock abutments are not strong or narrow enough for a thin arch dam. The curvature nevertheless reduces the volume considerably compared with a straight gravity dam on the same site.

Examples

The Hoover Dam on the Colorado River (completed 1936) is the classic example, rising 221 m in Black Canyon. Other well-known arch-gravity dams include the Grand Coulee Dam in Washington state and the Daniel-Johnson dam (Manic-5) in Quebec, which is of the multiple-arch-and-buttress variant.
