Aluminium

Aluminium (American spelling: aluminum) is a chemical element with the symbol Al and atomic number 13. It is a silvery-white, soft, non-magnetic, ductile metal and the most abundant metal in the Earth's crust, making up about 8 percent of its mass.

Aluminium is light, corrosion-resistant, and highly recyclable.

Properties and production

Aluminium has about one-third the density of steel, excellent corrosion resistance due to a self-forming oxide layer, and high electrical and thermal conductivity. Although abundant, it never occurs native because of its affinity for oxygen; it is extracted from bauxite ore, first refined to alumina by the Bayer process and then reduced by electrolysis in the Hall–Héroult process (1886), whose huge electricity demand places smelters near cheap power. In the mid-nineteenth century aluminium was a precious metal — Napoleon III served honoured guests with aluminium cutlery — before industrial electrolysis made it cheap.

Uses

The metal and its alloys are used in aircraft and vehicle structures, packaging (cans and foil), construction, power transmission lines, and consumer goods. Aluminium is one of the most recycled materials in the world: remelting scrap requires only about 5 percent of the energy of primary production, and roughly three-quarters of all aluminium ever produced remains in use.
