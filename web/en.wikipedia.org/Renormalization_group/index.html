Renormalization group

In theoretical physics, the term renormalization group (RG) refers to a formal apparatus that allows systematic investigation of the changes of a physical system as viewed at different scales. In particle physics, it reflects the changes in the underlying force laws (encoded in coupling constants) as the energy scale at which physical processes occur varies: couplings "run" with scale.

History

The idea originated with Ernst Stueckelberg and André Petermann (1953) and was developed by Murray Gell-Mann and Francis Low (1954), who derived the first renormalization group equation. Curtis Callan and Kurt Symanzik independently obtained the modern Callan–Symanzik equation in 1970. Kenneth Wilson revolutionised the subject in 1971 by applying RG ideas to critical phenomena in statistical mechanics, introducing concepts of coarse-graining, fixed points and universality — work that earned him the 1982 Nobel Prize in Physics.

Running couplings

The scale dependence of a coupling is encoded in its beta function. In quantum chromodynamics the beta function is negative, so the strong coupling decreases at high energies — the phenomenon of asymptotic freedom, discovered by Gross, Wilczek and Politzer — while the electromagnetic coupling increases slowly with energy. Running couplings explain how the three gauge couplings of the Standard Model nearly converge at grand-unification energies.

Fixed points and universality

Under successive coarse-graining, a statistical system flows in parameter space; at a critical point it reaches an RG fixed point where scale invariance emerges. Because many different microscopic theories flow to the same fixed point, systems as different as fluids and magnets share identical critical exponents — the essence of universality. Wilson's numerical RG and Leo Kadanoff's block-spin picture made these ideas concrete.
