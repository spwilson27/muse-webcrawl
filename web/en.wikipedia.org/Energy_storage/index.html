Energy storage is the capture of energy produced at one time for use at a later time to reduce imbalances between energy demand and energy production. A device that stores energy is generally called an accumulator or battery. Energy comes in multiple forms including radiation, chemical, gravitational potential, electrical potential, electricity, elevated temperature, latent heat and kinetic.

Methods

Chemical storage includes rechargeable batteries — lead-acid, lithium-ion, sodium-based and flow batteries — as well as fuels such as hydrogen produced by electrolysis. Mechanical storage includes pumped hydroelectric storage, by far the largest share of installed grid capacity worldwide, compressed-air energy storage and flywheels. Thermal storage stores heat or cold in water, molten salts or phase-change materials. Electrical storage in capacitors, supercapacitors and superconducting magnetic systems delivers very fast response for short durations.

Role in electricity systems

Storage helps integrate variable renewable generation by shifting surplus wind and solar power to periods of high demand, provides frequency regulation and reserves, defers network upgrades, and supplies backup power. Falling lithium-ion battery costs since the 2010s, driven by electric vehicle production, have made multi-hour battery installations common in grids such as those of California and Australia.

Metrics

Storage technologies are compared by capacity (energy, in watt-hours), power (watts), round-trip efficiency, response time, cycle life, self-discharge and cost. No single technology is optimal for all durations: supercapacitors discharge in seconds, batteries in minutes to hours, and pumped hydro or hydrogen in hours to seasons.
