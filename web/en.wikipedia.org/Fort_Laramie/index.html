Fort Laramie was a significant 19th-century trading post, diplomatic site, and military installation located in present-day eastern Wyoming, at the confluence of the Laramie River and the North Platte River. Founded in 1834 as the private fur-trading post Fort William, it was purchased by the American Fur Company in 1836 and rebuilt in adobe as Fort John. The United States Army bought the post in 1849 and renamed it Fort Laramie, after the French-Canadian trapper Jacques La Ramée.

Role on the trails

Situated on the Oregon Trail, the California Trail, and the Mormon Trail, Fort Laramie became an essential stopping point where hundreds of thousands of emigrants rested, repaired wagons, and traded for supplies on their way west. It later served as a station for the Pony Express and a stop on the transcontinental telegraph line.

Treaties

The fort hosted two landmark agreements between the United States and Plains Indian tribes. The Treaty of Fort Laramie of 1851 defined tribal territories and promised safe passage for emigrants. The Treaty of Fort Laramie of 1868, negotiated after Red Cloud's War, established the Great Sioux Reservation, including the Black Hills, and ended hostilities—for a time—between the United States and the Lakota, Dakota, and Arapaho.

Closure and preservation

With the completion of the transcontinental railroad and the end of the Indian Wars, the fort declined in importance and was abandoned by the army in 1890. Its buildings were auctioned off, but preservation efforts in the 20th century led to its designation as a National Historic Site in 1960. Many structures have been restored, and the site is administered by the National Park Service.
