National monument (United States)

In the United States, a national monument is a protected area that may be created from any land owned or controlled by the federal government by proclamation of the President under the Antiquities Act of 1906, or by an act of Congress. National monuments protect objects of historic, prehistoric or scientific interest — from archaeological sites and historic forts to vast desert and marine landscapes.

History

The Antiquities Act, signed by Theodore Roosevelt, was prompted by looting of Native American sites in the Southwest. The first monument, Devils Tower (1906), was followed by protections for the Grand Canyon, Petrified Forest and many others that later became national parks. More than 130 national monuments have been proclaimed; recent large designations include Bears Ears and Grand Staircase–Escalante in Utah, whose boundaries were reduced by President Trump in 2017 and restored by President Biden in 2021, sparking continuing legal and political debate over the Act's scope.

Management

Monuments are managed by the National Park Service, Bureau of Land Management, U.S. Forest Service, Fish and Wildlife Service or NOAA (for marine monuments), generally with fewer facilities and sometimes more permitted traditional uses — such as grazing — than national parks. Unlike parks, which require acts of Congress, monuments can be established swiftly by presidential action, making the designation both powerful and contested.
