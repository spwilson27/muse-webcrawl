Peloponnese

The Peloponnese (Greek: Πελοπόννησος, Pelopónnēsos) is a peninsula and geographic region in southern Greece. It is connected to the central part of the country by the Isthmus of Corinth, which was cut by the Corinth Canal in 1893, technically making the Peloponnese an island. It covers about 21,550 km² and is divided among the modern regions of Western Greece, the Peloponnese region proper, and part of Attica.

History

The Peloponnese was a heartland of Mycenaean civilisation in the Bronze Age, with palaces at Mycenae, Pylos and Tiryns. In classical antiquity it was dominated by Sparta and was the theatre of the Peloponnesian War between Sparta and Athens. Corinth, Argos and Olympia—site of the ancient Olympic Games—were among its major cities. The region later passed through Roman, Byzantine, Frankish, Venetian and Ottoman rule before becoming the cradle of the modern Greek state: the Greek War of Independence began in the Peloponnese in 1821, and Nafplio served as Greece's first capital.

Geography

The peninsula is mountainous, with the Taygetus range in the south and the fertile plains of Elis and Argolis. Its southern extremities—the Mani, Cape Matapan and the Argolic peninsulas—include the southernmost points of mainland Greece and of the Balkan Peninsula.
