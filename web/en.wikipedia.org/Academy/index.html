Academy

An academy (Attic Greek: Ἀκαδήμεια; Koine Greek ἀκαδημία) is an institution of tertiary education. The name traces back to Plato's school in Athens, the Akademia, founded around 387 BC in a grove dedicated to the hero Akademos. In modern usage the term covers a wide range of bodies: national academies of sciences and letters that honor scholars and advise governments, learned societies, specialist training schools such as military and police academies, and — particularly in England — state-funded schools independent of local authority control.

Ancient origins

Plato's Academy continued, with interruptions, for nearly nine centuries until the emperor Justinian closed the philosophical schools of Athens in 529 AD. Its curriculum of mathematics, dialectic and natural philosophy became a model of organized scholarship in the Western tradition, and the word "academic" still denotes scholarly pursuits generally.

National academies

From the Renaissance onward, rulers and states founded academies to cultivate the arts and sciences: the Accademia dei Lincei in Rome (1603), the Royal Society in London (1660), the Académie française (1635) and the academies of sciences in Paris, Berlin and St. Petersburg. Many countries today maintain national academies that elect distinguished members, publish research, and provide independent advice on science policy.

Schools and training academies

"Academy" is also used for specialized schools: military academies such as West Point and Sandhurst train officers, while police, fire and maritime academies train other services. In England, academies are a category of state school introduced from 2000, funded directly by central government and run by sponsors or multi-academy trusts.
