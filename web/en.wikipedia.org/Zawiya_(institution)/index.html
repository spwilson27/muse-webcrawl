Zawiya (institution)

A zawiya (Arabic: زاوية, romanized: zāwiya, literally "corner" or "nook"; also spelled zaouia or zawiyah) is an Islamic religious school and monastery, particularly associated with Sufi orders (tariqas), and especially characteristic of North and West Africa. The term originally denoted a corner of a mosque set aside for prayer and study, and came to mean an institution built around the tomb of a saint or the residence of a Sufi sheikh.

Functions

A typical zawiya combines a prayer hall or mosque, the tomb of the founding saint, rooms for students and travellers, and facilities for communal ritual, especially the dhikr (remembrance of God) ceremonies of its order. Zawiyas served as centres of Quranic education, legal scholarship, mediation between tribes and authorities, charity, and lodging for pilgrims and traders. In the Maghreb they often administered agricultural land and endowments (waqf) and played a central role in spreading Islam into rural and Saharan regions along trade routes.

History

Zawiyas proliferated in North Africa from the 13th century onward as Sufi orders such as the Shadhiliyya, Qadiriyya, Tijaniyya, and Rahmaniyya built networks of lodges. In the colonial period, some zawiya leaders resisted European rule while others accommodated it; French administrators in Algeria and Morocco studied and sought to control them as nodes of religious and political influence. Saintly lineages (marabouts) attached to famous zawiyas, such as those of Sidi Ahmed al-Tijani at Fez or the zawiya of Sidi Muhammad ibn Sliman al-Jazuli, attracted pilgrims for centuries. Zawiyas continue to operate today as schools, shrines, and charitable institutions across the Muslim world.
