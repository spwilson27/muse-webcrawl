Magnetohydrodynamic generator

A magnetohydrodynamic generator (MHD generator) is a device that converts thermal and kinetic energy directly into electricity by moving an electrically conducting fluid — usually an ionized gas (plasma) — through a magnetic field. It exploits the principles of magnetohydrodynamics and the Lorentz force: charged particles in the flow are deflected by the field toward electrodes, producing a direct current with no moving mechanical parts.

Principle

Combustion gases are heated to temperatures above roughly 2,000–3,000 K so that they ionize, often aided by "seeding" the gas with easily ionized alkali metals such as potassium or caesium. The plasma passes through a channel between the poles of a powerful magnet (in advanced designs, a superconducting magnet). Positive and negative charges separate under the magnetic force and are collected at electrodes, generating DC power that is converted to AC by an inverter.

History and applications

The concept builds on Faraday's experiments with mercury in the 1830s. Large experimental MHD plants were built in the Soviet Union (the U-25 facility near Moscow) and the United States from the 1960s to the 1990s, aiming at high-efficiency topping cycles for coal and natural-gas power stations. Practical deployment was hindered by electrode corrosion, materials limits at extreme temperatures, and the cost of superconducting magnets. MHD generation has also been studied for spacecraft power and for pulsed-power applications.
