Monotone convergence theorem

Monotone convergence theorem

Field: Real analysis, measure theory
Associated with: Henri Lebesgue, Beppo Levi

In mathematics, the monotone convergence theorem refers to several related results governing the convergence of monotone sequences and the interchange of limits and integrals. The most famous versions arise in real analysis and in Lebesgue's theory of integration.

Sequences of real numbers

Every monotone sequence of real numbers that is bounded converges. Specifically, a non-decreasing sequence that is bounded above converges to its supremum, and a non-increasing sequence that is bounded below converges to its infimum. This result follows from the completeness of the real numbers and is sometimes attributed to Weierstrass.

Lebesgue's monotone convergence theorem

In measure theory, Lebesgue's monotone convergence theorem states that if (fn) is a sequence of non-negative measurable functions on a measure space that increases pointwise to a limit f, then f is measurable and the integral of f equals the limit of the integrals of fn:

∫ f dμ = limn→∞ ∫ fn dμ.

The result is often credited in part to Beppo Levi, who proved an early version in 1906. Together with Fatou's lemma and the dominated convergence theorem, it is one of the fundamental convergence theorems of Lebesgue integration, and it underpins the proof that term-by-term integration of series of non-negative functions is valid.

Series version

An important corollary is that for a series of non-negative measurable functions, the integral of the sum equals the sum of the integrals, allowing the interchange of summation and integration without further hypotheses.
