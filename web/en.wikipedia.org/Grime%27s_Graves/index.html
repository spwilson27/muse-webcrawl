Grime's Graves is a large Neolithic flint-mining complex near Weeting in Norfolk, England. It lies in an area of heathland in Breckland and is one of the best-preserved prehistoric industrial sites in Britain. Over 37 hectares, more than 400 shafts and pits were dug to reach seams of high-quality black flint, known locally as floorstone, which was mined for making tools.

Mining

Between roughly 2600 and 2300 BCE, Neolithic miners excavated shafts up to 13 metres deep, using picks made from red deer antler, chalk rubble lamps and wooden tools. Radiating galleries at the base of each shaft followed the flint seam. The flint was knapped at the surface into axeheads, blades and other implements traded across Britain. One shaft preserved the antler picks and a small chalk shrine-like arrangement that has attracted speculation about ritual deposits.

Name and discovery

The pits were long thought to be Anglo-Saxon or Danish burials or fortifications; the name derives from the Anglo-Saxon god Grim (a byname of Woden), with "graves" meaning pits or ditches. Their true nature was established by Canon William Greenwell's excavation in 1870. The site is now managed by English Heritage, and visitors can descend into one excavated shaft.
