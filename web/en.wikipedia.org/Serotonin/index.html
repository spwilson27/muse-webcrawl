Serotonin


Serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine)

Serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine, 5-HT) is a monoamine neurotransmitter. Its biological function is complex, and it plays roles in mood, cognition, learning, memory, sleep, appetite, and numerous physiological processes including vomiting and vasoconstriction. Although popularly credited with feelings of well-being and happiness, its actual contribution to mood is complex and remains debated.

Distribution and synthesis

About 90% of the body's serotonin is produced in the gastrointestinal tract by enterochromaffin cells, where it regulates intestinal movements; much of the rest is carried by blood platelets. In the brain, serotonin is synthesized in the raphe nuclei of the brainstem from the essential amino acid tryptophan, via the intermediate 5-hydroxytryptophan.

Receptors and pharmacology

Serotonin acts through at least 14 receptor subtypes (5-HT1 to 5-HT7 families). Many drugs target the serotonergic system: selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) such as fluoxetine and sertraline are widely used antidepressants; triptans act on 5-HT1B/1D receptors for migraine; ondansetron blocks 5-HT3 receptors to prevent nausea; and psychedelic drugs such as LSD and psilocybin act mainly at 5-HT2A receptors. Excessive serotonergic activity can cause the potentially life-threatening serotonin syndrome.

History

Serotonin was isolated and named in 1948 by Maurice Rapport and colleagues as a serum vasoconstrictor; its identity with the earlier-studied "enteramine" from gut tissue was established in the early 1950s, and its presence in the brain was shown soon after.
