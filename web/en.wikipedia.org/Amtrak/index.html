Amtrak, the National Railroad Passenger Corporation, is a passenger railroad service that provides medium- and long-distance intercity service in the contiguous United States and to nine Canadian cities. Founded in 1971 as a quasi-public corporation to operate many US passenger rail services, it receives a combination of state and federal subsidies but is managed as a for-profit organization.

History

By the mid-20th century, private railroads' passenger services were losing money heavily to automobiles and airlines. The Rail Passenger Service Act of 1970 created Amtrak to relieve the freight railroads of their passenger obligations. Amtrak began operations on May 1, 1971, with a system roughly half the size of the network it replaced.

Network

Amtrak operates more than 300 trains daily over 21,400 miles of track, most of it owned by freight railroads, reaching over 500 destinations in 46 states and the District of Columbia. It owns most of the Northeast Corridor between Boston and Washington, D.C., where its Acela trains are the fastest in the Americas, reaching 150 mph (240 km/h) in regular service.

Services

Services range from the high-speed Acela and Northeast Regional to long-distance trains such as the California Zephyr, Empire Builder, and Southwest Chief, along with many state-supported corridor routes.

Funding

Amtrak has never been profitable overall and depends on annual federal appropriations and state payments, a recurring subject of political debate about national passenger rail.
