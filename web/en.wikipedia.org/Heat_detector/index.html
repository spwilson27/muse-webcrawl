Heat detector

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A heat detector is a device that responds to changes in ambient temperature, typically as part of a fire alarm system. Heat detectors are generally intended to signal the presence of a fire by detecting the thermal energy produced by combustion. They are used in environments where smoke detectors might produce false alarms, such as kitchens, garages and dusty industrial areas.

Types

Fixed-temperature detectors operate when the ambient temperature reaches a predetermined threshold, commonly around 57 °C (135 °F) or higher for special applications. A common design uses a eutectic alloy that melts at the rated temperature, releasing a spring-loaded contact. Rate-of-rise detectors respond when the temperature increases rapidly — for example faster than about 7–8 °C per minute — typically using a thermistor or a pneumatic chamber. Many modern devices combine both fixed-temperature and rate-of-rise elements.

Placement and standards

Heat detectors are classified under standards such as NFPA 72 in the United States and EN 54 in Europe. Because heat rises and spreads along ceilings, detectors are installed on ceilings or high on walls, with spacing reduced on ceilings with beams or high air movement. Heat detectors respond more slowly than smoke detectors, so they are generally not used alone in sleeping areas where early warning of smoke is required for life safety.
