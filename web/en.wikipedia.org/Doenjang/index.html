Doenjang

Doenjang (Korean: 된장, "thick sauce") is a traditional Korean fermented soybean paste. Its name literally means "thick paste" in Korean. A staple in Korean cooking, it is used as a condiment and as the base for soups and stews such as doenjang-jjigae. Along with ganjang (soy sauce), gochujang (chili paste), it is one of the essential jang (fermented sauces) of Korean cuisine, collectively central to the national diet.

Production

Doenjang is made from dried soybean blocks called meju, which are fermented with Aspergillus and Bacillus species, then aged in brine in large earthenware jars (onggi). When ready, the liquid portion becomes soy sauce (ganjang) and the solid portion is the doenjang, which may be aged for months or years. Commercial varieties often add wheat or other grains, while traditional home-made (jip-doenjang) paste is valued for its depth of flavor.

Culinary use

Doenjang is intensely savory (rich in umami) and pungent. It is dissolved into stews and soups, mixed with vegetables and garlic as ssamjang for wrapping grilled meat in lettuce, and used to season namul (vegetable dishes). Its fermentation profile is analogous to Japanese miso, though doenjang is typically chunkier and more pungent.

Nutrition

Doenjang contains protein, isoflavones, and various compounds produced by fermentation. It is high in sodium, so it is used in modest quantities; studies have examined its potential health effects, including those of its peptides and fermentation metabolites, in connection with disease prevention research.

History

Fermented soybean products have been made on the Korean Peninsula for well over a thousand years, with references to jang appearing in records from the Three Kingdoms period and detailed recipes in Joseon-era texts. In November 2024, Korean jang-making culture ("jang damgeugi") was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.
