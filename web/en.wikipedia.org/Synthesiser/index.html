Synthesiser

A synthesiser (also spelled synthesizer in American English) is an electronic musical instrument that generates audio signals. Synthesisers typically create sounds by generating waveforms through methods including subtractive synthesis, additive synthesis and frequency modulation synthesis. These sounds are shaped by components such as oscillators, filters, envelopes and low-frequency oscillators, and may be played via a built-in or external keyboard, sequencer or other controller.

Synthesisers can imitate traditional instruments such as the piano, organ or flute, generate natural sounds such as ocean waves, or create entirely new sounds with artificial timbres. They were popularised in the 1960s and 1970s and have been a defining element of genres including electronic dance music, synth-pop, hip hop and film scoring.

History

The earliest synthesisers grew out of electronic instruments such as the theremin (1920) and the Hammond organ's tonewheel technology. Robert Moog's modular synthesisers of the mid-1960s, followed by the portable Minimoog (1970), made voltage-controlled synthesis practical for touring musicians. The arrival of the microprocessor enabled polyphonic instruments such as the Sequential Circuits Prophet-5 (1978), and the digital Yamaha DX7 (1983) became one of the best-selling synthesisers of all time.

Components

A synthesiser voice typically begins with one or more oscillators producing periodic waveforms — sine, square, sawtooth or triangle. A filter shapes the harmonic content, an amplifier controlled by an envelope generator (commonly ADSR: attack, decay, sustain, release) shapes the volume over time, and low-frequency oscillators (LFOs) modulate parameters to produce vibrato, tremolo and other effects.

Types

Synthesisers are built as hardware keyboards, desktop and rack modules, and modular systems, and as software instruments ("softsynths") running on computers and mobile devices. Modern instruments emulate classic analogue circuitry through virtual analogue modelling, and workstations combine synthesis with sampling and sequencing.
