Taos Pueblo

Taos Pueblo (Tiwa: Təotho) is an ancient pueblo belonging to a Tiwa-speaking Native American tribe of Puebloan people, located about one mile (1.6 km) north of the modern city of Taos, New Mexico. It lies at the foot of the Sangre de Cristo Mountains and is bisected by Red Willow Creek. The multi-storey adobe complexes on both banks — Hlauuma (north house) and Hlaukwima (south house) — have been continuously inhabited for roughly a thousand years and are considered among the oldest continuously inhabited communities in the United States.

History

The present pueblo's main structures were probably built between 1000 and 1450 CE. Spanish explorers first reached Taos in 1540, and a mission church, San Geronimo de Taos, was built in the early 1600s. Taos Pueblo was a centre of the Pueblo Revolt of 1680, led by Popé, which expelled the Spanish from New Mexico for twelve years. The original mission church was destroyed during the U.S. Army's siege of the pueblo in the Taos Revolt of 1847 and was rebuilt nearby in 1850 — the picturesque new San Geronimo Church remains in use.

Blue Lake and land rights

In 1906 the U.S. government incorporated the tribe's sacred Blue Lake and surrounding watershed into Carson National Forest. After a 64-year campaign, President Richard Nixon signed legislation in 1970 returning 48,000 acres (19,000 ha), including Blue Lake, to Taos Pueblo — a landmark victory for Native American religious freedom and self-determination, achieved with broad public support.

The community today

About 150 people live full-time in the historic pueblo, in homes that traditionally lack running water and electricity; many other tribal members live in modern houses on pueblo lands nearby. The pueblo is governed by a tribal council of elders in accordance with unwritten custom; the traditional language is Northern Tiwa. Taos Pueblo was designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1992 and is open to visitors, closing periodically for private ceremonies.
