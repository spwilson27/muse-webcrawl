Honey bee

A honey bee (also spelled honeybee) is a eusocial flying insect of the genus Apis, within the family Apidae. Honey bees are renowned for producing and storing honey in wax combs and for building large perennial colonies — a single colony may contain tens of thousands of workers ruled by one queen. Only eight surviving species are recognised, of which the western honey bee (Apis mellifera) has been domesticated worldwide for honey production and crop pollination; it is among the most economically important insects on Earth and a keystone pollinator for wild plants.

Species and distribution

The genus Apis originated in Asia, where most species still live: the giant honey bees (A. dorsata, A. laboriosa) build single exposed combs under cliffs and branches; the dwarf honey bees (A. florea, A. andreniformis) nest in shrubs; and cavity-nesting species (A. cerana, A. koschevnikovi, A. nigrocincta) resemble the western honey bee. Apis mellifera, native to Europe, Africa, and western Asia with some two dozen subspecies, was carried by humans to the Americas, Australia, and East Asia and is now found on every continent except Antarctica.

Colony and castes

A colony consists of a single fertile queen, who lays up to 2,000 eggs a day in season; thousands of sterile female workers, who progress through tasks — nursing larvae, building comb, guarding, foraging — as they age; and, in summer, a few hundred male drones whose sole role is to mate with virgin queens from other colonies. Sex is determined by haplodiploidy (fertilised eggs become females, unfertilised eggs males), and whether a female larva becomes a queen or worker depends on diet: larvae fed exclusively on royal jelly develop into queens. Workers communicate the location of forage with the waggle dance decoded by Karl von Frisch, and colony behaviour is regulated by pheromones, notably the queen's mandibular pheromone. Colonies reproduce by swarming, in which the old queen leaves with much of the workforce to found a new home.

Honey and beekeeping

Bees make honey by evaporating nectar and processing it with enzymes, storing it in capped wax cells as winter food; a strong colony may store tens of kilograms. Humans have harvested honey since prehistory — an 8,000-year-old cave painting in Spain shows a honey seeker — and beekeeping with movable-frame hives (Langstroth, 1851) made the modern industry possible. Bees also yield beeswax, propolis, royal jelly, and pollen. Far more valuable than hive products is pollination: managed honey bees pollinate almonds, fruits, oilseeds, and many vegetables, with crop services valued in the tens of billions of dollars annually in the US alone, and migratory beekeepers trucking millions of colonies to blooms such as California's almond orchards.

Threats

Honey bees face interacting stressors: the parasitic mite Varroa destructor and the viruses it spreads, bacterial diseases (American and European foulbrood), microsporidian gut parasites (Nosema), pesticide exposure (especially neonicotinoids), habitat and forage loss, and climate change. Beginning in 2006, beekeepers in North America and Europe reported colony collapse disorder — the sudden disappearance of worker bees — whose precise causes remain unresolved and are generally attributed to a combination of the above factors. Wild pollinators, often more effective per visit, face parallel declines, making pollinator conservation a major scientific and policy concern.
