Plasma (physics)

Plasma (from Ancient Greek πλάσμα, plásma, 'moldable substance') is a state of matter in which a significant portion of the particles are ionized, forming a gas of ions, electrons, and neutral atoms that is overall electrically neutral (quasineutral). It is sometimes described as the fourth state of matter, distinct from solids, liquids, and gases. Plasma is the most common state of ordinary matter in the universe, constituting stars and much of the interstellar and intergalactic medium.

Properties

Because of its free charged particles, plasma responds strongly to electromagnetic fields and exhibits collective behaviour: it shields electric fields over the Debye length, supports waves such as Alfvén waves, and can be confined by magnetic fields. Whether a partially ionized gas qualifies as a plasma depends on the plasma parameter—the number of particles within a Debye sphere being large.

History

William Crookes identified a "radiant matter" in discharge tubes in 1879. Irving Langmuir coined the term "plasma" in 1928 for ionized gas in electric discharges, by analogy with blood plasma. The field expanded rapidly with astrophysics, the space age, and magnetic-confinement fusion research; Hannes Alfvén received the 1970 Nobel Prize in Physics for foundational work in magnetohydrodynamics.

Occurrence

Natural plasmas include the Sun and other stars, the solar wind, the ionosphere and magnetosphere, lightning, aurorae, and nebulae. Most of the visible universe—over 99% of ordinary matter by mass—is in the plasma state.

Applications

Artificial plasmas are used in fluorescent and neon lighting, plasma displays, semiconductor etching and deposition, plasma cutting and welding, sterilization, and fusion experiments such as tokamaks (including ITER) and stellarators, which seek to confine plasmas at temperatures exceeding 100 million degrees for controlled thermonuclear fusion power.

Description

Plasmas are modelled by magnetohydrodynamics (treating the plasma as a conducting fluid), by kinetic theory (particle distribution functions and the Vlasov equation), and by particle-in-cell simulations. Characteristic quantities include temperature (often expressed in electronvolts), density, the plasma frequency, and the Debye length.
