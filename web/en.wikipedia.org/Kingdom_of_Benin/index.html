The Kingdom of Benin was a pre-colonial kingdom in what is now southern Nigeria. Its capital was Edo, now known as Benin City in present-day Edo State. The kingdom, which should not be confused with the modern-day Republic of Benin, was one of the oldest and most highly developed states in the coastal hinterland of West Africa. It was formed around the 11th century AD and endured until it was annexed by the British Empire in 1897.

The kingdom was renowned for its sophisticated political organisation, its urban planning, and above all for the Benin Bronzes — thousands of brass plaques and sculptures cast by the lost-wax (cire perdue) method by specialist guilds of bronze-casters working in Igun Street in Benin City.

History

Origins and the Ogiso dynasty

According to Edo oral tradition, the earliest rulers of the Benin area were the Ogiso ("kings of the sky"), a dynasty said to comprise more than thirty rulers. The Ogiso period is traditionally dated from about 900 AD. Around the 12th century the Ogiso dynasty ended amid political disorder, and the elders of Edo are said to have sent to Ife, the holy city of the Yoruba, requesting a ruler. Oranmiyan, a son (or grandson) of Oduduwa, the legendary founder of Ife, came to Edo; his son by an Edo princess, Eweka, became the first Oba of Benin, founding the dynasty that continues, in restored form, to the present day.

The warrior kings and expansion

From the 14th and 15th centuries, a succession of able "warrior kings" expanded the kingdom. Oba Ewedo is credited with reorganising the capital and its administration. Oba Ozolua (c. 1481–1504) was a famous conqueror, while his successor Oba Esigie (c. 1504–1550) established diplomatic and trading relations with the Portuguese, who first reached Benin in 1485. Benin traded pepper, ivory, palm products and, increasingly, slaves in exchange for European goods, including the manillas and brass that fed its casting industry.

At its height in the 16th and 17th centuries the kingdom controlled a large territory stretching from the Niger Delta region toward Lagos and into the Yoruba-speaking lands to the northwest. European visitors described Benin City as a vast, well-ordered metropolis with long, straight streets and a great palace complex adorned with bronze plaques.

Decline and the Benin Expedition of 1897

From the late 17th century the kingdom weakened, troubled by civil wars over the succession and by shifting patterns of coastal trade. In 1897 a British trade mission under acting Consul-General James Phillips was ambushed and most of its members killed while travelling to Benin City during a ritual season when the Oba could not receive visitors. In retaliation, a British punitive expedition of some 1,200 men captured and burned much of Benin City in February 1897 — the event known as the Benin Expedition. Oba Ovonramwen was exiled to Calabar, where he died in 1914.

The expedition looted thousands of artworks — the Benin Bronzes — which were sold or distributed to museums in Britain, Germany and elsewhere to defray the cost of the campaign. The fate of these objects has become a major focus of debates over restitution; since the late 2010s several museums and universities in Europe and North America have returned or pledged to return bronzes to Nigeria.

Government and society

The Oba was both political and spiritual head of the kingdom, regarded as divine. He was advised by councils including the Uzama (hereditary kingmakers), palace associations of chiefs (the Iwebo, Iweguae and Ibiwe), and town chiefs. Queen mothers (Iyoba) held great influence; the most famous, Idia, mother of Oba Esigie, is commemorated in celebrated ivory and bronze mask portraits.

Art

Benin art is best known for its brass plaques, commemorative heads of Obas and queen mothers, ivory carvings, and terracotta sculpture. The casting guilds used sophisticated naturalism in early works, gradually becoming more stylised. The art served to record the history, ceremonies and military triumphs of the court.
