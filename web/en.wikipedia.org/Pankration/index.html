Pankration (Greek: pankrátion, "all of might" or "all-encompassing power") was a combat sport of the ancient Greek world that combined boxing and wrestling with kicking, joint locks, chokeholds and ground fighting. Introduced into the Olympic Games in 648 BC, it was among the most prestigious and brutal events of the ancient games, and is often described as an ancestor of modern mixed martial arts.

Rules

Competitors fought naked and bare-handed. Almost every technique was permitted: punches, kicks, throws, strangleholds and locks on fingers, arms and legs. In most festivals only biting and gouging the eyes were forbidden (Sparta reportedly allowed even those). There were no rounds and no weight classes — only men's and boys' divisions — and a bout ended when one fighter submitted, signalling by raising a finger, was knocked unconscious, or, occasionally, died; the legendary Arrhichion of Phigaleia was crowned Olympic champion posthumously after breaking his opponent's ankle in a hold while being strangled.

History and legacy

Pankration was contested at Olympia and at the other Panhellenic games — Pythian, Nemean and Isthmian — and victories there conferred immense fame; champions such as Theagenes of Thasos won well over a thousand crowns across boxing, pankration and running events. Greek myth attributed the art to Theseus and Heracles, and it also formed part of military training. Suppressed along with the pagan games in late antiquity, pankration was revived in the twentieth century: the Greek martial artist Jim Arvanitis codified a modern reconstruction from the 1970s, and amateur pankration is now governed internationally under United World Wrestling as a grappling-and-striking discipline with protective rules.
