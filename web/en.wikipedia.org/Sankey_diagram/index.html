Sankey diagrams are a type of flow diagram in which the width of the arrows is proportional to the flow rate. They are used to visualize the transfer of energy, materials or costs between the stages of a process, making sources, conversions and losses immediately apparent. The defining convention is conservation: the width of a flow entering a node equals the sum of the widths leaving it (except where losses or accumulation are shown).

History

The diagram is named after Irish engineer Captain Matthew Henry Phineas Riall Sankey, who used it in 1898 to compare the energy efficiency of a steam engine with an ideal engine. The form has older antecedents: Charles Minard's celebrated 1869 figurative map of Napoleon's Russian campaign of 1812 is often cited as a Sankey-like diagram, combining flow width with geography and a temperature chart.

Applications

Sankey diagrams are standard tools in energy analysis — national energy balances, such as those published by the International Energy Agency and Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory, use them to show primary energy supply flowing through conversion to end uses and rejected energy. They are also used in material flow analysis, life-cycle assessment, chemical engineering, ecology, and personal finance visualizations, where income splits into expenses and savings. Software libraries such as Google Charts and D3.js include Sankey generators.
