LocationLouisiana, United States

The Old River Control Structure is a floodgate system in a branch of the Mississippi River in central Louisiana. Completed in 1963 by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, it regulates the flow of water from the Mississippi into the Atchafalaya River, preventing the Mississippi from changing its present course and abandoning the cities of Baton Rouge and New Orleans.

Background

By the mid-20th century, the Atchafalaya River — a distributary of the Mississippi — offered a shorter, steeper route to the Gulf of Mexico and was capturing an increasing share of the Mississippi's flow. Geologist Harold Fisk documented the process, and by the 1950s engineers concluded that without intervention the Mississippi would switch channels, an event known as avulsion. Congress authorized the Corps to maintain the historical flow distribution, roughly 70 percent down the Mississippi and 30 percent down the Atchafalaya.

Components

The complex includes the Low Sill Structure, the Overbank Structure, a navigation lock, and, after the flood of 1973 nearly undermined the Low Sill gates, the Auxiliary Structure (completed 1986) and the Sidney A. Murray Jr. Hydroelectric Station (1990), which generates power from the diverted flow.

Significance

Failure of the control structures would redirect the Mississippi's main flow down the Atchafalaya, leaving the ports of Baton Rouge and New Orleans on a diminished channel, inundating communities in the Atchafalaya Basin such as Morgan City, and disrupting a large share of U.S. waterborne commerce and petrochemical industry. The writer John McPhee famously described the struggle in his 1987 essay "Atchafalaya," part of The Control of Nature.

The complex played a major role in managing the Mississippi River floods of 1973, 2011, and 2019, working in conjunction with the Morganza Spillway.
