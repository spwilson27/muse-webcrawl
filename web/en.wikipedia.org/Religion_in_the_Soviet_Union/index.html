Religion in the Soviet Union

Religion in the Soviet Union was shaped by the officially atheist ideology of the ruling Communist Party. Although Soviet constitutions formally guaranteed freedom of conscience, the state pursued the long-term goal of eliminating religion, confiscating church property, harassing believers, and promoting scientific atheism through education and propaganda organisations such as the League of Militant Atheists.

Early Soviet policy

The 1918 Decree on Separation of Church and State and of School and Church nationalised church land, banned religious instruction in schools, and stripped religious bodies of legal personhood. Under Lenin, and more aggressively under Stalin in the 1920s–1930s, thousands of churches, mosques, and synagogues were closed, demolished, or converted to warehouses and clubs; the Cathedral of Christ the Saviour in Moscow was dynamited in 1931. Clergy and active believers faced arrest, exile, and execution, particularly during the Great Terror.

Second World War and after

During the Second World War, Stalin relaxed repression to mobilise patriotic support: in 1943 he met Orthodox metropolitans, permitted the election of Patriarch Sergius I, and allowed many churches to reopen. The Russian Orthodox Church remained under tight state supervision through the Council for Religious Affairs. Nikita Khrushchev launched a renewed anti-religious campaign from 1958 to 1964, closing thousands of churches again.

Other faiths — Islam in Central Asia and the Caucasus, Judaism, Roman Catholicism (especially in Lithuania), Baptists and other Protestants, and Buddhism — were regulated through state-recognised administrations while unregistered congregations were persecuted. Jehovah's Witnesses and other groups were deported or imprisoned.

Late Soviet period

Despite decades of pressure, religious practice persisted privately. Under Gorbachev's glasnost, restrictions eased; the 1988 millennium of the Christianisation of Kievan Rus' was publicly celebrated, and new laws in 1990 guaranteed religious freedom shortly before the USSR dissolved in 1991, after which religious life expanded rapidly across the successor states.
