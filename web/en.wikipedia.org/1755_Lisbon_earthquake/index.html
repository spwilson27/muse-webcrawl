1755 Lisbon earthquake

Date1 November 1755
Local time09:40
Magnitude8.5–9.0 (estimated)
EpicentreAtlantic Ocean, ~200 km west-southwest of Cape St. Vincent
Areas affectedPortugal, Spain, Morocco; tsunami across the Atlantic
DeathsEstimated 10,000–100,000

The 1755 Lisbon earthquake, also known as the Great Lisbon earthquake, struck Portugal on the morning of Saturday, 1 November 1755 — All Saints' Day — at around 09:40 local time. In combination with the subsequent tsunami and fires, the earthquake almost totally destroyed Lisbon and adjoining areas. Seismologists estimate its magnitude at 8.5–9.0 on the moment magnitude scale, with an epicentre in the Atlantic Ocean about 200 km (120 mi) west-southwest of Cape St. Vincent in southern Portugal.

The earthquake is estimated to have killed between 10,000 and 100,000 people, making it one of the deadliest earthquakes in history. It had a profound effect on Portuguese society and on Enlightenment philosophy, and it spurred the development of seismology and earthquake engineering.

The disaster

The shock lasted between three and a half and six minutes, opening fissures up to 5 metres (16 ft) wide in the city centre. Survivors who fled to the open ground of the docks saw the sea recede, revealing shipwrecks, before a tsunami — waves estimated at up to 15 metres (50 ft) — rushed up the Tagus estuary about 40 minutes after the shock. Fires lit by overturned candles and kitchen hearths merged into a firestorm that burned for five days. Of Lisbon's 200,000 inhabitants, at least 10,000 died; roughly 85 per cent of the city's buildings, including the Ribeira Palace and the new opera house, were destroyed.

The tsunami reached Cornwall, Galway in Ireland, North Africa — where it killed thousands in Morocco — and crossed the Atlantic to be observed in the Caribbean. Shaking was felt across Europe, as far away as Finland.

Response and reconstruction

The king, Joseph I, survived but developed a lifelong fear of enclosed spaces. His secretary of state, Sebastião José de Carvalho e Melo (later Marquis of Pombal), organised the response with famous pragmatism — burying the dead, feeding the living, and hanging looters. Pombal directed the rebuilding of central Lisbon (the Baixa Pombalina) on a grid plan with wide streets and some of the world's first seismically resistant construction: timber lattice frameworks, the gaiola pombalina ("Pombaline cage"), embedded in masonry walls. He also circulated a parish-by-parish questionnaire about the earthquake — the Memórias Paroquiais of 1758 — a pioneering work of seismological data-gathering.

Philosophical impact

The disaster, striking a devout Catholic capital on a major feast day, shook Enlightenment confidence in divine providence and theodicy. Voltaire responded with his Poème sur le désastre de Lisbonne (1756) and made the earthquake a central motif of Candide (1759), satirising Leibnizian optimism. Kant wrote three essays on the earthquake's natural causes, and Rousseau's reply to Voltaire argued that human settlement patterns, not nature alone, made earthquakes deadly.
