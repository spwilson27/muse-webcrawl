Gauss–Bonnet theorem

The Gauss–Bonnet theorem is a fundamental result in differential geometry that links the geometry of a surface — its curvature — to its topology, expressed by its Euler characteristic. In its classical form, for a compact two-dimensional Riemannian manifold M without boundary, it states:

∫M K dA = 2π χ(M)

where K is the Gaussian curvature, dA the area element, and χ(M) the Euler characteristic of the surface.

Interpretation and examples

For a sphere of radius r, the curvature is constant (1/r²) and the total area is 4πr², so the integral equals 4π, matching 2πχ with χ = 2. For a torus, χ = 0, and indeed the positive curvature on the outer part exactly cancels the negative curvature on the inner part. The theorem shows that the total curvature depends only on topology, not on the surface's exact shape: bending a surface without stretching it leaves the integral unchanged.

Surfaces with boundary

For a compact surface with boundary, the formula gains terms for the geodesic curvature of the boundary curve and for the exterior angles at any corners:

∫M K dA + ∫∂M kg ds + Σ θi = 2π χ(M)

The local version of the theorem, applied to a geodesic triangle, shows that its angle sum exceeds π by exactly the integral of curvature inside it — generalising the fact that triangles on a sphere have angle sums greater than 180°.

History and generalisations

Versions of the result were known in special cases to Carl Friedrich Gauss, and the theorem is named for Gauss and Pierre Ossian Bonnet, who published a special case in 1848. Twentieth-century generalisations — most importantly the Chern–Gauss–Bonnet theorem for even-dimensional manifolds, proved by Shiing-Shen Chern in 1944 — connect characteristic classes to curvature and helped found the modern interplay between geometry and topology, culminating in index theorems such as the Atiyah–Singer theorem.
