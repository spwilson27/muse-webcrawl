Grassland

Temperate grassland: the North American prairie

Grasslands are areas where the vegetation is dominated by grasses (Poaceae). However, sedges and rushes can also be found along with variable proportions of legumes and other herbaceous plants. Grasslands occur naturally on all continents except Antarctica and are found in most terrestrial ecoregions, covering roughly a quarter to two-fifths of Earth's land area depending on definition.

Types

Tropical grasslands, or savannas, grow in warm climates with seasonal rainfall and scattered trees, as in East Africa and northern Australia. Temperate grasslands have hot summers, cold winters, and few trees; they include the prairies of North America, the steppes of Eurasia, the pampas of South America, and the veld of southern Africa. Montane and flooded grasslands form additional categories, and many grasslands are maintained or extended by human activity such as grazing and burning.

Ecology

Grassland ecosystems are shaped by grazing herbivores, periodic fire, and drought, which suppress woody plants. They support rich assemblages of grazing mammals — bison, wildebeest, antelope, wild horses — and their predators, as well as burrowing animals and grassland birds. Grassland soils, developed under millennia of root growth and decay, are among the most fertile on Earth, which is why temperate grasslands have been extensively converted to grain agriculture, earning regions such as the North American prairie the name "breadbasket."

Conservation

Grasslands are among the most threatened biomes: conversion to cropland, overgrazing, and woody encroachment driven by fire suppression and climate change have reduced their extent and biodiversity worldwide, and relatively little grassland area is protected compared with forests.
