Modern Hebrew


Native to: Israel
Speakers: about 9 million (L1 and L2)
Language family: Afro-Asiatic > Semitic > Canaanite > Hebrew
Writing system: Hebrew alphabet

Modern Hebrew (עִבְרִית חֲדָשָׁה, ʿIvrit ḥadashá) is the standard form of the Hebrew language spoken today. Spoken by about nine million people, it is the official language of Israel and the world's only successful large-scale example of a language revival: Hebrew had ceased to be a spoken vernacular around the 3rd century CE but survived as a liturgical and literary language before being revived as a spoken language in the late 19th and early 20th centuries.

Revival

The revival of Hebrew is closely associated with Eliezer Ben-Yehuda, who settled in Jerusalem in 1881 and championed Hebrew as the everyday language of the Jewish community in Palestine. Hebrew-speaking schools, newspapers, and institutions followed; the Academy of the Hebrew Language, founded in 1953, continues the work of coining terms for modern concepts.

Linguistics

Modern Hebrew preserves the consonantal writing system and much of the morphology of earlier Hebrew — a root-and-pattern system in which words are built from mostly triconsonantal roots — while its syntax, idioms, and some phonology have been influenced by the European languages of its early revivers, including Yiddish, Russian, Polish, and German. Word order is predominantly subject–verb–object, and the language has a system of binyanim (verb patterns) inherited from Biblical Hebrew.

Status

Hebrew is the primary language of government, education, and media in Israel. Arabic held co-official status until 2018, when the Nation-State Law designated Hebrew the "state language" and gave Arabic a "special status".
