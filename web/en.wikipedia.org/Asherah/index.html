Asherah

Asherah (Ugaritic: ʾAṯirat; Hebrew: אֲשֵׁרָה‎ ʾĂšērā) is a mother goddess in ancient Semitic religion. She appears in Akkadian, Hittite, Ugaritic and Hebrew sources. In the Ugaritic texts, Athirat is the consort of the god El, the head of the pantheon, and the mother of many gods, including the seventy sons of El. She is often associated with the sea — her full Ugaritic title is "Lady Athirat of the Sea" — and with trees and sacred groves.

In Ugaritic religion

At Ugarit (modern Ras Shamra in Syria), texts from the 13th century BCE describe Athirat as queen consort of El and "creatress of the gods". She intercedes with El on behalf of other gods, including Baal, and is invoked by kings seeking legitimacy. Her epithet "of the Sea" and her association with the Phoenician city of Tyre reflect her maritime connections.

In ancient Israel and Judah

In the Hebrew Bible, "Asherah" most often refers not to the goddess directly but to a wooden cultic object — a sacred pole or stylised tree — associated with her worship, which stood beside altars in Israelite and Judahite religion. Biblical writers repeatedly condemn the making of such objects, and reforming kings such as Hezekiah and Josiah are described as cutting them down. Archaeological evidence, including inscriptions from Kuntillet Ajrud and Khirbet el-Qom blessing people by "Yahweh and his Asherah", suggests that Asherah was venerated alongside Yahweh in popular religion. Hundreds of small "pillar figurines" found in Judahite homes of the 8th–6th centuries BCE are often interpreted as representations of Asherah or of her worshippers.

Interpretation

Scholars debate whether the biblical asherah was understood as the goddess herself or merely her symbol, and the meaning of the phrase "his Asherah" in inscriptions remains contested. The suppression of Asherah worship is commonly linked to the development of strict monotheism in ancient Judah during and after the Babylonian exile.
