Spanish missions in California

The Spanish missions in California (Spanish: Misiones españolas en California) comprise a series of 21 religious outposts established by Catholic priests of the Franciscan order between 1769 and 1823. They were founded to spread Christianity among the Native Americans of Alta California and to extend Spain's territorial claim along the Pacific coast, roughly along the route later known as El Camino Real.

History

The chain began with Mission San Diego de Alcalá in 1769, founded by Saint Junípero Serra, who established nine of the missions. Under his successors, including Fermín Lasuén, the system expanded northward to Mission San Francisco Solano (1823) in Sonoma. The missions, supported by presidios (forts) and pueblos (towns), introduced European agriculture, livestock and crafts to California.

Indigenous people — called Mission Indians — were gathered into the missions, often under coercion, where they were baptised, required to live and work, and exposed to European diseases that devastated their populations. Historians continue to debate and reassess the system's harsh discipline and demographic catastrophe. After Mexico secularised the missions in 1833–34, their lands were dispersed, buildings fell into ruin, and many were later restored in the 20th century; most remain active Catholic parishes or museums.

The 21 missions

From south to north the missions include San Diego de Alcalá, San Carlos Borromeo de Carmelo, San Antonio de Padua, San Gabriel Arcángel, San Luis Obispo de Tolosa, San Francisco de Asís (Mission Dolores), San Juan Capistrano, Santa Clara de Asís, San Buenaventura, Santa Barbara, La Purísima Concepción, Santa Cruz, Nuestra Señora de la Soledad, San José, San Juan Bautista, San Miguel Arcángel, San Fernando Rey de España, San Luis Rey de Francia, Santa Inés, San Rafael Arcángel and San Francisco Solano.

Legacy

The missions strongly influenced California's place names, architecture — the Mission Revival style — and historical memory; fourth-grade school projects on the missions are a longstanding California tradition, and Serra was canonised in 2015 amid ongoing controversy about the missions' treatment of Native peoples.
