Jewish mysticism

Jewish mysticism encompasses the mystical currents within Judaism, from the ancient Merkabah (chariot) traditions describing ascents to the heavenly palaces, through medieval Kabbalah, to the ecstatic spirituality of Hasidism and modern popular revivals.


The sefirot — the Tree of Life

Early mysticism

The earliest Jewish mystical texts, the Heikhalot literature of late antiquity, describe visionaries ascending through the heavenly palaces to behold the divine throne, echoing the vision of Ezekiel. The Sefer Yetzirah (Book of Formation) presents a cosmology based on the ten sefirot and the twenty-two letters of the Hebrew alphabet.

Kabbalah

Kabbalah emerged in 12th–13th century Provence and Spain. Its central text, the Zohar (Book of Splendour), traditionally attributed to the 2nd-century sage Shimon bar Yochai but composed largely by Moses de León, interprets the Torah through the framework of the ten sefirot — emanations through which the infinite God (Ein Sof) interacts with creation. In the 16th century, Isaac Luria of Safed transformed Kabbalah with teachings about tzimtzum (divine contraction), the breaking of the vessels, and tikkun (cosmic repair through human action).

Hasidism and beyond

In 18th-century Eastern Europe, Israel ben Eliezer, the Baal Shem Tov, founded Hasidism, which popularised mystical devotion, prayer and attachment (devekut) to God, centred on charismatic leaders called tzaddikim. Jewish mysticism continues to influence modern religion, scholarship — notably through Gershom Scholem's academic studies — and popular culture.
