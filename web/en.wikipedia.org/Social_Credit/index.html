Social Credit

Social Credit is an economic philosophy and political movement developed by the British engineer Clifford Hugh Douglas (1879–1952). Douglas argued that modern industrial economies chronically fail to distribute enough purchasing power to consumers to buy the goods that industry produces, a gap he attributed to the way money is created as interest-bearing bank debt. His remedy was a system of "social credit": a national dividend paid to every citizen and a mechanism of compensated (subsidized) prices, administered through a National Credit Office.

The A + B theorem

Douglas's best-known analytical device is the A + B theorem, presented in his books Economic Democracy (1920) and Credit-Power and Democracy (1920). In any productive enterprise, payments fall into two groups: A payments, made to individuals (wages, salaries, dividends), which become income and thus purchasing power; and B payments, made to other organizations (for raw materials, bank charges, and other costs), which do not directly become personal income. Since prices must recover both A and B payments, while only A payments are distributed as income in the same period, Douglas argued there is a structural shortfall of purchasing power relative to prices — a gap normally filled only by ever-increasing bank debt and export surpluses.

Proposed mechanisms

Douglas proposed two main instruments. The National Dividend would distribute newly created, debt-free credit directly to citizens, justified as an inheritance of the communal capital embodied in technology and social organization — "cultural heritage" made productive by the whole community. The Just Price (or compensated price) mechanism would subsidize retail prices so that goods sold below their accounting cost, with retailers reimbursed by the credit authority, reflecting the "real cost" of production measured in consumption rather than financial cost.

Political history

Social Credit ideas attracted a wide following during the Great Depression. In Canada, the Social Credit Party of Alberta under William Aberhart won the 1935 provincial election, though its attempts to implement Douglasite monetary schemes were struck down by the courts as encroaching on federal jurisdiction over banking and currency. Social Credit parties later held power in British Columbia, and the federal Social Credit Party of Canada remained a significant force, especially in Quebec under Réal Caouette. In New Zealand, the Social Credit Party won substantial vote shares (over 20 percent in 1978 and 1981) without winning many seats under first-past-the-post. Criticism

Mainstream economists have generally rejected the A + B theorem, arguing that B payments eventually become A payments in other firms, so the alleged gap double-counts costs across the production chain. Critics also warn that issuing consumption credit without regard to productive capacity could be inflationary. Social Credit's monetary ideas are nevertheless seen as precursors of later proposals for guaranteed income and citizen dividends, and of heterodox analyses of money creation.
