Herbal medicine

Herbal medicine (also called herbalism, phytomedicine or phytotherapy) is the study and practice of using plant materials — leaves, flowers, roots, bark, seeds and extracts — for medicinal purposes. It is the oldest and still the most widely used system of medicine in the world, and it forms the basis of traditional systems such as Ayurveda, traditional Chinese medicine and numerous indigenous practices.

History

Written records of herbal medicine date back more than 5,000 years, including Sumerian prescriptions, the Egyptian Ebers Papyrus (c. 1550 BC), Chinese materia medica such as the Shennong Bencao Jing, and the Greek works of Dioscorides, whose De Materia Medica remained authoritative for over 1,500 years. In the Islamic world and medieval Europe, herbals catalogued hundreds of medicinal plants. Many cornerstone drugs of modern medicine originated in this tradition: morphine from the opium poppy, quinine from cinchona bark, digoxin from foxglove, salicin (precursor of aspirin) from willow, and artemisinin from sweet wormwood, whose discovery earned Tu Youyou a share of the 2015 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

Practice and prevalence

The World Health Organization estimates that a large majority of the population in many developing countries relies on traditional, largely herbal, medicine for primary healthcare, and herbal products are also widely sold as supplements and remedies in industrialised countries. Preparations range from raw herbs and teas (infusions and decoctions) to tinctures, capsules and standardised extracts. Regulation varies greatly by country: some jurisdictions license herbal practitioners and control products as medicines, while elsewhere they are marketed as dietary supplements with limited oversight.

Efficacy and safety

Some herbal remedies have demonstrated efficacy in controlled trials, while for many others the evidence is weak or absent. Safety concerns include variability in potency, contamination or adulteration, toxicity of particular plants, and interactions with conventional drugs — St John's wort, for example, induces drug-metabolising enzymes and can reduce the effectiveness of many medicines. Scientific evaluation of herbal medicine, through pharmacognosy and clinical research, continues to investigate which traditional uses are supported by evidence.
