Gothic architecture
Periodc. 1140 – 16th century
OriginÎle-de-France, France
Key featuresPointed arch, rib vault, flying buttress, stained glass

Gothic architecture is an architectural style that was prevalent in Europe from the late 12th to the 16th century, during the High and Late Middle Ages, surviving into the 17th and 18th centuries in some areas. It evolved from Romanesque architecture and was succeeded by Renaissance architecture. It originated in the Île-de-France region of northern France as a development of Norman architecture.

The style is defined by the pointed arch, the ribbed vault, the flying buttress, and large windows with decorative tracery and stained glass, which together allowed taller, lighter, more brightly lit structures than Romanesque building. Famous early examples include the Basilica of Saint-Denis (rebuilt under Abbot Suger from 1140), Chartres Cathedral, and Notre-Dame de Paris.

Phases

Art historians divide the style into Early Gothic, High Gothic (including the Rayonnant style of the Sainte-Chapelle), and Late Gothic, known in France as Flamboyant and in England as Perpendicular. Regional variants flourished in England, Germany, Spain, Italy and Eastern Europe. Cathedrals, churches, abbeys, castles and universities were built in the style across Europe.

Revival and legacy

The style was revived in the 18th and especially the 19th century as the Gothic Revival, producing buildings such as the Palace of Westminster in London and Cologne Cathedral's completion. The term "Gothic" was initially a pejorative Renaissance label implying barbarism, attributing the style to the Goths; it is now understood as one of the defining artistic achievements of medieval Europe.
