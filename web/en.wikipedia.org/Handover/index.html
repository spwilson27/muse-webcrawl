Handover

In cellular telecommunications, a handover (or handoff in North American usage) is the process of transferring an ongoing voice call or data session from one cell site (base station) to another without disconnecting the session, as the user moves through the network's coverage area. Handover is a defining feature of mobile cellular systems, distinguishing them from earlier mobile radio services.

Types

Handovers may be hard ("break-before-make"), in which the old connection is released before the new one is established — typical of GSM — or soft ("make-before-break"), in which the device connects to the new cell before dropping the old one, as in CDMA systems. A softer handover occurs between sectors of the same base station. Handovers can also occur between different technologies (for example from 5G or LTE to Wi-Fi or 3G), called vertical handovers, as distinct from horizontal handovers within one technology.

Process

The mobile device continually measures the signal strength and quality of its serving cell and neighbours and reports these measurements to the network. When a neighbour becomes preferable by a configured margin (hysteresis and time-to-trigger conditions guard against rapid oscillation between cells, the "ping-pong effect"), the network commands the handover, reallocates radio resources, and routing is updated. In modern LTE and 5G networks the procedure is largely mobile-assisted and network-controlled, aiming to complete within tens of milliseconds for voice continuity.

Other uses

The term also refers generally to transfers of responsibility: clinical handover between medical staff, shift handovers in industry, and political handovers of power or territory (e.g., the 1997 handover of Hong Kong).
