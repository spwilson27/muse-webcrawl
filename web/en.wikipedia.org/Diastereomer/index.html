Diastereomer

D-erythrose and D-threose are diastereomers: epimers differing at one stereocentre.

In stereochemistry, a diastereomer (also spelled diastereoisomer) is a type of stereoisomer: two molecules with the same constitution that differ in the spatial arrangement of their atoms but are not mirror images of one another. This distinguishes diastereomers from enantiomers, which are non-superimposable mirror images.

Properties

Unlike enantiomers, which have identical scalar physical properties in an achiral environment, diastereomers generally have different melting points, boiling points, solubilities, and chemical reactivities, and can therefore be separated by ordinary techniques such as fractional crystallisation, distillation, and chromatography. Diastereomers arise whenever a molecule has two or more stereogenic centres and the configurations differ at some, but not all, of them; molecules with n stereocentres can have up to 2n stereoisomers, falling into pairs of enantiomers that are diastereomeric to one another. (Symmetric molecules with identical halves may instead give achiral meso forms.)

Epimers and anomers

Diastereomers differing at exactly one stereocentre are called epimers — for example, D-glucose and D-mannose differ only at C-2. In carbohydrate chemistry, the special epimers at the anomeric carbon are called anomers (α and β forms).

Diastereoselectivity

Because diastereomeric transition states differ in energy, reactions of chiral or prochiral substrates can favour one diastereomeric product over another — the basis of diastereoselective synthesis and of the use of chiral auxiliaries. A classical application is the resolution of a racemic mixture by converting enantiomers into diastereomeric salts with a chiral resolving agent, separating them, and then removing the agent.
