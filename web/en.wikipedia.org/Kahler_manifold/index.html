Kähler manifold

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In mathematics, and in particular differential geometry, a Kähler manifold is a manifold with three mutually compatible structures: a complex structure, a Riemannian structure, and a symplectic structure. It is named after the German mathematician Erich Kähler, who introduced the concept in the 1930s.

Definition

A Kähler manifold is a Hermitian manifold whose associated (1,1)-form — the Kähler form, built from the metric and the complex structure — is closed. Equivalently, the Levi-Civita connection preserves the complex structure, so that holonomy lies in the unitary group. Locally, the metric can be written as the second derivative of a single real-valued function, the Kähler potential. Because the Kähler form is symplectic and compatible with the metric, every Kähler manifold is at once a complex, Riemannian, and symplectic manifold.

Examples and significance

Basic examples include complex Euclidean space with the flat metric, complex projective space with the Fubini–Study metric, and all compact Riemann surfaces. By the Kodaira embedding theorem, compact Kähler manifolds with rational Kähler class are projective algebraic varieties, tying Kähler geometry closely to algebraic geometry. Calabi–Yau manifolds — Kähler manifolds with vanishing first Chern class and Ricci-flat metrics, whose existence was proved by Shing-Tung Yau — are central to string theory compactifications. Hodge theory takes an especially strong form on Kähler manifolds: harmonic forms decompose by type, the hard Lefschetz theorem holds, and the odd Betti numbers of a compact Kähler manifold are even.
