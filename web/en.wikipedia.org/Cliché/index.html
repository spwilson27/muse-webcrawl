Cliché

A cliché is a phrase, idea or element of an artistic work that has become overused to the point of losing its original meaning or effect, even to the point of being trite or irritating. The term is frequently used in modern culture to describe an idea or event thought to be predictable or based on a prior event.

Etymology

The word comes from French printing: a cliché was a stereotype plate — a solid block cast from a mould of set type — named for the sound made when the matrix struck the molten metal, clicher being an onomatopoeic verb meaning "to click". Just as the plate reproduced the same text endlessly, a verbal cliché reproduces a once-fresh expression endlessly. The figurative sense spread into English in the late 19th century.

Usage

Clichés appear in every register: stock phrases in speech and journalism ("at the end of the day", "think outside the box"), plot devices in fiction, and visual shortcuts in film. Writers generally treat them as faults of freshness, though defenders note that formulaic expressions aid rapid communication and that genres — romance, westerns, superhero films — rely on conventions audiences expect. Phrases typically become clichés because they were once strikingly apt, a process sometimes called the "cliché of the truth".
