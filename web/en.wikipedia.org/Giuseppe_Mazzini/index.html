Giuseppe Mazzini (22 June 1805 – 10 March 1872) was an Italian politician, journalist, and activist for the unification of Italy (Risorgimento) and spearhead of the Italian revolutionary movement. His efforts helped bring about the independent and unified Italy in place of the several separate states, many dominated by foreign powers, that existed until the 19th century. An Italian nationalist in the historical radical tradition and a proponent of a republican, social-democratic programme, Mazzini helped define the modern European movement for popular democracy in a republican state.

Life

Mazzini was born in Genoa, then part of the Ligurian Republic under French occupation. He joined the secret society of the Carbonari but, disillusioned, founded his own movement, Young Italy (La Giovine Italia), in Marseille in 1831, aiming for a united, free, independent republican Italy through popular insurrection. Exiled for most of his life, chiefly in London, he founded Young Europe to promote nationalism among other peoples and inspired revolutionary movements across the continent.

In 1849 he became a triumvir of the short-lived Roman Republic, which was crushed by French troops. Unlike the pragmatic statecraft of Cavour, who achieved unification under the Piedmontese monarchy, Mazzini insisted on a democratic republic and continued plotting insurrections until his death in Pisa in 1872, living in Italy under the false name George Brown.

Thought and legacy

Mazzini's writings, including The Duties of Man (1860), linked nationalism to democracy and social justice, emphasising duties alongside rights. His thought influenced later republican and nationalist movements worldwide, including figures in India and China. Historians regard him, with Garibaldi and Cavour, as one of the architects of Italian unification — its prophet, as he is often called.
