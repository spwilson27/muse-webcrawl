Greek lyric

PeriodArchaic Greece, c. 650–450 BC (later examples to Hellenistic era)
LanguageAncient Greek (Aeolic, Ionic, Doric dialects)
AccompanimentLyre
Canonical poetsNine Lyric Poets of Alexandria

Greek lyric is the body of poetry written in ancient Greece that was originally sung to the accompaniment of the lyre (from which the word "lyric" derives), as distinct from epic and drama. Flourishing in the Archaic period, roughly from the mid-seventh to the mid-fifth century BC, it marks the emergence of the personal, subjective voice in Western literature.

Forms

Scholars traditionally divide Greek lyric into two broad kinds. Monody was sung by a single performer, typically at the symposium; its metres include the elegiac couplet used by Archilochus (in his iambic verse), Mimnermus and Solon, and the varied stanzaic forms of Alcaeus and Sappho of Lesbos. Choral lyric was performed by a singing, dancing chorus at public festivals and religious occasions; Alcman, Stesichorus, Ibycus, Simonides, Bacchylides and above all Pindar — famed for his victory odes (epinikia) celebrating athletes at the Panhellenic games — are its masters.

Themes

Where epic celebrated heroic myth at a distance, lyric is intimate and immediate: love and desire, wine and the symposium, war and politics, grief, old age, praise of gods and men, and reflection on mortality. Sappho's poems of longing and Anacreon's convivial songs became bywords for their genres in later antiquity.

Transmission

Hellenistic scholars at Alexandria canonised nine lyric poets: Alcman, Sappho, Alcaeus, Anacreon, Stesichorus, Ibycus, Simonides, Pindar and Bacchylides. Most lyric survives only in fragments quoted by later authors or recovered on Egyptian papyri — notably substantial papyrus finds of Sappho, Archilochus and Bacchylides, the last of whom is the only choral poet besides Pindar to survive in bulk. The discovery of the Cologne papyrus of Archilochus and new Sappho fragments in the twenty-first century shows the corpus can still grow.

Influence

Greek lyric provided the models for Roman poets such as Catullus and Horace, whose Odes adapt Aeolic metres, and through them shaped the whole later European lyric tradition, down to the very word "ode".
