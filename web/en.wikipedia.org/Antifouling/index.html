Antifouling

Antifouling refers to coatings, treatments, and technologies used to prevent or slow biofouling — the unwanted accumulation of microorganisms, algae, barnacles, molluscs, and other organisms on surfaces submerged in water, especially the hulls of ships. Fouling increases hydrodynamic drag, fuel consumption, and corrosion, and can spread invasive aquatic species.

History

Ancient mariners sheathed hulls in lead and later copper; copper sheathing became standard on wooden warships in the 18th century because copper ions are toxic to fouling organisms. In the 20th century, paints containing tributyltin (TBT) were highly effective but proved extremely harmful to marine life, causing imposex in gastropods, and were banned worldwide by the International Maritime Organization's Anti-Fouling Systems Convention, which entered into force in 2008.

Modern methods

Modern antifouling paints typically use copper(I) oxide or copper thiocyanate with booster biocides, delivered in ablative or self-polishing copolymer matrices that release biocide at a controlled rate. Biocide-free approaches include silicone-based foul-release coatings with low surface energy that make it hard for organisms to adhere, ultrasonic systems, and biomimetic textures inspired by shark skin.
