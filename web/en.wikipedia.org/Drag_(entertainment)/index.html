Drag (entertainment)

Drag is a form of entertainment in which a person dresses in clothing and assumes a persona associated with a different gender, typically in an exaggerated fashion, for performance. Performers presenting femininely are called drag queens; performers presenting masculinely are drag kings. Drag acts — combining elaborate costume, make-up, wigs, lip-syncing, comedy, dance, and live singing — are a central feature of LGBT culture and gay nightlife, and since the 2000s have achieved broad mainstream popularity through television franchises such as RuPaul's Drag Race.

History

Gender-crossing performance is ancient — from boy actors playing women in Shakespeare's theatre to the onnagata of Japanese kabuki and the female impersonators of 19th-century minstrel and vaudeville stages, such as Julian Eltinge. In the early 20th century, female impersonation flourished in underground gay bars and in Harlem's drag balls, where Black and Latino queer communities developed the ball culture of houses, categories, and voguing later documented in the film Paris Is Burning (1990). Drag was frequently policed under laws against cross-dressing and "masquerading", and bars hosting drag were targets of raids that helped spark the modern gay rights movement. Performers such as Divine (in John Waters's films) and Britain's Danny La Rue and Lily Savage brought drag toward the mainstream.

Modern drag

RuPaul's Drag Race, first aired in 2009, transformed drag into a global television phenomenon with international franchises, touring shows, and conventions such as RuPaul's DragCon. Contemporary drag encompasses pageant elegance, comedy, horror ("dragula") aesthetics, and political satire; drag story hours and brunches have become popular — and in some jurisdictions politically contested — forms of family and mainstream entertainment. Language from ball culture ("shade", "reading", "werk") has entered general slang.

Terminology

The word "drag" is often said to derive from the trailing of long dresses on stage, or from a supposed Shakespearean stage direction acronym ("dressed resembling a girl"); the former explanation is generally favoured. A performer's drag persona is distinct from their gender identity: drag is a performance art, practised by people of all genders and orientations.
