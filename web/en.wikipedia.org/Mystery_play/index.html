Mystery play

Mystery plays and miracle plays (occasionally distinguished as two different forms, although the terms are often used interchangeably) are among the earliest formally developed plays in medieval Europe. Medieval mystery plays focused on the representation of Bible stories in churches as tableaux with accompanying antiphonal song.

Development

Beginning around the tenth century as liturgical tropes, performances gradually moved out of the church into the marketplace, where they were staged by town guilds — the word "mystery" in this context derives partly from the French mystère and partly from ministerium, the guilds or "mysteries" that mounted them. Performed in cycles covering biblical history from the Creation to the Last Judgment, the plays were mounted on pageant wagons that processed from station to station through a town.

Surviving cycles

The best-known English cycles are those of York, Chester, Wakefield (Towneley), and the N-Town plays. The Wakefield cycle includes the comic masterpiece The Second Shepherds' Play. The cycles declined under Protestant and Catholic censorship in the sixteenth century but have been revived in modern productions, including the York Mystery Plays.
