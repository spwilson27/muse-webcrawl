Cystatin C or cystatin 3 (formerly gamma trace, post-gamma-globulin, or neuroendocrine basic polypeptide) is a protein encoded in humans by the CST3 gene. It is a small (13.3 kilodalton) non-glycosylated basic protein belonging to the cystatin superfamily of cysteine protease inhibitors. Cystatin C is produced by all nucleated cells in the body at a relatively constant rate and is freely filtered by the glomerulus in the kidney, after which it is reabsorbed and catabolised in the proximal tubules.

Clinical significance

Because serum cystatin C concentration depends mainly on glomerular filtration, it is used as a biomarker of kidney function and for estimating the glomerular filtration rate (GFR). Unlike creatinine, its production is less affected by muscle mass, age, sex, and diet, which makes it attractive for detecting mild reductions in kidney function and for estimating GFR in patients in whom creatinine-based estimates are unreliable. Estimating equations combining creatinine and cystatin C generally predict measured GFR more accurately than either marker alone.

Structure and function

Cystatin C consists of a single polypeptide chain of 120 amino acid residues. It inhibits cysteine proteases such as cathepsins B, H, K, L, and S by binding tightly to their active sites, and it is thought to play a protective role against proteolytic damage in extracellular fluids. Cystatin C is found in all tissues and body fluids that have been examined, with particularly high concentrations in cerebrospinal fluid and seminal fluid.

History

The protein was first described in 1961 as a trace protein in cerebrospinal fluid and urine of patients with renal failure. Its amino acid sequence was reported in the early 1980s, and its use as a marker of glomerular filtration was proposed soon afterwards.
