Reintroduction

Wolves were reintroduced to Yellowstone National Park in 1995.

Reintroduction is the deliberate release of a species into the wild in an area where it was formerly native but has become locally extinct (extirpated). It is a tool of conservation biology, usually undertaken to restore an ecosystem's function or to save a threatened species, and is distinguished from reinforcement (adding individuals to an existing population) and from introduction (release outside the historic range, a form of assisted migration when done for conservation).

Guidelines and challenges

The IUCN's Guidelines for Reintroductions and Other Conservation Translocations (2013) call for feasibility assessment — confirming that the causes of the original decline have been removed — risk analysis, suitable source stock of good genetic health, soft- or hard-release protocols, and long-term monitoring. Captive-bred animals may lack survival skills; wild-caught translocated animals are often preferred. Reintroductions can fail through predation, disease, poor habitat, or conflict with people, as when predators take livestock.

Notable examples

Celebrated successes include the Arabian oryx in Oman, the California condor in the southwestern United States, the peregrine falcon across North America and Europe, black-footed ferrets on the Great Plains, Przewalski's horse in Mongolia, and the grey wolf in Yellowstone National Park in 1995 — whose ecological effects, including on elk behaviour and riparian vegetation, are widely studied (though the size of the "trophic cascade" remains debated). In Britain, beavers and red kites have been successfully returned; large-carnivore restoration, such as lynx and wolf recovery in Europe, remains contentious with farming communities.
