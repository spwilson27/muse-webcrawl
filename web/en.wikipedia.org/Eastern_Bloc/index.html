The Eastern Bloc, also known as the Communist Bloc or Soviet Bloc, was the group of communist states of Central and Eastern Europe, East Asia, Southeast Asia, Africa, and Latin America aligned with the Soviet Union during the Cold War (1947–1991). The term usually excludes the Western Bloc, led by the United States.

Formation

As the Red Army liberated Eastern Europe from Nazi occupation in 1944–45, the Soviet Union installed communist-dominated governments across the region. By 1949 the bloc included Poland, East Germany, Czechoslovakia, Hungary, Romania, Bulgaria, and Albania, bound to Moscow through bilateral treaties, the Cominform, and later the Council for Mutual Economic Assistance (Comecon) and the Warsaw Pact.

Control and dissent

Soviet dominance was enforced by military intervention against internal challenges: the Hungarian Revolution of 1956 and the Prague Spring of 1968 were both crushed by Warsaw Pact forces. Yugoslavia broke with Stalin in 1948, and Albania shifted its allegiance to China in the 1960s during the Sino-Soviet split.

Dissolution

Under Mikhail Gorbachev, the Soviet Union renounced intervention in its allies' affairs. In 1989 communist governments fell across Eastern Europe in a wave of mostly peaceful revolutions; the Berlin Wall fell in November 1989, Germany reunified in 1990, and the Warsaw Pact and the Soviet Union itself dissolved in 1991.
