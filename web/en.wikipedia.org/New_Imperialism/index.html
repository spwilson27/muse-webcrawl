New Imperialism

In historical contexts, New Imperialism characterises a period of colonial expansion by the European powers, the United States and Japan during the late 19th and early 20th centuries, roughly from the 1870s to the outbreak of the First World War in 1914. The period featured an unprecedented pursuit of overseas territorial acquisitions: European states came to hold direct political control over almost 85 percent of the Earth's surface, most dramatically in the "Scramble for Africa" and the opening of Asia to foreign spheres of influence.

Causes

Historians have debated the drivers of the era. Economic explanations, classically formulated by J. A. Hobson (1902) and adapted by Lenin in Imperialism, the Highest Stage of Capitalism (1917), emphasised the search for markets, raw materials and outlets for surplus capital. Others stress strategic rivalry among great powers, national prestige, the enabling technologies of steamships, telegraphs, quinine and modern weaponry, missionary activity, and pseudo-scientific racial ideologies that framed empire as a civilising mission.

Expansion

Between 1881 and 1914 European powers partitioned nearly all of Africa — only Ethiopia and Liberia retained independence — following rules laid down at the Berlin Conference of 1884–85. In Asia, Britain consolidated India and Burma, France took Indochina, the Dutch extended control over the East Indies, and China was carved into spheres of influence; the United States annexed Hawaii, Puerto Rico and the Philippines after 1898, and Japan emerged as an imperial power at the expense of Korea and Taiwan. Competition for colonies produced crises such as Fashoda (1898) and the Moroccan crises, contributing to the tensions that led to the First World War. Decolonisation after 1945 eventually dismantled these empires.
