The Cambrian is the first geological period of the Paleozoic Era and of the Phanerozoic Eon, lasting from about 538.8 million years ago to about 485.4 million years ago, a span of some 53 million years. It succeeded the Ediacaran Period of the Neoproterozoic and was followed by the Ordovician. The Cambrian is famous for the Cambrian explosion, a relatively rapid diversification of multicellular life in which most major animal phyla first appear in the fossil record, along with the first abundant mineralised skeletons, shells, and exoskeletons.

The period was named by Adam Sedgwick in 1835 after Cambria, the Latinised name for Wales, where rocks of this age were first studied. The base of the Cambrian is defined internationally by the first appearance of the trace fossil Treptichnus pedum, with the global stratotype section at Fortune Head, Newfoundland.

Subdivisions

The Cambrian is divided into four epochs and ten stages: the Terreneuvian (Fortunian and "Stage 2"), Series 2 (Stages 3 and 4), the Miaolingian (Wuliuan, Drumian, Guzhangian), and the Furongian (Paibian, Jiangshanian, and "Stage 10"). Many of these divisions were historically based on trilobite biostratigraphy, and trilobites remain key index fossils for the period.

Climate and geography

At the start of the Cambrian the supercontinent Pannotia was breaking apart; by the middle of the period the principal landmasses were Gondwana — a vast continent covering much of the Southern Hemisphere — and the smaller continents Laurentia, Baltica, and Siberia. Sea levels rose through the period, flooding continental margins and creating extensive shallow epicontinental seas. The climate was generally warm, with no evidence of polar ice caps, and atmospheric oxygen levels rose toward modern values.

Life

The Cambrian explosion saw the appearance of arthropods, brachiopods, molluscs, echinoderms, chordates, and many other groups. Trilobites dominated many marine ecosystems, while the giant apex predator Anomalocaris and other radiodonts hunted in the water column. Exceptional fossil deposits (Lagerstätten) such as the Burgess Shale of British Columbia (described by Charles Doolittle Walcott from 1909) and the Chengjiang biota of Yunnan, China preserve soft-bodied organisms including Hallucigenia, Opabinia, Wiwaxia, and the early chordate-like Haikouichthys and Pikaia. Reef building was carried out first by archaeocyath sponges and later by microbial communities after the archaeocyaths declined.

The period ended with a series of extinction events, including the end-Botomian and Dresbachian events, which hit trilobites and other groups hard but did not rival the largest mass extinctions of later periods.
