Blindness

Blindness is strictly defined as the state of having no light perception. However, the term is used more broadly to describe severe visual impairment — a lack of vision so profound that it cannot be corrected with glasses, contact lenses, medication, or surgery. Legal definitions vary by jurisdiction; in North America and much of Europe, "legal blindness" is defined as visual acuity of 20/200 (6/60) or less in the better eye with best correction, or a visual field of 20 degrees or less.

Causes

Globally, the leading causes of blindness include cataract, glaucoma, age-related macular degeneration, diabetic retinopathy, trachoma, and uncorrected refractive error. According to the World Health Organization, at least 2.2 billion people have a vision impairment, and in at least 1 billion of these cases the impairment could have been prevented or has yet to be addressed. Cataract remains the most common cause worldwide and is treatable with surgery.

Classification

Visual impairment ranges from mild to total blindness. The WHO classifies distance vision impairment by presenting visual acuity in the better eye, from mild (worse than 6/12) through moderate and severe to blindness (worse than 3/60).

Aids and adaptations

Blind people navigate with the long white cane or guide dogs, read Braille or use screen readers and refreshable Braille displays, and employ optical character recognition and audio description. Orientation and mobility training teaches independent travel skills.

Society

Blindness has been a frequent metaphor in literature and religion, and notable blind people include the poet Homer (by tradition), musician Stevie Wonder, and author and activist Helen Keller, whose education story became widely known.
