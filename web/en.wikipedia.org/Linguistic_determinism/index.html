Linguistic determinism

Linguistic determinism is the strong form of the idea that language determines thought: that the structure of the language a person speaks limits and shapes the categories and patterns of their cognition, so speakers of different languages effectively think in different ways. It is associated with the "strong" reading of the Sapir–Whorf hypothesis, after linguists Edward Sapir and Benjamin Lee Whorf.

Whorf's claims

Whorf argued, from his study of the Hopi language, that Hopi grammar encoded a fundamentally different conception of time from European languages; he also pointed to grammatical categories such as number, gender, and tense as moulding habitual thought. These claims influenced fields from anthropology to cognitive science, though Whorf's Hopi analysis was later criticised as partly inaccurate.

Modern assessment

Strong determinism is generally rejected today: translation is possible, pre-linguistic infants and non-human animals display sophisticated cognition, and speakers can learn concepts their language does not lexicalise. However, the weaker thesis of linguistic relativity — that language influences attention, memory, and categorisation in specific domains such as colour, space, and number — is supported by experimental research and remains an active research area.
