Horology

Horology (literally "the study of time"; from Greek hōra, "hour, time", and logos, "study") is the study of the measurement of time. Clocks, watches, clockwork, sundials, hourglasses, clepsydras, timers, time recorders, marine chronometers, and atomic clocks are all examples of instruments used to measure time. In current usage, horology refers mainly to the study of mechanical time-keeping devices, while chronometry more broadly includes electronic devices. People interested in horology are called horologists; the term is used both by professionals who deal in timekeeping apparatus (watchmakers, clockmakers) and by scholars and enthusiasts.

History

Timekeeping devices have developed from ancient Egyptian and Babylonian water clocks and sundials, through the verge escapement clocks of medieval Europe, Christiaan Huygens's pendulum clock (1656) and balance spring (1675), John Harrison's marine chronometers that solved the problem of longitude, and the standardisation of mean time and time zones driven by the railways in the 19th century. In the 20th century came electric clocks, the quartz watch (1969), which caused the "quartz crisis" in the Swiss mechanical watch industry, and atomic clocks, which now define the second and underpin Coordinated Universal Time.

Institutions

Horological learning is preserved by bodies such as the British Horological Institute (founded 1858), the Worshipful Company of Clockmakers of London (chartered 1631), and national associations of watch and clock collectors. Major museum collections include those of the British Museum, the Science Museum in London, the Patek Philippe Museum in Geneva, and the International Museum of Horology at La Chaux-de-Fonds, Switzerland.
