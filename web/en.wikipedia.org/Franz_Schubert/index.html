Franz Peter Schubert (31 January 1797 – 19 November 1828) was an Austrian composer of the late Classical and early Romantic eras. Despite his short life, he left behind a vast body of work — more than 600 secular vocal works (mainly Lieder), seven complete symphonies, sacred music, operas, and a large quantity of chamber and piano music. He is ranked among the greatest composers of Western classical music.

Life

Born in a suburb of Vienna, Schubert showed extraordinary musical talent early, studying with Antonio Salieri at the Imperial Court Chapel. He worked briefly as a schoolteacher in his father's school but devoted himself increasingly to composition. Living largely on the support of friends, he became the centre of musical evenings called Schubertiades. He died at 31, probably of typhoid fever, having been largely unrecognized by the broader public in his lifetime.

Music

Schubert is regarded as the father of the German art song, or Lied. His 1815 setting of Goethe's "Erlkönig", written at 18, demonstrated a revolutionary fusion of poetry, piano writing, and vocal line. Song cycles such as Die schöne Müllerin (1823) and Winterreise (1827) are cornerstones of the repertoire. His instrumental works — including the "Unfinished" Symphony No. 8, the "Great" Symphony No. 9, the Trout Quintet, and the String Quintet in C major — combine Classical forms with unprecedented harmonic daring and melodic lyricism.

Posthumous reputation

Much of Schubert's output was unpublished at his death. Through the advocacy of Schumann, Liszt, Brahms, and others, his reputation grew steadily through the 19th century; George Grove and Arthur Sullivan's 1867 trip to Vienna recovered numerous manuscripts, including lost symphonies.
