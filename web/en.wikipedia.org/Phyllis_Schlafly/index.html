Phyllis Stewart Schlafly (born Phyllis McAlpin Stewart; August 15, 1924 – September 5, 2016) was an American attorney, conservative activist, and author. She is best known for mobilising grassroots opposition that defeated ratification of the proposed Equal Rights Amendment (ERA) to the United States Constitution during the 1970s, and for founding the Eagle Forum, a conservative advocacy organisation. Her self-published 1964 book A Choice Not an Echo, supporting Barry Goldwater, sold millions of copies and helped shape the modern conservative movement.

Early life and education

Schlafly was born in St. Louis, Missouri, and grew up during the Great Depression; her father was a machinist and salesman who lost his job in 1932. She worked her way through Washington University in St. Louis, including night shifts testing weapons at an ordnance plant during the Second World War, graduating Phi Beta Kappa in 1944. She earned a master's degree in government from Radcliffe College in 1945, married attorney Fred Schlafly in 1949, and much later, in 1978, earned a law degree from Washington University and was admitted to the Illinois bar.

Early political career

Schlafly first ran for Congress in 1952, losing in the general election, and again in 1970. A specialist in national defence, she co-authored books criticising arms-control agreements and arguing for American nuclear superiority. A Choice Not an Echo (1964) attacked the moderate Republican establishment ("kingmakers") and is credited with aiding Goldwater's capture of the Republican nomination.

STOP ERA campaign

After Congress passed the Equal Rights Amendment in 1972 and sent it to the states, Schlafly founded the National Committee to Stop ERA, later folded into the Eagle Forum. She argued the amendment would strip homemakers of protections, subject women to the military draft and combat duty, eliminate sex-segregated facilities, and transfer family law to the federal courts. Her network of state-level volunteers — famous for delivering homemade bread and pies to legislators — lobbied decisively in unratified states such as Illinois. Although 35 states ratified (three short of the required 38), the campaign, alongside disputed rescissions and the lapse of the extended deadline in 1982, prevented the amendment from entering the Constitution.

Later career and legacy

Schlafly remained a prominent voice on the right for four decades: opposing abortion and feminism, supporting school prayer, criticising international agreements she viewed as threats to US sovereignty, and hosting a long-running radio commentary. She endorsed Donald Trump in 2016, a decision that split her organisation shortly before her death that September. Historians regard her as a central architect of the New Right's grassroots style and of the Republican Party's turn toward social conservatism.
