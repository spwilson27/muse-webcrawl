Hieratic (from Greek hieratikos, "priestly") is the cursive writing system used for the ancient Egyptian language from the earliest dynasties until the Roman period. Written with a reed pen and ink, chiefly on papyrus and ostraca, it is a cursive counterpart of the monumental hieroglyphic script, with simplified sign forms and frequent ligatures that allowed scribes to write quickly.

Development

Hieratic developed alongside hieroglyphs from around 3200 BCE and was the everyday script of administration, letters, accounts, literature, and science, while hieroglyphs were reserved for carved monumental inscriptions. In the Middle Kingdom its letter forms were standardised in vertical columns and later in horizontal lines; a more cursive "abnormal hieratic" was used for business documents in Upper Egypt in the Third Intermediate Period. From about the 7th century BCE, the even more cursive Demotic script largely displaced hieratic for secular purposes, and hieratic survived mainly in priestly and religious use — hence its Greek name — until the 3rd century CE.

Texts

Most surviving Egyptian literature is preserved in hieratic, including the Rhind Mathematical Papyrus, the medical Ebers and Edwin Smith papyri, the Westcar tales, and wisdom literature such as the Maxims of Ptahhotep.
