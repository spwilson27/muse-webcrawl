Yaoguai

Yaoguai (Chinese: 妖怪; pinyin: yāoguài) are malevolent supernatural beings in Chinese folklore and mythology, roughly translated as "demons" or "monsters." A yaoguai is most often an animal, plant or object that has acquired magical powers — and usually a human or semi-human form — through long cultivation of spiritual energy, or a fallen celestial being or corrupted spirit sent down or escaped to the mortal world.

Origins and nature

In traditional cosmology, any creature or thing that lives long enough may absorb the essence of the sun, moon and earth and awaken to sentience; with further cultivation it can transform, cast illusions and work magic. Foxes, snakes, spiders, centipedes, trees and even old household objects are all common yaoguai origins. Those that cultivate through righteous practice may aspire to immortality as xian; those that devour humans or absorb human essence for a shortcut become the villains of folktale and fiction. Yaoguai are typically countered by Taoist priests, Buddhist monks and deities, whose talismans, mirrors and ritual implements reveal a yaoguai's true form.

In literature

The most famous yaoguai appear in the Ming-dynasty novel Journey to the West, in which the monk Tang Sanzang's pilgrimage is repeatedly obstructed by demons hoping that eating his flesh will grant immortality — among them the White Bone Demon (Baigujing), the spider women of the Silken Web cave and the Bull Demon King and his wife Princess Iron Fan. Qing-dynasty collections such as Pu Songling's Strange Tales from a Chinese Studio (which features many fox spirits, or huli jing) and Ji Yun's writings catalogue hundreds more.

Related concepts

The term is cognate with the Japanese yōkai (written with the same characters) and Korean yogoe, reflecting the shared folklore of the Sinosphere. Distinct but related beings include gui (ghosts of the dead), jing (essence-spirits) and mo (demons).
