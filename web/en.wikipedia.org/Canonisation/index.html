Canonization

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Canonization is the declaration of a deceased person as an officially recognised saint, specifically, the official act of a Christian communion declaring a person worthy of public veneration and entering their name in the canon, or authorised list, of that communion's recognised saints.

In the Catholic Church

In the Catholic Church, canonization is a papal act that follows a long process of investigation, usually beginning at the diocesan level. The candidate passes through the stages of Servant of God, Venerable (upon recognition of heroic virtue), and Blessed (after beatification, normally requiring a verified miracle). A further miracle is generally required before canonization, after which the saint may be venerated by the universal Church.

In other traditions

The Eastern Orthodox Church recognises saints through a process often called glorification, typically formalised by a synod after popular veneration has developed. Some Anglican and Lutheran churches also commemorate saints, though without a centralised judicial process.

History

In the early Church, martyrs were venerated locally by acclamation. Papal reservation of canonization developed in the Middle Ages; the first recorded papal canonization was that of Saint Ulrich of Augsburg in 993, and Pope Alexander III asserted exclusive papal authority over the process in the 12th century.
