Fentanyl

Clinical data
Class: Synthetic opioid analgesic
Formula: C22H28N2O
Routes: IV, transdermal, nasal, buccal, oral
Addiction liability: High

Fentanyl is a highly potent synthetic opioid used as an analgesic and anaesthetic. It is roughly 100 times stronger than morphine and about 50 times stronger than heroin as an analgesic. Fentanyl was first synthesized by Paul Janssen in 1959 and was approved for medical use in the United States in 1968. It is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines, and in medicine it is widely used for surgical anaesthesia, severe pain management, and palliative care, delivered by injection, transdermal patch, lozenge, or nasal spray.

Pharmacology

Fentanyl acts primarily on the μ-opioid receptor. Its high lipid solubility lets it cross the blood–brain barrier rapidly, giving a fast onset and short duration of action after a single dose. Its effects include analgesia, sedation, euphoria, miosis, and dose-dependent respiratory depression — the last being the usual cause of death in overdose. Overdose can be reversed by the opioid antagonist naloxone, though repeated doses may be required.

Illicit use and public health

Since the mid-2010s, illicitly manufactured fentanyl and its analogues (such as carfentanil) have been a leading driver of the opioid epidemic, particularly in North America, where they are frequently mixed into or sold as heroin, cocaine, methamphetamine, and counterfeit prescription pills. Extremely small quantities can be lethal, making dosing errors common and deadly. Synthetic opioids were involved in tens of thousands of overdose deaths annually in the United States at the peak of the crisis in the early 2020s.

Regulation

Fentanyl is a controlled substance internationally under the Single Convention on Narcotic Drugs. In the United States it is a Schedule II drug; China, Mexico, and other countries figure in supply chains for precursor chemicals, and its trafficking has become a significant diplomatic and law-enforcement issue.
