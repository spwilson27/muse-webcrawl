Neolithic

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Neolithic or New Stone Age is an archaeological period, the final division of the Stone Age in Europe, Asia, Mesopotamia and Africa. It saw the Neolithic Revolution: a wide-ranging transition in which many human cultures shifted from hunting and gathering to agriculture and permanent settlement, allowing much larger populations to be supported. The term was coined by Sir John Lubbock in 1865.

Origins

Farming began independently in several regions. In the Fertile Crescent of Southwest Asia, wheat, barley, lentils and peas were domesticated from around 9500 BCE, and sites such as Göbekli Tepe, Jericho and Çatalhöyük document the emergence of sedentary village life. Agriculture later arose independently in China (rice and millet), Mesoamerica (maize, beans, squash), the Andes, eastern North America, sub-Saharan Africa and New Guinea. Domesticated animals — sheep, goats, cattle and pigs — became central to farming economies.

Characteristics

Neolithic cultures are recognised by polished stone tools, pottery, ground stone querns, permanent houses of mudbrick or timber, and monumental architecture including megalithic tombs, stone circles such as Stonehenge, and long barrows. Sedentism brought new social organisation, storage of surplus, craft specialisation, and long-distance trade in materials like obsidian — but also crowding diseases, nutritional stress and social inequality.

Spread and end

Farming spread from Southwest Asia into Europe between about 7000 and 4000 BCE, by migration of farming populations and by adoption among hunter-gatherers. The Neolithic ends locally with the adoption of metalworking — copper in the Chalcolithic, then bronze and iron — and the rise of early states.
