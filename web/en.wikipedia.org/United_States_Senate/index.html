United States Senate

The United States Senate is the upper chamber of the United States Congress, the lower chamber being the House of Representatives. Each of the fifty states is represented by two senators, for a total of 100, serving staggered six-year terms — about one-third of the chamber is elected every two years. Senators must be at least 30 years old, a citizen for nine years, and an inhabitant of the state they represent.

Powers

The Senate has several exclusive powers under the Constitution: the "advice and consent" role, ratifying treaties by a two-thirds vote and confirming presidential nominations to the executive and judicial branches (Cabinet secretaries, ambassadors, federal judges and Supreme Court justices); and trying impeachments, acting as jury with the Chief Justice presiding when the President is tried — conviction requires two-thirds. The Vice President serves as President of the Senate and can break ties. Because the Senate's rules historically protected extended debate, most legislation effectively requires 60 votes to invoke cloture and overcome a filibuster, though exceptions have grown (the "nuclear option" removed the filibuster for nominations in 2013 and for Supreme Court nominations in 2017, and budget reconciliation bypasses it for fiscal bills).

History and organisation

Originally chosen by state legislatures, senators have been directly elected since the Seventeenth Amendment (1913). The chamber is led by the majority and minority leaders (a role pioneered by Lyndon B. Johnson) and its standing committees — Judiciary, Foreign Relations, Armed Services, Finance, Appropriations and others — conduct most of its detailed work. A quorum is 51. The Senate's smaller size, longer terms and statewide constituencies give it a reputation as the more deliberative, prestigious "upper house," sometimes called "the world's greatest deliberative body."
