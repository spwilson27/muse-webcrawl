Analytic combinatorics

Analytic combinatorics is a branch of mathematics that uses methods from complex analysis to solve problems in enumerative combinatorics — in particular, to obtain precise asymptotic estimates for the number of objects in combinatorial classes. It is based on describing combinatorial classes symbolically, translating those descriptions into equations for generating functions, and then analysing those functions' singularities to extract the growth of their coefficients. The field was systematically developed by Philippe Flajolet and Robert Sedgewick, whose book Analytic Combinatorics (2009) is the standard reference.

Symbolic method

The symbolic method assigns to each combinatorial class an ordinary or exponential generating function and translates constructions — disjoint union, Cartesian product, sequence, set, cycle, substitution — into algebraic operations on the functions. For example, if a class is the sequence construction over another class, its generating function is 1/(1 − f(z)); for labelled classes, the set construction corresponds to exponentiation, ef(z).

Singularity analysis

Because a power series' coefficients are governed by its dominant singularities, combinatorial generating functions are typically analysed by locating their singularities nearest the origin and expanding around them. Standard "transfer theorems" then turn local behaviour — e.g. a singularity of type (1 − z/ρ)−α — into coefficient asymptotics of the form C·ρ−n·nα−1. This gives, for instance, the asymptotic count of plane trees and Catalan-like structures and precise analyses of permutations, partitions, and words.

Multivariate generating functions

To study parameters of random combinatorial objects (such as the number of components of a random permutation), analytic combinatorics uses bivariate generating functions marking the parameter of interest, from which means, variances, and limit laws — frequently Gaussian — are extracted by singularity perturbation techniques.

Applications

Analytic combinatorics is central to the analysis of algorithms: average-case costs of sorting and searching, properties of random trees, graphs, mappings and permutations, and models in statistical physics and computational biology.
