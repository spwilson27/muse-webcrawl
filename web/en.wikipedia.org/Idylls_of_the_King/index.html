Idylls of the King

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Idylls of the King, published between 1859 and 1885, is a cycle of twelve narrative poems by the English poet Alfred, Lord Tennyson (1809–1892), retelling the legend of King Arthur, his knights of the Round Table, and his queen Guinevere. The poems are written in blank verse and are drawn principally from Sir Thomas Malory's Le Morte d'Arthur and the Welsh Mabinogion.

The twelve idylls

The cycle follows Arthur from his mysterious coming to his death: "The Coming of Arthur," "Gareth and Lynette," "The Marriage of Geraint," "Geraint and Enid," "Balin and Balan," "Merlin and Vivien," "Lancelot and Elaine," "The Holy Grail," "Pelleas and Ettarre," "The Last Tournament," "Guinevere," and "The Passing of Arthur." The work traces the rise and corruption of Camelot—undone by the adultery of Lancelot and Guinevere and the spread of doubt and disloyalty among the knights.

Composition and publication

Tennyson had written Arthurian verse as early as the 1830s ("Morte d'Arthur," published 1842). The first four idylls appeared in 1859 and sold some 10,000 copies in their first week; he added further poems over the following decades, reaching the final twelve-poem arrangement with "Balin and Balan" in 1885. The work is dedicated to the memory of Prince Albert, the Prince Consort, whose virtues Tennyson saw reflected in his Arthur.

Themes and reception

The Idylls are read as an allegory of Victorian Britain: the conflict of sense and soul, faith and doubt, and the fragility of civilisation. Though some later critics found them moralising, the poems were central to the 19th-century Arthurian revival and remain Tennyson's most ambitious sustained narrative.
