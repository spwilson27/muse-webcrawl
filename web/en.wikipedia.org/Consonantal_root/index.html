Consonantal root

In Semitic linguistics, a consonantal root is a sequence of consonants, usually three (a triconsonantal root), that carries the core meaning of a family of related words. Vowels and certain consonantal patterns are interwoven with the root to produce concrete words — a system often called root-and-pattern or templatic morphology.

Examples

A classic Arabic example is the root k-t-b, associated with writing: kataba "he wrote," kitāb "book," kātib "writer," maktaba "library," and maktab "office" are all derived from it. Hebrew shows the same structure, for example the root š-l-m underlying shalom "peace" and related forms. Most Semitic roots are triconsonantal, though biliteral and quadriliteral roots also exist.

Analysis

Medieval Arab and Hebrew grammarians developed root-based dictionaries in which words are listed under their roots. Modern linguistics debates how roots are represented in speakers' minds, with psycholinguistic evidence from priming experiments suggesting consonantal roots are real units of lexical organization in languages such as Hebrew and Arabic.
