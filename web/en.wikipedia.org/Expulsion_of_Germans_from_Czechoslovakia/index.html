Expulsion of Germans from Czechoslovakia

The expulsion of Germans from Czechoslovakia after the Second World War removed most of the country's ethnic German population, the Sudeten Germans, who had lived mainly in the border regions of Bohemia, Moravia and Czech Silesia. Between 1945 and 1947 roughly three million people were expelled to Germany and Austria, in one of the largest population transfers in European history.

Background

Czechoslovakia's German minority numbered over three million in a country of some 14 million. In 1938 Nazi Germany annexed the Sudetenland under the Munich Agreement, and during the war the Czech lands were subjected to a harsh occupation that included the Lidice massacre. The Czechoslovak government-in-exile under Edvard Beneš resolved that the republic must become ethnically homogeneous, and the Allied Potsdam Conference of 1945 authorised the "orderly and humane" transfer of German populations.

The expulsions

A first, spontaneous "wild" phase in the summer of 1945 involved forced marches, massacres — such as those at Ústí nad Labem and Postoloprty — and confiscation of property under the Beneš decrees, which also stripped most Germans of citizenship. From 1946, organised transfers by rail moved the remaining population into the American and Soviet occupation zones of Germany and into Austria. Estimates of deaths among the expellees vary widely, from around 15,000–30,000 documented cases to much higher figures claimed by expellee organisations.

Aftermath

The borderlands were resettled by Czechs, Slovaks and others, but many districts remained depopulated for decades. The Beneš decrees remained a source of friction in Czech–German and Czech–Austrian relations after 1989; the 1997 Czech–German Declaration expressed mutual regret without reopening property questions.
