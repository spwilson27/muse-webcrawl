Enterochromaffin-like cell

Enterochromaffin-like cells, or ECL cells, are neuroendocrine cells in the gastric mucosa. They release histamine that stimulates parietal cells to secrete hydrochloric acid, linking hormonal signals from gastrin to acid production.

Location and function

ECL cells are concentrated in the oxyntic glands of the stomach body and fundus. When gastrin binds receptors on ECL cells, they synthesize and release histamine; histamine then activates H2 receptors on nearby parietal cells.

Regulation

Somatostatin inhibits ECL activity, while acetylcholine and gastrin promote it. This arrangement allows the stomach to integrate neural, hormonal, and local signals during the cephalic and gastric phases of digestion.

Clinical significance

Chronic hypergastrinemia, such as in Zollinger–Ellison syndrome or long-standing atrophic gastritis, can cause ECL-cell hyperplasia and gastric neuroendocrine tumors. Proton-pump inhibitor therapy also raises gastrin, though the clinical risk in typical use is low. See also gastrin, parietal cell, and histamine.
