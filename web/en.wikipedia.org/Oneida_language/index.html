The Oneida language (Oneida: Onʌyotaʼa:ka, "the language of the People of the Standing Stone") is an Iroquoian language spoken by the Oneida people of Wisconsin, New York and southern Ontario. It is closely related to Mohawk and more distantly to Cayuga, Onondaga, Seneca and Tuscarora, the other languages of the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois Confederacy).

Structure

Like other Iroquoian languages, Oneida is polysynthetic: complex words built from many morphemes can express what English needs a whole sentence to say, with verbs carrying pronominal prefixes that encode agent and patient, incorporated nouns, and markers of aspect and mood. Its sound inventory is small — a handful of consonants and the oral vowels a, e, i, o, u plus the nasalised vowels ʌ (often written u in older materials and as ʌ in the current orthography) and nasal e — but it makes extensive use of glottal stops, accent and vowel length, which are written with diacritics in the modern Latin-based alphabet.

Decline and revitalisation

Removal, allotment and boarding-school policies drove steep language loss through the 20th century; by the 1990s only a few hundred fluent first-language speakers remained, mostly elders, with somewhat larger numbers in Ontario than in Wisconsin or New York. All three Oneida communities now run language programmes: immersion preschools and schools (such as the Oneida Nation School in Wisconsin and programmes at Six Nations and Oneida Settlement in Ontario), adult classes, dictionaries, recordings of elders and curriculum materials aim to produce a new generation of speakers.
