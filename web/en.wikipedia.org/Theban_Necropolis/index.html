Theban Necropolis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Theban Necropolis (Arabic: مدينة طيبة الجنائزية) is a necropolis on the west bank of the Nile, opposite Thebes (modern Luxor) in Upper Egypt. It was used for ritual burials by the ancient Egyptians for nearly two millennia, above all during the New Kingdom (c. 1550–1070 BC). Together with ancient Thebes it was designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1979.

Areas

The necropolis comprises a series of distinct burial grounds and mortuary temples spread along the edge of the Western Desert:

  • Valley of the Kings — rock-cut tombs of the pharaohs of the 18th, 19th, and 20th dynasties, including the tomb of Tutankhamun (KV62).
  • Valley of the Queens — burial place of queens and royal children, home to the richly painted tomb of Nefertari.
  • Deir el-Medina — the village of the craftsmen who built the royal tombs, with its own small tombs and temples.
  • Tombs of the Nobles — several hundred painted private tombs at Sheikh Abd el-Qurna, Qurnet Murai, and elsewhere.
  • Mortuary temples — including the terraced temple of Hatshepsut at Deir el-Bahari, the Ramesseum of Ramesses II, and Medinet Habu of Ramesses III.

Significance

The necropolis preserves an extraordinary record of ancient Egyptian art, religion, and daily life. Tomb paintings in the nobles' cemeteries depict agriculture, crafts, feasts, and funerary rituals, while the royal tombs are decorated with underworld books such as the Amduat and the Book of Gates, guiding the dead king through the afterlife.
