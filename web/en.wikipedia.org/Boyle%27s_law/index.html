Boyle's law

Boyle's law, also referred to as the Boyle–Mariotte law or Mariotte's law, is an experimental gas law that describes the relationship between pressure and volume of a confined gas. It states that the absolute pressure exerted by a given mass of an ideal gas is inversely proportional to the volume it occupies if the temperature and amount of gas remain unchanged within a closed system: p ∝ 1/V, or pV = k, where p is pressure, V is volume, and k is a constant.

History

The law was first stated by Robert Boyle in 1662, based on experiments with air performed with the assistance of Robert Hooke. The French physicist Edme Mariotte independently discovered the same relation in 1676–1679, noting its dependence on constant temperature.

Relation to kinetic theory

Kinetic theory explains the law: reducing the volume of a gas at fixed temperature increases the frequency of molecular collisions with the container walls, raising pressure in proportion — provided intermolecular forces are negligible, as assumed for an ideal gas.

Applications

Boyle's law governs practical phenomena from the working of syringes and internal combustion engines to human breathing, and is vital to scuba diving safety, where gas expands as a diver ascends and ambient pressure falls.
