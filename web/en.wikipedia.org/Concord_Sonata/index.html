Concord Sonata

Charles Ives's Piano Sonata No. 2, Concord, Mass., 1840–60 (commonly known as the Concord Sonata) is one of the composer's best-known and most highly regarded compositions, published in 1920 (with revisions in 1947). Each of its four movements is named after a figure associated with transcendentalism and with Concord, Massachusetts: Ralph Waldo Emerson, Nathaniel Hawthorne, the Alcotts, and Henry David Thoreau.

Movements

  1. Emerson — a vast, rhapsodic movement evoking the prose of the essayist.
  2. Hawthorne — a fantastic scherzo suggesting the writer's tales, including episodes of "celestial" music.
  3. The Alcotts — a gentle movement recalling the Alcott family home, quoting Beethoven and hymn tunes.
  4. Thoreau — a meditative finale on Walden Pond, ending with an optional flute part evoking Thoreau's flute.

Musical language

The sonata famously deploys polytonality, tone clusters (at one point played with a strip of wood), quotations of Beethoven's Fifth Symphony, hymns and patriotic songs, and passages of free rhythm. Ives published the sonata together with his Essays Before a Sonata, explaining the literary programme. It is now considered a landmark of American modernism and among the greatest piano works of the 20th century.
