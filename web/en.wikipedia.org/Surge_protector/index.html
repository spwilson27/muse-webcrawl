Surge protector

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A surge protector (or surge suppressor, surge diverter, surge protection device (SPD) or transient voltage surge suppressor (TVSS)) is an appliance or device intended to protect electrical devices in alternating current (AC) circuits from voltage spikes with very short durations measured in microseconds, such as those caused by lightning strikes, though the most common cause of spikes is the turning off of high-demand appliances. A surge protector attempts to limit the voltage supplied to an electric device by either blocking or shorting to ground any voltage spikes above a safe threshold.

How it works

Most point-of-use surge protectors use metal-oxide varistors (MOVs), components whose resistance drops sharply when the voltage across them exceeds a clamping level, diverting the surge current to ground or absorbing it as heat. Other technologies include gas discharge tubes, silicon avalanche diodes, and series-mode filters. Protection may be provided between line and neutral, line and ground, and neutral and ground. Over repeated surges, MOVs degrade; quality devices include indicator lights and thermal fuses to signal end of life or disconnect the worn components.

Ratings

Surge protectors are commonly rated by:

  • Joule rating — the total energy the device can absorb before failing (higher is better);
  • Clamping voltage — the voltage at which the device begins to divert current (lower is generally better); and
  • Response time — typically a few nanoseconds for MOV-based devices.

In North America, devices are covered by UL 1449, which assigns a Voltage Protection Rating (VPR); in Europe, surge protective devices are classified under IEC/EN 61643 as Type 1, 2, or 3 according to where they are installed.

Types

Whole-house surge protectors are installed at the service panel and protect all circuits in a building, often against externally generated surges. Plug-in protectors — commonly built into power strips — protect equipment connected through them and are best used as a second layer of defence. Some models also protect telephone, coaxial cable, and network lines. A surge protector is distinct from an uninterruptible power supply (UPS), which additionally provides battery backup during outages, and from a simple power strip, which may offer no protection at all.

Limitations

No consumer surge protector can guarantee protection against a direct lightning strike. Protectors are sacrificial devices that wear out, and protection is most effective when a building also has proper grounding and, for large installations, staged protection at the panel and at the point of use.
