Smear campaign

Smear campaigns seek to damage a reputation through false or distorted claims

A smear campaign (also known as a smear tactic or simply a smear) is an effort to damage or call into question someone's reputation, by propounding negative propaganda. It makes use of discrediting tactics. It can be applied to individuals or groups, and is a common tactic in politics, journalism and public life generally.

Tactics

Common smear tactics include insinuation, innuendo, guilt by association, and the circulation of rumours or outright falsehoods that are difficult to disprove. Because a reputation is hard to rebuild, the tactic can be effective even when the charges are later refuted; the strategy is sometimes summarised as "throw enough mud and some will stick". Smears often focus on personal matters — finances, relationships, health — rather than on the target's public record, and may be coordinated through surrogate voices so the sponsor's hand is hidden.

Legal position

Where a smear involves false statements of fact it may amount to defamation (libel or slander), giving the target a legal remedy, though public figures in many jurisdictions must meet a higher burden of proof, such as the "actual malice" standard in United States law established by New York Times Co. v. Sullivan (1964). Truthful but selectively presented material, and pure opinion, are generally protected.
