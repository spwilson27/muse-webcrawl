Malthusianism

Malthusianism is the theory that population growth is potentially exponential while the growth of the food supply and other resources is linear, so that unchecked population will eventually outstrip the means of subsistence and produce famine, disease and poverty. It derives from the writings of the English cleric and scholar Thomas Robert Malthus (1766–1834), principally his An Essay on the Principle of Population, first published anonymously in 1798.

The Malthusian model

Malthus argued that populations, when unchecked, increase in a geometrical ratio, whereas subsistence increases only in an arithmetical ratio. Population is held in check by two classes of restraint: preventive checks, which lower the birth rate (such as moral restraint, delayed marriage and celibacy), and positive checks, which raise the death rate (famine, war, disease and misery). The point at which population presses against food supply is sometimes called the Malthusian trap.

Influence and criticism

Malthusian ideas influenced Charles Darwin and Alfred Russel Wallace in formulating natural selection, and shaped debates over poor relief, including criticism of the English Poor Laws. Later critics — including Marxists, who located poverty in distribution and social organisation rather than overpopulation, and agricultural economists such as Ester Boserup — argued that technological innovation and induced intensification allow food production to keep pace with or outrun population. The neo-Malthusian movement of the late 19th and 20th centuries extended Malthusian reasoning to advocacy of birth control, and mid-20th-century works such as Paul Ehrlich's The Population Bomb (1968) revived Malthusian predictions of mass famine.
