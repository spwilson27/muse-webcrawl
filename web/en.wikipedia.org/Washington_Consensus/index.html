The Washington Consensus is a set of ten economic policy prescriptions considered to constitute the "standard" reform package promoted for crisis-wracked developing countries by Washington, D.C.-based institutions such as the International Monetary Fund (IMF), the World Bank and the United States Department of the Treasury. The term was coined in 1989 by the English economist John Williamson, who framed it in a background paper for a conference on Latin American adjustment.

The ten policies

Williamson's list comprised: (1) fiscal discipline; (2) redirection of public spending toward education, health and infrastructure; (3) tax reform broadening the base with moderate marginal rates; (4) market-determined, positive but moderate interest rates; (5) competitive exchange rates; (6) trade liberalisation; (7) liberalisation of inward foreign direct investment; (8) privatisation of state enterprises; (9) deregulation of barriers to entry and exit; and (10) secure property rights.

Criticism and legacy

In popular and critical usage the term quickly broadened to become a label — often pejorative — for market fundamentalism or "neoliberal" policies imposed through structural adjustment programmes. Williamson himself objected to this expanded meaning and later wrote about how the term had been "damaged" by its use as a synonym for policies he did not include. Critics, including economist Joseph Stiglitz, argued that the policies were applied one-size-fits-all without regard to local conditions and that they neglected equity and institutional development; defenders pointed to their context in the debt crisis and hyperinflation of the 1980s. Later frameworks, sometimes called the "augmented" or "post-Washington Consensus", added institutional reforms, social safety nets and governance to the original list.
