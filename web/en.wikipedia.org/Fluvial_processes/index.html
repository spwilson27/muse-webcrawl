Fluvial processes

Fluvial processes are the physical interactions between flowing water in rivers and streams and the Earth's surface. They comprise the erosion, transport, and deposition of sediment, which together create fluvial landforms such as valleys, floodplains, deltas, alluvial fans, and terraces. The study of these processes is a central part of fluvial geomorphology.

Erosion

Rivers erode their beds and banks by hydraulic action (the force of water dislodging material), abrasion (sediment grinding against the bed), attrition (particles breaking down as they collide), and solution of soluble rock. Erosion is concentrated where energy is highest — on the outside of meander bends and in steep headwater reaches.

Transport

Streams carry sediment as dissolved load, suspended load (fine silt and clay), and bed load (sand and gravel moved by rolling, sliding, and saltation). A river's competence (the largest particle it can move) and capacity (total load) rise steeply with discharge, so most geomorphic work is done during floods. The Hjulström curve describes how erosion, transport, and deposition vary with flow velocity and grain size.

Deposition

When energy falls — on the inside of bends, where a river meets a lake or the sea, or when floodwaters spread across a plain — sediment is deposited, building point bars, levees, floodplains, and deltas. Over time, the competition between erosion and deposition drives meander migration, channel avulsion, and the graded adjustment of a river's long profile toward base level.
