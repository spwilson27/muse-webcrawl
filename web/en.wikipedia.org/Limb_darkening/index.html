Limb darkening

Limb darkening is an optical effect seen in stars, including the Sun, where the centre of the stellar disc appears brighter than its edge, or limb. The effect arises because a star's outer layers decrease in temperature with height: looking at the centre of the disc, the line of sight penetrates to deeper, hotter, brighter layers, whereas at the limb the view grazes only the cooler upper atmosphere.

Explanation

In radiative-transfer terms, the observed intensity at an angle θ from the local normal samples the source function at an optical depth of roughly cos θ. Since temperature increases with depth in a stellar photosphere, the intensity declines toward the limb approximately as I(μ) = I(1)[1 − u(1 − μ)], where μ = cos θ and u is the limb-darkening coefficient. More accurate treatments use quadratic or four-parameter laws (such as the Claret coefficients) derived from model atmospheres, with stronger darkening at shorter wavelengths.

Applications

Limb darkening must be modelled precisely in the study of exoplanet transits, where the shape of the light curve depends on the brightness profile of the part of the star being occulted; it is also important in eclipsing binary analysis, stellar interferometry, and microlensing. Measurements of the Sun's limb darkening inform models of its atmosphere and irradiance.
