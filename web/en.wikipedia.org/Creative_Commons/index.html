Creative Commons (CC) is an American nonprofit organisation and international network devoted to expanding the range of creative works available for others to build upon legally and to share. Founded in 2001 by law professor Lawrence Lessig with Hal Abelson and Eric Eldred, it is best known for the free, standardised Creative Commons licences, which allow creators to grant the public permission to use their works under conditions of their choice — a middle ground between full copyright ("all rights reserved") and the public domain.

The CC logo and the icons for its four licence elements: BY, NC, ND and SA.

The licences

The licences are composed from four elements: BY (attribution required), NC (non-commercial use only), ND (no derivatives), and SA (share-alike — derivatives under the same licence). Their combinations yield six standard licences, from the most permissive, CC BY, to the most restrictive free licence, CC BY-NC-ND. CC also provides CC0, a public-domain dedication tool. The current licence suite, version 4.0 (2013), is designed to work internationally without porting. Wikipedia's text is published under CC BY-SA, and billions of photos, articles, videos, datasets, and open-access papers now carry CC licences.

Work and influence

Beyond licences, Creative Commons promotes open access in science and education (open educational resources), runs a global network of chapters, and maintains search tools for openly licensed media. It grew out of concern — reinforced by the Eldred v. Ashcroft challenge to copyright term extension, which Lessig argued and lost in 2003 — that lengthening copyright terms and digital restrictions were shrinking the creative commons that copyright was meant to enrich.
