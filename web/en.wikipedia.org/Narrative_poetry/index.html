Narrative poetry

GenrePoetry
Major formsEpic, ballad, lay, verse novel

Narrative poetry is a form of poetry that tells a story, presenting a sequence of events through characters, narration, dialogue and description. It is among the oldest literary forms, predating written literature in oral traditions, and includes the epic, the ballad and the verse novel.

Forms and history

The earliest surviving narrative poems are the great epics of the ancient world — the Epic of Gilgamesh, Homer's Iliad and Odyssey, Virgil's Aeneid, and the Sanskrit Mahabharata and Ramayana. In medieval Europe, narrative verse flourished in works such as Beowulf, the Song of Roland, Dante's Divine Comedy and Chaucer's Canterbury Tales. The ballad, a shorter sung narrative poem, carried storytelling into folk tradition, while metrical romances and lays served courtly audiences.

Later developments

Early modern and Romantic poets revived narrative verse in works like Milton's Paradise Lost, Keats's The Eve of St. Agnes and Longfellow's The Song of Hiawatha. The nineteenth century produced the verse novel (such as Pushkin's Eugene Onegin), a form revived in the modern era by writers including Vikram Seth (The Golden Gate) and Derek Walcott (Omeros). Narrative poetry is distinguished from lyric poetry, which primarily expresses a moment of feeling, and from dramatic poetry, which is performed through character voices.
