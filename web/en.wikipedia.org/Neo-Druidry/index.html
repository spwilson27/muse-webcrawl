Neo-Druidry

Neo-Druidry or Neo-Druidism is a form of modern spirituality that promotes harmony and reverence for the natural world, drawing inspiration from the ancient druids — the priestly class of the Celtic peoples described by classical authors such as Julius Caesar and Pliny the Elder. Adherents, called Neo-Druids or simply Druids, generally acknowledge that their movement is a modern creation rather than an unbroken survival from antiquity.

History

The Druid revival began in 18th-century Britain amid Romantic interest in Celtic antiquity. The Ancient Order of Druids was founded in London in 1781 as a fraternal society, while in Wales Iolo Morganwg devised druidic rituals and the Gorsedd of Bards, first performed in 1792 and later incorporated into the National Eisteddfod. In the 20th century the movement took a more explicitly spiritual turn: the Order of Bards, Ovates and Druids (OBOD), founded by Ross Nichols in 1964, and the British Druid Order developed correspondence-course teaching and open rituals, including celebrations at Stonehenge. In North America, Ár nDraíocht Féin (ADF), founded by Isaac Bonewits in 1983, built a structured, scholarly form of Indo-European pagan practice.

Beliefs and practice

Neo-Druidry is diverse, but common features include veneration of nature, reverence for ancestors and Celtic deities, and observance of an annual cycle of eight festivals (the Wheel of the Year) including the solstices, equinoxes and the fire festivals Samhain, Imbolc, Beltane and Lughnasadh. Many Druids conceive divinity as immanent in nature, variously pantheist, polytheist or animist. A central concept in some orders is awen, the flowing spirit of inspiration. Rituals commonly invoke the three elements of land, sea and sky and the three realms.

Recognition

In 2010 the Charity Commission for England and Wales accepted Druidry, in the form of the Druid Network, as a religion for charitable purposes — the first pagan tradition to gain such recognition in the United Kingdom.
