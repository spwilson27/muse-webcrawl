Schokland

LocationNoordoostpolder, Flevoland, Netherlands
FormerlyIsland in the Zuiderzee

Schokland is a former island in the Dutch Zuiderzee, now surrounded by the reclaimed land of the Noordoostpolder in the province of Flevoland. Inhabited since prehistoric times, it symbolises the centuries-long Dutch struggle against the water. In 1995 Schokland and its surroundings became the first Dutch site inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List.

History

Schokland began as a peat peninsula that erosion by the Zuiderzee gradually turned into a long, narrow island, about four kilometres long and a few hundred metres wide. Its villages — including Emmeloord, Molenbuurt and Ens — stood on artificial dwelling mounds (terps) above the reach of ordinary floods, and its people lived by fishing and seafaring. Repeated storm surges, notably those of 1825, destroyed land and buildings, and maintaining the sea defences of so small a community became unsustainable.

Evacuation and reclamation

By royal decree, the island was evacuated in 1859: its roughly 650 inhabitants were moved to the mainland, mainly to Vollenhove, Kampen and Brunnepe, and the houses were demolished, leaving only the church at Middelbuurt, a lighthouse keeper's house and a few structures. The Schokkers' descendants preserved a distinctive dialect and identity on the mainland.

When the Noordoostpolder was drained in 1942 under the Zuiderzee Works, Schokland was left stranded in the middle of new farmland, its outline still legible in the landscape. The site's archaeological remains — prehistoric settlements, medieval churches and the traces of the island's defences — are protected, and a museum at Middelbuurt tells its story.
