Magellan expedition

Dates: 1519–1522
Sponsor: Charles I of Spain
Commander: Ferdinand Magellan (to 1521), Juan Sebastián Elcano (from 1521)
Ships: 5; one (Victoria) completed the voyage
Achievement: First circumnavigation of the Earth

The Magellan expedition, also known as the Magellan–Elcano expedition, was a Spanish expedition of the 16th century that sailed from Spain in 1519, commanded by Portuguese explorer Ferdinand Magellan, in search of a western route to the Spice Islands (the Moluccas). It achieved the first circumnavigation of the Earth when one of its ships, Victoria, captained by Juan Sebastián Elcano, returned to Spain in 1522.

Background

Following Columbus's voyages, Spain and Portugal competed for control of the spice trade. The Treaty of Tordesillas (1494) divided the non-European world between them, prompting Spain to seek a westward passage to Asia. Magellan, a Portuguese navigator in Spanish service, proposed reaching the Moluccas by sailing west around South America.

The voyage

The fleet of five ships — Trinidad, San Antonio, Concepción, Victoria, and Santiago — departed Seville in August 1519 with about 270 men. After crossing the Atlantic, the expedition wintered in Patagonia, where a mutiny was suppressed and the Santiago was wrecked. In October–November 1520 the fleet threaded the strait now named for Magellan; the San Antonio deserted and returned to Spain.

The three remaining ships crossed the Pacific — a crossing far longer than expected, during which the crew suffered scurvy and starvation. They reached Guam and then the Philippines in March 1521. There Magellan planted Magellan's Cross in Cebu, but was killed at the Battle of Mactan on 27 April 1521 while intervening in a local conflict.

Completion under Elcano

After further losses, the Concepción was burned, and the Trinidad was later captured by the Portuguese. Only Victoria, under Elcano, loaded spices in the Moluccas, crossed the Indian Ocean, rounded the Cape of Good Hope, and arrived back in Spain on 6 September 1522 with just 18 survivors. The Italian scholar Antonio Pigafetta, who sailed with the expedition, left its most famous chronicle.

Legacy

The expedition proved the Earth could be circumnavigated by sea, revealed the true scale of the Pacific Ocean, and provided practical evidence of the planet's size. It also demonstrated the need for an international date line, since the returning crew found their carefully kept log was one day behind the local date.
