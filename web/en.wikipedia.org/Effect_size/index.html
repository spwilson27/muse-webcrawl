Effect size

In statistics, an effect size is a value measuring the strength of the relationship between two variables in a population, or a sample-based estimate of that quantity. Effect sizes describe the magnitude of an observed phenomenon and complement statistical significance testing: unlike a p-value, an effect size indicates how large a difference or association actually is.

Common measures

For differences between group means, the standardised mean difference Cohen's d expresses the difference in units of pooled standard deviation; conventions suggested by Jacob Cohen label 0.2 a small, 0.5 a medium and 0.8 a large effect. Related measures include Hedges' g, which corrects small-sample bias, and Glass's Δ.

For associations, effect sizes include Pearson's correlation coefficient r, the coefficient of determination r², and, for categorical data, the odds ratio, relative risk, Cramér's V and Cohen's w. In analysis of variance, η² and partial η² report the proportion of variance explained.

Interpretation

Effect sizes are indispensable in meta-analysis, where results from many studies are combined into a common metric, and in planning studies, where an expected effect size determines the sample size needed for adequate statistical power. Reporting guidelines in psychology, medicine and education increasingly require effect sizes with confidence intervals alongside significance tests, since a statistically significant result may be practically trivial and a non-significant one may conceal an important effect in an underpowered study.
