Call option

A call option (or simply a call) is a financial contract between two parties, the buyer and the seller, in which the buyer has the right — but not the obligation — to buy an agreed quantity of an underlying asset from the seller at a certain time (the expiration date) for a certain price (the strike price). The seller (or "writer") is obliged to sell the asset if the buyer decides to "exercise" the option. The buyer pays a fee (the premium) for this right.

Payoff

A call buyer profits when the underlying price rises above the strike price plus the premium paid; at expiration the payoff is max(S − K, 0) minus the premium, where S is the spot price and K the strike. The buyer's loss is limited to the premium, while the writer's potential loss, for a "naked" call, is theoretically unlimited. A call is "in the money" when the spot price exceeds the strike price.

Uses

Call options are used for speculation on rising prices, hedging, and income strategies (such as covered calls, where the writer already owns the underlying). Employee stock options are typically calls. Along with put options, calls are the basic building blocks of options strategies and of pricing models such as Black–Scholes, in which the call price depends on the underlying price, strike, time to expiry, volatility, and the risk-free interest rate.
