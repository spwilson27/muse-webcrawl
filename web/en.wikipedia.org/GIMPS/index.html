The Great Internet Mersenne Prime Search (GIMPS) is a collaborative project of volunteers who use freely available software to search for Mersenne prime numbers — primes of the form 2p − 1. Founded in 1996 by computer scientist George Woltman, GIMPS was one of the earliest and most successful large-scale distributed computing projects on the Internet, harnessing the idle processing power of tens of thousands of personal computers.

Method

Volunteers run the Prime95 (or mprime) client, which requests work assignments from a central server (PrimeNet): candidate exponents are first screened by trial factoring and P−1 factoring, then tested for primality with the Lucas–Lehmer test, and since 2020 verified with a probable-prime test plus error-checking certification that drastically reduced double-checking costs. GPU software (gpuOwl, later PRPLL) moved the frontier onto graphics cards and cloud instances.

Discoveries

GIMPS participants have discovered the largest known prime numbers for nearly three decades, including the 51st known Mersenne prime, 282,589,933 − 1 (24,862,048 digits), found by Patrick Laroche in December 2018, and the 52nd, 2136,279,841 − 1 (41,024,320 digits), announced in October 2024 by Luke Durant using cloud GPUs — the first GIMPS prime found primarily with GPU software. The Electronic Frontier Foundation's Cooperative Computing Awards (US$100,000 for the first prime of at least 10 million digits, won by the 2008 GIMPS discovery M47, and $150,000 for 100 million digits) have rewarded record finds.

Significance

Beyond records, GIMPS demonstrated the viability of volunteer computing, stress-tested hardware (Prime95's torture test famously exposed the Pentium FDIV era's sensitivity and is still used to detect overheating and overclocking instability), and popularised the Lucas–Lehmer test.
