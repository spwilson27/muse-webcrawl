Intransitive verb

In grammar, an intransitive verb is a verb that does not take a direct object. In other words, the action of an intransitive verb is not done to someone or something; the sentence makes sense without an object receiving the action. Examples include "the dog barks", "she sleeps", and "the sun rose".

Intransitive verbs contrast with transitive verbs, which require one or more objects ("she reads a book"), and ditransitive verbs, which take two objects ("he gave her a gift").

Ambitransitivity

Many verbs are ambitransitive: they can be used transitively or intransitively depending on context, as in "the window broke" versus "she broke the window", or "he eats" versus "he eats lunch". Languages differ in how they mark such alternations; in English the causative–inchoative alternation often leaves the verb form unchanged.

Classes

Linguists distinguish unergative intransitives, whose subject is agent-like ("run", "laugh"), from unaccusative intransitives, whose subject is patient-like ("arrive", "fall", "melt"). The distinction, central to the unaccusative hypothesis, is reflected in auxiliary selection in languages such as Italian and Dutch and in other syntactic behaviour.

Valency

Transitivity is a matter of valency — the number of arguments a verb takes. Some languages treat certain verbs as impersonal, with no true subject, such as weather verbs ("it rains"). In ergative–absolutive languages, the subject of an intransitive verb patterns grammatically with the object of a transitive verb.
