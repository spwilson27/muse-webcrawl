Infantry

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Infantry is a military specialisation of soldiers who fight on foot, distinguished from cavalry, artillery, and armoured forces. Although the oldest and historically largest combat arm, infantry remains the backbone of most armies: infantry units seize and hold ground, clear terrain, and conduct operations in environments — cities, mountains, jungles, and trenches — where vehicles cannot easily go.

The word derives through French infanterie from Italian infanteria, from infante, "youth" or "foot soldier".

History

From the phalanxes of Greece and the legions of Rome through medieval spearmen and early-modern pikemen and musketeers, foot soldiers dominated battlefields for millennia. The spread of firearms, and then of automatic weapons, artillery, and air power, transformed infantry tactics from dense formations to dispersed small-unit manoeuvre built around the fire team and squad. The Second World War, Korea, Vietnam, and later counterinsurgencies confirmed the infantryman's continuing centrality despite mechanisation.

Types of infantry

  • Light infantry — fights primarily on foot, emphasising mobility and stealth.
  • Mechanised/motorised infantry — transported by armoured personnel carriers or infantry fighting vehicles.
  • Airborne infantry — delivered by parachute or glider; air assault troops deploy by helicopter.
  • Marine infantry — trained for amphibious operations from the sea.
  • Mountain infantry — specialised in alpine terrain and cold-weather warfare.

Equipment

The individual infantryman typically carries an assault rifle or service rifle, bayonet, grenades, body armour, helmet, communications gear, and increasingly night-vision and thermal sights. Squad-level weapons include light machine guns, marksman rifles, mortars, and anti-tank guided missiles.
