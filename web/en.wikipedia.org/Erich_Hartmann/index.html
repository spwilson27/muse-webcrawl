Erich Alfred Hartmann (19 April 1922 – 20 September 1993) was a German fighter pilot during World War II and the most successful fighter ace in the history of aerial warfare. He flew 1,404 combat missions with Jagdgeschwader 52 (JG 52) on the Eastern Front and claimed 352 aerial victories, all but seven of them Soviet aircraft.

World War II career

Hartmann, nicknamed "Bubi" ("kid") by his comrades, learned to fly gliders as a teenager under his mother, one of Germany's first female glider pilots. Posted to JG 52 in October 1942, he adopted a close-range combat tactic — holding fire until very near the target — which made his gunnery devastating but repeatedly damaged his own aircraft with debris; he crash-landed or bailed out numerous times but was never wounded and never lost a wingman.

He received the Knight's Cross with Oak Leaves, Swords and Diamonds, one of only 27 recipients of the Diamonds. On 8 May 1945, the last day of the war in Europe, he claimed his final victory.

Captivity and later life

Handed over to the Soviet Union, Hartmann spent ten and a half years in Soviet camps, convicted by a show trial of war crimes he denied; he was released in 1955 in the repatriation negotiated by Konrad Adenauer. He then served as the first commander of the West German Air Force's first all-jet wing, JG 71 "Richthofen", before retiring in 1970. His biography The Blond Knight of Germany popularised his story in the West.
