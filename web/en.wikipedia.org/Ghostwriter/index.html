Ghostwriter

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A ghostwriter is a person hired to write literary or journalistic works, speeches, or other texts that are officially credited to another person as the author. Celebrities, executives, and political leaders hire ghostwriters to produce or edit autobiographies, magazine articles, speeches, and manifestos when they lack the time, skill, or inclination to write themselves. Ghostwriters are typically paid a flat fee, a percentage of royalties, or a salary, and usually work under confidentiality agreements that keep their role undisclosed; some receive partial credit, such as an "as told to" or "with" byline.

Areas of work

Books and memoirs

Publishing makes heavy use of ghostwriters for celebrity memoirs, political autobiographies, and business books. John F. Kennedy's Profiles in Courage was substantially drafted by his speechwriter Ted Sorensen, a fact Kennedy's camp long disputed. Donald Trump's The Art of the Deal (1987) was written by Tony Schwartz, who later spoke publicly about his role. In fiction, popular series have long been produced by teams of ghosts writing under a single house name — the Stratemeyer Syndicate's Nancy Drew and Hardy Boys books, credited to the pseudonymous "Carolyn Keene" and "Franklin W. Dixon," are the classic example.

Speeches and politics

Most senior politicians employ speechwriters, who function as acknowledged ghosts; the author of record is the speaker. Ghostwriters also write op-eds and articles placed under the names of officials, academics, and executives, a practice that shades into controversy in medicine, where industry-funded "ghost authorship" of journal articles has been condemned by editors and medical associations as a distortion of the scientific record.

Music

In popular music and especially hip-hop, ghostwriters write lyrics for performers who present them as their own. The practice is commercially routine but sometimes controversial within hip-hop culture, which prizes personal authenticity of writing.

Ethics and legality

Ghostwriting by mutual agreement is legal: the named author typically owns the copyright as a work made for hire or by assignment, and the ghost's anonymity is contractual rather than deceptive in law. Ethical judgements vary by field. It is broadly accepted for speeches and celebrity memoirs, contested for student work — essay mills that ghostwrite academic assignments are treated as academic dishonesty — and condemned in scientific publishing when undisclosed. Ghostwriters themselves describe the craft as ventriloquism: capturing the client's voice, structure of thought, and experience so completely that the finished text sounds like the client at their most articulate.
