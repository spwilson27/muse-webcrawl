Kaizen

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Kaizen (Japanese: 改善, "improvement") is a Japanese business philosophy and practice of continuous improvement involving all employees, from executives to shop-floor workers. In the West the term is closely associated with the Toyota Production System and lean manufacturing, and with the work of quality-management consultant Masaaki Imai, whose 1986 book Kaizen: The Key to Japan's Competitive Success popularised it.

Principles and practices

Kaizen seeks many small, incremental improvements rather than occasional radical change. Typical tools include the PDCA (plan–do–check–act) cycle, standardised work, the "five whys" root-cause method, gemba walks (going to the actual workplace), suggestion systems, and short focused "kaizen events" or blitzes in which a team improves one process over several days. Improvement is expected to eliminate muda (waste), reduce mura (unevenness) and muri (overburden), and to respect the people doing the work, whose knowledge of the process is treated as essential.

History and spread

The approach grew out of post-war Japanese industry, drawing on American Training Within Industry programmes and the teaching of statisticians such as W. Edwards Deming, and was refined at Toyota by figures including Taiichi Ohno. From the 1980s, kaizen ideas spread worldwide through lean manufacturing and were later adapted to healthcare, software development, government services, and personal productivity.
