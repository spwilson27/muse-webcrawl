Degree (angle)

A degree (in full, a degree of arc, arc degree, or arcdegree), usually denoted by the symbol °, is a unit of measurement of a plane angle in which one full rotation is divided into 360 degrees. Because a full rotation equals 2π radians, one degree is equivalent to π/180 radians. The degree is not an SI unit, but it is accepted for use with the SI.

History

The reason for choosing 360 as the number of degrees in a circle is not known with certainty. One theory is that the ancient Babylonians, whose sexagesimal (base-60) number system was used in astronomy, divided the ecliptic into 360 parts corresponding approximately to the days of the year. Another is that 360 is a conveniently divisible number: it has 24 divisors, making it easy to split a circle into many equal whole-number parts. The degree was further subdivided into 60 minutes of arc and each minute into 60 seconds of arc, a system inherited from Babylonian astronomy through Greek and medieval sources.

Subdivisions and notation

One degree is divided into 60 arcminutes (symbol ′) and one arcminute into 60 arcseconds (symbol ″). Angles may therefore be written in degrees-minutes-seconds notation, such as 40° 26′ 46″, or as a decimal fraction, 40.446°. In navigation and geodesy, positions are commonly given in degrees of latitude and longitude. For most mathematical work, the radian is preferred because it simplifies calculus, but degrees remain dominant in everyday, engineering, and navigational contexts.
