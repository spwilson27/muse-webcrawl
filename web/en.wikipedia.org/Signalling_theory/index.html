Signalling theory

Within evolutionary biology, signalling theory is a body of theoretical work examining communication between individuals, both within species and across species. The central question is when organisms with conflicting interests, such as in sexual selection, should be expected to provide honest signals rather than cheating. Mathematical treatments have shown that signalling is evolutionarily stable when signals are costly — the principle formalised by Amotz Zahavi as the handicap principle (1975).

Honest signalling and cost

A signal is honest when it reliably correlates with an underlying quality that cannot be faked. Costliness guarantees honesty: a peacock's heavy, conspicuous tail advertises health precisely because a weak or parasite-laden bird could not afford to grow and carry it. Signals may impose production costs, maintenance costs, or social costs (punishment of cheaters), and receivers evolve to attend only to signals whose costs enforce reliability.

Types of signals

Theory distinguishes assessment signals (indices physically tied to quality, such as a frog's croak pitch tied to body size), conventional signals (whose meaning is arbitrary but enforced by social cost), and handicap signals. Alarm calls, warning colouration (aposematism), stotting in gazelles, and human behaviours such as religious ritual and conspicuous consumption have all been analysed within the framework.

Relation to economics

Signalling theory in biology developed in parallel with signalling in economics, where Michael Spence modelled education as a costly signal of ability; the two fields now share a common game-theoretic language of senders, receivers, costs, and equilibria.
