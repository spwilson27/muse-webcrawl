Eric Arthur Blair (25 June 1903 – 21 January 1950), known by his pen name George Orwell, was an English novelist, essayist, journalist and critic. His work is characterised by lucid prose, social criticism, opposition to all forms of totalitarianism (both fascism and Stalinism) and support of democratic socialism.

Orwell in 1943, during his wartime work for the BBC.

Life

Born in India to a colonial civil servant's family, Blair was educated at Eton, then served in the Indian Imperial Police in Burma (1922–1927) — an experience that left him hostile to imperialism, recorded in Burmese Days (1934) and the essays "A Hanging" and "Shooting an Elephant". He lived in poverty in Paris and London (Down and Out in Paris and London, 1933), reported on working-class life in northern England (The Road to Wigan Pier, 1937), and fought in the Spanish Civil War with the POUM militia, being shot through the throat; Homage to Catalonia (1938) described his disillusion with Soviet communism. During the Second World War he worked for the BBC Eastern Service and wrote for Tribune.

Major works

His allegorical novella Animal Farm (1945) satirised Stalinism, and his dystopian novel Nineteen Eighty-Four (published 8 June 1949) introduced concepts — Big Brother, doublethink, Newspeak, thoughtcrime, the memory hole, Room 101 — that entered the language ("Orwellian"). His essay "Politics and the English Language" (1946) remains a classic plea for clear prose. Orwell died of tuberculosis in London in January 1950, aged 46, months after Nineteen Eighty-Four appeared.

Legacy

Orwell is routinely ranked among the greatest English writers of the 20th century; the Orwell Prize and an annual Orwell Day commemorate him. His posthumously controversial "list" of suspected crypto-communists, given to the Foreign Office's Information Research Department in 1949, complicates his reputation as a purely independent voice.
