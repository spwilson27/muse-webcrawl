Credit

Credit allows goods, services or money to be received now in exchange for a promise of future payment.

Credit (from Latin credit, "(he/she/it) believes") is the trust which allows one party to provide money or resources to another party wherein the second party does not reimburse the first party immediately (thereby generating a debt), but promises either to repay or return those resources at a later date. In other words, credit is a method of making reciprocity formal, legally enforceable, and extensible to a large group of unrelated people.

History

Credit has existed since at least 3500 BCE, with records of consumption loans in ancient Mesopotamia. Interest-bearing loans were recorded in the Code of Hammurabi. In medieval Europe, credit instruments such as bills of exchange allowed merchants to trade across long distances without transporting coin. The development of modern banking in Renaissance Italy, and later in Amsterdam and London, institutionalised credit on a large scale, culminating in central banking and the modern credit card in the twentieth century.

Types of credit

Major categories include trade credit between businesses, consumer credit (credit cards, personal loans), mortgages secured on real property, and sovereign borrowing through bond markets. Revolving credit allows repeated borrowing up to a limit, while instalment credit is repaid in fixed payments over a set term. Secured credit is backed by collateral; unsecured credit relies on the borrower's creditworthiness, assessed through credit histories and credit scores.

Credit and the economy

Credit creation by banks is a central mechanism of modern monetary systems: when banks lend, they create deposits, expanding the money supply. Access to credit enables investment, home ownership and consumption smoothing, but excessive credit growth has been implicated in financial crises, including the 2008 financial crisis, when defaults on subprime mortgages cascaded through the banking system.
