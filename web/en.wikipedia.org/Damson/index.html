Damson

The damson (Prunus domestica subsp. insititia, or Prunus insititia) is an edible drupaceous fruit, a subspecies of the plum tree. Damsons are small, ovoid plums with dark blue or indigo skin — the word "damson" is also used as a colour name for a deep blue-purple — and strongly flavoured yellow-green flesh.

Characteristics and uses

The fruit is relatively sour and astringent when eaten raw, even when fully ripe, which makes it prized mainly for cooking. Damsons are widely used for jams, jellies, compotes, and fruit cheeses, and for flavouring spirits similar to sloe gin. Historically, the skins were used as a source of dye for cloth and leather, and the trees were planted around textile mills in parts of Britain. The name derives from "damascene", referencing Damascus, from where the plum was thought to have been introduced to Europe.

Cultivation

Damson trees are hardy, compact, and tolerant of poor soils and exposed sites, and were traditionally planted in orchards and hedgerows in Britain, especially in the Lyth Valley of Cumbria and in Shropshire. Varieties include the Shropshire prune damson and the Merryweather. The fruit ripens in late summer and early autumn.
