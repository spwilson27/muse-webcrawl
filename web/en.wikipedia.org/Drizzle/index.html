Drizzle

Drizzle is a light liquid precipitation consisting of small water droplets, generally smaller than 0.5 mm (0.02 in) in diameter. Drizzle tends to be the most frequent form of precipitation over large areas of the subtropics and mid-latitudes, and its droplets appear to float as they fall, following even slight air movements.

Characteristics

Drizzle normally falls from low stratus clouds or fog and reduces visibility, sometimes to less than a kilometre. Because the droplets are so small, drizzle accumulates slowly — typically less than 1 mm per hour — but prolonged drizzle can saturate soil and vegetation. When drizzle falls through sub-freezing air it may become freezing drizzle, coating surfaces with glaze ice that is particularly hazardous to aircraft and road traffic. In weather observations, drizzle is reported with the METAR code DZ.

Formation

Drizzle forms in clouds with weak updrafts in which droplets grow mainly by condensation and coalescence but remain small. It is common in marine stratocumulus decks off the western coasts of continents, including the garúa of Peru and the haar and mizzle of coastal Britain — "mizzle" being a regional term for fine drizzle or misty rain.

Effects

Although light, drizzle keeps surfaces damp, raises humidity, and can contribute significantly to total precipitation in coastal climates. It rarely causes flooding but can make roads slippery, especially after a dry spell when oil and dust mix with the first moisture.
