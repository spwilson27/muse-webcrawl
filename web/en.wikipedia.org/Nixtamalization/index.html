Nixtamalization

OriginPre-Columbian Mesoamerica
Main ingredientMaize (corn)
AgentAlkaline solution (calcium hydroxide / slaked lime, or wood ash lye)
ProductsNixtamal, masa, tortillas, tamales, pozole

Nixtamalization is a process for the preparation of maize, or other grain, in which the grain is soaked and cooked in an alkaline solution, usually limewater (but sometimes aqueous alkali metal carbonates), washed, and then hulled. This term can also refer to the removal via an alkali process of the pericarp from other grains such as sorghum.

Process

Dried maize kernels are simmered in water containing calcium hydroxide ("cal"), then steeped for hours. The softened pericarp loosens and is rinsed away, and the resulting hominy-like grain, called nixtamal, is ground into a dough (masa) for tortillas, tamales, and other foods. Whole nixtamal is used in the stew pozole.

Nutritional effects

The alkaline treatment dramatically increases the bioavailability of niacin (vitamin B3), adds calcium, improves the balance of essential amino acids, and reduces mycotoxins. Populations that adopted maize without nixtamalization — in Europe, Africa, and the American South — suffered epidemics of pellagra, the niacin-deficiency disease, whereas Mesoamerican cultures that always processed their corn were protected.

History

The process was developed in Mesoamerica several thousand years ago and spread with maize cultivation. The word comes from the Nahuatl nextli ("ashes, lime") and tamalli ("dough"). It remains the foundation of Mexican and Central American corn cookery.
