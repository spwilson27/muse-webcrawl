Natural killer cell

Natural killer cells (NK cells) are a type of lymphocyte of the innate immune system that respond quickly to virally infected cells and tumour cells without prior sensitisation. They constitute roughly 5–20% of circulating lymphocytes in humans and are critical for early control of infections and for immune surveillance against cancer.

Function

Unlike T cells, NK cells do not rearrange antigen-receptor genes. Instead, their activity is governed by a balance of activating and inhibitory receptors. Inhibitory receptors such as killer-cell immunoglobulin-like receptors (KIRs) recognise MHC class I molecules on healthy cells and switch the NK cell off — the basis of the "missing self" hypothesis proposed by Klas Kärre. Cells that downregulate MHC class I (a common viral and tumour escape tactic) lose this protection and are killed. NK cells destroy targets by releasing perforin and granzymes and can also kill antibody-coated cells via antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) through CD16.

Receptors and subsets

Human NK cells are commonly divided into CD56dim subsets, specialised for cytotoxicity, and CD56bright subsets, which produce abundant cytokines such as interferon-gamma. Activating receptors include NKG2D, which detects stress-induced ligands, and the natural cytotoxicity receptors NKp30, NKp44, and NKp46.

Clinical significance

NK cell deficiencies predispose to severe herpesvirus infections. In therapy, NK cells are being exploited in cancer immunotherapy, including cytokine-activated preparations, monoclonal antibodies that work through ADCC, and engineered CAR-NK cells, while NK cell education ("licensing") shapes their responsiveness during development.
