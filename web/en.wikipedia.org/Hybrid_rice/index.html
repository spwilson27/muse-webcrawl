Hybrid rice

Hybrid rice is any variety of rice bred by crossing two genetically different parent lines to exploit hybrid vigor (heterosis). First-generation (F1) hybrid rice typically yields 15–20% more grain than the best conventional inbred varieties under comparable conditions, and it has played a major role in food security in China and other Asian countries.

Development

Because rice is a self-pollinating crop, producing hybrid seed commercially required a system for preventing self-fertilization. Chinese scientists led by Yuan Longping (1930–2021), often called the "Father of Hybrid Rice," achieved this in the 1970s using cytoplasmic male sterility — the "three-line" system of male-sterile, maintainer, and restorer lines. Hybrid rice was released for commercial production in China in 1976 and spread rapidly; China later developed "two-line" systems based on environment-sensitive male sterility, and Yuan's teams pursued "super hybrid rice" with progressively higher yield targets.

Production and spread

Hybrid seed must be produced afresh each season, since seed saved from an F1 crop segregates and loses vigor. At its peak, hybrid rice was planted on roughly half of China's rice area. It has also been adopted in India, Vietnam, the Philippines, Bangladesh, Indonesia, and parts of Africa and Latin America, though adoption outside China has been constrained by seed cost, grain quality preferences, and the complexity of seed production.
