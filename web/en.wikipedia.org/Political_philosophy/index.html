Political philosophy

The foundations of political philosophy were laid by Plato and Aristotle.

Political philosophy, or political theory, is the study of fundamental questions about the state, government, politics, liberty, justice, and the enforcement of law: what they are, whether and why they are needed, what makes a government legitimate, and what rights and freedoms it should protect.

History

The Western tradition begins with Plato's Republic and Aristotle's Politics, which analysed constitutions and the purpose of the polis. Medieval thinkers such as Augustine and Aquinas placed politics within a divine order. Early modern theory was transformed by Machiavelli's realism, and by the social contract theories of Hobbes (Leviathan, 1651), Locke, and Rousseau, which grounded authority in the consent of the governed. Mill defended liberty in On Liberty (1859); Marx offered a critique of capitalism and a theory of historical change.

Contemporary debates

Twentieth-century analytic political philosophy was revitalized by John Rawls's A Theory of Justice (1971), which defended "justice as fairness" behind a veil of ignorance. Robert Nozick's libertarian reply in Anarchy, State, and Utopia (1974), communitarian critiques by Michael Sandel and Charles Taylor, feminist political theory, and debates over multiculturalism, global justice, and democracy remain central themes.

Key concepts

Core notions include authority, legitimacy, rights, equality, freedom, distributive justice, and the relationship between the individual and the community.
