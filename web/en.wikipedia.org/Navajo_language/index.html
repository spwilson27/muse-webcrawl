Navajo (Diné bizaad) is a Southern Athabaskan language of the Na-Dené family, spoken in the southwestern United States by the Navajo people. With about 170,000 speakers it is the most widely spoken Native American language north of Mexico.

Features

Navajo is a tonal, strongly agglutinative language in which the verb is famously complex: a single verb word can encode subject, object, tense, aspect, and manner through templatic prefixes. It has four tones, nasalised vowels, and glottalised consonants. Word order is typically subject–object–verb, and the grammar famously distinguishes animate and inanimate categories.

History

Navajo speakers, the "code talkers", used the language as an unbroken oral code for US Marine communications in the Pacific theatre of the Second World War. A practical orthography was created in the 1930s; newspapers, dictionaries, and school programmes followed. Speaker numbers have declined among children, prompting immersion schools and media efforts, yet Navajo remains among the most viable indigenous languages of North America.
