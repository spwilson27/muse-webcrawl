Soul music is a popular music genre that originated in the African-American community in the United States in the 1950s and early 1960s. It combines elements of African-American gospel music, rhythm and blues and jazz: emotive vocals, call-and-response between singer and chorus, handclaps and improvised body moves, and a strong rhythmic groove. Soul became a major force in the civil-rights era and produced enduring stars including Ray Charles, Sam Cooke, Aretha Franklin, Otis Redding, James Brown and Marvin Gaye.

Origins

The term "soul" was used by African-American musicians to describe music expressing their experience. Ray Charles's "I Got a Woman" (1954) is often cited as a foundational record, adapting gospel song to secular lyrics. Sam Cooke carried the style to pop audiences, while the independent labels of Detroit (Motown), Memphis (Stax) and Muscle Shoals (FAME Studios) each developed distinctive regional sounds in the 1960s.

Regional styles

Motown polished soul for the pop charts with acts such as the Supremes, the Temptations and Stevie Wonder. Southern soul (Stax/Volt, Atlantic) was rawer, built on the Memphis Horns and Booker T. & the M.G.'s, with Otis Redding and Wilson Pickett. Chicago soul (Curtis Mayfield and the Impressions) featured softer harmonies and socially conscious lyrics.

Later developments

In the late 1960s James Brown's emphasis on rhythm turned soul toward funk; in the 1970s Philadelphia soul added lush strings, and artists such as Marvin Gaye and Stevie Wonder made the album a vehicle for personal and political statements. "Neo soul" revived the sound in the 1990s with D'Angelo and Erykah Badu.
