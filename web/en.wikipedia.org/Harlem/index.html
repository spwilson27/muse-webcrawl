Harlem is a neighborhood in Upper Manhattan, New York City, bounded roughly by the Hudson River, the Harlem River, and Central Park. Originally a Dutch village founded in 1658 and named after the city of Haarlem in the Netherlands, Harlem became a center of African-American culture in the twentieth century and remains one of the most historically significant Black neighborhoods in the United States.

History

After an era as a fashionable suburb, Harlem was overbuilt in the early 1900s, and Black real-estate entrepreneur Philip Payton and Black churches began moving African-American residents uptown. The Great Migration swelled the population, and in the 1920s the neighborhood gave its name to the Harlem Renaissance, a flowering of literature, music, and art associated with figures such as Langston Hughes, Zora Neale Hurston, and Duke Ellington. The Apollo Theater, opened to Black audiences in 1934, became a legendary music venue.

Later decades

Harlem struggled with poverty and disinvestment in the mid-to-late twentieth century, and from the 1990s underwent extensive redevelopment and gentrification, bringing both renewed investment and debate about displacement. Landmarks include the Abyssinian Baptist Church, the Studio Museum in Harlem, the Schomburg Center for Research in Black Culture, and the brownstone blocks of Strivers' Row.
