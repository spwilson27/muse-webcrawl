The Benin Empire, also known as the Edo Kingdom or the Benin Kingdom (Edo: Ẹdo), was a kingdom in what is now southern Nigeria. It must not be confused with the modern Republic of Benin, a separate country to the west. Renowned for its monumental city walls, highly organised court, and the extraordinary brass and ivory artworks known as the Benin Bronzes, it was one of the most developed states of pre-colonial West Africa, and one of the oldest, tracing its origins as a state to before the 11th century.

History

Origins

According to Edo oral tradition, the earliest rulers were the Ogiso ("kings of the sky"). Around the 13th–14th centuries the dynasty changed with the arrival of Oranmiyan, a prince from Ife, whose son Eweka I became the first Oba (king). Under a succession of warrior obas in the 15th and 16th centuries — notably Ewuare the Great (r. c. 1440–1473), Ozolua, and Esigie — the kingdom expanded by conquest across the rainforest between the Niger River and the Yoruba states.

Trade with Europe

The Portuguese reached Benin in 1485, and a long trading relationship followed in pepper, ivory, and later slaves; Benin, however, restricted the export of male slaves for much of its history, and its participation in the Atlantic slave trade was limited compared with its neighbours. Benin City was described by 17th-century Dutch visitors as comparable in size and orderliness to Amsterdam, with a great royal palace and broad streets. The city was ringed by the Walls of Benin, an immense system of ramparts and moats totalling thousands of kilometres — among the largest earthworks in the world.

Decline and destruction

From the 18th century, civil wars and the shifting terms of trade weakened the kingdom. Relations with Britain deteriorated in the 1890s; after a British consular party was ambushed in January 1897, a punitive expedition captured and burned Benin City, exiled Oba Ovonramwen, and looted thousands of artworks — the Benin Bronzes — which were sold into European and American collections. The monarchy was restored in 1914 under indirect rule, and the Oba remains a major traditional institution in Edo State, Nigeria. Since the late 20th century, restitution claims have gathered force; museums in Germany, the UK, and the US have returned or committed to return hundreds of bronzes to Nigeria.

Art

Benin's royal workshops produced cast brass plaques, commemorative heads, figures, and carved ivory tusks of extraordinary refinement by the lost-wax method, cast by hereditary guilds on Igun Street in Benin City. The art served as a visual archive of court history, recording obas, queen mothers (Iyoba), warriors, and Portuguese traders.
