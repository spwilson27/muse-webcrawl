Johns Hopkins University

Johns Hopkins University (often abbreviated as Johns Hopkins, Hopkins, or JHU) is a private research university in Baltimore, Maryland, United States. Founded in 1876, it is widely considered the first research university in the United States, modeled on the German research university system.

History

The university is named for its first benefactor, the American entrepreneur and philanthropist Johns Hopkins, who left $7 million — at the time the largest philanthropic gift in U.S. history — to fund a university and hospital. Under its first president, Daniel Coit Gilman, Hopkins pioneered graduate education, seminars, and laboratory research in America. The Johns Hopkins Hospital (1889) and School of Medicine (1893) revolutionized medical education, introducing residency training and admitting women on equal terms from the start, thanks to the fundraising of Mary Elizabeth Garrett and the "Women's Fund Committee".

Organization and research

The university comprises ten divisions, including the Krieger School of Arts and Sciences, the Whiting School of Engineering, the Bloomberg School of Public Health, the School of Medicine, the Peabody Institute, and the Applied Physics Laboratory, a major defense research center. Johns Hopkins has consistently ranked among the top recipients of federal research funding in the United States. Its alumni and faculty include numerous Nobel laureates, and Woodrow Wilson earned his PhD there.
