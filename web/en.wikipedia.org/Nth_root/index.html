nth root

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In mathematics, an nth root of a number x is a number r which, when raised to the power n, equals x:

rn = x,   where n is a positive integer.

The integer n is called the degree of the root. A root of degree 2 is called a square root and a root of degree 3, a cube root. Roots of higher degrees are referred to by the degree, as in "fourth root" or "twentieth root." The nth root is written using the radical symbol:

ⁿ√x

Definition and notation

Every positive real number has exactly one positive real nth root, called the principal nth root, and the operation can be written as a rational exponent: ⁿ√x = x1/n. Every non-zero number, real or complex, has exactly n distinct complex nth roots. For negative real numbers, a real root exists only when n is odd; when n is even, the roots are complex.

Examples

  • 2 is a square root of 4, since 2² = 4; −2 is also a square root of 4.
  • 2 is a cube root of 8, since 2³ = 8.
  • The principal fifth root of 32 is 2, since 2⁵ = 32.

Computing roots

Nth roots can be computed using Newton's method, which converges rapidly, or via exponential and logarithmic functions: x1/n = e(ln x)/n. In the era before electronic calculators, roots were commonly extracted by hand with digit-by-digit algorithms analogous to long division.

Complex roots and roots of unity

The nth roots of a complex number are evenly spaced around a circle in the complex plane, forming the vertices of a regular n-gon. The nth roots of 1 are called roots of unity and are of central importance in algebra and number theory, for instance in the discrete Fourier transform and in the theory of cyclotomic fields.

History

Root extraction was practised by Babylonian mathematicians, whose tablets give accurate approximations of √2. The radical symbol √ was introduced by Christoff Rudolff in 1525, and the general notation with the degree written in the "crook" of the sign was standardised by the 17th century.
