Tort

A tort is a civil wrong that causes someone else to suffer loss or harm resulting in legal liability for the person who commits the tortious act. It can include the intentional infliction of emotional distress, negligence, financial losses, injuries, invasion of privacy, and many other things. The word derives from the Old French tort, "wrong", from Latin tortum, "twisted".

The primary aims of tort law are to provide relief to injured parties for harms caused by others, to impose liability on those responsible for the harm, and to deter others from committing harmful acts. Tort law can be contrasted with criminal law, which deals with wrongs against the state, and with contract law, which arises from agreements.

Categories

Intentional torts include battery, assault, false imprisonment, trespass, and defamation. Negligence, the largest area of tort, requires a duty of care, breach of that duty, causation, and damages; the landmark English case Donoghue v Stevenson (1932) established the modern "neighbour principle". Strict liability imposes responsibility without fault for certain activities, such as keeping dangerous animals or, in many systems, defective products (product liability). Nuisance and the rule in Rylands v Fletcher address interferences with land and escapes of dangerous things.

Remedies and reform

The usual remedy is compensatory damages; punitive damages may be available for egregious conduct, and courts may grant injunctions. Periodic "tort reform" debates concern damage caps, litigation costs, and the balance between compensating victims and encouraging economic activity.
