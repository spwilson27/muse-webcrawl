Glycosaminoglycan

Glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) or mucopolysaccharides are long, linear polysaccharides consisting of repeating disaccharide units, typically an amino sugar (N-acetylgalactosamine or N-acetylglucosamine) alternating with a uronic acid. They are highly polar and attract water, and are major components of the extracellular matrix and connective tissues, where they provide lubrication, resilience, and a molecular scaffold. Most GAGs attach to core proteins to form proteoglycans.

Types

The main classes are hyaluronic acid (hyaluronan) — unusual in being unsulfated and not attached to protein, and a major component of synovial fluid and skin — chondroitin sulfate (cartilage), dermatan sulfate, keratan sulfate (cornea and cartilage), heparan sulfate (cell surfaces and basement membranes), and heparin, the highly sulfated anticoagulant stored in mast cells and used clinically to prevent blood clotting.

Function and disease

GAGs cushion joints, maintain tissue hydration, regulate the passage of molecules through the matrix, and bind growth factors and enzymes. Inherited deficiencies of the lysosomal enzymes that degrade GAGs cause the mucopolysaccharidoses (such as Hurler and Hunter syndromes), in which partially degraded GAGs accumulate in tissues with serious systemic effects.
