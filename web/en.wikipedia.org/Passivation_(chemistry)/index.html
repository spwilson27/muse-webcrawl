Passivation (chemistry)

Passivation
DisciplineCorrosion science; surface chemistry
MechanismFormation of a protective surface layer
Classic examplesAluminium, stainless steel, silicon
Term coinedMid-19th century (Faraday, Schönbein)

Passivation, in physical chemistry and engineering, refers to a material becoming "passive", that is, less affected or corroded by the environment. Passivation involves creation of an outer layer of shield material that is applied as a micro-coating, created by chemical reaction with the base material, or allowed to build by spontaneous oxidation in the air. As a technique, passivation is the use of a light coat of a protective material, such as a metal oxide, to create a shell for protection of the material against corrosion.

Mechanisms

In many cases the passivation layer is a thin, adherent film of the metal's own oxide — alumina on aluminium, chromium oxide on stainless steel, and silica on silicon. The film is only a few nanometres thick but is self-healing: if scratched in the presence of oxygen it reforms. The phenomenon of iron becoming passive in concentrated nitric acid was studied by Keir (1790) and by Faraday and Schönbein in the 1830s.

Passivation of specific materials

Aluminium naturally forms a hard, stable oxide skin that makes it resistant to weathering; anodizing deliberately thickens this layer. Stainless steel depends on chromium (at least about 10.5%) to form a chromium(III) oxide passive film; industrial passivation treatments with nitric or citric acid remove free iron from the surface and enhance the film. Silicon passivation, especially thermal oxidation to silicon dioxide, underpins planar semiconductor technology. Zinc coatings on steel (galvanizing) are often chromate-passivated to delay white rust.

Electrochemistry

In electrochemistry, a metal is said to be passive when anodic polarization produces an abrupt drop in corrosion current as the protective film forms. The potential at which this occurs is called the Flade potential. Passivity breaks down in the presence of chloride ions, leading to localized pitting corrosion, or at high potentials through transpassive dissolution.

Applications

  • Passivation of stainless steel equipment for food, pharmaceutical and semiconductor manufacture (standards such as ASTM A967)
  • Surface passivation of semiconductors, improving solar-cell efficiency
  • Passivation of lithium and other battery electrodes (the solid electrolyte interphase)
