Iphicrates

Iphicrates (Ancient Greek: Ιφικράτης; c. 418 – c. 353 BC) was an Athenian general, who flourished in the earlier half of the 4th century BC. He is credited with important infantry reforms that revolutionized ancient Greek military tactics by regularizing light-armed peltasts.

Reforms

Serving as a mercenary commander during the Corinthian War, Iphicrates reformed his peltasts' equipment: he lengthened their spears and swords, replaced heavy bronze shields with lighter wicker-and-leather peltae, and issued linen cuirasses and improved footwear — "Iphicratids" — enabling faster movement. With these troops he won his most famous victory at the Battle of Lechaeum in 391 BC, where his peltasts destroyed a mora of Spartan hoplites through repeated hit-and-run attacks, a landmark demonstration that light infantry could defeat heavy infantry in open battle.

Career

Iphicrates later campaigned in Thrace, where he married a daughter of King Cotys, served the Persian king Artaxerxes II in Egypt, and commanded Athenian forces in the Hellespont and during the Social War. In 356 BC he was tried along with his fellow generals after refusing to engage the enemy during a storm at the Battle of Embata; he was acquitted but disappears from the record soon after, dying around 353 BC.

Legacy

His reforms influenced the later development of the Macedonian phalanx under Philip II, and ancient writers such as Xenophon, Diodorus Siculus, and Cornelius Nepos preserved accounts of his campaigns.
