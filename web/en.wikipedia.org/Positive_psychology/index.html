Positive psychology

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Positive psychology studies the conditions under which individuals and communities flourish.

Positive psychology is the scientific study of what makes life most worth living, focusing on human strengths, well-being, and optimal functioning rather than exclusively on mental illness and dysfunction. The field is generally dated to 1998, when psychologist Martin Seligman chose it as the theme of his presidency of the American Psychological Association, although its roots lie in earlier humanistic psychology and in the work of figures such as Abraham Maslow, who used the term as early as 1954.

Positive psychology

Founded: 1998 (as an organised movement)

Key figures: Martin Seligman, Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi, Christopher Peterson

Core topics: Flow, character strengths, gratitude, resilience, well-being

Overview

Seligman proposed that psychology had become preoccupied with pathology — with treating mental illness — while neglecting the study of what makes people thrive. Positive psychology instead examines positive subjective experiences (happiness, joy, contentment), positive individual traits (courage, perseverance, wisdom), and positive institutions (families, schools, and communities that support flourishing). Seligman's PERMA model identifies five elements of well-being: Positive emotion, Engagement, Relationships, Meaning, and Accomplishment.

Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi's research on flow — the state of complete absorption in a challenging activity — became one of the field's foundational contributions. Other major research areas include character strengths and virtues (catalogued by Peterson and Seligman in the VIA classification), gratitude interventions, post-traumatic growth, and the psychology of hope and optimism.

Applications and criticism

Positive psychology has been applied in education ("positive education"), coaching, the military (such as the US Army's Comprehensive Soldier Fitness programme), and organisational settings. It has also attracted criticism: some scholars argue that it overstates the role of individual mindset relative to social and economic circumstances, that certain findings have replicated poorly, and that relentless positivity can shade into blaming people for their misfortune. Defenders reply that the field is a corrective supplement to, not a replacement for, clinical psychology.
