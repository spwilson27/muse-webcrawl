Conservation in New Zealand

Around a third of New Zealand's land is protected conservation land.

Conservation in New Zealand concerns the protection of the country's unique native flora, fauna and ecosystems. New Zealand separated from Gondwana roughly 80 million years ago, evolving a distinctive biota with many endemic species, including flightless birds such as the kiwi and kākāpō. Following human settlement — first by Māori around the 13th century and later by Europeans — many species declined or became extinct, including the moa.

History

Formal conservation began in the late 19th century, with scenic reserves established under the Scenery Preservation Act 1903. Tongariro National Park, gifted by Te Heuheu Tūkino IV in 1887, became one of the world's earliest national parks. The Department of Conservation (Te Papa Atawhai) was created by the Conservation Act 1987 and administers about a third of the country's land area.

Threats and responses

The greatest current threats are introduced predators — possums, rats, stoats and feral cats — which prey on native birds and invertebrates. Offshore island sanctuaries, mainland predator-free "mainland islands", and the ambitious Predator Free 2050 programme aim to eradicate key pests. Organisations such as Forest & Bird and community groups play a major role in restoration.
