God in Judaism

In Judaism, God has been conceived in a variety of ways. Traditionally, Judaism holds that Yahweh — the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob and the national god of the Israelites — delivered the Israelites from slavery in Egypt and gave them the Law of Moses at Mount Sinai, as described in the Torah. Judaism is strictly monotheistic: God is one, indivisible, incorporeal, eternal, the creator of the universe, and the ultimate source of morality, to be worshipped alone.

Names of God

The most sacred name of God in the Hebrew Bible is the Tetragrammaton, YHWH, which religious Jews do not pronounce, substituting Adonai ("my Lord") in prayer. Other names and epithets include Elohim, El Shaddai (God Almighty), HaShem ("the Name"), and descriptive titles such as "the Holy One, blessed be He". The revelation of the name in Exodus 3:14, "Ehyeh asher ehyeh" ("I am that I am"), has been the subject of extensive philosophical interpretation.

Attributes and theology

Maimonides codified Jewish belief in his Thirteen Principles of Faith, affirming God's unity, incorporeality, eternity, and omniscience, and teaching (by negative theology) that God can be described only by what God is not. Rabbinic tradition balances the attributes of justice and mercy, and conceives of God as personally involved in history and in covenant with Israel, while also transcendent. The Shema prayer — "Hear, O Israel: the LORD our God, the LORD is one" — is the central declaration of this faith. Kabbalistic Judaism developed elaborate doctrines of God's emanations (the sefirot) and the divine presence (Shekhinah), while modern movements range from traditional theism to Mordecai Kaplan's naturalistic conception of God as the power that makes for salvation.
