Alcohol withdrawal syndrome is a set of symptoms that can occur following a reduction in alcohol use after a period of excessive use. It results from the brain's adaptation to chronic alcohol exposure: alcohol enhances inhibition (via GABA receptors) and suppresses excitation (via NMDA glutamate receptors), so when it is suddenly removed the nervous system becomes hyperexcitable.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms begin typically 6 to 24 hours after the last drink and progress along a spectrum. Mild withdrawal includes anxiety, tremor ("the shakes"), sweating, nausea, insomnia, headache, palpitations and craving. More severe features include hallucinations (often visual) while otherwise oriented, generalised tonic-clonic seizures — usually within 48 hours — and delirium tremens, arising 48–96 hours after cessation, marked by confusion, agitation, fever, rapid heartbeat and high blood pressure. Historically, delirium tremens carried high mortality; with modern treatment it is a few percent.

Assessment and management

Severity is commonly scored with the CIWA-Ar scale. The mainstay of treatment is benzodiazepines (such as chlordiazepoxide, diazepam or lorazepam), given on a reducing or symptom-triggered regimen to control agitation and prevent seizures. Thiamine (vitamin B1) is given to prevent Wernicke's encephalopathy, together with fluids and correction of electrolytes such as magnesium and potassium. Severe cases with delirium tremens require intensive care. Repeated withdrawals appear to worsen subsequent episodes, a phenomenon called "kindling".
