Astronomical transit

An astronomical transit (or astronomical passage) is a phenomenon during which a celestial body passes directly between a larger body and the observer. As viewed from a particular vantage point, the transiting body appears to move across the face of the larger body, covering a portion of its disc. The word "transit" is also used for a body crossing the observer's meridian.

Types of transit

From Earth, only Mercury and Venus — the planets with orbits inside Earth's — can transit the Sun. Transits of Mercury occur about 13 times per century; transits of Venus occur in pairs separated by eight years, with more than a century between pairs (the most recent were 2004 and 2012; the next is in 2117). Moons transit their planets routinely: the Galilean moons cross Jupiter, and Titan crosses Saturn.

Transits of Venus

Edmond Halley proposed using Venus transits to measure the astronomical unit by parallax. Expeditions observed the 1761 and 1769 transits worldwide — including Captain Cook's voyage to Tahiti — yielding the first accurate scale of the Solar System. Observers noted the "black drop effect", caused by instrumental and atmospheric blurring and the Sun's limb darkening.

Exoplanet transits

The transit method is one of the most productive techniques for discovering exoplanets: a planet crossing its star dims its light periodically by a small fraction, revealing the planet's radius and orbital period. The Kepler space telescope used this method to find thousands of planets, and TESS continues the survey across the whole sky. Transit spectroscopy can also probe exoplanet atmospheres.

Phases

A transit is described by four contacts: first contact (ingress, exterior), second contact (ingress, interior), third contact (egress, interior) and fourth contact (egress, exterior), marking when the transiting disc touches the limb of the larger body.
