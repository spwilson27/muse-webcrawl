Four Great Inventions

Chinese四大發明 (Sì dà fāmíng)
The fourCompass, gunpowder, papermaking, printing

The Four Great Inventions (simplified Chinese: 四大发明; traditional Chinese: 四大發明) are inventions from ancient China that are celebrated in Chinese culture for their historical significance and as symbols of China's advanced science and technology. They are the compass, gunpowder, papermaking and printing.

The four inventions

  • Papermaking — Traditionally credited to the Han-dynasty eunuch Cai Lun (c. 105 AD), who improved a process of making paper from bark, hemp and rags; archaeological finds suggest earlier origins.
  • Printing — Woodblock printing flourished by the Tang dynasty (the Diamond Sutra of 868 is the earliest dated printed book); Bi Sheng invented movable type around 1040, centuries before Gutenberg.
  • Gunpowder — Discovered by Tang-dynasty alchemists seeking an elixir of life (9th century), it was used in fireworks and then weapons such as fire lances and bombs.
  • Compass — Lodestone compasses were used for geomancy in Han China; by the Song dynasty (by 1119) they were used for maritime navigation, transforming sea trade.

Transmission and influence

Francis Bacon, without knowing their origins, remarked that printing, gunpowder and the magnet "have changed the whole face and state of things throughout the world". The inventions spread westward via the Silk Road and the Islamic world, profoundly shaping European warfare, navigation and learning. Joseph Needham's monumental Science and Civilisation in China documented them in detail. The phrase "Four Great Inventions" is a modern construction, prominent in Chinese textbooks and national symbolism, sometimes compared in cultural role to the Four Books of Confucianism.
