Prunus domestica, the European plum, is a species of flowering plant in the family Rosaceae. It is a deciduous tree grown worldwide for its edible fruit, the plum, and is believed to have arisen as a hybrid — most likely involving the cherry plum (Prunus cerasifera) and the blackthorn or sloe (Prunus spinosa) — possibly in the region around the Caucasus and the Caspian Sea. The species is hexaploid, with six sets of chromosomes, consistent with a hybrid origin.

Description

The tree typically grows 5–12 metres tall, with white flowers appearing in early spring before or with the leaves. The fruit is a drupe, 2–7 cm long, with skin ranging from yellow-green to deep blue-black, often covered with a waxy bloom, and sweet flesh surrounding a single flattened stone. Many hundreds of cultivars have been bred, including the freestone plums used for drying into prunes.

Subspecies and groups

Cultivated forms are commonly grouped into several subspecies and cultivar groups: subsp. domestica (common plums, including zwetschge/quetsche types), subsp. insititia (damsons and bullaces), subsp. italica (gages and greengages), and subsp. syriaca (mirabelle plums). Japanese plums, by contrast, belong to the separate species Prunus salicina.

Cultivation and uses

European plums are grown in temperate regions on all continents. The fruit is eaten fresh, dried as prunes, canned, and made into jams, juices, and spirits such as the plum brandies of Central and Eastern Europe — slivovica and related drinks. The wood is fine-grained and occasionally used for turnery and inlay. Plums are susceptible to the viral disease plum pox (sharka), aphid-transmitted and a major concern of European stone-fruit growing.
