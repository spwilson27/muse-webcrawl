Pugachev's Rebellion

Pugachev's forces during the uprising of 1773–1775.

Pugachev's Rebellion (Russian: Восстание Пугачёва), also called the Peasants' War of 1773–1775, was the largest peasant uprising in the history of the Russian Empire. It was led by Yemelyan Pugachev, a Don Cossack who claimed to be the murdered emperor Peter III, husband of Catherine the Great. Between September 1773 and early 1775, a coalition of Yaik Cossacks, serfs, Old Believers, Bashkirs, Tatars, factory workers of the Urals, and other disaffected groups seized much of the Volga and Ural regions before the revolt was crushed by imperial forces.

Course of the rebellion

Pugachev appeared on the Yaik (Ural) River in 1773 proclaiming himself Peter III and promising land, freedom from serfdom, tax relief, and the old faith. His army besieged Orenburg for six months, defeated several government expeditions, and swept through the Ural mining and metallurgical district, where industrial serfs joined in large numbers. In July 1774 the rebels stormed and burned much of Kazan, their greatest success, and then moved down the Volga, proclaiming liberation manifestos to the serfs of the heartland and massacring landlords and officials. Defeated by Michelson's troops at Tsaritsyn in August 1774, Pugachev fled south and was betrayed by his own Cossack colonels, who handed him over to the authorities in September.

Suppression and consequences

Pugachev was transported to Moscow in an iron cage and executed — beheaded and quartered — on 21 January 1775, along with several associates. Catherine's government carried out reprisals, renamed the Yaik River and the Yaik Cossacks the "Ural" to erase the revolt's memory, and abolished the Zaporozhian Sich the same year. Yet the rebellion also revealed the fragility of serfdom: it hardened Catherine against emancipation, provoking instead the consolidation of noble privilege in the Charter to the Nobility (1785), though it also spurred provincial administrative reform. Pugachev became a central figure of Russian popular memory, treated sympathetically in Alexander Pushkin's history of the revolt (1834) and his novel The Captain's Daughter (1836).
