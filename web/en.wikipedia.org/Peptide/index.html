Peptide

Peptides are short chains of amino acids linked by peptide bonds. A peptide bond forms between the carboxyl group of one amino acid and the amino group of the next, with loss of a water molecule. Chains are conventionally called peptides up to a few dozen residues; longer chains are polypeptides or proteins, though the boundary is informal (often around 50 amino acids).

Classification

Short peptides are named by length: dipeptides (2 residues), tripeptides (3), oligopeptides (roughly 2–20). Ribosomal peptides are synthesised by translation of mRNA, while nonribosomal peptides are assembled by specialised enzyme complexes in bacteria and fungi and include many antibiotics.

Biological roles

Many hormones and signalling molecules are peptides, including insulin (51 residues, two chains), glucagon, oxytocin, and vasopressin. Others act as neurotransmitters (endorphins, substance P), antimicrobials (defensins), or toxins. Peptides are also important drugs — for example GLP-1 receptor agonists — and are studied in origin-of-life chemistry and materials science.
