Unix philosophy

Unix programs chained with pipes: each tool does one thing, and text streams connect them.

The Unix philosophy, originated by Ken Thompson and articulated by early Unix developers at Bell Labs, is a set of cultural norms and design principles for building software: small, simple, modular programs that do one thing well and work together through text streams.

Principles

Doug McIlroy, inventor of Unix pipes, summarized the philosophy as: "Write programs that do one thing and do it well. Write programs to work together. Write programs to handle text streams, because that is a universal interface." Other formulations include the KISS principle ("Keep it simple, stupid"), the rule of least surprise, "worse is better," and Eric S. Raymond's rules in The Art of Unix Programming (2003) — among them the rules of modularity, clarity, composition, separation, simplicity, and economy.

"Unix is simple. It just takes a genius to understand its simplicity." — attributed to Dennis Ritchie

Origins

The philosophy grew out of practical experience at Bell Labs in the 1970s, as Unix was rewritten in C and spread through universities. Brian Kernighan and Rob Pike's The Unix Programming Environment (1984) codified the style for a wider audience: build on existing programs, use pipes and shell scripts as glue, and prefer portable text formats over binary data.

Influence and criticism

The philosophy strongly influenced Linux, the free software movement, and modern practices such as microservices and command-line tooling. Critics, including the authors of The Unix-Haters Handbook (1994), argue the philosophy can excuse missing features and inconsistent interfaces.
