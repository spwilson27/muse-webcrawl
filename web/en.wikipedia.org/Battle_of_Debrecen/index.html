Battle of Debrecen

Date6–29 October 1944
LocationDebrecen and the Hungarian Great Plain, Hungary
ResultSoviet victory (Axis forces withdraw in good order)
BelligerentsSoviet Union and Romania vs. Germany and Hungary
CommandersRodion Malinovsky, Issa Pliyev vs. Johannes Friessner, Maximilian Fretter-Pico

The Battle of Debrecen, also called the Debrecen Offensive Operation (6–28 October 1944), was a major battle on the Eastern Front of the Second World War fought in eastern Hungary between the Soviet 2nd Ukrainian Front and German Army Group South. One of the largest armoured engagements on the Eastern Front in 1944, it saw the Red Army take Debrecen and advance across the Hungarian Great Plain, while the German forces conducted a fighting withdrawal that avoided encirclement.

Background

After Romania changed sides in August 1944, Marshal Rodion Malinovsky's 2nd Ukrainian Front advanced into Transylvania and toward the Hungarian plain. The Soviet plan called for a double envelopment: Pliyev's Cavalry-Mechanised Group would drive north from Arad toward Debrecen and Nyíregyháza to cut off the German 8th Army in Transylvania, while the 6th Guards Tank Army thrust toward the city from the south. Opposing them, Army Group South under Johannes Friessner, centred on Maximilian Fretter-Pico's 6th Army, assembled several panzer divisions, including the 1st, 13th and 23rd Panzer Divisions and the Feldherrnhalle panzergrenadiers.

Battle

The offensive opened on 6 October 1944. Soviet armoured spearheads broke through and raced across the puszta; Debrecen fell on 20 October after hard fighting. Around Nyíregyháza, German panzer counterattacks temporarily cut off Pliyev's group, which had to fight its way back south — one of the few successful German counterstrokes of this period. Both sides committed hundreds of tanks: German claims and Soviet records alike reflect armoured losses in the many hundreds, with tank battles raging around Hajdúhadház, Nyíregyháza and Debrecen itself.

Aftermath

By the end of October, the Soviets held a broad bridgehead west of the Tisza river and had advanced up to 150 kilometres, positioning themselves for the offensive against Budapest. Although the Germans escaped destruction and inflicted heavy losses, Hungary's fate was sealed: Horthy's attempt to leave the Axis war had failed on 15 October, and the Arrow Cross regime kept Hungary in the war as the Red Army closed on the capital.
