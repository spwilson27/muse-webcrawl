Anamorphic format

The anamorphic format is a cinematography technique in which a widescreen picture is recorded on a standard film or sensor area by horizontally compressing ("squeezing") the image with a special anamorphic lens. On projection, a complementary lens (or digital correction) unsqueezes the image to its intended wide aspect ratio, most famously 2.39:1.

History

The technology derives from Henri Chrétien's Hypergonar lens of the 1920s and was commercialised by 20th Century Fox as CinemaScope in 1953, beginning with The Robe. Anamorphic capture let studios present spectacular widescreen images while using ordinary 35 mm film stock and existing cameras and projectors with only a lens change. Panavision became the dominant supplier of anamorphic lenses from the late 1950s onward.

Characteristics

Classic anamorphic photography has a distinctive look: oval bokeh, horizontal lens flares, shallower apparent depth of field and subtle geometric distortion. A 2× squeeze was standard for 35 mm; digital cinema uses a variety of squeeze ratios, and the format remains popular with filmmakers seeking its characteristic aesthetic even when final delivery is digital.
