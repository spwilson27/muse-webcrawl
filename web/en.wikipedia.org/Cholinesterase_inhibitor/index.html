Cholinesterase inhibitor

Cholinesterase inhibitors are chemical compounds that prevent the breakdown of the neurotransmitter acetylcholine by inhibiting the enzymes acetylcholinesterase and butyrylcholinesterase. The resulting increase in acetylcholine levels in synapses has applications in medicine, agriculture, and, notoriously, chemical warfare.

Medical uses

Reversible cholinesterase inhibitors such as donepezil, rivastigmine, and galantamine are used to treat the cognitive symptoms of Alzheimer's disease, in which cholinergic neurons are lost. Related drugs are used in myasthenia gravis (neostigmine, pyridostigmine), glaucoma, and to reverse neuromuscular blockade after surgery.

Pesticides and nerve agents

Many organophosphate and carbamate insecticides act by irreversibly or semi-permanently inhibiting acetylcholinesterase. The same mechanism underlies the toxicity of nerve agents such as sarin and VX, which cause a cholinergic crisis: excessive secretions, bronchoconstriction, convulsions, and respiratory failure. Antidotes include atropine and oximes such as pralidoxime.

Mechanism

Acetylcholinesterase normally hydrolyses acetylcholine within milliseconds of its release. Inhibitors bind at or near the enzyme's active site, slowing this hydrolysis so that acetylcholine accumulates and overstimulates muscarinic and nicotinic receptors.
