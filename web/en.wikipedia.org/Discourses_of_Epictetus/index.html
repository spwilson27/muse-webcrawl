Discourses of Epictetus

The Discourses of Epictetus (Greek: Ἐπικτήτου Διατριβαί, Epiktētou Diatribai) are a series of informal lectures by the Stoic philosopher Epictetus written down by his pupil Arrian around 108 AD. They are among the most influential works of Stoic philosophy that survive, and they form the principal source for Epictetus's teaching.

Background

Epictetus was a Greek-speaking Stoic philosopher who was born a slave in Hierapolis, Phrygia, and later freed after moving to Rome. He founded a school of philosophy at Nicopolis in north-western Greece, where students came to hear him lecture. One of these students, the historian and philosopher Arrian, took shorthand notes of the lectures and later published them as the Discourses.

Epictetus himself wrote nothing. His teaching was delivered orally, in a lively, conversational style that mixed exposition with imaginary dialogues with hecklers, students and historical figures.

Contents

Of the original eight books of Discourses, four survive complete or nearly so, and fragments of others exist. Together with the Enchiridion (the "Handbook"), a condensed summary of the same doctrines, the Discourses present the core of Epictetus's practical ethics.

The recurring themes include:

  • The dichotomy of control: some things are up to us, and some things are not up to us.
  • The proper use of impressions, and the discipline of assent.
  • Freedom understood as wanting things to happen as they do happen.
  • The role of the philosopher as physician of the soul.
  • Living in accordance with nature and one's assigned roles in life.

Style

The Discourses are written in the koine Greek of everyday speech rather than in a literary Attic style. Arrian defended this plainness in a prefatory letter, insisting that he had recorded the words "just as they were spoken", preserving Epictetus's characteristic directness, repetition and rhetorical questioning.

Influence

The Discourses, together with the Enchiridion, were read throughout late antiquity and were widely translated during the Renaissance. They influenced the emperor Marcus Aurelius, whose Meditations echo many of Epictetus's themes, and they remain a standard entry point into Stoic practice today.
