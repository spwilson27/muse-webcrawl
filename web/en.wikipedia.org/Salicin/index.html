Salicin

Salicin is a glycoside found in willow bark.

Salicin is an alcoholic β-glucoside containing D-glucose, found naturally in the bark of willow trees (Salix species) and in smaller amounts in other plants. It is chemically related to aspirin (acetylsalicylic acid) and has a long history of use as an analgesic and antipyretic in traditional medicine.

History

Willow bark preparations were used for pain and fever in ancient Egypt, Sumer and Greece; Hippocrates described a bitter powder from willow bark easing pain. Salicin was first isolated in crystalline form in 1828 by the German pharmacist Johann Andreas Buchner, and refined by Henri Leroux. In the body, salicin is metabolised to salicylic acid, which produces its anti-inflammatory effects.

Pharmacology

After ingestion, salicin is converted in the gut and liver to saligenin and then to salicylic acid, which inhibits prostaglandin synthesis. Its effects are generally considered milder than those of aspirin, and it lacks aspirin's acetyl group, so it does not irreversibly inhibit platelet cyclo-oxygenase in the same way.

Uses and safety

Willow bark extracts standardised for salicin content are sold as herbal supplements for back pain and osteoarthritis. Like other salicylates, salicin should be avoided by children with viral infections because of the risk of Reye's syndrome, and by people with salicylate sensitivity.
