Elizabethan drama

Elizabethan drama refers to the theatre of England during the reign of Queen Elizabeth I (1558–1603) and, by extension, the flourishing of English drama into the Jacobean era — one of the most celebrated periods in theatre history.

Development

Permanent public playhouses were built in London from 1576 (The Theatre), followed by the Curtain, the Rose, the Swan and the Globe (1599). Acting companies under noble patronage performed to mixed audiences of all classes. Playwrights wrote rapidly for the commercial stage, often in collaboration.

Playwrights and plays

The era's leading dramatist was William Shakespeare, whose company, the Lord Chamberlain's Men, became the King's Men in 1603. Contemporaries included Christopher Marlowe, author of Doctor Faustus and Tamburlaine; Thomas Kyd, whose The Spanish Tragedy popularised revenge tragedy; and later Ben Jonson, John Webster and Thomas Middleton.

Characteristics

Plays mixed verse and prose, tragedy and comedy, and drew on classical, historical and popular sources. Performances used minimal scenery, daylight, and boy actors for female roles. The theatres were closed by Parliament in 1642.
