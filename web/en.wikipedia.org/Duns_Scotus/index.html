John Duns Scotus (c. 1266 – 1308), commonly called Duns Scotus, was a Scottish Catholic priest and Franciscan friar, philosopher and theologian, and one of the most influential thinkers of the High Middle Ages. His brilliance earned him the title Doctor Subtilis, the "Subtle Doctor".

Life

Born at Duns in Berwickshire, Scotus joined the Franciscan order and studied and taught at Oxford and Paris. He was expelled from Paris in 1303 for siding with Pope Boniface VIII against King Philip IV, but returned to complete his doctorate, later moving to Cologne, where he died in 1308. His major work, the Ordinatio, is an expanded commentary on the Sentences of Peter Lombard.

Philosophy

Scotus argued for the univocity of being: the concept of "being" applies in the same sense to God and creatures, against the analogy theory of Thomas Aquinas. He developed the idea of haecceity (haecceitas, "thisness") — the property that makes an individual the unique individual it is — and the formal distinction, a distinction intermediate between real and merely conceptual. In theology he gave a famous defence of the Immaculate Conception of Mary, later dogma in the Catholic Church, and emphasised the primacy of the will and of love in God's action. His thought founded the school known as Scotism.

Legacy

Scotus was beatified by Pope John Paul II in 1993. Ironically, his name became the origin of the word "dunce": Renaissance humanists and later Reformers attacked his followers, the "Dunsmen", as pedantic obstacles to learning, and the name passed into English as a term for a dullard.
