The baseball color line was the unwritten rule that excluded players of African descent from Major League Baseball and its affiliated minor leagues in the United States from the late 19th century until 1947. It mirrored the broader system of racial segregation in American life.

Origins

Black players such as Moses Fleetwood Walker appeared in the major leagues in the 1880s, but by the 1890s team owners and league officials, under pressure from players like Cap Anson and the climate of Jim Crow, adopted a "gentlemen's agreement" barring Black players. In response, Black players, managers, and entrepreneurs built their own professional structure — the Negro leagues, including the Negro National League founded by Rube Foster in 1920 — which produced stars such as Satchel Paige, Josh Gibson, and Cool Papa Bell.

Breaking the line

After World War II, Brooklyn Dodgers executive Branch Rickey signed Jackie Robinson, who played for the Montreal Royals in 1946 and debuted for Brooklyn on 15 April 1947, becoming the first Black player in the modern major leagues. Robinson won Rookie of the Year honors and withstood intense racism with public restraint, opening the door for Larry Doby, who integrated the American League weeks later, and many others. Integration proceeded unevenly; the Boston Red Sox were the last team to field a Black player, in 1959.

Legacy

The color line's fall is a landmark in American civil rights history, though its decades of exclusion distorted records and delayed recognition of Negro league players, many of whom were elected to the Hall of Fame only from the 1970s onward.
