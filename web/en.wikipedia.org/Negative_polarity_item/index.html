Negative polarity item

A negative polarity item (NPI) is a lexical item that is licensed only in negative (or otherwise "affective") contexts. In English, words and phrases such as any, ever, yet, at all, budge an inch, and lift a finger are NPIs: compare the grammatical "I didn't see anyone" with the ungrammatical "*I saw anyone" (on the relevant reading). NPIs are the subject of extensive study in linguistics because their distribution illuminates the semantics of negation and the architecture of grammar.

Licensing environments

NPIs are licensed not only by overt negation (not, never, no one) but in a family of related environments: questions ("Did you see anything?"), conditional antecedents ("If you see anything, tell me"), comparatives ("better than ever"), with words like only, few, hardly, and in the scope of downward-entailing operators generally. Gilles Fauconnier and, in influential work, William Ladusaw proposed that NPIs are licensed in downward-entailing contexts — contexts that support inferences from sets to subsets. Some NPIs are "strong," requiring true negation, while "weak" NPIs accept the broader class of nonveridical or affective environments.

Cross-linguistic aspects and theory

Many languages have NPI systems with similar patterns (e.g., Dutch ook maar, Greek kanenas, Japanese indeterminate pronouns with -mo). Competing analyses treat NPIs as indefinites with special scope properties, as scalar endpoints (in the tradition of Edward Klima and Paul Postal), or as items triggering alternatives, following Danny Fox and others. The counterpart phenomenon is the positive polarity item, such as some or already, which is excluded from some negative contexts.
