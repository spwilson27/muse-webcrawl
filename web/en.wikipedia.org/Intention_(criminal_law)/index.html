Intention (criminal law)

In criminal law, intent is a subjective state of mind that must accompany the acts of certain crimes to constitute a violation. Along with the actus reus (the guilty act), intent is a principal form of mens rea (the guilty mind). A more formal, generally synonymous legal term is scienter: intent or knowledge of wrongdoing.

Direct and oblique intent

Direct intent exists where the defendant acts with the purpose of bringing about a result. Oblique intent arises where the result is not the defendant's aim but is a virtually certain consequence of their actions, and the defendant appreciates that — a formulation developed in English cases such as R v Woollin. Crimes of "specific" or "ulterior" intent require a purpose beyond the act itself, in contrast to crimes of basic intent.

Approaches

The American Model Penal Code replaces a patchwork of intent terms with four graded mental states: purposely, knowingly, recklessly, and negligently. Intent may also be "transferred" in many jurisdictions when a defendant harms an unintended victim while aiming at another.
