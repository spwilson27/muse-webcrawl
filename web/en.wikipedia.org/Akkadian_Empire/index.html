The Akkadian Empire was the first ancient empire of Mesopotamia, reaching its political peak between the 24th and 22nd centuries BC, following the conquests of its founder, Sargon of Akkad. It is generally considered the first true empire in history, uniting the Akkadian-speaking and Sumerian-speaking city-states of Mesopotamia under one rule.

History

Before Sargon, Mesopotamia consisted of competing city-states such as Kish, Uruk, Ur and Lagash. Around 2334 BC, Sargon, a former court official at Kish, defeated Lugal-zage-si of Uruk and rapidly conquered the whole of southern Mesopotamia. His successors — Rimush, Manishtushu, Naram-Sin and Shar-Kali-Sharri — extended Akkadian control from the Persian Gulf to the Mediterranean Sea and into Anatolia and Elam.

Under Naram-Sin, the fourth king, the empire reached its greatest extent and the king was deified during his lifetime, styling himself "King of the Four Quarters" of the world. The empire maintained trade as far as the Indus Valley, Oman and Dilmun.

Decline and fall

After Shar-Kali-Sharri, the empire weakened under pressure from the Gutians, a people from the Zagros Mountains, combined with internal revolts and possibly a severe drought associated with the 4.2-kiloyear event. By about 2154 BC the empire had collapsed, and Mesopotamia reverted to independent city-states before the rise of the Neo-Sumerian Empire under Ur-Namma.

Administration and culture

The Akkadians introduced a centralised administration with appointed governors, a standardised system of weights and measures, and an imperial road network with a postal service. Akkadian became the lingua franca of the region and was written in cuneiform borrowed from the Sumerians. Sargon's daughter Enheduanna, high priestess at Ur, is considered the earliest named author in history.

Legacy

The Akkadian Empire set the template for later Mesopotamian states, and Sargon and Naram-Sin became legendary figures in later Mesopotamian literature. The empire's unification of north and south shaped the political geography of the region for millennia.
