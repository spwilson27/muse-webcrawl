Hydrogen storage is the set of methods for storing hydrogen for later use as an energy carrier, in applications ranging from fuel-cell vehicles to grid-scale energy storage. Hydrogen has the highest energy per unit mass of any fuel, but because it is the lightest element it has a very low volumetric energy density at ambient conditions, making compact and safe storage a central technical challenge of the hydrogen economy.

Physical storage

Compressed hydrogen is stored in high-pressure tanks, typically at 350 or 700 bar in fuel-cell vehicles, using carbon-fibre-reinforced composite vessels. Liquid hydrogen requires cryogenic cooling to about 20 K and suffers from boil-off losses, but achieves a much higher density; it is used in spaceflight and some large-scale transport. Underground storage in salt caverns is used for bulk quantities.

Material-based storage

Metal hydrides store hydrogen by reversible absorption into metals and alloys such as LaNi5 and magnesium hydride; they offer high volumetric density but often low gravimetric density and slow kinetics. Chemical carriers include ammonia, methanol, and liquid organic hydrogen carriers (LOHCs). Hydrogen can also be physisorbed in porous materials with high surface areas, such as metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) and activated carbons, usually at cryogenic temperatures.

Targets and applications

The United States Department of Energy has set system-level targets for onboard vehicular storage covering gravimetric and volumetric capacity, cost, refuelling time, and cycle life. No single method yet meets all targets simultaneously, and most fuel-cell vehicles in production use compressed gas storage.
