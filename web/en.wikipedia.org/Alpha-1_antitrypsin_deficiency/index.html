Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency

Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency (A1AD or AATD) is a genetic disorder that may result in lung disease or liver disease. It is caused by mutations in the SERPINA1 gene, which encodes alpha-1 antitrypsin (A1AT), a protease inhibitor produced mainly in the liver that protects tissues — especially the lungs — from the enzyme neutrophil elastase.

Signs and symptoms

In the lungs, a lack of functional A1AT allows elastase to destroy alveolar walls unchecked, causing early-onset panacinar emphysema and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, often beginning between the ages of 25 and 50 and accelerated greatly by cigarette smoking. In the liver, certain mutant forms of the protein (notably the Z variant) misfold and accumulate within hepatocytes, causing neonatal hepatitis, cirrhosis, and an increased risk of liver cancer. The most severe genotype, PiZZ, affects roughly 1 in 2,000–5,000 people of northern European ancestry.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is by blood testing of A1AT levels followed by genetic testing or phenotyping. There is no cure for the underlying defect; management includes avoidance of smoking and other lung irritants, standard COPD therapy, and, for lung disease, augmentation therapy with intravenous A1AT purified from human plasma. Severe liver or lung disease may require transplantation. The condition was first described in 1963 by Carl-Bertil Laurell and Sten Eriksson in Sweden.
