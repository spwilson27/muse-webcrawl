Serfdom

Serfdom was the status of many peasants under feudalism, specifically relating to manorialism, and similar systems. It was a condition of debt bondage and indentured servitude with similarities to and differences from slavery. Serfs were bound to the land, owed labour and dues to their lord, and could not leave the manor without permission; unlike slaves, they held certain customary rights and could not normally be sold apart from the land.

History

Serfdom developed in late antiquity and the early Middle Ages as free peasants and Roman coloni became tied to estates. In Western Europe it declined from the late Middle Ages onward, hastened by the Black Death, peasant revolts, and the growth of money rents, and had largely disappeared by about 1500. In Central and Eastern Europe, by contrast, a "second serfdom" intensified from the 16th century as estates produced grain for export. Serfdom in Russia was codified in the 17th century and applied to roughly half the peasantry before its abolition by Alexander II's Emancipation Reform of 1861, one of the last abolitions in Europe after France (1789), the Habsburg lands (1781/1848), and Prussia (1807).

Characteristics

Serfs typically owed labour services (corvée) on the lord's demesne, rents in kind or money, and fees for marriage, inheritance, and the use of the mill or oven. They were subject to the lord's court and could not marry, migrate, or take up other work without consent, though custom limited the lord's powers and serfs could hold and bequeath property among themselves.
