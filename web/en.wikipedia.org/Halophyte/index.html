Halophyte

A halophyte is a salt-tolerant plant that grows in waters of high salinity, coming into contact with salty water through its roots or by salt spray, such as in saline semi-deserts, mangrove swamps, marshes and seashores. The word derives from the Greek hals (salt) and phyton (plant). Halophytes are a small minority of plant species — perhaps two percent — but are of great interest because of their adaptations to conditions that kill most plants.

Adaptations

Halophytes cope with salt in several ways: excluding it at the roots, excreting it through specialized salt glands or bladders on the leaves (as in many mangroves and Atriplex species), or tolerating it in their tissues by sequestering ions in vacuoles and balancing them with compatible solutes in the cytoplasm. Many halophytes are succulents, diluting accumulated salt with stored water.

Plants can also be classified along this dimension as obligate halophytes, which require salt, and facultative halophytes, which merely tolerate it.

Uses

Genera such as Salicornia (glasswort, samphire) are gathered or cultivated as vegetables and for oilseed, and halophytes are increasingly studied for agriculture on salinized land and with seawater irrigation, as freshwater scarcity and soil salinization grow.
