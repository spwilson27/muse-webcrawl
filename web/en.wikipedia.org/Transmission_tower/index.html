Transmission tower

Electricity pylons carrying three-phase high-voltage lines across open country.

Other namesElectricity pylon, hydro tower
MaterialGalvanised steel lattice, tubular steel, concrete
Typical height15–55 m (up to 380 m for river crossings)
PurposeSupport overhead power lines

A transmission tower (also known as an electricity pylon in British English and a hydro tower in parts of Canada) is a tall structure, usually a lattice tower made of steel, used to support an overhead power line. In electrical grids, transmission towers carry high-voltage transmission lines that transport bulk electric power from generating stations to electrical substations, from which electricity is delivered to end consumers. Utility poles are used for lower-voltage sub-transmission and distribution lines.

Design

Most transmission towers are lattice structures assembled from angle-steel members, giving a high strength-to-weight ratio and good resistance to wind loads. The tower must support the weight of the conductors, ice and wind loading, and maintain safe clearance between the energised conductors, the tower itself, and the ground. Conductor bundles are suspended from the tower arms by insulator strings made of glass, porcelain, or polymer discs.

Common configurations include the barrel tower, the delta tower, and guyed towers held upright by tensioned cables. Towers are classified by function: suspension towers carry lines in a straight run, while angle and dead-end (anchor) towers withstand the unbalanced tension where lines change direction or terminate.

Voltages and circuits

Towers may carry a single circuit or, more commonly, two circuits (one on each side) of three-phase alternating current, with an earth wire strung along the top for lightning protection. Transmission voltages commonly range from 69 kV to 765 kV for AC lines; high-voltage direct current (HVDC) lines use bipolar conductor pairs. The tallest crossing towers in the world, on the Yangtze River in China, exceed 340 metres.

History

The lattice tower form emerged in the early 20th century as transmission distances and voltages grew. The classic British pylon design, the L6 standard tower, was selected in 1928 following a competition overseen by architect Reginald Blomfield for the new national grid.
