Serialism

Serialism is a method of musical composition that uses a series of values to manipulate different musical elements. It began with Arnold Schoenberg's twelve-tone technique, in which a row (series) of the twelve pitch classes, arranged in a fixed order, provides the basic material of a work and is used in its original, inverted, retrograde, and retrograde-inversion forms.

Twelve-tone technique

Schoenberg developed the method in the early 1920s, describing it as a way to ensure the equality of all twelve notes and avoid the hierarchies of tonal music. His pupils Alban Berg and Anton Webern adopted and extended the technique; Webern's highly concentrated works became especially influential after the Second World War.

Total serialism

After 1945, a younger generation — including Pierre Boulez, Karlheinz Stockhausen, Luigi Nono, and Milton Babbitt — extended serial ordering beyond pitch to rhythm, dynamics, articulation, and timbre, producing what is called total or integral serialism. Olivier Messiaen's Mode de valeurs et d'intensités (1949) was a key precursor. Serialism dominated avant-garde composition in the 1950s and was institutionalised at venues such as the Darmstadt Summer Courses.

Criticism and legacy

Critics have found serial music overly cerebral or perceptually opaque, and many composers later relaxed or abandoned strict serial procedures. Nevertheless, serial thinking profoundly influenced 20th-century music, from concert works to film scores, and remains a standard topic in music theory.
