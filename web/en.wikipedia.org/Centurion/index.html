Centurion

In the Roman army during classical antiquity, a centurion (Latin: centurio; Greek: κεντυρίων, kentyríōn, or ἑκατόνταρχος, hekatóntarkhos) was a commander, nominally of a century (Latin: centuria), originally a military unit of 100 legionaries, but in practice about 80 men including officers and specialists. Centurions formed the backbone of the professional Roman army, combining the roles of company commander, drill instructor and disciplinarian, and they were promoted from the ranks largely on merit and experience.

Role and duties

The centurion led his century in battle, stationed at the front-right of the formation, and was responsible for training, discipline, and administration on campaign and in camp. He carried a vine staff (vitis) as a badge of office, with which he could beat soldiers — a symbol so distinctive that centurions were nicknamed vitis bearers; the mutinous soldiers in AD 14 mocked the brutal centurion Lucilius as "Cedo alteram" ("Give me another"), because he kept breaking his staff on their backs. Centurions also performed duties such as supervising work parties, guard duty, and acting as the legion's liaison on detached service, and could command auxiliary units or vexillations.

Hierarchy and promotion

A legion's cohorts each had six centuries (five in the first cohort, whose five double-strength centuries were led by the primi ordines). The senior centurion of the legion was the primus pilus ("first spear"), commander of the first century of the first cohort, who typically served a one-year term and could rise to equestrian status and posts such as camp prefect (praefectus castrorum). Promotion through the cohorts followed an intricate seniority system debated by scholars. Most centurions were promoted from the rank and file after 15–20 years of service, though some entered directly by commission, including men of equestrian background seeking a military career.

Pay and status

Centurions were paid far more than ordinary legionaries — perhaps 15 to 60 times a common soldier's wage depending on grade — and received a large discharge bonus. Their social standing was considerable, and many retired to become leading citizens in provincial towns. Epigraphic evidence (career inscriptions) shows wide geographical mobility: centurions transferred between legions across the empire, contributing to Romanisation.

Equipment

The centurion was distinguished by a transverse (side-to-side) crest on his helmet, greaves on both legs, a cuirass often of scale or mail, and his sword (gladius) worn on the left side, opposite to the common soldier's right, so it would not clash with the shield in the front rank. Decorations (dona militaria) such as torcs, armillae and phalerae were displayed on a leather harness in parade.
