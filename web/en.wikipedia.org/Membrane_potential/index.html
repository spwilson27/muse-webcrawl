Membrane potential

Membrane potential (also transmembrane potential or membrane voltage) is the difference in electric potential between the interior and the exterior of a biological cell. It arises from differences in the concentrations of ions on opposite sides of the cell membrane and from the membrane's selective permeability to those ions. Typical values for animal cells range from about −40 to −80 millivolts, with the interior negative relative to the exterior.

Origins

The membrane potential results from the combined action of ion pumps and ion channels. The Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pump expels three sodium ions for every two potassium ions imported, consuming ATP. Potassium leak channels allow K⁺ to diffuse out down its concentration gradient until the resulting electrical gradient balances it; the potential at which a single ion species is in equilibrium is given by the Nernst equation. The Goldman–Hodgkin–Katz equation generalises this to membranes permeable to several ions.

Resting potential

The resting membrane potential of a typical neuron is about −70 mV. It is dominated by potassium permeability, since the resting membrane is far more permeable to K⁺ than to Na⁺. Virtually all cells maintain a resting potential, which is used for transport of nutrients, signalling and energy transduction in mitochondria, bacteria and plant cells.

Action potentials

In excitable cells such as neurons and muscle fibres, rapid, transient changes in membrane potential — action potentials — carry signals. Depolarising stimuli open voltage-gated sodium channels, driving the potential toward the sodium equilibrium potential; delayed opening of potassium channels and inactivation of sodium channels restore the resting value.

Measurement

Membrane potential can be measured with intracellular microelectrodes, the patch-clamp technique, or voltage-sensitive fluorescent dyes. The first direct measurements in the giant axon of the squid in 1939 underpinned the Hodgkin–Huxley model of 1952.
