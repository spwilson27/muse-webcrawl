The French Revolution of 1830, also known as the July Revolution (French: Révolution de Juillet) or Les Trois Glorieuses ("The Three Glorious [Days]"), was a second French revolution after the Bourbon Restoration following the fall of Napoleon. It saw the overthrow of King Charles X, the French Bourbon monarch, and the ascent of his cousin Louis Philippe, Duke of Orléans, who himself, after 18 precarious years on the throne, would be overthrown in 1848.

Causes

On 25 July 1830 Charles X signed the Ordinances of Saint-Cloud, which suspended the liberty of the press, dissolved the newly elected Chamber of Deputies, and altered the electoral system to favour ultra-royalists. Journalists in Paris, led by Adolphe Thiers of Le National, issued a protest and called for resistance.

The Three Glorious Days

On 27 July crowds in Paris raised barricades; fighting spread over the following two days as students, workers and members of the disbanded National Guard battled royal troops under Marshal Marmont. The tricolour was raised over the Hôtel de Ville and Notre-Dame. By 29 July the royal troops had withdrawn, and Charles X abdicated on 2 August, fleeing into exile in Britain and later Austria.

Outcome

The Marquis de Lafayette presented Louis Philippe to the crowd as a compromise between monarchy and republic. A revised Charter stripped the king of legislative initiative, abolished censorship and lowered the voting age and property qualification. The event is commemorated in Eugène Delacroix's painting Liberty Leading the People and inspired parallel uprisings in Belgium and Poland.
