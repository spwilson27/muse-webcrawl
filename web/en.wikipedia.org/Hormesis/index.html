Hormesis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hormesis is a two-phase dose-response relationship to an environmental agent characterized by a low-dose stimulation or beneficial effect and a high-dose inhibitory or toxic effect. In biology and medicine, it is defined as an adaptive response of organisms to a moderate (usually intermittent) stress. Examples include responses to ischemic preconditioning, exercise, dietary energy restriction, and exposures to low doses of certain chemical agents or radiation. The term derives from the Greek hormáein, "to set in motion, impel, urge on".

Because the hormetic response involves overcompensation by homeostatic control systems — the organism reacts to a mild stress by producing a response somewhat larger than necessary — the beneficial effects are typically modest, with maximum stimulation commonly 30–60 percent above the control response. Hormesis is quantitatively distinct from the "no observed adverse effect level" (NOAEL) approach in toxicology and from the linear no-threshold model used in much radiation and carcinogen regulation, a difference that has made the concept controversial in risk assessment.

History

Observations of biphasic dose responses date to the late 19th century: Hugo Schulz's experiments on disinfectants and yeast gave rise to the Arndt–Schulz rule, which stated that weak stimuli excite physiological activity while strong ones inhibit it. The term "hormesis" was coined in 1943 by Chester M. Southam and John Ehrlich, reporting that extracts of western red cedar inhibited fungal growth at high doses but stimulated it at low doses. Interest revived in the 1980s and 1990s, notably through the work of Edward Calabrese, who assembled large databases of hormetic dose responses across chemicals, species, and endpoints, and founded a professional society and journal (Dose-Response) devoted to the topic.

Mechanisms

Hormetic effects are generally understood as adaptive stress responses rather than direct effects of the agent. Mild stresses activate cellular defence and repair pathways — antioxidant enzymes, heat-shock proteins, DNA repair, autophagy, and mitochondrial biogenesis — often coordinated by transcription factors such as Nrf2 and FOXO. Because the activation overshoots, the net effect at low dose can be protective against subsequent, larger insults: this is the basis of ischemic preconditioning, in which brief episodes of ischemia protect heart or brain tissue from a later, sustained episode. At higher doses, damage overwhelms these systems and toxicity results.

Examples

  • Exercise: moderate exertion generates reactive oxygen species that trigger adaptations improving strength, insulin sensitivity, and antioxidant capacity, whereas extreme overtraining is harmful.
  • Caloric restriction: intermittent fasting and energy restriction induce stress-resistance pathways associated in animal studies with increased lifespan.
  • Radiation hormesis: the contested hypothesis that low doses of ionizing radiation stimulate repair and are net-beneficial; supported by some cell and animal studies but not accepted by most regulatory bodies.
  • Chemical agents: low doses of many antibiotics, herbicides, and toxins stimulate growth in target or non-target organisms in laboratory assays.
  • Temperature and hypoxia: mild heat or low-oxygen exposures condition organisms against later severe exposures (acclimation).

Controversy and policy

Hormesis challenges the precautionary assumptions embedded in much regulatory toxicology: the threshold model and, for carcinogens and radiation, the linear no-threshold (LNT) model. Critics argue that hormetic responses are often endpoint-, species-, and timing-specific; that a benefit for one endpoint may coexist with harm for another; and that generalizing low-dose benefits (especially for radiation and carcinogens) into public policy is premature. Supporters reply that hormesis is the most common dose-response pattern in their databases and demand that default regulatory models be re-examined. Major bodies such as the U.S. National Research Council's BEIR VII committee have retained the LNT model for radiation protection.
