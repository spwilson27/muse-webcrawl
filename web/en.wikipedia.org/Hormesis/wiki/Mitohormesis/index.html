Mitohormesis

Mitohormesis, or mitochondrial hormesis, is a name given to a hypothesis in biogerontology according to which mild mitochondrial stress and the resulting increase in reactive oxygen species (ROS) within mitochondria can act as signalling molecules that trigger increased stress resistance, metabolic changes and ultimately extended lifespan. It is a specific case of the general phenomenon of hormesis applied to mitochondria.

Background

The free-radical theory of ageing held that ROS are purely damaging by-products of metabolism. Experiments in the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans in the 2000s complicated this picture: certain interventions that impaired mitochondrial respiration or glucose metabolism extended lifespan and increased ROS formation, while antioxidants abolished that extension—suggesting the ROS acted as beneficial signals. The term "mitohormesis" was coined by Michael Ristow and colleagues in 2007 in work showing that glucose restriction extended the lifespan of C. elegans by increasing mitochondrial respiration and ROS, inducing catalase activity.

Mechanisms

Proposed mechanisms include activation of the mitochondrial unfolded protein response (UPRmt), increased expression of antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase and catalase, the metabolic sensors AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) and sirtuins, and retrograde signalling from mitochondria to the nucleus altering gene expression. Exercise is proposed to act partly through mitohormesis: transient ROS produced during exertion induce endogenous defences, an effect blunted in some human studies by high-dose antioxidant supplementation, which prevented the insulin-sensitising benefits of exercise.

Implications

Mitohormesis has been invoked to explain effects of calorie restriction, intermittent fasting, and metformin, and it challenges the simple strategy of supplementing antioxidants to slow ageing. Evidence in humans remains indirect, and the hypothesis is still debated in the field of ageing research.
