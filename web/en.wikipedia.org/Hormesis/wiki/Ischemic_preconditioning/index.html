Ischemic preconditioning

Ischemic preconditioning (IPC) is an experimental technique for producing resistance to the loss of blood supply, and thus oxygen, to tissues of many types. In the heart, IPC is an intrinsic process whereby repeated short episodes of ischaemia protect the myocardium against a subsequent ischaemic insult. It was first identified in 1986 by Charles Murry, Robert Jennings and Keith Reimer, who showed that four brief circumflex artery occlusions in dogs dramatically reduced the size of a subsequent myocardial infarction.

Phases of protection

Protection occurs in two phases. The early phase ("classic" preconditioning) begins immediately after the preconditioning stimulus and lasts one to two hours; it involves the release of triggers such as adenosine, bradykinin and opioids, activation of protein kinase C, and the opening of ATP-sensitive potassium channels. A second window of protection appears about 24 hours later and lasts up to 72 hours, involving synthesis of new protective proteins such as inducible nitric oxide synthase and heat-shock proteins.

Remote ischemic preconditioning

Remote ischemic preconditioning (RIPC) is the observation that brief ischaemia of a distant tissue—commonly induced by inflating a blood-pressure cuff on a limb—protects organs such as the heart from subsequent ischaemic injury. The signal appears to be transmitted by humoral and neural pathways. RIPC is attractive clinically because it is non-invasive, but large randomised trials in cardiac surgery patients have produced mixed or neutral results on hard outcomes.

Clinical significance

Preconditioning is regarded as one of the most powerful known forms of protection against myocardial infarction, and understanding its mechanisms has been a major research goal in cardiology. The phenomenon has also been demonstrated in brain, liver, kidney, skeletal muscle and other tissues, and is considered an example of hormesis—protection conferred by a sub-lethal dose of a harmful stimulus.
