Arndt–Schulz rule

The Arndt–Schulz rule or Arndt–Schulz law is a claimed law concerning the effects of pharmaca or poisons in various concentrations. It states that for every substance, small doses stimulate, moderate doses inhibit, and large doses kill. The rule was formulated by the pharmacologist Hugo Schulz and the psychiatrist Rudolf Arndt in the late 19th century, based on Schulz's experiments (1888) on the effect of various poisons on yeast fermentation.

History

Rudolf Arndt, professor of psychiatry at Greifswald, formulated a "basic law of biology" from his studies of homeopathic remedies, and Hugo Schulz, a pharmacologist at the same university, independently observed that disinfectants at very low concentrations appeared to stimulate the metabolism of yeast. Together their observations became known as the Arndt–Schulz rule.

Relation to hormesis and homeopathy

The rule is sometimes cited by proponents of homeopathy as a scientific basis for the efficacy of extremely dilute remedies, although the dilutions used in homeopathy typically far exceed any concentration at which a stimulatory effect could plausibly occur, and the rule itself is not generally accepted in modern pharmacology. A related but better-documented phenomenon is hormesis, the biphasic dose–response in which low doses of a stressor produce effects opposite to those of high doses; the Arndt–Schulz rule is regarded as an early, more sweeping formulation of this idea. Schulz's experiments were later reinterpreted: the apparent stimulation was small and depended on specific conditions, and the "law" does not hold generally across substances and systems.
