Radiation hormesis

Radiation hormesis is the hypothesis that low doses of ionizing radiation (within the region of and just above natural background levels) are beneficial, stimulating the activation of repair mechanisms that protect against disease, rather than harmful as assumed by the linear no-threshold (LNT) model of dose–response. The hypothesis proposes a J-shaped or U-shaped curve, with low-dose exposure reducing cancer and other diseases below the spontaneous rate.

Evidence cited

Supporters point to studies of populations in areas of high natural background radiation (such as Ramsar in Iran and parts of Kerala, India), to occupational studies including the Taiwan cobalt-60 contaminated apartment cohort, to the effects of radon exposure in some ecological studies, and to laboratory evidence for adaptive responses, in which a small "priming" dose reduces chromosomal damage from a later larger dose. Biologist T. D. Luckey was a prominent early proponent, and the hypothesis was further popularised by Edward Calabrese's work on hormesis generally.

Opposition and policy

Major scientific bodies have not accepted radiation hormesis as established. The US National Academies' BEIR VII report (2006) concluded that the LNT model remains the most reasonable basis for risk assessment and found the evidence for hormesis insufficient; the International Commission on Radiological Protection likewise maintains LNT-based standards. Critics note that ecological studies are prone to confounding, that adaptive responses are transient and tissue-specific, and that epidemiological studies lack the statistical power to resolve effects at very low doses. Proponents, in turn, argue that the regulatory costs imposed by LNT—such as evacuations and remediation standards—are disproportionate to any plausible benefit.

Radon spas

In parts of Europe and Japan, "radon therapy" spas and mines (for example the Gastein Healing Gallery in Austria) deliberately expose visitors to low levels of radon, on the belief that it relieves inflammatory conditions such as arthritis; this practice predates the formal hormesis debate and remains controversial among health physicists.
