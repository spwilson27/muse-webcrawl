Dose–response relationship

The dose–response relationship, or exposure–response relationship, describes the magnitude of the response of an organism, as a function of exposure (or doses) to a stimulus or stressor (usually a chemical) after a certain exposure time. Dose–response relationships can be described by dose–response curves. It is an important concept in pharmacology and toxicology, underpinning the determination of safe exposure levels and therapeutic dosing.

Shape of the curve

When plotted on a semi-logarithmic graph, dose–response curves are usually sigmoidal. Key quantities include the EC50 (the concentration giving half-maximal effect), the ED50 (the median effective dose), and, for toxic endpoints, the TD50 and LD50 (median lethal dose). The ratio of toxic to effective dose gives the therapeutic index. The Hill equation is commonly used to model the curve mathematically.

Threshold and non-monotonic models

Classical toxicology usually assumes a threshold below which no adverse effect occurs, or, for carcinogens, a linear no-threshold model in which risk is proportional to dose down to zero. Some substances show non-monotonic, biphasic curves—most notably the phenomenon of hormesis, in which low doses produce stimulation or apparently beneficial effects while high doses are inhibitory or toxic. Whether hormetic responses are common and significant for regulation remains a subject of debate.

Applications

Dose–response data are central to drug development, determining recommended drug doses in clinical trials, and to chemical risk assessment, where reference doses and acceptable daily intakes are derived from points of departure such as the no-observed-adverse-effect level (NOAEL) or benchmark dose.
