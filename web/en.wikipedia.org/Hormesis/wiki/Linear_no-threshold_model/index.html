Linear no-threshold model

The linear no-threshold model (LNT) is a dose-response model used in radiation protection to estimate stochastic health effects such as cancer at low doses of ionizing radiation. The model assumes that the risk of cancer is directly proportional to dose, with no threshold below which exposure is harmless, so that even the smallest dose carries a (very small) proportional risk. The LNT model underlies international radiation-protection recommendations, including the ALARA ("as low as reasonably achievable") principle.

History and basis

The model developed from mid-20th century studies of genetic and carcinogenic effects of radiation, including Hermann Joseph Muller's work on radiation-induced mutation in fruit flies, and was recommended by committees such as the US Biological Effects of Atomic Radiation (BEAR) committee in 1956. Epidemiological data on survivors of the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki, who received moderate to high doses, are extrapolated linearly down to low doses for regulatory purposes.

Debate

The LNT model is contested. Some researchers argue that it overestimates low-dose risk, citing the phenomenon of radiation hormesis and studies of high natural-background areas showing no excess cancer; others argue that experimental evidence (such as bystander effects) could imply even supralinear risk at low doses. Organisations including the International Commission on Radiological Protection maintain the LNT as a prudent basis for protection policy, while noting it should not be used to project cancer deaths from trivial collective doses. The French Academy of Sciences (2005) and various professional societies have issued reports on both sides of the question.

Collective dose controversy

A related controversy surrounds the "collective dose" concept: summing tiny individual doses over huge populations to predict numbers of cancers. Critics call such calculations speculative; regulators generally discourage using collective dose to forecast health effects from very small exposures.
