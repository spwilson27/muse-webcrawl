Intermodal freight transport

Intermodal freight

Key unitISO intermodal container (20 ft TEU, 40 ft FEU)
Modes combinedShip, rail, truck, (rarely) air
PioneerMalcolm McLean, first container ship voyage 1956
Transfer equipmentGantry cranes, reach stackers, straddle carriers

Intermodal freight transport involves the transportation of freight in an intermodal container or vehicle, using multiple modes of transportation (such as ship, rail, and truck), without any handling of the freight itself when changing modes. The method reduces cargo handling, and so improves security, reduces damage and loss, and allows freight to be transported faster.

Standardised containers move between ships, trains and trucks without the cargo being touched.

Origins

The modern system grew out of containerization. In April 1956 the American trucking entrepreneur Malcolm McLean loaded 58 truck bodies onto the converted tanker Ideal-X for a voyage from Newark to Houston, demonstrating that a standard box could slash loading costs from dollars per ton to cents per ton. International standardisation of container dimensions and corner fittings by ISO in the late 1960s made the system globally interoperable.

How it works

A typical intermodal movement begins with a truck carrying an empty container to a shipper, where it is loaded and sealed. The truck (drayage) leg delivers it to a port or rail terminal; cranes transfer the box to a ship or to a well car on a train — in North America frequently in double-stack configuration. At destination the process reverses. Because the container itself is the unit of handling, transfer between modes takes minutes per box.

Equipment and infrastructure

  • Containers — standard 20-foot (TEU) and 40-foot (FEU) units, plus refrigerated, tank, and high-cube variants.
  • Intermodal terminals — facilities with rail sidings, crane gantries, and truck gates.
  • Chassis and well cars — road chassis and double-stack railcars designed for containers.
  • Swap bodies and trailers — in Europe, piggyback carriage of whole trailers on trains is also common.

Advantages and limits

Intermodal transport lowers cost on long hauls by exploiting rail and water economies of scale while retaining truck flexibility at the ends. It reduces handling damage and theft, and typically produces lower emissions per ton-kilometre than all-road transport. Its limits include the need for terminal infrastructure, less frequent schedules than trucking, and poor economics on short routes where transfer costs dominate.
