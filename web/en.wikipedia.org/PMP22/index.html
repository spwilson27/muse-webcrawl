Peripheral myelin protein 22 (PMP22), also known as growth-arrest-specific protein 3 (GAS-3), is a protein that in humans is encoded by the PMP22 gene on chromosome 17. It is a 22-kilodalton integral membrane glycoprotein with four transmembrane domains, produced mainly by Schwann cells, where it is a major component of compact myelin in the peripheral nervous system. PMP22 is thought to play roles in myelin formation and maintenance, Schwann cell growth regulation, and cell adhesion.

Dosage sensitivity

PMP22 is notable for its sensitivity to gene dosage: both too much and too little of the protein cause demyelinating peripheral neuropathies. A duplication of a roughly 1.4–1.5 megabase segment of chromosome 17p12 containing the gene — giving three copies instead of two — is the most common cause of Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease type 1A (CMT1A), the most common inherited peripheral neuropathy. Conversely, a deletion of the same segment, leaving one copy, causes hereditary neuropathy with liability to pressure palsies (HNPP), characterised by recurrent episodes of numbness and weakness at sites of nerve compression. Point mutations in PMP22 can cause CMT1E and severe early-onset phenotypes of the Dejerine–Sottas type.

Mechanism

Duplication and deletion arise through non-allelic homologous recombination between flanking low-copy repeats (the CMT1A-REP sequences) during meiosis. Excess PMP22 disrupts myelin assembly and Schwann cell function, leading to a uniformly slowed nerve conduction, segmental demyelination and remyelination with characteristic "onion bulb" formations, and length-dependent weakness and sensory loss in the limbs. HNPP nerves similarly show focal thickenings of myelin called tomacula.

Clinical relevance

Genetic testing for the PMP22 duplication or deletion is a standard first step in evaluating unexplained demyelinating neuropathy. No curative treatment exists; management is supportive, with physiotherapy, orthotics, and avoidance of prolonged pressure on nerves in HNPP. Because PMP22 expression is dosage-sensitive, down-regulating its expression — for example by antisense oligonucleotides or other genetic medicines — is an active area of research for CMT1A.
