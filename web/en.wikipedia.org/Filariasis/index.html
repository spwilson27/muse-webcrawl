Filariasis

Filariasis is a group of parasitic diseases caused by infection with filarial nematodes (roundworms) of the family Onchocercidae, transmitted to humans through the bites of infected blood-feeding insects such as mosquitoes and black flies. The adult worms live in the lymphatic system, subcutaneous tissues or body cavities, and produce microscopic larvae called microfilariae that circulate in the blood or skin.

Forms

The most widespread form is lymphatic filariasis, caused mainly by Wuchereria bancrofti and by Brugia species; chronic infection can damage the lymphatic system and produce gross swelling of the limbs known as elephantiasis, as well as hydrocele. Onchocerciasis (river blindness), caused by Onchocerca volvulus and transmitted by black flies, infects the skin and eyes and is a leading infectious cause of blindness. Loiasis, caused by the African eye worm Loa loa, is transmitted by deer flies.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis traditionally relies on microscopic examination of blood (often drawn at night, matching the periodicity of microfilariae) or skin snips, supplemented by antigen detection tests. Treatment uses anthelmintic drugs such as ivermectin, albendazole and diethylcarbamazine, often combined in mass drug administration campaigns. The World Health Organization's Global Programme to Eliminate Lymphatic Filariasis, launched in 2000, has delivered billions of treatments, and a number of countries have eliminated the disease as a public health problem.
