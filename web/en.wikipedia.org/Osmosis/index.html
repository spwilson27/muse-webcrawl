Osmosis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Osmosis (from Ancient Greek ὠσμός, "pushing") is the spontaneous net movement or diffusion of solvent molecules through a selectively permeable membrane from a region of higher water potential (lower solute concentration) to a region of lower water potential (higher solute concentration), in the direction that tends to equalise solute concentrations on the two sides. It is a passive process requiring no metabolic energy.

Biological importance

Osmosis is the principal means by which water is transported into and out of cells. In animal cells, a hypotonic environment causes water to enter and may burst the cell, while a hypertonic environment draws water out and shrivels it. Plant cells become turgid in hypotonic solutions, the turgor pressure supporting non-woody tissues; in hypertonic solutions the membrane pulls away from the wall (plasmolysis). Osmoregulation — control of water and salt balance — is essential in freshwater and marine organisms alike, and in humans the kidneys regulate blood osmolarity via the hormone ADH.

Applications

Applying pressure greater than the osmotic pressure reverses the flow: reverse osmosis is widely used to desalinate seawater and purify drinking water. Forward osmosis and pressure-retarded osmosis are studied for water treatment and power generation. Osmotic effects also preserve food (in brines and syrups) and drive oral rehydration therapy.
