Crash cymbal

Classification: Percussion (idiophone)
Typical size: 14–20 inches diameter
Use: Accents in drum kit and orchestra

A crash cymbal is a type of cymbal that produces a loud, sharp "crash" and is used mainly for occasional accents, as opposed to the steadier pattern-keeping of a ride cymbal. In a drum kit, one or more crash cymbals are mounted on stands, usually to the drummer's left and right above the toms, and are struck with drumsticks at climactic moments.

Sound and construction

Crashes are usually 14 to 20 inches in diameter and are thinner than ride cymbals, which lets them respond quickly, speak with a burst of high-frequency energy, and decay relatively fast — though larger, heavier "crash rides" sustain longer. They are cast or sheet-formed from bronze alloys such as B20 (80% copper, 20% tin) and lathed and hammered to shape their tone.

Technique

Drummers typically strike the edge of the cymbal with the shoulder of the stick in a glancing blow, which produces the fullest sound and reduces cracking; a crash can also be choked by grabbing it after the stroke to silence it abruptly. In orchestral music, crash cymbals usually refers instead to a pair of hand cymbals clashed together.

Variants

Related types include the splash cymbal (smaller, for short bright accents), the china cymbal (trashy, gong-like), and sizzle crashes fitted with rivets. Nearly every drum kit configuration since the swing era has included at least one crash cymbal.
