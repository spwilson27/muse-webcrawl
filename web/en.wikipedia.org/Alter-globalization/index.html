Alter-globalization

Alter-globalization (French: altermondialisme) is a social movement whose supporters advocate global cooperation and interaction but oppose the negative social, political, economic, and ecological effects of corporate-led economic globalization, arguing instead for a globalization grounded in human rights, democracy, environmental protection, and economic justice. The name distinguishes the movement from simple "anti-globalization": its slogan, popularized by the World Social Forum, is "Another world is possible." In the Spanish-speaking world the term altermundismo is used; in France, altermondialisme is the standard name for what Anglo-American media long labelled the anti-globalization movement.

Origins

The movement crystallized in the late 1990s: the 1994 Zapatista uprising against NAFTA, the campaigns against the proposed Multilateral Agreement on Investment (1998), the founding of ATTAC in France in 1998 to campaign for a tax on financial transactions (the Tobin tax), and the mass protests against the World Trade Organization at Seattle in 1999 ("Battle in Seattle"), where some 50,000 demonstrators helped shut down the ministerial conference. Subsequent counter-summits accompanied meetings of the G8, IMF, World Bank, and WTO, including Genoa (2001) and the enormous 2003 global protests against the Iraq War.

The World Social Forum

The World Social Forum, first held in Porto Alegre, Brazil, in 2001 as a counterpart to the World Economic Forum in Davos, became the movement's principal open meeting space for trade unions, peasant movements (such as Via Campesina), environmentalists, indigenous movements, NGOs, and left parties. Regional and thematic forums followed worldwide. Its Charter of Principles defines it as an open space rather than a body that issues unified declarations.

Ideas and demands

Recurring themes include cancellation of developing-country debt, fair trade, food sovereignty, participatory democracy, defence of public services and common goods (water, seeds, knowledge), taxes on speculation, and climate justice. Thinkers associated with the movement include Joseph Stiglitz (Globalization and Its Discontents), Naomi Klein (No Logo), Susan George, Walden Bello, and Arundhati Roy. Critics argue its constituent groups lack a coherent common programme; supporters reply that this diversity — "one no, many yeses" — is the point.
