The koala (Phascolarctos cinereus) is an arboreal herbivorous marsupial native to Australia. It is the only living representative of the family Phascolarctidae; its closest living relative is the wombat. Koalas inhabit eucalypt woodlands of eastern and southern mainland Australia.

Description

Koalas have thick grey fur, large fluffy ears, a vestigial tail and sharp claws for climbing. They have two thumbs on their front paws and fingerprints similar to those of humans. Adults weigh 6–12 kg. Males have a scent gland on the chest and produce loud bellowing calls.

Diet and behaviour

Koalas feed almost entirely on eucalyptus leaves, which are fibrous, low in protein and contain toxic compounds; an enlarged caecum and detoxifying liver allow them to subsist on this diet. Because the diet yields little energy, koalas sleep or rest 18–22 hours a day. They are mostly nocturnal and solitary, occupying home ranges of trees.

Reproduction

Females give birth to a single joey, about 2 cm long, which develops in the backward-opening pouch for about six months, feeding on milk and later on a special maternal faecal material called "pap" that inoculates its gut.

Conservation

Hunted for fur in the early 20th century, koalas now face habitat loss, bushfires, drought, disease (notably chlamydia) and dog and vehicle strikes. In 2022 populations in New South Wales, Queensland and the ACT were listed as Endangered under Australian law.
