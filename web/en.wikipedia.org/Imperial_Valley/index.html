Imperial Valley

The Imperial Valley (Spanish: Valle Imperial) is an agricultural area in southeastern California that lies in Imperial and Riverside counties in the California region known as the Colorado Desert. It is bordered by the Colorado River to the east, Mexico to the south, and the Salton Sea to the northwest.

The valley is one of the most productive agricultural regions in the United States, sometimes called the nation's "winter salad bowl" because it produces a large share of the country's winter vegetables, including lettuce, broccoli, and cauliflower. Despite receiving only about three inches of rain per year, the region is irrigated with water diverted from the Colorado River, principally via the All-American Canal.

Geography

Much of the Imperial Valley lies below sea level, in the Salton Trough, a low-lying depression running from the Coachella Valley in the north to the Gulf of California in the south. The floor of the valley reaches about 235 feet (72 m) below sea level near the Salton Sea. The climate is arid desert, with summer temperatures routinely exceeding 110 °F (43 °C).

History

Large-scale settlement began in 1901 with the opening of irrigation works by the California Development Company. In 1905 the Colorado River broke through an irrigation cut and flowed into the Salton Sink for two years, creating the modern Salton Sea. The valley's principal cities include El Centro, the county seat of Imperial County; Brawley; Calexico; and Imperial. Across the border lies Mexicali, the capital of the Mexican state of Baja California, forming a binational metropolitan area.

Economy

Agriculture dominates the economy, supported by irrigation districts that hold some of the most senior rights to Colorado River water in California. Cattle feedlots, alfalfa, wheat, sugar beets, and dates are also important. The region has significant geothermal energy production around the Salton Sea and growing interest in lithium extraction from geothermal brines.
