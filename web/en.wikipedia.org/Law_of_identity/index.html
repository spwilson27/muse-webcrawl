Law of identity

The law of identity: everything is identical with itself

In logic, the law of identity states that each thing is identical with itself. It is traditionally regarded as the first of the three classical laws of thought, together with the law of noncontradiction and the law of excluded middle. In symbolic form it is often written as A = A, or ∀x (x = x).

History

The principle is implicit in Aristotle's philosophy and was articulated explicitly by later logicians. Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz formulated a related principle, the identity of indiscernibles, holding that things sharing all their properties are identical. In modern first-order logic with identity, the law is expressed by the reflexivity of the identity relation.

Interpretations

Philosophers debate whether the law is merely a convention of language or a substantive metaphysical claim about the world. George Boole treated the laws of thought as foundational axioms of his algebraic logic. Some non-classical logics and philosophies of change have questioned how identity applies to objects that change over time.
