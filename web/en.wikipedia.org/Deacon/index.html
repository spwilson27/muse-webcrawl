A deacon is a member of the diaconate, an office in many Christian churches. The word derives from the Greek diakonos (διάκονος), meaning "servant" or "minister." In the historic episcopal churches — Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, Anglican, and some Lutheran traditions — the diaconate is the first of the three major orders of ordained ministry, below priest and bishop. Many Protestant traditions also recognise deacons, though often as a lay office focused on service and administration.

Biblical origins

The office is traditionally traced to the sixth chapter of the Acts of the Apostles, which describes the appointment of seven men, including Stephen the first martyr and Philip the Evangelist, to oversee the distribution of food to widows in the Jerusalem church. The First Epistle to Timothy lists qualifications for deacons alongside those for bishops.

Functions

Historically, deacons assisted bishops in works of charity and administration and assisted at the liturgy by proclaiming the Gospel, preparing the altar, leading intercessions, and distributing Communion. In the Roman Catholic Church, deacons may also baptise, witness marriages, preside at funerals, and preach. Since the Second Vatican Council, married men may be ordained to the permanent diaconate rather than serving only as a transitional step to the priesthood; the Eastern Orthodox and Eastern Catholic churches likewise ordain married men as deacons.

Deaconess

Deaconesses served in the early church, with Phoebe named in Romans 16:1 as a diakonos of the church at Cenchreae. The office declined in the West during the Middle Ages but was revived in several Protestant churches in the nineteenth century and in the Anglican Communion; some Orthodox churches have also restored the ordination of deaconesses.

Vestments and insignia

The distinctive vestment of the deacon is the dalmatic, and the stole worn diagonally over the left shoulder. In the Orthodox tradition the deacon wears the orarion, a long narrow stole, and carries the censer during services.
