Golden Age of Radio


A family listening to a 1930s radio set.

The Golden Age of Radio, also called old-time radio, was the era of American radio programming from the early 1930s through the late 1940s and 1950s, when radio was the dominant home entertainment medium before the rise of television.

Programming

The major networks — NBC, CBS, ABC and Mutual — broadcast a rich schedule of drama, comedy, variety, soap operas, adventure serials, westerns, mysteries and news. Landmark programmes included Amos 'n' Andy, The Jack Benny Program, Fibber McGee and Molly, The Lone Ranger, The Shadow, Suspense and the Mercury Theatre's 1938 War of the Worlds broadcast by Orson Welles. Daytime serial dramas sponsored by soap manufacturers gave the "soap opera" its name.

Impact

Radio brought national culture into the home: President Franklin D. Roosevelt's "fireside chats", live sports, big-band music and breaking war news reached mass audiences simultaneously for the first time. Advertising sponsorship shaped formats, and stars of radio — Jack Benny, Burns and Allen, Edgar Bergen — became household names.

Decline

As television spread in the 1950s, sponsors and stars migrated to the new medium; many radio formats were adapted directly for TV. Network entertainment radio faded by about 1960, though old-time radio survives through recordings, collector communities, re-broadcasts and modern audio drama revivals.
