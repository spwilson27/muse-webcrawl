Celtic Revival

The Celtic Revival (also referred to as the Celtic Twilight) is a variety of movements and activities with the goal of promoting the Celtic peoples' culture, languages, arts and traditions during the 19th, 20th and 21st centuries. It paralleled similar movements in other European nations and influenced the development of national identity in Ireland, Scotland, Wales, Cornwall, Brittany and the Isle of Man.

History

Scholarly interest in Celtic antiquity grew in the eighteenth century with works such as James Macpherson's Ossian poems. In the nineteenth century, antiquarians and philologists such as George Petrie, Eugene O'Curry and John O'Donovan recovered and published Irish manuscripts and music, while the Young Ireland movement used this heritage politically.

Ireland

The Irish Literary Revival centred on figures including W. B. Yeats, Lady Gregory and J. M. Synge, and the founding of the Abbey Theatre in 1904. The Gaelic League (founded 1893 by Douglas Hyde and Eoin MacNeill) promoted the Irish language, and the Gaelic Athletic Association (1884) revived native sports. The revival fed directly into Irish nationalism and the independence movement.

Scotland and Wales

In Scotland the revival involved the rehabilitation of Highland culture after its post-Culloden suppression, tartan pageantry, and Gaelic scholarship. In Wales it encompassed the eisteddfod movement, Welsh-language publishing and the Druidic ceremonies of Iolo Morganwg.

Arts and crafts

Celtic interlace, zoomorphic ornament and Insular manuscript illumination influenced Arts and Crafts metalwork, jewellery, book design — notably the work of Archibald Knox for Liberty & Co. — and Art Nouveau graphics.
