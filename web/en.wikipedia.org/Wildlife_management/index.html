Wildlife management

Wildlife management is the management process influencing interactions among and between wildlife, its habitats, and people to achieve predefined impacts. It attempts to balance the needs of wildlife with the needs of people using the best available science, and encompasses game keeping, wildlife conservation, and pest control.

History

The discipline emerged in the United States in the 1930s, spurred by the overhunting and habitat loss that had devastated many species. Aldo Leopold's 1933 textbook Game Management is considered foundational, and the Federal Duck Stamp Act (1934) and the Pittman–Robertson Act (1937) created durable funding streams from hunting licenses and excise taxes on sporting arms. In North America the resulting "North American Model of Wildlife Conservation" treats wildlife as a public trust resource managed by state and federal agencies.

Practices

Managers manipulate habitat (through burning, planting, or water management), regulate harvest with seasons and bag limits, translocate or reintroduce species, control predators or invasive species, and monitor populations through surveys and banding. Modern wildlife management increasingly addresses non-game species, endangered species recovery under laws such as the Endangered Species Act of 1973, human–wildlife conflict, and the effects of climate change.
