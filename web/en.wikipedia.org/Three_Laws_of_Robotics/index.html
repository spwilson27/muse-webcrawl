The Three Laws of Robotics (often shortened to the Three Laws or Asimov's Laws) are a set of rules devised by science fiction author Isaac Asimov to govern the behaviour of the robots in his stories. They were introduced in his 1942 short story "Runaround", although Asimov credited editor John W. Campbell with helping formulate them in conversation. The Laws, as quoted from the fictional Handbook of Robotics, 56th Edition, 2058 A.D., are:

  1. A robot may not injure a human being or, through inaction, allow a human being to come to harm.
  2. A robot must obey the orders given it by human beings except where such orders would conflict with the First Law.
  3. A robot must protect its own existence as long as such protection does not conflict with the First or Second Law.

Use in Asimov's stories

Rather than serving as an infallible safety system, the Laws are a plot engine: Asimov's robot stories typically explore how the Laws interact in ambiguous situations, producing apparently paradoxical or harmful behaviour that his characters must reason through. In later works Asimov added a "Zeroth Law" — a robot may not harm humanity, or, by inaction, allow humanity to come to harm — which takes precedence over the others, most fully developed in Robots and Empire (1985).

Influence

The Three Laws have become a standard reference point in discussions of robot ethics and artificial intelligence safety, even though roboticists frequently point out that they are too vague to implement literally: notions such as "harm" and "human being" require precisely the understanding that real systems lack. Variants and parodies abound in later science fiction, and the Laws are routinely invoked — often critically — in modern debates over machine ethics and AI alignment.
