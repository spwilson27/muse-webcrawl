The platinum-group metals (abbreviated as the PGMs; alternatively, the platinoids, platinides, platidises, platinum group, platinum metals, platinum family or platinum-group elements) are six noble, precious metallic elements clustered together in the periodic table. They are ruthenium (Ru), rhodium (Rh), palladium (Pd), osmium (Os), iridium (Ir) and platinum (Pt). All six are transition metals lying in groups 8, 9 and 10, periods 5 and 6 of the d-block.

These elements share similar physical and chemical properties: they are dense, have high melting points, resist corrosion and tarnishing, and are excellent catalysts. They tend to occur together in the same mineral deposits, commonly alongside nickel and copper ores, and are frequently divided into the light platinum-group elements (ruthenium, rhodium, palladium) and the heavy platinum-group elements (osmium, iridium, platinum) according to atomic weight.

Properties

The platinum-group metals are among the rarest elements in Earth's crust. Platinum and palladium are the most abundant of the six; osmium is the densest naturally occurring element, and iridium is one of the most corrosion-resistant materials known. Their catalytic properties underpin their largest industrial uses: catalytic converters in vehicle exhausts (palladium, platinum, rhodium), petroleum refining, and chemical manufacture.

Occurrence and production

The great majority of the world's platinum-group metals come from the Bushveld Igneous Complex in South Africa, which contains the largest known reserves, particularly in the Merensky Reef and UG2 chromitite layer. Other major producers include the Norilsk deposits in Russia, the Stillwater Complex in Montana, the Sudbury Basin in Canada, and the Great Dyke in Zimbabwe. PGMs are typically recovered as by-products of nickel and copper mining and refining.

Applications

Beyond catalytic converters, platinum-group metals are used in jewellery (especially platinum and palladium), electronics and hard-disk coatings, glass manufacture, fuel cells, dental and medical devices (platinum-based chemotherapy drugs such as cisplatin), spark plugs and oxygen sensors. Because of their scarcity and strategic importance, they are extensively recycled.

History

Platinum was known to pre-Columbian peoples of South America and was described in Europe in the 18th century; the other five members were isolated from crude platinum ores in the early 19th century: palladium and rhodium by William Hyde Wollaston (1803), and osmium and iridium by Smithson Tennant (1803–04). Ruthenium, the last to be discovered, was isolated by Karl Ernst Claus in 1844.
