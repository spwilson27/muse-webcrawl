A reservoir is an enlarged artificial lake or storage pond created to store water, usually by building a dam across a river or by excavating a basin surrounded by embankments. Reservoirs are among the most important elements of water infrastructure, supplying drinking water, irrigation, flood control, hydroelectric generation and recreation, and collectively store thousands of cubic kilometres of water worldwide.

Types

Valley-dammed reservoirs are formed by damming a river valley; their capacity depends on the valley's topography and the dam's height. Bank-side reservoirs are built beside a river, enclosed by embankments, and filled by pumping or diverting water — common for storing treated drinking water near cities. Service reservoirs are smaller covered or open tanks that store treated water close to the distribution network, balancing supply and demand through the day. Pumped-storage reservoirs come in pairs at different elevations, storing energy by moving water uphill when power is cheap and releasing it to generate electricity at peak times.

Operation and uses

Reservoir operators manage water levels against competing demands: keeping space for flood inflows, holding enough storage for droughts, releasing water for downstream ecosystems and irrigation, and generating power. The usable "live storage" lies between the minimum operating level and the full supply level; below it lies dead storage, and above it a flood surcharge zone. Famous examples include Lake Mead behind Hoover Dam in the United States, Lake Nasser behind the Aswan High Dam in Egypt, and the reservoir of China's Three Gorges Dam, the world's largest power station by installed capacity.

Environmental and social impacts

Creating a reservoir floods land, displaces communities, fragments river ecosystems, blocks fish migration and traps sediment that would otherwise nourish downstream floodplains and deltas. Reservoirs can also emit methane from decomposing vegetation, and their weight and water infiltration have occasionally triggered seismicity. These costs are weighed, in planning, against the benefits of reliable water and low-carbon power, and have made large dams subjects of sustained public debate.
