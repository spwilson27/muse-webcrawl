Ernest Hemingway

Born: 21 July 1899, Oak Park, Illinois, U.S.

Died: 2 July 1961, Ketchum, Idaho, U.S.

Notable works: The Sun Also Rises; A Farewell to Arms; For Whom the Bell Tolls; The Old Man and the Sea

Awards: Pulitzer Prize (1953); Nobel Prize in Literature (1954)

Ernest Miller Hemingway (21 July 1899 – 2 July 1961) was an American novelist, short-story writer and journalist. His economical, understated prose — which he termed the "iceberg theory" — strongly influenced later twentieth-century fiction, while his adventurous lifestyle and public image shaped generations of writers and readers.

Early life and war service

Hemingway was born and raised in Oak Park, Illinois. After high school he worked as a reporter for The Kansas City Star before volunteering as an ambulance driver on the Italian front in the First World War, where he was seriously wounded in 1918. His war experiences furnished the material for A Farewell to Arms (1929).

Paris and major works

In the 1920s Hemingway lived in Paris as part of the expatriate circle Gertrude Stein called the "Lost Generation", publishing the story collection In Our Time (1925) and his first novel, The Sun Also Rises (1926). Later decades brought For Whom the Bell Tolls (1940), drawn from his reporting on the Spanish Civil War, and The Old Man and the Sea (1952), which won the Pulitzer Prize and contributed to his Nobel Prize in Literature in 1954.

Later life and death

Hemingway lived at various times in Key West, Cuba and Idaho, pursuing big-game hunting, deep-sea fishing and bullfighting. Plagued by injury, illness and depression, he died by suicide at his home in Ketchum, Idaho, in 1961. Several works, including the memoir A Moveable Feast, were published posthumously.
