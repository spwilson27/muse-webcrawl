Sir Isaiah Berlin (6 June 1909 – 5 November 1997) was a Russian-British social and political theorist, philosopher, and historian of ideas. Regarded as one of the leading liberal thinkers of the 20th century, he is best known for his distinction between positive and negative liberty and for his defence of value pluralism — the idea that human values can be genuinely in conflict and not resolvable into a single harmonious system.

Life

Berlin was born in Riga, then part of the Russian Empire, to a wealthy Jewish timber-merchant family. He witnessed the Russian Revolutions of 1917 as a child in Petrograd — including an episode, seeing a lynch mob drag a policeman to his death, that he later said gave him a lifelong horror of violence. The family emigrated to Britain in 1921. Berlin was educated at St Paul's School and Corpus Christi College, Oxford, and in 1932, at 23, became the first Jew elected to a fellowship at All Souls College, Oxford. During the Second World War he worked for the British government in New York and Washington, and briefly in Moscow in 1945–46. He was Chichele Professor of Social and Political Theory at Oxford from 1957 to 1967 and the founding president of Wolfson College, Oxford.

Two Concepts of Liberty

Berlin's inaugural lecture as Chichele Professor, "Two Concepts of Liberty" (1958), distinguished negative liberty — the area within which a person is free from interference by others — from positive liberty — freedom as self-mastery, being one's own master. Berlin argued that positive liberty, though a genuine value, had historically been the more dangerous of the two, because the idea of a "true" or rational self could be used to justify coercing people in the name of their own freedom. The lecture became one of the most influential works of 20th-century political philosophy and helped revive analytical political theory.

The Hedgehog and the Fox

His essay The Hedgehog and the Fox (1953), on Tolstoy's view of history, took its title from a fragment of Archilochus — "a fox knows many things, but a hedgehog knows one big thing" — and divided thinkers into hedgehogs, who relate everything to a single central vision, and foxes, who pursue many ends. Berlin counted himself a fox and extended the contrast into his pluralism: against monism, he held that liberty, equality, justice, and happiness are real values that can collide without any one of them being reducible to the others.

Other work

Berlin wrote widely on the history of ideas, including studies of Karl Marx (his 1939 biography Karl Marx: His Life and Environment), the Enlightenment and its critics, Russian thinkers such as Herzen, Bakunin, and Turgenev, and figures of the Counter-Enlightenment including Vico, Herder, and Hamann, whose thought he treated with sympathy even while remaining a committed liberal. Much of his reputation rested on essays, lectures, and broadcasts; his papers were edited after his death by Henry Hardy.
