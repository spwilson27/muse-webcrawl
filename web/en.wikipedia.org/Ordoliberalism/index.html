Ordoliberalism is a school of economic thought developed chiefly by German economists and jurists of the Freiburg School during the 1930s and 1940s. It holds that markets produce good outcomes only within a strong legal and institutional framework — an economic "order" (German: Ordnung) — maintained by the state, which must guarantee competition, stable money and the rule of law while generally abstaining from discretionary intervention in day-to-day economic processes.

Principles

Walter Eucken set out "constituting principles" of a competitive order: a functioning price system, primacy of monetary stability, open markets, private property, freedom of contract, full liability, and constancy of economic policy. Regulating principles addressed monopolies, abnormal labour supply and external effects. Ordoliberals therefore supported strong competition law and independent central banking, but opposed both laissez-faire and central planning.

Influence

Ordoliberal ideas shaped the social market economy of post-war West Germany associated with Ludwig Erhard, and through it the competition and monetary framework of the European Union, including the European Central Bank's stability orientation.

Criticism

Critics argue that ordoliberalism underestimates demand failures and inequality, and that its rule-based austerity was visible in the eurozone debt crisis. Defenders reply that it remains a coherent middle way between state socialism and unregulated capitalism.
