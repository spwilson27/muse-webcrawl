The empty tomb refers to the tomb of Jesus, described in the four canonical Gospels as found empty by women followers on the Sunday after the crucifixion. According to the accounts, Jesus had been buried on Friday in a rock tomb belonging to Joseph of Arimathea; when women came to anoint the body after the Sabbath, the stone sealing the entrance had been moved and the body was gone. In Christian theology the empty tomb is traditionally linked with the resurrection of Jesus, though the Gospels differ in detail and the earliest account, Mark's, ends with the women fleeing in fear and saying nothing to anyone.

The Gospel accounts

In Mark (c. 70 CE), Mary Magdalene, Mary the mother of James, and Salome find the stone rolled away and a young man in white who announces that Jesus has been raised; no resurrection appearance follows in the original ending. Matthew adds an earthquake, an angel descending, guards at the tomb, and a rival story that the disciples stole the body. Luke has two men in dazzling clothes and a reminder of Jesus's predictions, with Peter running to verify the tomb. In John, Mary Magdalene discovers the tomb empty alone and fetches Peter and the "beloved disciple", whose inspection of the folded burial cloths leads to belief; Mary then encounters the risen Jesus in the garden.

Historical discussion

Scholars debate the historicity of the empty tomb. Arguments in favour include the early burial tradition reflected in Paul's letters (1 Corinthians 15), the involvement of Joseph of Arimathea, and the prominence of women as witnesses, which some argue would not have been invented in the ancient world. Sceptical scholars note that Mark is the first narrative source, written about forty years after the events; that Paul never mentions an empty tomb or the women explicitly; and that alternative explanations — relocation of the body, an unknown or common burial place, or legendary development — have been proposed since antiquity. The debate remains a focal point in historical Jesus research.

Sites

The traditional location is the edicule inside the Church of the Holy Sepulchre in Jerusalem, identified since the fourth century under Constantine. An alternative site, the Garden Tomb north of the old city walls, was promoted in the nineteenth century and is popular with some Protestant visitors, though most archaeologists date its tombs to the Iron Age rather than the first century.
