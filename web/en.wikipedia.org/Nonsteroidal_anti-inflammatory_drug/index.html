Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) are a class of medications that reduce pain, decrease fever, prevent blood clots, and, at higher doses, decrease inflammation. They work by inhibiting the cyclooxygenase (COX) enzymes that synthesize prostaglandins, the lipid mediators of pain and inflammation. NSAIDs are among the most widely used medicines in the world; common examples include ibuprofen, naproxen, diclofenac, and aspirin — though aspirin, chemically distinct, is often discussed separately. They are "nonsteroidal" to distinguish them from corticosteroid anti-inflammatory drugs.

Ibuprofen, one of the most widely used NSAIDs worldwide

Mechanism of action

Most NSAIDs reversibly inhibit both COX-1 and COX-2. COX-1 maintains the protective gastric mucosal barrier and supports platelet function, while COX-2 is induced at sites of inflammation. Aspirin is exceptional in irreversibly acetylating COX enzymes, which underlies its lasting antiplatelet effect and use in low doses to prevent heart attacks and strokes. Selective COX-2 inhibitors (coxibs), such as celecoxib, were developed to reduce gastrointestinal side effects.

Medical uses

NSAIDs treat musculoskeletal pain, arthritis, dysmenorrhea, dental pain, headaches, and fever, and are used perioperatively and for conditions such as gout flares and patent ductus arteriosus closure in infants (indomethacin, ibuprofen). Topical formulations provide local relief with fewer systemic effects.

Adverse effects

The principal risks are gastrointestinal: dyspepsia, ulceration, and potentially serious bleeding, increased by age, alcohol, anticoagulants, and corticosteroids. Non-aspirin NSAIDs carry an increased risk of cardiovascular events, which led to the withdrawal of rofecoxib (Vioxx) in 2004. NSAIDs can also impair kidney function, raise blood pressure, precipitate heart failure, and provoke asthma in susceptible individuals. Risk is minimized by using the lowest effective dose for the shortest duration.
