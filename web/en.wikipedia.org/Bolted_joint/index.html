Bolted joint

A simple bolted joint fastening two plates

A bolted joint is one of the most common elements in construction and machine design. It consists of male threaded fasteners — typically a bolt or screw with a nut — that capture and join other parts, held together by the clamping force generated when the fastener is tightened. Bolted joints can be disassembled for maintenance and are used in everything from bridges and buildings to engines and pressure vessels.

Mechanics

When a bolt is tightened, it is stretched elastically and the clamped members are compressed, creating a preload. External loads applied to the joint are shared between the bolt and the clamped parts according to their relative stiffness; as long as the external load does not overcome the preload, the joint faces remain in contact and the bolt experiences only a fraction of the load. The relationship between tightening torque and preload is commonly approximated by T = K·F·d, where K is a friction-dependent "nut factor."

Joint types

Tension joints

In tension joints the applied load acts along the bolt axis, tending to separate the members. These are analyzed in terms of preload margins against gapping, fatigue, and embedment relaxation.

Shear joints

In shear joints the load acts perpendicular to the bolt axis. Friction-type (slip-critical) connections rely on clamp force, while bearing-type connections transfer load through the bolt shank in shear and the connected material in bearing.

Failure modes

Bolted joints may fail by bolt tensile or shear overload, thread stripping, fatigue cracking at the thread root, crushing of the joint material, self-loosening under vibration, or stress-corrosion cracking. Locking devices, proper preload, thread-locking compounds, and controlled tightening methods (such as torque wrenches, turn-of-nut, or hydraulic tensioners) mitigate these risks. Design standards include the VDI 2230 guideline and structural codes such as Eurocode 3 and AISC specifications.
