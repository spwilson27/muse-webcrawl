Spontaneous emission

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Spontaneous emission of a photon by an excited atom

Spontaneous emission is the process in which a quantum mechanical system (such as an atom or molecule) transitions from an excited energy state to a lower energy state and emits a photon. It is the mechanism underlying fluorescence, phosphorescence, most light from lamps and stars, and the operation of light-emitting diodes.

Einstein coefficients

Albert Einstein introduced the phenomenon in 1917, deriving Planck's law by balancing absorption, stimulated emission and spontaneous emission between two energy levels. The probability per unit time of spontaneous emission is given by the Einstein A coefficient, A21, which is independent of the ambient radiation field; the mean lifetime of the excited state is 1/A21.

Quantum explanation

In quantum electrodynamics, spontaneous emission arises from the interaction of the atom with vacuum fluctuations of the electromagnetic field: the excited state is not a true eigenstate of the total Hamiltonian, so it decays even in the absence of incident light. The emission rate can be modified by the environment, as in the Purcell effect exhibited in optical cavities.
