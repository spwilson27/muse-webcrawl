Jet engine

A jet engine is a type of reaction engine that discharges a fast-moving jet of fluid to generate thrust by jet propulsion, in accordance with Newton's third law of motion. The term most commonly refers to airbreathing engines — turbojets, turbofans, turboprops and ramjets — which power most modern aircraft.

Working principle

An airbreathing jet engine takes in air, compresses it, mixes it with fuel and burns it in a combustion chamber, then expels the hot gases through a nozzle. In a gas-turbine engine, part of the energy of the exhaust drives a turbine that powers the compressor (the Brayton cycle). In a turbofan, the turbine also drives a large fan at the front, which accelerates a much greater mass of bypass air around the core; modern airliner engines have bypass ratios above 10:1, improving efficiency and reducing noise.

History

The turbojet was developed independently in Britain by Frank Whittle, whose company Power Jets ran its first engine in 1937, and in Germany by Hans von Ohain, whose engine powered the Heinkel He 178 on the first jet flight on 27 August 1939. The Gloster Meteor and Messerschmitt Me 262 saw limited service late in the Second World War. Post-war development produced the engines that made mass jet travel possible, from the Rolls-Royce Avon and the Boeing 707's turbojets to today's high-bypass turbofans such as the GE90 and Rolls-Royce Trent family.

Types

Principal types include the turbojet, turbofan, turboprop and turboshaft (for helicopters), ramjet and scramjet (which have no rotating compressor and work only at high speed), and the pulsejet. Rocket engines are also reaction engines but carry their own oxidiser and so are usually classed separately.
