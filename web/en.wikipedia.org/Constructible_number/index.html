Constructible number

The regular pentagon is constructible with compass and straightedge.

In geometry, a constructible number is a real number whose corresponding length can be constructed with compass and unmarked straightedge in a finite number of steps, starting from a given unit length. Equivalently, a real number is constructible if it can be obtained from the rational numbers by a finite sequence of additions, subtractions, multiplications, divisions, and square roots.

Algebraic characterisation

The constructible numbers form a field, the smallest subfield of the real numbers closed under taking square roots of non-negative elements. A real number is constructible if and only if it lies in a tower of field extensions of the rationals in which every step has degree 2; consequently every constructible algebraic number has degree a power of two over the rationals.

Impossibility results

This characterisation, essentially established by Pierre Wantzel in 1837, settles the three classical problems of antiquity. Doubling the cube requires constructing the cube root of 2, an algebraic number of degree 3, so it is impossible. Trisecting an arbitrary angle would require solving a cubic equation, and is likewise impossible in general. Squaring the circle would require constructing pi; this was shown impossible by Ferdinand von Lindemann in 1882, who proved that pi is transcendental and therefore not constructible at all.

Constructible polygons

Carl Friedrich Gauss showed in 1796 that a regular polygon with a prime number p of sides is constructible exactly when p is a Fermat prime; the full criterion, completed by Wantzel, states that a regular n-gon is constructible if and only if n is a power of two times a product of distinct Fermat primes. Thus the pentagon and the 17-gon are constructible, while the heptagon and the 9-gon are not.
