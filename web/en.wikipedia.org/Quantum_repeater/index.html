Quantum repeater

A quantum repeater is a device proposed for use in a quantum network to extend the range over which entanglement can be distributed. Unlike a classical repeater, which amplifies a signal by copying it, a quantum repeater cannot copy an unknown quantum state, because of the no-cloning theorem. Instead, repeaters divide a long channel into shorter segments, create entanglement within each segment, store it in quantum memories, and connect the segments by entanglement swapping, so that the end points of the whole link become entangled.


Entanglement swapping across repeater segments

Motivation

In an optical fibre, photon loss grows exponentially with distance: a photon has only a small chance of surviving a journey of several hundred kilometres. Direct transmission therefore limits the rate of entanglement distribution and of quantum key distribution over long distances. Trusted-node networks can relay keys classically but require the intermediate nodes to be trusted; a true quantum repeater removes that requirement and preserves end-to-end quantum correlations.

Operation

The elementary link of a repeater chain generates heralded entanglement between neighbouring nodes, typically by interfering photons emitted by the two memories on a beam splitter. Because generation is probabilistic, quantum memories hold successful pairs until adjacent segments are ready. Entanglement purification can then raise the fidelity of the stored pairs. Once neighbouring links are established, a Bell state measurement at the middle node swaps the entanglement onto the outer pair, doubling the distance covered. Nesting this procedure produces entanglement over the full channel.

Implementations

Candidate memory platforms include single atoms and ions in optical cavities, atomic ensembles, rare-earth-doped crystals, nitrogen-vacancy centres in diamond, and quantum dots. Experimental demonstrations have shown teleportation and entanglement between memory nodes separated by metropolitan-scale fibre links, though a fully functional repeater with rates exceeding direct transmission remains an active research goal.
