Māori King movement

The Māori King movement, called the Kīngitanga in the Māori language, is a movement that arose among some of the Māori peoples of New Zealand in the 1850s to establish a role similar in status to that of a European monarch. Its purpose was to halt the alienation of Māori land and to unite Māori under a single sovereign figure who could deal with the British Crown and the settler government on equal terms.

Origins

The movement grew out of conferences of chiefs in the 1850s, influenced by Māori who had seen the British monarchy and by Old Testament ideas of kingship. In 1858 the Waikato chief Pōtatau Te Wherowhero was installed as the first Māori King at Ngāruawāhia. He was succeeded by his son Tāwhiao, under whom the Kīngitanga endured the invasion of the Waikato in 1863–64 and the confiscation of its lands.

Subsequent monarchs

Later monarchs included Mahuta, Te Rata, Korokī, and Te Atairangikaahu, who reigned for forty years from 1966 and became a widely respected national figure. Her son Tūheitia became king in 2006. Following his death in August 2024, his daughter Ngā Wai hono i te Pō was anointed as the eighth Māori monarch. The movement's headquarters remain at Tūrangawaewae Marae in Ngāruawāhia, and its monarch, while not recognised in New Zealand constitutional law, holds significant cultural and moral authority.
