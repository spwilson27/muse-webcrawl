Common mycorrhizal network

A common mycorrhizal network (CMN) is an underground network of fungal hyphae that connects the roots of two or more plants, often of different species. Mycorrhizal fungi colonise plant roots and extend far into the soil to gather water and nutrients; when the same fungal individual links several plants, the resulting network can move carbon, nutrients, water, and chemical signals between them.

Research

Experiments using isotope tracers have shown carbon moving from birch to Douglas-fir and back, and studies by ecologist Suzanne Simard and others reported that large old trees — nicknamed "mother trees" — may supply shaded seedlings through such networks. Defence signals also appear to travel via CMNs: bean plants linked to an aphid-attacked neighbour raised defensive chemistry before being attacked themselves.

Popularisation and debate

The networks were popularised as the "wood wide web", an analogy to the internet. In the 2020s, reviewers urged caution: evidence for net resource transfer benefiting receivers at the scale of whole forests remains contested, citation bias has been alleged, and the extent to which fungi rather than soil pathways carry the observed signals is unresolved. The existence of the networks themselves is not in dispute.
