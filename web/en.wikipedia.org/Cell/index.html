The cell is the basic structural and functional unit of all known living organisms. It is the smallest unit of life that can replicate independently, and cells are often called the "building blocks of life". All cells arise from pre-existing cells by division, contain hereditary information in DNA, and carry out metabolism within a membrane-bound interior. Organisms may be unicellular (bacteria, many protists) or multicellular (animals, plants, fungi); the human body contains an estimated 37 trillion cells.

Cell theory

Robert Hooke coined the term "cell" in 1665 after viewing cork through a microscope, and Antonie van Leeuwenhoek soon observed living microorganisms. In 1838–39 Matthias Schleiden and Theodor Schwann proposed that all organisms are composed of cells; Rudolf Virchow added in 1855 that all cells come from pre-existing cells (omnis cellula e cellula). These propositions form the classical cell theory.

Two great types

Prokaryotic cells (bacteria and archaea) are small (1–10 μm) and lack a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles; their DNA lies in a nucleoid, and they often bear flagella, pili and a rigid wall. Eukaryotic cells (10–100 μm) compartmentalise functions in organelles: the nucleus houses the genome; mitochondria (and, in plants, chloroplasts) generate energy; the endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus process proteins and lipids; lysosomes digest waste; and the cytoskeleton organises shape and movement.

Key processes

Cells convert nutrients to energy (cellular respiration, photosynthesis), express genes through transcription and translation, communicate via chemical signalling, adhere to neighbours through specialised junctions, and reproduce by mitosis or meiosis. Errors in these processes underlie diseases from cancer to neurodegeneration; understanding cells underpins all of medicine and biotechnology. Cells can be grown outside the body in cell culture, a foundational laboratory technique.
