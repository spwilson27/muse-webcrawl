Exothermic process

An exothermic process is a thermodynamic process or reaction that releases energy from the system to its surroundings, usually in the form of heat, but also in the form of light (as in explosions and burning sparklers), electricity, or sound. The term comes from the Greek exo ("outside") and thermē ("heat"). Its counterpart, an endothermic process, absorbs heat from the surroundings.

In chemistry

In an exothermic reaction, the total chemical energy of the products is lower than that of the reactants; the enthalpy change ΔH is negative. Bond formation releases more energy than is consumed in breaking the bonds of the reactants. Familiar examples include combustion of fuels, the neutralization of acids by bases, cellular respiration, the condensation and freezing of substances, and the setting of concrete. Nuclear fission and fusion are also exothermic, releasing vastly more energy per unit mass.

Exothermic reactions underlie many technologies and hazards alike: hand warmers exploit the oxidation of iron, while uncontrolled exothermic reactions can cause thermal runaway, as in some industrial accidents and lithium-ion battery fires. In calorimetry, the heat released is measured to determine enthalpy changes and bond energies.
