Foot-and-mouth disease virus

Foot-and-mouth disease virus (FMDV) is the pathogen that causes foot-and-mouth disease, a highly contagious viral disease of cloven-hoofed animals including cattle, pigs, sheep and goats. It is a member of the genus Aphthovirus in the family Picornaviridae, with a positive-sense single-stranded RNA genome of about 8,500 nucleotides.

Serotypes

Seven serotypes of FMDV are recognised — O, A, C, Asia 1, and the Southern African Territories types SAT 1, SAT 2 and SAT 3 — and infection with one does not confer immunity to the others, which complicates vaccination. Within serotypes, numerous strains circulate, and vaccines must be matched to the outbreak strain.

Disease and control

The virus causes fever and blister-like sores in the mouth and on the feet; mortality in adult animals is usually low, but production losses are severe and outbreaks trigger trade bans. Control measures include movement restrictions, culling of infected herds, and vaccination. The 2001 outbreak in the United Kingdom led to the slaughter of millions of animals and cost billions of pounds. The disease is not normally a threat to human health and should not be confused with hand, foot and mouth disease, a mild human illness caused by different viruses.
