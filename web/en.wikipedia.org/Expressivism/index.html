Expressivism

Expressivism is a theory in metaethics and the philosophy of language holding that sentences which appear to state facts — most notably moral sentences such as "stealing is wrong" — primarily express the speaker's attitudes or emotions rather than describing the world. On this view, to say that stealing is wrong is closer to booing stealing, or expressing disapproval of it, than to reporting a moral fact.

Development

The theory grows out of emotivism, defended by A. J. Ayer and C. L. Stevenson in the 1930s and 1940s, which treated moral utterances as expressions of feeling. Later expressivists such as Simon Blackburn developed quasi-realism, an attempt to explain why moral discourse can legitimately mimic factual, truth-apt talk even though it fundamentally expresses attitudes. Allan Gibbard's norm-expressivism holds that moral judgements express acceptance of systems of norms. Mark Timmons and Terry Horgan have defended cognitivist variants under the label "cognitivist expressivism".

The Frege–Geach problem

The best-known objection to expressivism is the Frege–Geach problem: moral sentences occur in unasserted contexts, as in the conditional "if stealing is wrong, then encouraging stealing is wrong", where the speaker expresses no attitude toward stealing, yet the sentence must keep the same meaning for the argument to be valid. Expressivists have proposed compositional semantics for attitudes to answer this challenge, and the debate remains central to contemporary metaethics.

Generalisations

Expressivist strategies have been extended beyond ethics to talk of probability, causation, knowledge attributions, conditionals and modality. "Global expressivism" attempts to explain all apparently representational discourse in expressivist terms.
