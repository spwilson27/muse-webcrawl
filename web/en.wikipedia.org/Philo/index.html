Philo of Alexandria (c. 20 BCE – c. 50 CE), also called Philo Judaeus, was a Hellenistic Jewish philosopher who lived in Alexandria in the Roman province of Egypt. He attempted to reconcile Jewish scripture with Greek philosophy, especially Platonism and Stoicism, through allegorical interpretation, and his synthesis profoundly influenced early Christian thought.

Life

Philo came from one of Alexandria's wealthiest and most prominent Jewish families; his brother Alexander was a senior customs official, and his nephew Tiberius Julius Alexander later became procurator of Judea. In 40 CE Philo led the Jewish embassy to the emperor Caligula in Rome to protest anti-Jewish violence in Alexandria and the emperor's plan to install his statue in the Temple in Jerusalem — an episode he recounts in his Embassy to Gaius.

Thought

Writing in Greek, Philo interpreted the Pentateuch as both literal history and allegory of the soul's journey to God. Central to his system is the Logos, the divine Word or reason, mediator between the utterly transcendent God and the material world — a concept later taken up by Christian theologians. He also developed doctrines of creation, providence, and the virtues that blended biblical teaching with Middle Platonism.

Legacy

Philo's works were preserved mainly by Christians rather than Jews and shaped patristic exegesis, especially at Alexandria through Clement and Origen. Modern scholarship regards him as the most important figure of Hellenistic Judaism.
