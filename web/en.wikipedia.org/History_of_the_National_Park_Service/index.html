History of the National Park Service


The Roosevelt Arch, Yellowstone National Park (dedicated 1903)

The history of the National Park Service begins with the creation of Yellowstone National Park in 1872 and the establishment of the National Park Service (NPS) as a bureau of the US Department of the Interior on 25 August 1916. The NPS grew from administering a handful of western parks to managing more than 400 units — parks, monuments, battlefields, recreation areas, trails and historic sites — covering over 85 million acres.

Origins: Yosemite and Yellowstone

In 1864 Abraham Lincoln signed the Yosemite Grant, transferring Yosemite Valley and the Mariposa Grove to California for public preservation — the first such federal land set-aside. On 1 March 1872, Ulysses S. Grant signed the act creating Yellowstone National Park, the world's first national park. Early parks were administered haphazardly by the Interior Department, with the US Cavalry protecting Yellowstone from 1886 to 1918. The Antiquities Act of 1906 empowered presidents to proclaim national monuments, used by Theodore Roosevelt for sites such as Devils Tower and the Grand Canyon.

Creation of the Service

Businessman Stephen Mather and his aide Horace Albright campaigned for a unified parks bureau. The National Park Service Organic Act, signed by Woodrow Wilson on 25 August 1916, directed the new service "to conserve the scenery and the natural and historic objects and the wild life therein and to provide for the enjoyment of the same in such manner and by such means as will leave them unimpaired for the enjoyment of future generations" — a dual mandate of preservation and use. Mather became the first director (1917–29), promoting tourism, roads and concessions; Albright succeeded him (1929–33).

Expansion and the New Deal

In 1933 Franklin D. Roosevelt's reorganisation transferred national monuments from the Forest Service and military parks and memorials in the East to the NPS, greatly broadening its remit to history and recreation. Civilian Conservation Corps crews built roads, trails and rustic-style facilities across the system. Eastern parks such as Great Smoky Mountains (1934) and Shenandoah (1935) were assembled from purchased land. The Park Service also helped plan state parks nationwide.

Post-war growth and Mission 66

Visitation surged after 1945, overwhelming facilities. Director Conrad Wirth's Mission 66 programme (1956–66) invested over a billion dollars in visitor centres, roads and staff housing, modernising the parks for the automobile age — and drawing criticism for overdevelopment. The system's growth continued with urban recreation areas (Gateway, Golden Gate, 1972) and the massive Alaska additions of 1980, which more than doubled NPS acreage.

Environmental era

The Wilderness Act (1964), National Environmental Policy Act (1970) and Endangered Species Act (1973) recast park management toward ecosystem science. Controversies included fire policy — reversed after the 1988 Yellowstone fires toward letting natural fires burn — predator restoration (wolves returned to Yellowstone in 1995), and managing overcrowding, air pollution and climate change. The NPS centennial in 2016 was marked by the "Find Your Park" campaign; visitation reached record levels (over 330 million visits in 2016 and again in recent years). The Great American Outdoors Act (2020) provided major funding to address the multibillion-dollar maintenance backlog.
