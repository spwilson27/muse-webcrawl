Stop motion

Stop motion (also called stop frame animation) is an animated filmmaking and special effects technique in which objects are physically manipulated in small increments between individually photographed frames so that they appear to exhibit independent motion or change when the series of frames is played back. Any kind of object can be animated in this way, but puppets with movable joints (puppet animation) and plasticine (clay animation) are most commonly used.

Technique

An animator moves the subject a fraction, exposes one or two frames of film or digital images, moves the subject again, and repeats the process — traditionally 24 times for every second of screen time. Because the work is painstaking, feature-length stop motion productions require large teams and long schedules.

History

Stop motion was used in early cinema for trick effects and was developed into a major special-effects tool by Willis H. O'Brien (King Kong, 1933) and his protégé Ray Harryhausen, whose "Dynamation" creatures defined fantasy filmmaking for decades. Television producers Arthur Rankin Jr. and Jules Bass built a franchise of beloved holiday specials — including Rudolph the Red-Nosed Reindeer (1964) — using their "Animagic" puppet process in Japan.

Modern era

Studios such as Aardman (Wallace and Gromit) and Laika (Coraline, Kubo and the Two Strings) keep the technique commercially vital, combining hand-crafted puppets with digital tools. Guinness World Records recognises The Tale of the Fox (1937) among the earliest surviving stop-motion features.
