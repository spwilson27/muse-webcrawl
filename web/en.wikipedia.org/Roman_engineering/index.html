Roman engineering

Roman engineering was the large-scale civil and military engineering of ancient Rome, famed for roads, aqueducts, bridges, harbours, baths, amphitheatres and concrete construction that endured for millennia. Roman engineers combined inherited Greek and Etruscan techniques with systematic organisation, surveying and logistics.

Materials and techniques

The Romans perfected opus caementicium — concrete faced with stone or brick — and the round arch, vault and dome. The Pantheon's unreinforced concrete dome, completed under Hadrian around AD 126, remains the world's largest of its kind. Standardised bricks, pozzolana volcanic ash that set underwater, and iron and lead plumbing enabled durable construction across the empire.

Roads and water

A network of more than 80,000 km of paved Roman roads, including the Appian Way, bound the empire together. Eleven aqueducts supplied Rome with over a billion litres of water daily, feeding public baths, fountains and latrines; Frontinus's De aquaeductu documents their administration. The Cloaca Maxima drained the Forum from the 6th century BC.

Legacy

Roman surveying instruments such as the groma and chorobates, military engineering described by Vitruvius in De architectura, and works like Trajan's Bridge over the Danube influenced engineering through the Renaissance and beyond. See also Roman architecture and aqueduct.
