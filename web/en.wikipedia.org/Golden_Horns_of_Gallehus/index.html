Golden Horns of Gallehus

The Golden Horns of Gallehus were two drinking horns made of gold, dating to the early 5th century AD (the Germanic Iron Age), found near Gallehus in southern Jutland, Denmark. They are among the most famous artefacts of early Germanic archaeology, celebrated for their elaborate embossed imagery.

Discovery and theft

The longer horn was found in 1639 by a girl named Kirsten Svendsdatter, and the shorter in 1734 by a farmer, Erik Lassen. The shorter horn bears a runic inscription in the Elder Futhark, ek Hlewagastiz Holtijaz horna tawidō — "I, Hlewagastiz, son of Holt, made the horn" — a key linguistic source for Proto-Norse. In 1802 the horns were stolen from the Royal Cabinet of Curiosities in Copenhagen by the goldsmith Niels Heidenreich, who melted them down; the originals are lost and known only from drawings. Replicas were made from the drawings in the 19th century, and new reconstructions, based on surviving illustrations, were produced in the 20th century; these are displayed at the National Museum of Denmark.

Iconography

The horns were decorated with bands of human and animal figures whose meaning remains debated — interpretations have invoked Germanic mythology, Roman influence, and ritual scenes. Their loss in 1802 remains a national trauma in Danish cultural memory, commemorated in Adam Oehlenschläger's famous poem "The Golden Horns" (1802).
