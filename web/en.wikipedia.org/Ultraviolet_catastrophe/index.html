Ultraviolet catastrophe

The ultraviolet catastrophe, also called the Rayleigh–Jeans catastrophe, was the prediction of late 19th-century and early 20th-century classical physics that an ideal black body at thermal equilibrium would emit radiation in all frequency ranges, emitting more energy as the frequency increases. Since the total energy emitted per unit time diverges towards the ultraviolet end of the spectrum, classical theory predicted an infinite total power — clearly contradicted by observation.

The problem

According to the Rayleigh–Jeans law, derived from the equipartition theorem, the spectral radiance of a black body is proportional to frequency squared, so at high (ultraviolet) frequencies the predicted emission grows without bound. In reality, measured spectra rise to a peak and fall to zero at short wavelengths, as described by Wien's displacement law.

Solution

In 1900 Max Planck derived the correct spectrum by postulating that electromagnetic energy could be emitted only in quantised packets, E = hν, proportional to the frequency of the radiation. Planck's law suppresses the high-frequency modes because a mode of frequency ν cannot be excited unless thermal energy exceeds hν. The term "ultraviolet catastrophe" was coined in 1911 by Paul Ehrenfest. Planck's fix is regarded as the birth of quantum mechanics; Albert Einstein extended the idea in 1905 with the photon explanation of the photoelectric effect.
