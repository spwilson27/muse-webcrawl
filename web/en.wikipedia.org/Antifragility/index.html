Antifragility

Antifragility is a property of systems in which they increase in capability to thrive as a result of stressors, shocks, volatility, noise, mistakes, faults, attacks, or failures. The concept was developed by Nassim Nicholas Taleb in his book Antifragile: Things That Gain from Disorder (2012) and in technical papers.

Concept

Taleb distinguishes three states: the fragile, which is harmed by volatility; the robust, which is resilient and unaffected; and the antifragile, which benefits from volatility. Examples given include evolution, the immune system, entrepreneurship, and certain political and economic systems. Antifragility is related to nonlinear responses: an antifragile system has a convex response to stressors, gaining more from positive surprises than it loses from negative ones.

Taleb argues that modern interventions often increase fragility by suppressing small stressors — for example, preventing small forest fires leads to accumulation of fuel and more destructive fires — a phenomenon he describes through the idea of iatrogenics, harm caused by intervention.

Criticism

Critics have noted that the concept can be difficult to quantify and apply rigorously, and that some biological and social examples are metaphorical rather than measurable. Nonetheless, the term has entered common usage in risk management, finance, and systems engineering.
