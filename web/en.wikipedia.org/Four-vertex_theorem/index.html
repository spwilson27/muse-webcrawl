Four-vertex theorem

The four-vertex theorem of differential geometry states that the curvature of a smooth, simple closed plane curve has at least four local extrema — at least two local maxima and at least two local minima. Such points are called vertices of the curve. The theorem is named for these four vertices, and it applies, with suitable modifications, to many other classes of curves, including curves on surfaces and certain piecewise-smooth curves.

Statement and examples

For an ellipse, the curvature attains its maxima at the ends of the major axis and its minima at the ends of the minor axis — exactly the four vertices promised by the theorem. More wiggly oval curves can have more vertices, but never fewer than four. A circle, having constant curvature, has infinitely many. The result fails for non-convex or self-intersecting curves in its basic form; extensions handle curves whose curvature is defined piecewise, such as polygons, where vertices occur at corners.

History

The theorem was first proved for convex curves by Syamadas Mukhopadhyaya in 1909, and a proof for the general smooth case was given by Adolf Kneser in 1912. Many alternative proofs exist, including elementary arguments based on the theorem of turning number, on osculating circles, and on the geometry of circumscribed circles: any smooth closed curve has a smallest enclosing circle tangent at two points, which forces extrema of curvature. A converse was established much later by Herman Gluck (1971) for convex curves and by Björn Dahlberg: any continuous function on a circle with at least two maxima and two minima can be realised as the curvature function of a closed convex plane curve.

Generalizations

Analogues of the four-vertex theorem have been proved for curves in three-dimensional space, for curves on the sphere and other surfaces, and for discrete (polygonal) curves, where the result underlies algorithms in computer graphics and curve fairing. The theorem is a standard example in textbooks on global differential geometry, alongside the Fenchel theorem and the Fáry–Milnor theorem for closed space curves.
