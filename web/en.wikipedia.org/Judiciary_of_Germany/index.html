The judiciary of Germany is the system of courts of the Federal Republic of Germany, an independent branch of government operating under the Basic Law (Grundgesetz). Germany has a civil-law system in the Romano-Germanic tradition, in which codes — above all the Civil Code (BGB) and the Criminal Code (StGB) — are the primary source of law, and judges have an active, inquisitorial role in fact-finding in many proceedings.

Structure

Unlike the unified court systems of common-law countries, German jurisdiction is divided into five specialized branches, each with its own hierarchy culminating in a federal supreme court: ordinary jurisdiction (civil and criminal matters, headed by the Federal Court of Justice in Karlsruhe), administrative courts (Federal Administrative Court, Leipzig), labour courts (Federal Labour Court, Erfurt), social courts (Federal Social Court, Kassel) and fiscal courts (Federal Fiscal Court, Munich). Most courts belong to the Länder (states); the federal courts sit at the apex of each branch and mainly hear appeals on points of law (Revision).

Federal Constitutional Court

Standing outside the five branches, the Federal Constitutional Court (Bundesverfassungsgericht) in Karlsruhe guards the Basic Law. It reviews the constitutionality of legislation, resolves disputes between state organs, and hears individual constitutional complaints (Verfassungsbeschwerde), which any person may lodge claiming a violation of basic rights. Its decisions bind all state organs.

Judges and prosecutors

Judges are independent and subject only to the law (Article 97 GG); professional judges are appointed after completing two state examinations in law and a preparatory service (Referendariat). Lay judges (Schöffen) participate in some criminal trials. Public prosecutors are organized hierarchically under the state and federal justice ministries.
