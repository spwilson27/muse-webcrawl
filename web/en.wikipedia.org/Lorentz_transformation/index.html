Lorentz transformation

The Lorentz transformations are a set of linear transformations between two coordinate frames moving at constant velocity relative to each other. They are the mathematical core of Albert Einstein's special theory of relativity (1905), replacing the Galilean transformations of classical mechanics. Named after the Dutch physicist Hendrik Lorentz, who derived them within ether theory, the transformations preserve the speed of light in all inertial frames and imply the relativity of simultaneity, time dilation and length contraction.

A Lorentz boost tilts the time and space axes of a moving frame

The transformations

For two frames S and S′ in standard configuration, S′ moving at velocity v along the x-axis of S, the transformations are:

t′ = γ(t − vx/c²)
x′ = γ(x − vt)
y′ = y
z′ = z

where the Lorentz factor is:

γ = 1 / √(1 − v²/c²)

Here c is the speed of light. At velocities small compared with c, γ ≈ 1 and the transformations reduce to their Galilean counterparts. The transformations form a group (the Lorentz group); successive boosts in different directions also generate a spatial rotation, the Thomas–Wigner rotation.

Physical consequences

  • Time dilation: a clock moving relative to an observer ticks more slowly by the factor γ.
  • Length contraction: an object moving relative to an observer is measured shorter along its direction of motion by 1/γ.
  • Relativity of simultaneity: events simultaneous in one frame need not be simultaneous in another.
  • Velocity addition: velocities combine as u′ = (u − v)/(1 − uv/c²), so no combination of subluminal speeds exceeds c.

History

Precursors appear in the work of Woldemar Voigt (1887), George FitzGerald (1889) and Joseph Larmor (1897). Lorentz published near-final forms in 1899 and 1904, interpreting them in terms of a stationary ether; Henri Poincaré named them for Lorentz and established their group structure in 1905. Einstein's 1905 derivation from the two postulates of relativity gave them their modern physical meaning, and Hermann Minkowski's spacetime formalism (1908) recast them as rotations in four-dimensional spacetime.
