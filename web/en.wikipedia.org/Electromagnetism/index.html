Electromagnetism

In physics, electromagnetism is an interaction that occurs between particles with electric charge via electromagnetic fields. The electromagnetic force is one of the four fundamental forces of nature and is responsible for most phenomena encountered in daily life, including the chemical behaviour of atoms and molecules.

The electromagnetic spectrum, from radio waves to gamma rays. Visible light occupies a narrow band in the middle.

Theory

The classical theory is expressed by Maxwell's equations, formulated by James Clerk Maxwell in the 1860s, which unify electricity and magnetism and predict electromagnetic waves travelling at the speed of light. The force on a charged particle is given by the Lorentz force law, combining electric and magnetic contributions. In the 20th century, quantum electrodynamics (QED) provided the quantum description, mediated by photons.

History

Hans Christian Ørsted discovered in 1820 that an electric current deflects a compass needle. André-Marie Ampère developed the mathematical theory of such forces, Michael Faraday discovered electromagnetic induction in 1831, and Maxwell unified the phenomena, showing that light is an electromagnetic wave.

Phenomena and applications

Electromagnetism underlies electric power generation and transmission, radio and telecommunications, optics, and electronics. At the atomic scale, electromagnetic interactions bind electrons to nuclei and govern chemical bonds.
