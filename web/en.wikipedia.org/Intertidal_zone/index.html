Intertidal zone

The intertidal zone, also known as the littoral zone in marine contexts, is the area of a seashore that lies above the water at low tide and is submerged at high tide. Organisms living there must survive dramatic daily swings in temperature, salinity, moisture and wave action, making it one of the most physically demanding habitats on Earth.

Zonation

Marine biologists divide the intertidal into subzones by height and exposure time. The spray zone is wetted only by splashing waves and inhabited by hardy lichens, periwinkles and isopods. The high intertidal is flooded only at the highest tides and dominated by barnacles. The middle intertidal, submerged and exposed for roughly equal periods, hosts dense communities of mussels, barnacles, limpets and seaweeds. The low intertidal, exposed only at the lowest tides, is the most marine in character, with kelps, sea anemones, starfish and rich tidepool life.

Adaptations

Intertidal organisms cope with desiccation by sealing in moisture (as limpets and barnacles do with their shells), retreating into tidepools, or tolerating extensive water loss. Strong attachment — byssal threads in mussels, suckered tube feet in starfish, cemented bases in barnacles — resists dislodgement by waves. Zonation patterns are shaped both by physical tolerance at the upper limits and by competition and predation lower down, a classic finding of rocky-shore ecology.

Human impact

Intertidal habitats are threatened by coastal development, pollution, oil spills, trampling and harvesting, and by warming seas and ocean acidification that shift the ranges of key species.
