Vacuum

A vacuum (Latin: vacuum, "empty") is a space devoid of matter. In practice, a vacuum is a volume of space in which the pressure is much lower than the atmospheric pressure, so that the density of gas molecules is greatly reduced. The word stems from the Latin adjective vacuus meaning "vacant" or "void".

Measurement and degrees of vacuum

Vacuum quality is measured by the residual pressure, expressed in pascals or torr. Ranges are conventionally described as low vacuum (105 to 102 Pa), medium vacuum (102 to 10−1 Pa), high vacuum (10−1 to 10−6 Pa), and ultra-high vacuum (below 10−6 Pa). Even the best laboratory vacuums still contain thousands of molecules per cubic centimetre, and interstellar space — with only a few hydrogen atoms per cubic metre — is a far better vacuum than any produced on Earth, though quantum field theory holds that even perfectly empty space has fluctuating vacuum energy.

History

Whether a vacuum could exist in nature was debated by philosophers from Aristotle, who denied it, onwards. In the seventeenth century, Evangelista Torricelli's mercury barometer (1643) produced a sustained vacuum above the mercury column, and Otto von Guericke's air pump and his Magdeburg hemispheres demonstration (1654) showed the power of atmospheric pressure. These experiments established the vacuum as a physical reality and enabled later work on gases and electricity.

Uses

Vacuums are used in vacuum tubes and cathode-ray tubes, electron microscopes, particle accelerators, semiconductor fabrication, freeze-drying of food and pharmaceuticals, vacuum packaging, incandescent and fluorescent lighting, and in vacuum cleaners, which use a partial vacuum to draw in air and dust. Outer space provides a natural high vacuum exploited and endured by spacecraft and astronauts, for whom pressure suits are essential.
