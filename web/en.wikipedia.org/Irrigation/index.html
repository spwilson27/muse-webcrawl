Irrigation

Irrigation
PurposeArtificial application of water to soil or land for agriculture
Main methodsSurface, sprinkler, drip, subsurface, manual
Global areaRoughly 330 million hectares equipped (early 21st century estimates)

Irrigation is the artificial process of applying controlled amounts of water to land to help grow crops, landscape plants, and lawns. Irrigation has been a key aspect of agriculture for over 5,000 years and has been developed by many cultures around the world. It helps to maintain landscapes and revegetate disturbed soils in dry areas and during times of low rainfall.

History

Archaeological investigation has identified evidence of irrigation in Mesopotamia and Egypt as far back as the 6th millennium BCE, where barley was grown in areas where the natural rainfall was insufficient. In the Zana Valley of the Andes Mountains in Peru, archaeologists have found remains of three irrigation canals radiocarbon-dated from the 4th millennium BCE. The Indus Valley Civilisation developed elaborate systems of irrigation and water storage, including reservoirs at Girnar dated to 3000 BCE.

Ancient Nubia developed basin irrigation, and farmers in ancient Persia used the qanat, an underground aqueduct system tapping water from aquifers in hills. The Sri Lankan irrigation works, dating from about 300 BCE in the reign of King Pandukabhaya, were among the most complex systems of the ancient world.

Methods

Surface irrigation

In surface irrigation, water moves over the surface of agricultural land by gravity flow. It is the most common form of irrigation throughout the world and has been practised in many areas for thousands of years. Subtypes include furrow, border strip, and basin irrigation.

Sprinkler irrigation

Sprinkler or overhead irrigation applies water through a system of pipes and sprinklers that spray water into the air so that it breaks up into small droplets that fall to the ground. Centre-pivot systems, in which equipment rotates around a pivot, create the distinctive circular field patterns visible from above.

Drip irrigation

Drip (or micro) irrigation delivers water slowly to the roots of plants, either onto the soil surface or directly onto the root zone, through a network of valves, pipes, tubing, and emitters. It minimises evaporation and runoff and is among the most water-efficient methods.

Subsurface irrigation

Subsurface irrigation applies water below the soil surface, raising the water table so that roots can draw moisture upward, or uses buried drip lines.

Water sources and efficiency

Irrigation water can come from groundwater (springs or wells), from surface water (rivers, lakes, or reservoirs), or from non-conventional sources such as treated wastewater or desalinated water. Irrigation efficiency describes the fraction of applied water that is beneficially used by the crop; conveyance losses, evaporation, and deep percolation reduce it.

Environmental impacts

Large irrigation schemes can deplete rivers and aquifers — the shrinking of the Aral Sea is a well-known consequence of irrigation diversions. Waterlogging and salinization of soils affect roughly a fifth of irrigated land worldwide. On the other hand, managed irrigation supports food production for a large share of the world's population: irrigated land makes up about 20 percent of cultivated land but produces roughly 40 percent of the world's food.
