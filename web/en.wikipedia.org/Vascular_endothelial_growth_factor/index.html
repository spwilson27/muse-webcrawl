Vascular endothelial growth factor

Vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), originally known as vascular permeability factor (VPF), is a signal protein produced by many cells that stimulates the formation of blood vessels. To be specific, VEGF is a sub-family of growth factors, the platelet-derived growth factor family of cystine-knot growth factors. They are important signaling proteins involved in both vasculogenesis (the de novo formation of the embryonic circulatory system) and angiogenesis (the growth of blood vessels from pre-existing vasculature).

Function

VEGF binds to receptor tyrosine kinases (VEGFR-1, VEGFR-2 and VEGFR-3) on endothelial cells, triggering proliferation, migration, increased vascular permeability, and new vessel formation. VEGF production is induced in cells that are not receiving enough oxygen: when a cell is deficient in oxygen, it produces hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF), which stimulates the release of VEGF.

Role in disease

Cancers that express VEGF are able to grow and metastasise by building their own blood supply. Solid tumours cannot grow beyond a limited size without an adequate blood supply, so VEGF overexpression is a key step in tumour progression. VEGF is also implicated in diabetic retinopathy and the "wet" form of age-related macular degeneration, where abnormal vessels grow under the retina.

Anti-VEGF therapy

Drugs that inhibit VEGF — such as the monoclonal antibody bevacizumab (Avastin), and the intraocular agents ranibizumab and aflibercept — are used in cancer treatment and in ophthalmology to slow vision loss. Side effects can include hypertension and impaired wound healing, reflecting VEGF's normal physiological roles.
