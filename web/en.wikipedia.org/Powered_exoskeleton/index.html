Powered exoskeleton

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Powered exoskeletons augment the wearer's strength and endurance.

A powered exoskeleton (also known as a power suit, exosuit, or exoframe) is a wearable mobile machine consisting of an external framework worn by a person, with motors or other actuators that supply part of the energy for limb movement. The device amplifies the wearer's strength and endurance, or restores movement to people with paralysis or weakness. It differs from a passive exoskeleton, which redistributes loads using springs or elastic materials without an external power source.

Powered exoskeleton

Applications: Medical rehabilitation, military, industry, logistics

Early example: Hardiman (General Electric, 1965–71)

Medical devices: ReWalk, Ekso, HAL

History and development

Early attempts date to the 19th century, but the first true powered device was General Electric's Hardiman (1965–71), a hydraulic and electric frame intended to let one operator lift loads of up to 680 kg; it proved dangerously unwieldy and was never used with a human inside at full power. Modern work accelerated in the 2000s with military programmes such as DARPA's Exoskeletons for Human Performance Augmentation, which produced devices like the Berkeley Lower Extremity Exoskeleton (BLEEX) and Lockheed Martin's HULC, and Sarcos's XOS full-body prototype.

Uses

The most established use is medical: devices such as ReWalk, Ekso Bionics' EksoNR, and Cyberdyne's HAL allow people with spinal cord injuries to stand and walk in therapy or daily life, with HAL notably controlled by bioelectric signals detected on the skin. Industrial exoskeletons — mostly passive or semi-powered back, shoulder, and grip supports — are used in automotive plants, warehouses, and construction to reduce fatigue and musculoskeletal injuries. Consumer and military systems remain limited by battery life, weight, cost, and the difficulty of matching a machine's motion to the wearer's body safely.
