Lunar water

Surface water ice (blue) in lunar polar craters

Forms: Ice in shadowed craters; hydroxyl in regolith

Key detections: 1994 Clementine; 2009 M³; 2009 LCROSS; 2020 SOFIA

Lunar water is water that is present on the Moon. It exists chiefly as ice trapped in permanently shadowed craters at the lunar poles, and as trace amounts of water and hydroxyl bound in the lunar regolith at all latitudes. Liquid water cannot persist on the sunlit lunar surface, where it would photodissociate and escape to space.

Discovery

Apollo-era samples were long thought essentially dry, and although some Apollo 14 samples and later reanalyses suggested traces of water, decisive evidence came from orbiters. In 1994 the Clementine mission's bistatic radar experiment hinted at polar ice; Lunar Prospector's neutron spectrometer (1998) found enhanced hydrogen at the poles. In 2009, the Moon Mineralogy Mapper (M³) aboard India's Chandrayaan-1 detected water/hydroxyl absorption across the surface, and NASA's LCROSS impactor kicked up a plume from the crater Cabeus containing an estimated 155 kg of water. In 2020 the SOFIA airborne observatory confirmed water molecules in sunlit terrain in Clavius crater.

Significance

Polar ice could be mined as a resource for life support, radiation shielding, and — split into hydrogen and oxygen — rocket propellant, making it central to plans for sustained lunar exploration under programmes such as Artemis. Its isotopic composition may also record the history of cometary and asteroidal delivery of volatiles to the inner Solar System.
