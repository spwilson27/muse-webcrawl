Coin

A coin is a small, flat, usually round piece of metal or plastic used primarily as a medium of exchange or legal tender. Coins are standardized in weight and composition and are usually issued by a government mint. Along with banknotes, they make up the physical cash of modern economies.

History

The first coins were struck in Lydia (in modern Turkey) around the 7th century BC from electrum, a natural alloy of gold and silver. Coinage spread through the Greek world and was adopted by Persia, Rome, India, and China, where cast bronze coins with square holes developed independently. For centuries coins carried intrinsic value in precious metal; debasement by rulers was a recurring problem.

Modern coins

Today most circulating coins are token money, worth more as currency than as metal, and are made from alloys such as cupronickel and plated steel. The front is called the obverse ("heads") and the back the reverse ("tails"); reeded edges, introduced to deter clipping, remain a familiar feature.

Collecting

The study and collection of coins is called numismatics. Rare and historic coins can command prices far above their face value.
