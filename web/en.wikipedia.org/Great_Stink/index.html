Great Stink

DateJuly–August 1858
LocationLondon, United Kingdom
CauseUntreated sewage in the River Thames during hot weather
OutcomeConstruction of the London sewerage system

The Great Stink was an event in central London during July and August 1858 when the hot weather exacerbated the smell of untreated human waste and industrial effluent present on the banks of the River Thames. The problem had been mounting for years: London's growing population had overwhelmed the cesspools and primitive drains, and increasing use of flush toilets discharged ever more sewage directly into the river, the city's main source of drinking water.

The crisis

Summer 1858 was unusually hot, and the river's stench became unbearable. The curtains of the newly built Palace of Westminster were soaked in chloride of lime to mask the smell, and there was talk of Parliament moving upriver to Hampton Court. Newspapers campaigned fiercely, and public fear of cholera — wrongly attributed at the time to "miasma", foul air — added urgency. Within eighteen days Parliament passed legislation empowering the Metropolitan Board of Works to build a comprehensive sewer system.

Bazalgette's sewers

Chief engineer Joseph Bazalgette designed a network of intercepting sewers — eventually over 1,100 miles of street sewers feeding some 82 miles of main interceptors — that carried waste eastward to outfalls downstream of the city, aided by great pumping stations at Crossness and Abbey Mills. Construction began in 1859; the system opened progressively from 1865 and was substantially complete by 1875. The Victoria, Albert and Chelsea Embankments, built over the intercepting sewers, narrowed and remodelled the Thames waterfront. Cholera never again struck London on a large scale.
