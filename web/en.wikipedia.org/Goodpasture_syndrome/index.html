Goodpasture syndrome, also known as anti-glomerular basement membrane (anti-GBM) disease, is a rare autoimmune condition in which antibodies attack the basement membrane of the lungs and kidneys, leading to bleeding inside the lungs (pulmonary haemorrhage) and rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis, which can result in kidney failure. It is named for American pathologist Ernest Goodpasture, who first described the disorder in 1919 in a young man who died of lung haemorrhage and kidney disease during an influenza pandemic.

Pathophysiology

The target antigen is the NC1 domain of the α3 chain of type IV collagen, found in the basement membranes of glomeruli and alveoli. Autoantibodies bind in a linear pattern along the glomerular basement membrane, triggering complement activation and destructive inflammation. Environmental triggers — such as smoking, hydrocarbon solvent exposure, or viral infection — may expose the normally "hidden" (cryptic) epitopes. There is a strong genetic association with HLA-DR15.

Signs and symptoms

Patients typically present with some combination of haemoptysis (coughing up blood), breathlessness, fatigue, nausea, and signs of rapidly worsening kidney function, sometimes with blood or protein in the urine. When only the kidneys are affected, the condition is often called anti-GBM glomerulonephritis.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on detecting circulating anti-GBM antibodies and on kidney biopsy showing linear IgG deposition along the glomerular basement membrane. Treatment is urgent: plasmapheresis to remove the antibodies, combined with corticosteroids and cyclophosphamide to suppress new antibody production. With early treatment most patients survive, though many are left requiring dialysis; delays worsen renal prognosis.
