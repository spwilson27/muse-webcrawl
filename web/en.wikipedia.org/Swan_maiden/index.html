Swan maiden

The swan maiden is a figure of myth and folktale found in traditions across Europe, Asia and beyond: a supernatural woman who can transform between human and swan form by means of a garment of feathers or a feather robe. In the typical tale, a man spies on the maiden while she bathes, steals her feather garment so that she cannot fly away, and marries her; years later she recovers the garment and returns to her own people, sometimes taking her children with her.

Variants

Versions of the tale occur in Germanic tradition, where swan maidens are associated with the Valkyries, in Russian folklore, in Japanese legend — where the stolen garment is the hagoromo, the feather robe of a celestial maiden (tennyo) — and in Korean, Chinese, Filipino and Native American traditions. Related tales involve other animal brides, such as seal women (selkies) of Scotland and Iceland and crane maidens in Japan.

Interpretation

Folklorists classify the story among supernatural spouse tales in the Aarne–Thompson–Uther index. Interpretations of the tale's meaning vary: it has been read as a story about marriage across social or cosmic boundaries, about the capture and loss of the wild, and about separation and grief. The motif influenced Tchaikovsky's ballet Swan Lake, in which the heroine Odette is a woman cursed to live as a swan by day.

In popular culture

The swan maiden continues to appear in modern fantasy literature, film and animation, and the Japanese hagoromo legend is retold in Noh drama, notably the play Hagoromo.
