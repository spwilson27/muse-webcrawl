Paradigm

A paradigm is a distinct set of concepts or thought patterns, including theories, research methods, postulates, and standards, that defines a scientific discipline at a particular period of time. The word comes from the Greek paradeigma, meaning "pattern" or "example".

The term acquired its modern philosophical prominence through Thomas Kuhn's influential 1962 book The Structure of Scientific Revolutions. Kuhn used "paradigm" for the constellation of beliefs, values, and techniques shared by a scientific community — the framework within which "normal science" is conducted as puzzle-solving. When accumulated anomalies can no longer be explained within the framework, a crisis may produce a paradigm shift, in which the old framework is replaced by a new, often incommensurable one.

Usage

In linguistics, a paradigm is the full set of inflectional forms of a word, such as the declension of a noun or the conjugation of a verb. In computer science, a programming paradigm is a style or way of programming — such as imperative, functional, or object-oriented programming. In ordinary usage since the late twentieth century, "paradigm" has come to be used loosely for any worldview, model, or pattern of thought.

Criticism

Kuhn's usage was famously ambiguous — the philosopher Margaret Masterman counted over twenty distinct senses of the word in his book — and Kuhn later refined the concept, distinguishing the broader "disciplinary matrix" from concrete "exemplars". Critics have questioned whether sciences outside physics possess paradigms in Kuhn's strict sense at all.
