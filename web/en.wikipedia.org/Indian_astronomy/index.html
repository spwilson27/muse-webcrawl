Indian astronomy

Indian astronomy is the tradition of astronomy developed in the Indian subcontinent over more than two millennia, intertwined with calendrical computation, astrology (jyotiṣa), and mathematics. Its classical period (roughly 500–1200 CE) produced sophisticated planetary models and trigonometric methods that influenced Islamic and, indirectly, European astronomy.

Early and classical periods

The earliest surviving text is the Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa (first millennium BCE), a calendrical treatise reconciling lunar and solar cycles. In the classical era, Āryabhaṭa's Āryabhaṭīya (499 CE) presented a rotating-Earth hypothesis for the daily motion of the stars, computed π accurately, and gave solutions for planetary longitudes. Varāhamihira's Pañcasiddhāntikā summarized five astronomical systems, including ones with Greco-Babylonian input. Brahmagupta (7th century) systematized computation and treated zero and negative numbers; Bhāskara II (12th century) advanced methods for planetary motion and instant velocity. The Kerala school of Mādhava (14th–16th centuries) developed infinite series for trigonometric functions and π centuries before their European discovery.

Observatories and modern era

In the 18th century, Maharaja Jai Singh II built the monumental masonry Jantar Mantar observatories in Jaipur, Delhi, and other cities. Modern Indian astronomy is led by institutions such as the Indian Institute of Astrophysics and by ISRO missions including the AstroSat observatory (2015) and the Aditya-L1 solar mission (2023).
