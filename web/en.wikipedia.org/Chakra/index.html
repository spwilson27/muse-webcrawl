Chakra

A chakra (Sanskrit: चक्र, cakra, "wheel, circle"; Pali: cakka) is a concept originating in early Indian traditions of Hinduism, Buddhism and Jainism, denoting centres of subtle energy in the body. Chakras are part of the subtle body (sūkṣma śarīra), visualised as spinning wheels or lotus-like vortices located along the central channel (suṣumṇā nāḍī) of the spine, through which vital energy (prāṇa, or kuṇḍalinī) is said to flow. Different traditions describe different numbers and locations; the system best known in the West has seven principal chakras.

The seven-chakra system

The standard model derives largely from the Sanskrit Ṣaṭ-cakra-nirūpaṇa ("Description of the Six Centres", 1577) by Pūrṇānanda, translated by John Woodroffe ("Arthur Avalon") in The Serpent Power (1919). The centres, from base to crown, are: Mūlādhāra (root, perineum; earth), Svādhiṣṭhāna (sacral; water), Maṇipūra (navel; fire), Anāhata (heart; air), Viśuddha (throat; ether), Ājñā (brow; command), and Sahasrāra (crown; the thousand-petalled lotus, sometimes counted apart from the six). Each is assigned a seed syllable (bīja), deity, colour, element, petal number and psychological associations.

History

The word cakra in the Vedas denotes a wheel — of the sun, of time, or a weapon. References to energy centres emerge in later Upanishads and become systematic in medieval tantric traditions (both Hindu Śaiva/Śākta and Buddhist Vajrayāna, which often uses systems of four or five chakras), where they function in ritual visualisation, mantra placement (nyāsa) and kuṇḍalinī yoga. Jain texts describe related centres tied to meditation. Leadbeater's Theosophical work The Chakras (1927) and later New Age writers associated the chakras with rainbow colours, endocrine glands and psychological states — correspondences not found in the Sanskrit sources but now ubiquitous in Western yoga and alternative medicine.

Practices and modern reception

Chakras feature in haṭha yoga, kuṇḍalinī practice, meditation, reiki and "chakra healing" in complementary medicine, where blockages are said to cause physical or emotional symptoms. There is no scientific evidence for chakras as anatomical or physical structures, and they are treated by scholars as elements of religious physiology and contemplative practice.
