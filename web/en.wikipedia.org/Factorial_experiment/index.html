Factorial experiment

A factorial experiment is an experiment whose design consists of two or more factors, each with discrete possible values or "levels", and whose experimental units take on all possible combinations of these values across all such factors. A factorial experiment can be implemented as a series of independent experiments following a specific model, or it can be designed so that the experiments are interdependent.

Overview

Factorial designs were used in the 19th century by John Bennet Lawes and Joseph Henry Gilbert of the Rothamsted Experimental Station. Ronald Fisher argued in 1926 that "complex" designs (such as factorial designs) were more efficient than studying one factor at a time. A factorial design allows the effect of several factors and even interactions between them to be determined with the same number of trials as are necessary to determine any one factor's effect by itself with the same degree of accuracy.

Terminology

Each combination of factor levels is called a treatment combination or cell. The effect of a factor averaged over the levels of the other factors is its main effect. When the effect of one factor depends on the level of another, the factors are said to show an interaction. A design in which every combination appears the same number of times is balanced; runs of the experiment in different combinations are replicates.

Full and fractional factorial designs

In a full factorial design, all possible combinations of levels are run. When the number of combinations is too large, a fractional factorial design runs only a carefully chosen subset, accepting that some high-order interactions will be confounded with other effects. Two-level designs are written 2k, where k is the number of factors.

Analysis

Factorial experiments are usually analysed with analysis of variance (ANOVA), effect estimates, and normal probability plots of effects (especially in unreplicated designs, where Daniel's method can be used to judge which effects are real).
