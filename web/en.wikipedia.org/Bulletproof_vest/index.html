Bulletproof vest

A bulletproof vest, also known as a ballistic vest or bullet-resistant vest, is body armour that absorbs and reduces the impact of firearm projectiles and shrapnel. Soft vests, made of many layers of woven or laminated fibres, are worn by police officers and security personnel; military and SWAT units add hard ceramic or metal plates for protection against rifle fire.

How it works

Modern soft armour is woven from high-strength fibres such as Kevlar (an aramid developed by Stephanie Kwolek at DuPont in 1965) or ultra-high-molecular-weight polyethylene. When a bullet strikes, the fibres stretch and spread the projectile's energy across a wide area, deforming the bullet and arresting it before penetration — though the wearer still suffers blunt trauma, which trauma pads and strict backface-deformation standards seek to limit.

Standards

The U.S. National Institute of Justice rates armour from Level IIA (low-velocity handgun rounds) through Level IIIA (magnum handgun) for soft armour, and Levels III and IV for hard plates proof against rifle and armour-piercing rifle fire. Ratings consider multiple hits, environmental conditioning and the depth of deformation into the wearer's body.

History

Precursors include the silk vests tested in the 1890s, the flak jackets of the Second World War, and nylon vests of the Korean War. The introduction of Kevlar in the 1970s made concealable armour practical; the NIJ's demonstration programme showed vests saved thousands of officers' lives, and body armour is credited with saving more than 3,000 police officers in the United States alone.

Limitations

Vests do not reliably stop knives, spikes or rifle fire unless rated for them, and protection degrades with age, moisture and ultraviolet exposure, so carriers typically replace panels every five years.
