Mahdist Sudan

Mahdist Sudan (Arabic: الدولة المهدية, al-Dawla al-Mahdiyya) was a state in northeastern Africa from 1885 to 1899, founded by the religious leader Muhammad Ahmad, who proclaimed himself the Mahdi (the guided one expected in Islamic eschatology) and led a revolt against Turco-Egyptian rule. After taking Khartoum in 1885, the Mahdist state governed most of present-day Sudan from its capital at Omdurman until its defeat by Anglo-Egyptian forces at the Battle of Omdurman in 1898.

The Mahdi's followers, the ansar, wore patched jibba tunics as a symbol of humility.

Rise and rule

Muhammad Ahmad's call to purify Islam and expel foreign rule won rapid support among riverine tribes, merchants hurt by the suppression of the slave trade, and Sufi orders. His forces annihilated expeditionary columns under Hicks Pasha (1883) and captured Khartoum in January 1885, in an assault in which the British governor-general Charles George Gordon was killed. The Mahdi died of typhus six months later and was succeeded by his deputy Abdallahi ibn Muhammad, the Khalifa, who consolidated a centralised theocracy: sharia courts, a state treasury (beit el-mal), provincial emirs, and armies organised under coloured banners.

Wars and decline

The Mahdist state fought Ethiopia (winning at Gallabat in 1889, where Emperor Yohannes IV was killed), repelled an Egyptian advance at Toski (1889, a defeat for the Mahdists), and faced revolts and famine that sharply reduced the population. From 1896 Herbert Kitchener's Anglo-Egyptian army advanced up the Nile with gunboats, railways, and Maxim guns; at Omdurman on 2 September 1898 some 11,000 Mahdist soldiers were killed against fewer than 50 Anglo-Egyptian dead. The Khalifa fled south and was killed at Umm Diwaykarat in November 1899.

Legacy

The Mahdiyya is remembered in Sudan as both a national resistance movement and a harsh theocracy. The Mahdi's tomb in Omdurman remains a major shrine, and his descendants founded the Umma Party; the Ansar religious movement, led by the Mahdi's family (including Sadiq al-Mahdi, twice prime minister), remained central to Sudanese politics into the 21st century.
