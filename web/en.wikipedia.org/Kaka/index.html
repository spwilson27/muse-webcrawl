Kaka

New Zealand kākā (Nestor meridionalis)
Family: Nestoridae (New Zealand parrots)
Endemic to: New Zealand
Conservation status: Endangered (declining)

The kākā (Nestor meridionalis) is a large species of parrot in the family Nestoridae endemic to the native forests of New Zealand. Closely related to the kea and the extinct Norfolk and Chatham kākā, it is a deep forest specialist known for its olive-brown plumage with scarlet underwings, its strong curved beak, and its varied repertoire of whistles, clucks, and harsh "kaa-aa" calls from which its Māori name derives.

Description and behaviour

Kākā measure about 45 cm in length. They are primarily arboreal, feeding on seeds, fruit, nectar, sap, and invertebrates; with their brush-tipped tongues they lap nectar from flax and rātā flowers, and they use their beaks to tear bark in search of grubs and to extract sap. They nest in hollow trees, laying two to four eggs, and the female alone incubates, fed by the male. Kākā are social and often gather at dawn and dusk, and they are strong fliers that regularly travel between forest fragments.

Conservation

Once abundant throughout New Zealand, kākā have declined sharply because of habitat clearance and predation by introduced mammals — especially possums, stoats, and rats — which kill nesting females disproportionately. The species survives in good numbers mainly where sustained pest control operates, such as Kapiti Island, Zealandia in Wellington, and intensively managed mainland forests; conservationists have also worked to deter urban kākā in Wellington from eating lead roofing nails and other toxic materials.
