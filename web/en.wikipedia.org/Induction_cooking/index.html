Induction cooking

Induction cooking is a method of cooking that uses electromagnetic induction to heat a cooking vessel directly, rather than heating a burner or flame that transfers heat to the vessel. An induction cooktop contains a coil of copper wire beneath a glass-ceramic surface; an alternating current through the coil produces an oscillating magnetic field, which induces eddy currents (and magnetic hysteresis losses) in ferromagnetic cookware such as cast iron and many stainless steels, heating the pan itself.

The pan is heated directly by induced currents; the cooktop surface stays comparatively cool.

Advantages and limitations

Induction offers rapid heating and precise, repeatable temperature control comparable or superior to gas, with high energy efficiency — typically around 84–90% of energy reaches the food, versus roughly 40% for gas. Because there is no open flame or glowing element, the kitchen stays cooler and indoor air pollution from combustion is eliminated. Drawbacks include the need for induction-compatible (magnetic) cookware, higher initial cost, and audible hum from some units. Induction stoves have been promoted in building-decarbonisation policies as an alternative to gas ranges.

History

Patents for induction heating date to the early 1900s; the first commercial induction ranges appeared in the 1970s, and the technology became widespread in Europe and Asia before gaining popularity in North America in the 2010s and 2020s.
