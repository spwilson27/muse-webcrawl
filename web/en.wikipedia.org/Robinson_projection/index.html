Robinson projection
TypePseudocylindrical compromise projection
InventorArthur H. Robinson
Introduced1963
PropertiesNeither conformal nor equal-area; minimises overall distortion

The Robinson projection is a map projection of the world designed to show the whole Earth at once with pleasing, low-error appearance rather than exact mathematical properties. It was developed by American geographer and cartographer Arthur H. Robinson in 1963, at the request of the Rand McNally company, and became one of the most widely used world-map projections of the late 20th century.

Design

Rather than deriving the projection from a formula alone, Robinson constructed it from a table of coordinates: he specified, for latitudes at 5° intervals, how long the parallels should be and how far apart they should lie, tuning the values by eye until the continents "looked right." Only later were equations fitted to the table. The result is a pseudocylindrical projection — parallels are straight horizontal lines, meridians are evenly spaced curved lines bulging outward — that deliberately compromises among area, shape, distance and direction distortion so that none is extreme.

Use and reception

Rand McNally adopted it for its world maps from the 1960s, and in 1988 the National Geographic Society replaced the Van der Grinten projection with the Robinson for its world maps, greatly increasing its visibility. It was in turn replaced at National Geographic in 1998 by the Winkel tripel projection, which reduces area distortion further. Critics note that the Robinson still stretches high-latitude landmasses such as Russia and Canada and is unsuitable for navigation or precise measurement, but it remains a standard choice for thematic world maps in atlases and textbooks.
