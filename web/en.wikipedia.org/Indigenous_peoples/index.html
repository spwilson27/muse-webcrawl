Indigenous peoples

Indigenous peoples are the original inhabitants of a region and their descendants, who maintain historical continuity with pre-colonial societies and distinct social, economic, cultural, and political institutions. There are an estimated 476 million Indigenous people worldwide, belonging to some 5,000 distinct groups in about 90 countries, and speaking the majority of the world's roughly 7,000 languages. Examples include the Sámi of northern Europe, the Māori of New Zealand, Quechua and Aymara peoples of the Andes, Aboriginal Australians, and the hundreds of Native American and First Nations peoples of the Americas.

Definition

There is no single universally accepted legal definition. Working definitions developed by the United Nations emphasize self-identification, historical continuity with pre-invasion societies, distinctiveness, non-dominance within present-day states, and a determination to preserve ancestral territories and cultures. The International Labour Organization's Convention 169 (1989) and the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (2007) are the principal international instruments recognizing their collective rights.

History

Colonization brought disease, dispossession, forced assimilation, and violence that devastated many Indigenous populations, particularly in the Americas and Australia after 1492. Policies such as residential schools in Canada and the United States, and the Stolen Generations in Australia, sought to erase Indigenous languages and cultures. Since the late 20th century, Indigenous movements have won growing recognition of land rights, self-government, and treaty obligations, though disputes over resources, territory, and sovereignty continue.

Contemporary issues

Indigenous peoples disproportionately experience poverty, poorer health, and barriers to education, while also serving as stewards of much of the world's remaining biodiversity — their territories intersect with about 40% of protected areas and ecologically intact landscapes. Issues of land tenure, free, prior and informed consent for resource projects, language revitalization, and climate change are central to current debates.
