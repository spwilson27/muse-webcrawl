Pramāṇa (Sanskrit: प्रमाण) is a concept in Indian philosophy meaning "proof" or "means of valid knowledge". The pramāṇas are the reliable instruments by which a knower (pramātā) obtains accurate knowledge (pramā) of an object. Specifying which means of knowledge are valid, and how they work, is a central topic of Indian epistemology, and the schools of Indian philosophy differ famously in the number and nature of pramāṇas they accept.

The principal pramāṇas

Most schools accept pratyakṣa (perception), direct awareness through the senses or mind; anumāna (inference), knowledge derived from a sign or reason, formalized in the Nyāya syllogism; and śabda (verbal testimony), the word of reliable authorities, crucially including the scriptures. Nyāya adds upamāna (comparison or analogy — learning what a word denotes by resemblance to something known). The Bhatta Mīmāṃsā and Advaita Vedānta schools further accept arthāpatti (postulation, presumption from circumstances) and anupalabdhi (non-perception, knowledge of absence).

By school

The materialist Cārvāka school accepted perception alone, rejecting inference about unobservable things. Buddhism (notably Dignāga and Dharmakīrti) accepted two pramāṇas: perception and inference. Jainism accepted perception, inference and testimony, embedding them in its doctrine of the manifoldness of reality. Sāṃkhya and Yoga accepted three, while Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika accepted four, Mīmāṃsā five or six, and Vedānta generally six.

Theories of validity

Schools debated whether the validity of knowledge is intrinsic (svataḥ prāmāṇya, held by Mīmāṃsā and Advaita — knowledge is presumed valid until defeated) or extrinsic (parataḥ prāmāṇya, held by Nyāya — validity must be confirmed by successful activity or further cognition). These debates engaged questions of error, scepticism and the definition of truth that parallel, and in part predate, discussions in Western epistemology.
