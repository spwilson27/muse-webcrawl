Rude boy

Rude boy (or rudie) is a subculture that developed in Jamaica in the early 1960s, among marginalised young men in the ghettos of Kingston. The term — from Jamaican patois usage meaning a delinquent or tough — came to describe youths who affected the sharp clothes and defiant style of American gangster films, listened to ska and rocksteady, and were often associated with unemployment, poverty and street violence in the years around Jamaican independence (1962).

Music and themes

Rude boy culture is inseparable from the music of the era. Ska and then rocksteady songs addressed rudies directly — celebrating, warning or lamenting them — including Prince Buster's "Judge Dread", the Wailers' "Simmer Down" (which pleaded with rude boys to cool down), Desmond Dekker's "007 (Shanty Town)" and Alton Ellis's "Dance Crasher" and "Cry Tough". Sound system dances were central social spaces, and clashes between rival rude boy gangs sometimes erupted at dances, prompting the government to impose curfews in West Kingston in 1966.

Emigration and the two-tone revival

Many rude boys emigrated to Britain with the Windrush generation's successors, where the style merged with mod fashion and influenced skinhead culture. In the late 1970s the British 2 Tone ska revival — led by bands such as The Specials, Madness, The Selecter and The Beat — consciously revived rude boy dress (tonic suits, pork pie hats, loafers) and iconography; The Specials' "A Message to You, Rudy" reworked Dandy Livingstone's 1967 song "Rudy, A Message to You", a direct address to the original rudies. The rude boy look and ethos have recurred in later ska, punk and street-style revivals.
