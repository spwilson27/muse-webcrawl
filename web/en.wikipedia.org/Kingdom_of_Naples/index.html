The Kingdom of Naples (Italian: Regno di Napoli) was a state in southern Italy that occupied the mainland portion of the former Kingdom of Sicily after its division in 1282. Despite the split, its rulers continued to style themselves kings of Sicily, and the state is sometimes called the "Kingdom of Sicily citra Pharum" (on this side of the strait).

History

The division followed the War of the Sicilian Vespers (1282), which left the island of Sicily to the Crown of Aragon while Charles I of Anjou retained the mainland. The Angevin dynasty ruled from Naples, which grew into one of Europe's great capitals. Alfonso V of Aragon reunited Naples and Sicily in 1442, but on his death the crowns separated again, Naples passing to his illegitimate son Ferdinand I.

During the Italian Wars, France and Spain contested Naples; by 1504 Spain had secured it, and the kingdom became a Spanish viceroyalty for two centuries. After the War of the Spanish Succession, Naples passed briefly to Austria, then in 1734 was conquered by Charles of Bourbon, founding the Bourbon Kingdom of Naples. Ferdinand IV fled before French Revolutionary armies, which set up the short-lived Parthenopean Republic in 1799; a Napoleonic kingdom followed under Joseph Bonaparte and Joachim Murat (1806–1815). Restored in 1815, the kingdom merged with Sicily in 1816 to form the Kingdom of the Two Sicilies, which lasted until Italian unification in 1861.

Society and culture

Naples was among Europe's largest cities and a major centre of Baroque art, music and philosophy — the home of Giordano Bruno's early career, Tommaso Campanella, and Giambattista Vico. Yet the kingdom's interior was marked by feudal landholding, brigandage, and a sharp divide between capital and countryside that persisted into the modern era.
