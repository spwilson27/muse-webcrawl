Hyperthermophile

Hot springs host hyperthermophilic bacteria and archaea.

A hyperthermophile is an organism that thrives in extremely hot environments—from 60 °C (140 °F) upward, with an optimal temperature for many species above 80 °C (176 °F). Hyperthermophiles are often within the domain Archaea, although some bacteria are able to tolerate temperatures of around 100 °C (212 °F), as well. Some of these bacteria are even able to survive temperatures above 100 °C, where water exists as a liquid under the high pressures found in deep-sea hydrothermal vents.

Record holders

The archaeon Methanopyrus kandleri strain 116 can grow at 122 °C, the highest verified growth temperature of any organism. Pyrolobus fumarii grows optimally at about 106 °C, and the famous "Strain 121" (Geogemma barossii) was reported to double for 24 hours at 121 °C near a vent in the northeastern Pacific.

Adaptations

Hyperthermophiles stabilise their proteins with extra ionic bonds and compact, highly charged surfaces; their membrane lipids contain ether linkages and, in archaea, monolayer-forming tetraethers that resist heat. Specialised enzymes such as reverse gyrase introduce positive supercoils into DNA, protecting it from melting. Many hyperthermophiles require elemental sulfur, which they reduce to hydrogen sulfide, and many are strict anaerobes because oxygen solubility is low and its reactivity high at their growth temperatures.

Habitats and applications

Natural habitats include hydrothermal vents, solfataras, hot springs, and geothermally heated subsurface rock. Hyperthermophile enzymes are industrially valuable: the DNA polymerase from the thermophile Thermus aquaticus (Taq polymerase), though from a thermophile rather than a hyperthermophile, made the polymerase chain reaction practical, and enzymes from hyperthermophiles power high-temperature industrial processes.
