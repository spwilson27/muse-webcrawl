Chia seed

Chia seeds are the edible seeds of Salvia hispanica, a flowering plant in the mint family (Lamiaceae) native to central and southern Mexico and Guatemala. The seeds are oval and grey with black and white spots, about 1–2 mm in diameter. When soaked, they absorb many times their weight in liquid and develop a mucilaginous coating that gives chia-based beverages a distinctive gel-like texture.

History

Chia was a staple crop in pre-Columbian Mexico, cultivated by the Aztecs and used in food, drink, medicine, and tribute payments. Its cultivation declined sharply after the Spanish conquest, partly because of its role in Aztec religious ceremonies. It survived in isolated farming communities and was revived commercially in the late 20th century.

Nutrition and uses

Chia seeds are rich in alpha-linolenic acid (an omega-3 fatty acid), dietary fibre, protein, and minerals such as calcium, phosphorus, and manganese. They are eaten in puddings, smoothies, baked goods, and the traditional Mexican and Central American drink agua de chía (chia fresca). Claims of dramatic health benefits are widely marketed, though clinical evidence for specific effects is limited.

Cultivation

Modern commercial production is centred in Mexico, Paraguay, Bolivia, Argentina, Peru, and Australia. The plant is day-length sensitive, flowering as days shorten, which has guided breeding of varieties for different latitudes.
