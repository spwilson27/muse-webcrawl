Māori (te reo Māori, "the Māori language") is an Eastern Polynesian language spoken by the Māori people, the indigenous population of mainland New Zealand. Closely related to Cook Islands Māori and Tahitian, it arrived with Polynesian voyagers who settled New Zealand in the 13th and 14th centuries.

History

Māori was the predominant language of New Zealand until the mid-19th century, when colonisation and English-medium schooling drove a steep decline; by the 1970s fewer than 20% of Māori children could speak it. A revitalisation movement followed, centred on kōhanga reo ("language nest") preschools from 1982 and Māori-medium schooling. The Māori Language Act 1987 made it an official language, and the 2016 Act established Te Mātāwai to lead revitalisation.

Phonology and writing

Māori has ten consonants and five vowels, each short or long; long vowels are written with macrons (ā, ē, ī, ō, ū). Syllables are open, and the language is written in a Latin-based alphabet codified in the 19th century. Stress typically falls on the first long vowel or diphthong, otherwise the first syllable.

Grammar

The language is analytic, uses particles extensively, and distinguishes two forms of possession (a- and o-categories). Word order is typically verb–subject–object. A well-known feature is the inclusive/exclusive distinction in pronouns, separating "we including you" from "we excluding you".
