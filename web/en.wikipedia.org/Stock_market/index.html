Stock market

A stock market, equity market, or share market is the aggregation of buyers and sellers of stocks — shares representing fractional ownership claims in businesses — together with the networks of exchanges and marketplaces on which those shares are issued and traded. It enables companies to raise capital from public investors and gives investors a liquid way to buy and sell ownership stakes.

How it works

Companies first sell shares to the public in an initial public offering (IPO) on the primary market; thereafter shares change hands between investors on the secondary market through stock exchanges such as the New York Stock Exchange and Nasdaq (United States), the London Stock Exchange, Euronext, the Tokyo Stock Exchange, and the Shanghai and Hong Kong exchanges. Prices are set by supply and demand, processed today overwhelmingly by electronic matching engines, with market makers and high-frequency traders supplying liquidity.

Indices and participants

Market performance is summarised by stock market indices such as the S&P 500, the Dow Jones Industrial Average, the Nasdaq Composite, the FTSE 100 and the Nikkei 225. Participants include retail investors, pension funds, mutual funds, hedge funds, and index funds, whose rise has made passive investing a dominant force. Total global market capitalisation is on the order of a hundred trillion US dollars, with the United States accounting for the largest share.

History and behaviour

The first recognisable stock market arose in Amsterdam in the early 17th century around shares of the Dutch East India Company, the first company to issue public stock. Markets have periodically experienced booms, bubbles and crashes — including the Wall Street Crash of 1929, Black Monday in 1987, the dot-com bubble, and the 2008 financial crisis. Economists debate how efficiently prices reflect information; behavioural finance documents recurring patterns of over- and under-reaction.
