Kart racing, or karting, is a road racing variant of motorsport with open-wheel, four-wheeled vehicles known as go-karts or shifter karts. They are usually raced on scaled-down circuits, although some professional kart races are held on full-size motorsport circuits. Karting is widely regarded as the first step in a racing driver's career and the purest form of motorsport.

History

The sport began in the United States in the 1950s; Art Ingels is generally credited with building the first kart in Los Angeles in 1956, using a surplus lawnmower engine on a simple tubular frame. The hobby spread rapidly, with the Go Kart Club of America founded in 1957 and karting reaching Europe by the end of the decade. The FIA created the Commission Internationale de Karting (CIK) in 1962 to govern international competition.

Karts

A racing kart consists of a steel tube chassis with no suspension and no differential, a seat mounted very low, and a small engine — historically two-stroke single-cylinder units, increasingly supplemented by four-stroke classes. Direct-drive, TaG (Touch-and-Go), and shifter karts (with sequential gearboxes) form the main categories. Top shifter karts can exceed 130 km/h and accelerate to 100 km/h in about three seconds, while rental karts at indoor tracks are heavily detuned for safety.

Competition

Karting categories are organised by age and engine class, from cadets as young as seven or eight through junior and senior classes to world championships run by the FIA. Many Formula One drivers — including Ayrton Senna, Michael Schumacher, Lewis Hamilton, and Max Verstappen — began in karts; Senna called karting the purest racing he ever experienced. Indoor rental karting is also a popular leisure activity and corporate entertainment.
