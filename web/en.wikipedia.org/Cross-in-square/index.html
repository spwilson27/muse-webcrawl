Cross-in-square

The cross-in-square plan: a dome on four columns inscribed within a square naos.

The cross-in-square plan (also known as a cross-domed or inscribed cross plan) was the dominant form of church architecture in the middle and later Byzantine Empire. It features a square naos divided by four columns or piers into nine bays; the central bay is surmounted by a dome, while the corner bays are typically covered by groin vaults or small domes, giving the interior the form of a cross inscribed within a square.

Development

The type emerged in the 8th–9th centuries, possibly developing from earlier cross-domed basilicas and centrally planned martyria. Early examples include the church of the Theotokos at Dereagzi in Lycia and the katholikon of the Great Lavra's predecessors; the mature form is represented by the Myrelaion in Constantinople (c. 920s) and, in Greece, by the great monastic churches of Hosios Loukas and Daphni (11th century), celebrated for their mosaic programmes.

Symbolism and spread

The plan expressed a hierarchical image of the cosmos: the dome, bearing Christ Pantocrator, represented heaven; the cross-shaped vaults, the created world below. Its compact, modular geometry adapted readily to parish churches and monasteries alike, and the cross-in-square spread throughout the Orthodox world — to Kievan Rus', Serbia, Bulgaria, Armenia and Georgia — and influenced later Ottoman mosque architecture through converted and imitated Byzantine models.
