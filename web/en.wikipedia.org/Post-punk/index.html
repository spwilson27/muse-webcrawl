Post-punk

Post-punk
Stylistic originsPunk rock, krautrock, dub, funk, electronic, avant-garde, disco
Cultural originsLate 1970s, United Kingdom and United States
Typical instrumentsGuitar, bass, drums, synthesiser, drum machine
Derivative formsAlternative rock, gothic rock, new wave, industrial, indie rock, dance-punk

Post-punk is a broad genre and movement of rock music that emerged in the late 1970s in the wake of punk rock. Where punk stripped rock back to basics, post-punk artists retained punk's energy and do-it-yourself ethos while experimenting with production techniques, synthesisers, and influences from dub, funk, krautrock, disco and the avant-garde. The term was used by journalists including Jon Savage and Greil Marcus to describe groups such as Siouxsie and the Banshees, Public Image Ltd and Joy Division.

Characteristics

Post-punk is characterised by angular, effects-treated guitar textures, prominent and melodic basslines, motorik or danceable drumming, and the use of studio effects such as echo and reverb as instruments in themselves. Lyrical themes often drew on modernist literature, politics, and alienation. Producers such as Martin Hannett at Factory Records helped define the spacious, atmospheric sound associated with the genre.

History

The first wave of post-punk (c. 1978–1984) included British groups such as Wire, Magazine, Gang of Four, The Cure, The Fall and Talking Heads and Pere Ubu in the United States. Post-punk fed directly into new wave, gothic rock and alternative rock. A prominent post-punk revival occurred in the early 2000s with bands such as Interpol, Franz Ferdinand, Editors and Bloc Party, followed by a further generation of groups in the late 2010s.
