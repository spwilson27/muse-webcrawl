Massif Central

The Massif Central is a highland region in south-central France consisting of mountains and plateaus covering about 15 per cent of metropolitan France. It is an old massif formed during the Variscan (Hercynian) orogeny, later uplifted and partly covered by volcanoes. Its highest point is the Puy de Sancy at 1,886 metres, the highest mountain in France outside the Alps and Pyrenees.

Geography

The massif includes the ranges and plateaus of Auvergne, the Cévennes, the Causses, Margeride, Aubrac and the Monts du Lyonnais and Forez. Major rivers including the Loire, Allier, Dordogne, Lot and Tarn rise here. The Chaîne des Puys, a chain of young volcanic cones in Auvergne, was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2018, and the Cévennes National Park protects the south-east.

Geology

The basement is largely granite and metamorphic rock of Palaeozoic age. Cenozoic volcanism produced the Cantal stratovolcano — one of Europe's largest extinct volcanoes — the Monts Dore, and the Chaîne des Puys, whose eruptions continued into the Holocene, only a few thousand years ago.

Economy and culture

Historically a poor, rural region of livestock farming, chestnuts and emigration, the Massif Central is known for cheeses such as Saint-Nectaire, Cantal, Bleu d'Auvergne and Laguiole, for the spa towns of Vichy and La Bourboule, for Michelin (founded in Clermont-Ferrand), and for cutlery from Thiers and gloves and lace from smaller centres. The Millau Viaduct crosses the Tarn gorge at its southern edge.
