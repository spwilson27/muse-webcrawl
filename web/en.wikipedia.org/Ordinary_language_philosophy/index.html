Ordinary language philosophy

Ordinary language philosophy is a philosophical methodology that approaches traditional philosophical problems by attending closely to the ordinary, everyday uses of words, on the view that many conceptual confusions arise when philosophers twist language away from its home in ordinary contexts. It flourished at Oxford in the mid-twentieth century and is associated with Gilbert Ryle, J. L. Austin, and Peter Strawson, and with the later work of Ludwig Wittgenstein at Cambridge.

Approach

Rather than constructing theories or formal languages, ordinary language philosophers mapped the "logical geography" of concepts: Ryle's The Concept of Mind (1949) diagnosed mind–body dualism as a "category mistake"; Austin developed the theory of speech acts, showing how utterances do things as well as state things; Strawson distinguished descriptive from revisionary metaphysics. Wittgenstein's slogan that "meaning is use" and his aphorism that philosophy "leaves everything as it is" supply the movement's motto.

Decline and legacy

The school waned from the 1960s as analytic philosophy turned toward systematic theory-building and as critics, including Ernest Gellner in Words and Things (1959), charged it with conservatism and mere lexicography. Its legacy persists in speech act theory, in contextualist approaches in philosophy of language, and in a lasting caution about technical redefinition of everyday terms.
