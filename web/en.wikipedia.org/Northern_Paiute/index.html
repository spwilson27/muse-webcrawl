The Northern Paiute (also spelled Paviotso) are a Native American people of the Great Basin region of the United States. Their traditional territory covers present-day central and western Nevada, eastern Oregon, southeastern Idaho, western Utah, and parts of eastern California, including the Owens Valley. They speak the Northern Paiute language, a Numic language of the Uto-Aztecan family.

Traditional life

Northern Paiute groups were organised in small, mobile bands named for a principal food resource or locality, such as the "cui-ui eaters" of Pyramid Lake. Subsistence depended on seasonally gathered seeds — especially pine nuts — roots, jackrabbits (harvested communally with nets and drives), pronghorn, and fish such as the cui-ui and Lahontan cutthroat trout. The annual pine nut harvest was also a major social occasion.

History

Contact with Euro-American settlers intensified after the California Gold Rush and the discovery of the Comstock Lode in 1859, disrupting food resources and leading to conflicts such as the Pyramid Lake War of 1860. The prophet Wovoka, a Northern Paiute from Nevada, founded the Ghost Dance movement in 1889, which spread widely among Plains peoples. Sarah Winnemucca, daughter of Chief Winnemucca, became a prominent author and advocate for her people in the 1880s.

Modern tribes

Federally recognised Northern Paiute tribes today include the Pyramid Lake Paiute Tribe, Walker River Paiute Tribe, Burns Paiute Tribe, Fort McDermitt Paiute and Shoshone Tribe, Summit Lake Paiute Tribe, and several Paiute-Shoshone communities in the Owens Valley and elsewhere, many operating reservations and colonies in Nevada, Oregon, and California.
