Abyssal fan

Abyssal fan
Type: Submarine fan
Setting: Base of continental slope
Formed by: Turbidity currents

An abyssal fan, also known as a submarine fan or deep-sea fan, is an underwater geological structure associated with large-scale sediment deposition, formed by turbidity currents. It is a fan-shaped deposit of sediment that accumulates at the base of a continental slope, typically where a submarine canyon delivers material to the deep ocean floor.

Formation

Abyssal fans form when sediment-laden turbidity currents slow as they emerge from submarine canyons onto the flatter abyssal plain, dropping their load in a pattern of distributary channels and levees. Coarse material settles first near the canyon mouth, while finer silts and clays travel farther, producing a graded, fan-like geometry.

Examples

The largest known example is the Bengal Fan in the Bay of Bengal, fed by the Ganges and Brahmaputra rivers, extending roughly 3,000 km. Other major fans include the Indus Fan, the Amazon Fan, and the Mississippi Fan in the Gulf of Mexico.

Significance

Abyssal fans are important reservoirs in petroleum geology and record the erosional history of adjacent mountain ranges and river systems.
