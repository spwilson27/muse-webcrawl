Public housing in the United States is administered by the federal government through the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD) and locally by public housing agencies (PHAs). The programme provides decent and safe rental housing for eligible low-income individuals and families, the elderly, and persons with disabilities, with rents typically set at about 30 percent of a household's adjusted gross income.

History

Federal public housing began with the Public Works Administration's housing division in 1933 and was placed on a permanent footing by the United States Housing Act of 1937 (the Wagner-Steagall Act), aimed at slum clearance and housing for the working poor. After the Second World War, the Housing Act of 1949 pledged "a decent home and suitable living environment for every American family" and authorized large-scale construction. Early developments were often segregated by race, and programmes were administered locally in ways that reinforced residential segregation.

From the 1950s through the 1970s, many cities built high-rise family developments that later became notorious for concentrated poverty and crime—Chicago's Cabrini–Green and Robert Taylor Homes, and St. Louis's Pruitt–Igoe, whose demolition began in 1972 and became a symbol of modernist public housing failure. The Brooke Amendment (1969) capped rents at a fraction of income, and the Section 8 programme (1974) shifted policy toward vouchers for private-market housing.

Modern era

The HOPE VI programme (1992–2010) demolished many distressed developments and replaced them with mixed-income communities, though usually with fewer deeply subsidised units. The Faircloth Amendment (1999) capped the number of public housing units at 1999 levels, effectively prohibiting net growth of the stock. Since 2012, the Rental Assistance Demonstration (RAD) has allowed PHAs to convert public housing to long-term Section 8 contracts to leverage private financing for repairs; the nationwide capital-needs backlog has been estimated in the tens of billions of dollars. The largest agency, the New York City Housing Authority (NYCHA), houses several hundred thousand residents.

Criticism and debate

Public housing has been debated throughout its history: criticised for concentrating poverty, for racial discrimination in siting and admissions, and for underfunding, but defended as an essential source of stable, affordable housing in tight markets. Court cases such as Hills v. Gautreaux (1976) addressed segregation and led to mobility programmes that informed later housing-voucher research.
