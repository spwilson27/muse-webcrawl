Quantity theory of money

The quantity theory of money is a theory from monetary economics which states that the general price level of goods and services is directly proportional to the amount of money in circulation (the money supply), or in other words that inflation is directly proportional to the supply of money in an economy. The theory is based on the equation of exchange:

M × V = P × Q

where M is the nominal money supply, V is the velocity of money (the average frequency with which a unit of money is spent), P is the price level, and Q is an index of real expenditures (the volume of transactions or real output).

Development

Early formulations appear in the work of Nicolaus Copernicus and Martín de Azpilcueta in the 16th century, prompted by the influx of American silver and the resulting European price inflation. David Hume gave the theory a classical statement in the 18th century. In the early 20th century Irving Fisher formalised the equation of exchange. John Maynard Keynes challenged the theory in the short run, arguing that velocity is unstable and prices sticky; monetarism, led by Milton Friedman, later revived the theory, asserting that "inflation is always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon."

Criticism

Critics argue that velocity is not constant, that the causality may run from prices to money rather than the reverse, and that in the short run changes in the money supply can affect real output and employment rather than only prices. Nevertheless, the long-run correlation between money growth and inflation across countries remains a central empirical regularity in monetary economics.
