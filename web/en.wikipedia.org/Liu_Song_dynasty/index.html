The Liu Song dynasty, also known simply as the Song dynasty (420–479) — no relation to the later Song dynasty of 960–1279 — was the first of the four Southern dynasties of China during the Northern and Southern dynasties period. It ruled the south from its capital at Jiankang (modern Nanjing) and at its height controlled territory up to the Huai River and beyond into the Yellow River valley.

Founding

The dynasty was founded by Liu Yu, a general of humble birth who rose through the Northern Garrisons Army of the Eastern Jin. After suppressing the rebellion of Huan Xuan and leading successful northern expeditions that recovered Luoyang and Chang'an, Liu Yu deposed the last Jin emperor and took the throne in 420 as Emperor Wu. His conquests marked the northernmost extent reached by any southern regime of the period.

The Yuanjia era and northern wars

Under Emperor Wen (reigned 424–453), the dynasty enjoyed the prosperous "Yuanjia era" of relative stability, administrative reform, and cultural flourishing. Emperor Wen launched three major northern expeditions against the Northern Wei in 430, 450, and 452, seeking to recover the Central Plain. The 450 invasion ended disastrously when the Tuoba Wei counterattacked deep into Song territory, reaching Guabu on the Yangtze opposite the capital before withdrawing. The wars drained the treasury and are widely seen as the turning point of the dynasty's fortunes.

Decline and fall

The later decades of Liu Song were marred by savage succession struggles. Emperor Xiaowu and especially Emperor Ming purged the imperial clan on a large scale, while the tyrannical Liu Ziye (Emperor Qianfei) was assassinated in 465. Power progressively fell to the general Xiao Daocheng, who in 479 forced the last emperor, Shun, to abdicate and founded the Southern Qi dynasty; the deposed Liu Song imperial family was largely exterminated.

Culture

The Liu Song period saw major achievements in literature and scholarship: the historian Fan Ye compiled the Book of the Later Han, Pei Songzhi produced his celebrated annotated edition of the Records of the Three Kingdoms, and the poet Xie Lingyun pioneered landscape poetry. Buddhism continued to grow under imperial patronage, and the capital Jiankang was among the largest cities in the world.
