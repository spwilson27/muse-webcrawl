Meissner effect

Meissner effect
Discovered: 1933
Discoverers: Walther Meissner, Robert Ochsenfeld
Domain: superconductivity, condensed matter physics

The Meissner effect (also known as the Meissner–Ochsenfeld effect) is the expulsion of a magnetic field from a superconductor during its transition to the superconducting state when it is cooled below its critical temperature. The effect was discovered by the German physicists Walther Meissner and Robert Ochsenfeld in 1933 and demonstrated that superconductors are not merely perfect conductors but exhibit perfect diamagnetism.


Magnetic levitation from flux expulsion

Description

When a material becomes superconducting in an applied magnetic field weaker than a critical value, surface currents arise that exactly cancel the magnetic flux in the interior, so the field penetrates only within a thin layer characterized by the London penetration depth (typically tens to hundreds of nanometres). This distinguishes a superconductor from a hypothetical perfect conductor, in which flux present before cooling would be frozen in. The effect underlies the levitation of magnets above superconductors, though stable levitation also involves flux pinning in type-II superconductors.

Theory

Fritz and Heinz London's phenomenological equations (1935) reproduced the effect by postulating that the electromagnetic free energy of a superconductor is minimized when the field is expelled. The microscopic BCS theory (1957) explained it in terms of Cooper pairs forming a coherent quantum state. In type-II superconductors, above a lower critical field the Meissner state gives way to a mixed state containing quantized vortices (Abrikosov vortices) that each carry one flux quantum.

Applications

The Meissner effect is used in sensitive magnetometers such as SQUIDs, in magnetic levitation demonstrations and bearings, and as a standard test for superconductivity in new materials, including the high-temperature cuprate superconductors discovered from 1986 onward.
