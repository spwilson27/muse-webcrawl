Axial precession

In astronomy, axial precession is a gravity-induced, slow, and continuous change in the orientation of an astronomical body's rotational axis. In the case of Earth, the axis traces out a cone over a cycle of approximately 26,000 years, a phenomenon also known as the precession of the equinoxes. Over this cycle the direction in which the axis points moves relative to the fixed stars, changing which star serves as the pole star and slowly shifting the positions of the equinoxes along the ecliptic.

History

The precession of the equinoxes was discovered by the Greek astronomer Hipparchus in the 2nd century BC, by comparing his observations of star positions with records from earlier centuries. Isaac Newton later explained the phenomenon physically as the effect of the gravitational pull of the Sun and Moon on Earth's equatorial bulge.

Cause

Earth is not a perfect sphere but an oblate spheroid, bulging at the equator. The gravitational forces of the Sun and Moon act on this bulge, producing a torque that tends to align Earth's axis with the axis of the ecliptic. Because Earth is rotating, the response to this torque is a slow conical motion of the axis — precession — rather than a simple tipping.

Effects

Axial precession causes the vernal equinox to drift westward along the ecliptic by about 50.3 arcseconds per year, so the tropical year differs slightly from the sidereal year. It also means that the celestial poles wander: Thuban was the pole star in ancient Egyptian times, Polaris is the current northern pole star, and in about 12,000 years Vega will lie near the north celestial pole. Together with changes in eccentricity and axial tilt, precession is one of the Milankovitch cycles influencing long-term climate.
