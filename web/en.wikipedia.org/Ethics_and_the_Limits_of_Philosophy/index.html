Ethics and the Limits of Philosophy (1985) is a book by the British philosopher Bernard Williams. It is widely regarded as one of the most important works of moral philosophy of the late 20th century, offering a sustained critique of systematic ethical theory—especially utilitarianism and Kantian morality—and of what Williams called "the morality system".

Argument

Williams argues that ethical thought does not need, and cannot have, the kind of theoretical foundation philosophers have sought. He defends a conception of ethical knowledge modelled on history rather than science, emphasises "thick" ethical concepts such as treachery and brutality that combine description and evaluation, and contends that moral obligations have been given an inflated, distorting centrality in modern moral philosophy. The book closes with a sympathetic but critical discussion of Nietzsche.

Reception

The work helped revive interest in virtue ethics, moral particularism, and the history of ethics, and it remains a standard reference point in debates about moral theory, moral luck, and the place of reflection in ethical life.
