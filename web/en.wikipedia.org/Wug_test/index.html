Wug test

A wug: "Now there are two of them. There are two ___."

The wug test is a classic experiment in child language acquisition devised by psycholinguist Jean Berko (later Jean Berko Gleason) and reported in 1958. It demonstrates that young children have internalized the morphological rules of their language — such as how to form plurals and past tenses — rather than merely memorizing word forms.

The experiment

Berko presented children aged four to seven with cards showing invented creatures and objects bearing nonsense names, then prompted them to supply the missing inflected form. In the most famous item, a drawing of a small bird-like creature is captioned: "This is a wug. Now there is another one. There are two of them. There are two ___." Children reliably answered "wugs" — pronouncing the plural with a /z/ sound, the correct allomorph even though they had never heard the word before.

Other items probed past tense ("This man knows how to spow. He is spowing. Yesterday he ___"), possessives, comparatives, and compound words. Performance improved with age: preschoolers handled common plural and progressive forms well, while third-person singular, past tense, and less frequent allomorphs were mastered later.

Significance

The study was a landmark in developmental psycholinguistics and in the debate over language acquisition: children's productive use of rules with novel words showed that grammar cannot be acquired by imitation alone, supporting arguments for rule-based learning associated with Noam Chomsky's critique of behaviourism. The wug has since become an icon of linguistics, appearing on merchandise of the Linguistic Society of America, and wug-style (nonce-word) tests remain standard tools in research on language development, impairment, and second-language learning.
