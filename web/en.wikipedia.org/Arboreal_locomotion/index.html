Arboreal locomotion

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Arboreal locomotion is the locomotion of animals in trees. In habitats in which trees are present, animals have evolved to move in them. Some animals may scale trees only occasionally (scansorial species), but others are exclusively arboreal, never descending to the ground. Trees present mechanical challenges — narrow and discontinuous supports, the need to ascend against gravity, and the risk of falling — and arboreal animals show a wide range of anatomical and behavioural solutions, including grasping extremities, prehensile tails, claws, adhesive pads, and gliding membranes.

Biomechanics

Arboreal substrates are typically small relative to the animal's body, angled in all directions, and separated by gaps. Arboreal mammals tend to have longer limbs, mobile joints, and lower centres of mass relative to their terrestrial relatives; many use diagonal-sequence gaits that keep the body's mass balanced over the supports. Falling is a major selective pressure: small animals may survive falls by virtue of low terminal velocity, while larger arboreal animals favour cautious, grasping locomotion or controlled gliding and parachuting.

Forms

  • Quadrupedal climbing and walking — used by most arboreal primates, opossums, and squirrels, often with grasping hands and feet.
  • Brachiation — hand-over-hand swinging beneath branches, specialised in gibbons and, to a lesser degree, spider monkeys with prehensile tails.
  • Vertical clinging and leaping — used by tarsiers, galagos, and indriids, with elongated hindlimbs for propulsion between trunks.
  • Bridging and cantilevering — the animal reaches across a gap while anchored by hindlimbs or tail.
  • Gliding — flying squirrels, colugos, gliding possums, Draco lizards, and some frogs extend membranes or flattened bodies to travel between trees.
  • Undulatory climbing — snakes and some lizards use lateral undulation or concertina movement against bark irregularities.

Adaptations

Recurring adaptations include opposable thumbs or big toes, nails replacing claws (in primates) or sharp recurved claws (in squirrels and sloths), friction-enhancing skin ridges and adhesive toe pads (in geckos and tree frogs), binocular vision for judging gaps, and tails used as balancers, fifth limbs, or stores of fat. Sloths exemplify extreme specialisation, hanging inverted beneath branches with hook-like limbs and moving so slowly that algae grow in their fur.
