David Lewis (philosopher)

David Kellogg Lewis (28 September 1941 – 14 October 2001) was an American philosopher, widely regarded as one of the most important analytic philosophers of the twentieth century. He taught at Princeton University for most of his career. Lewis is best known for his defense of modal realism — the view that all possible worlds are as real as the actual world — and for influential work on causation, counterfactuals, conventions, and the metaphysics of mind.

Life

Lewis was born in Oberlin, Ohio, studied chemistry and then philosophy at Swarthmore College, and completed his PhD at Harvard in 1967 under W. V. O. Quine. After a year at UCLA he joined Princeton in 1970, remaining there until his death from complications of diabetes in 2001. He visited Australia frequently and was embraced by Australian philosophy as one of its own.

Philosophy

In Convention (1969) Lewis used game theory to explain how languages can be governed by conventions without explicit agreement. Counterfactuals (1973) proposed a possible-worlds semantics for conditional statements based on comparative similarity between worlds. On the Plurality of Worlds (1986) systematically defended modal realism, arguing that a plenitude of concrete possible worlds, unified by what he called Humean supervenience, provides the best theoretical account of modality, causation, probability, and content. His counterpart theory treated individuals as world-bound, with counterparts in other worlds standing in for de re modal claims.

Influence

Lewis's systematic, theory-driven style set an agenda across metaphysics, philosophy of language, and philosophy of science; his collected papers run to five volumes. A well-known remark by Robert Stalnaker captured his standing: many regarded Lewis simply as the most important philosopher of his generation.
