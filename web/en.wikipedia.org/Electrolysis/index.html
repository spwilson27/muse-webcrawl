Electrolysis

In chemistry and manufacturing, electrolysis is a technique that uses direct electric current (DC) to drive an otherwise non-spontaneous chemical reaction. Electrolysis is commercially important as a stage in the separation of elements from naturally occurring sources, and in electroplating and the production of industrial chemicals.

Electrolysis of water produces hydrogen at the cathode and oxygen at the anode in a 2:1 volume ratio.

Process

An electrolyte containing mobile ions is placed between two electrodes connected to a DC source. Cations migrate to the cathode, where they gain electrons (reduction); anions migrate to the anode, where they lose electrons (oxidation). The minimum voltage required is set by the thermodynamics of the reaction, described by the Nernst equation, plus overpotentials.

Faraday's laws

Michael Faraday, working in the 1830s, formulated two laws: the mass of a substance altered at an electrode is proportional to the quantity of electricity passed, and the masses of different substances altered by the same quantity of electricity are proportional to their equivalent weights.

Applications

Electrolysis is used to extract aluminium (Hall–Héroult process), sodium, and chlorine and sodium hydroxide (chloralkali process); to produce hydrogen from water; for electrorefining of copper; and in electroplating. William Nicholson and Anthony Carlisle performed the first electrolysis of water in 1800, soon after Volta's invention of the pile.
