Golden age of alpinism

The golden age of alpinism was the decade or so, roughly 1854 to 1865, during which most of the major peaks of the Alps received their first ascents. It is conventionally dated from Alfred Wills's ascent of the Wetterhorn in 1854 to Edward Whymper's first ascent of the Matterhorn in 1865.

Overview

The era was dominated by British climbers of the Alpine Club (founded 1857), working with Swiss, French, and Italian professional guides such as Michel Croz, Christian Almer, and Melchior Anderegg. Peaks climbed for the first time in the period include the Eiger (1858), Mont Blanc's later routes, Monte Rosa's highest summit (1855), the Barre des Écrins (1864), and the Matterhorn. Motivations mixed scientific inquiry, sport, and the Victorian spirit of exploration.

End of the era

The golden age closed with the triumph and tragedy of the Matterhorn ascent of 14 July 1865, in which four of Whymper's party fell to their deaths on the descent — an event that brought public criticism (including from Queen Victoria's circle) and marked a psychological turning point. There followed a "silver age" of ascents of remaining lesser summits and harder routes, lasting until the outbreak of the First World War redirected alpinism toward the Greater Ranges.
