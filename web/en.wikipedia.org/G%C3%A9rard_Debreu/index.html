Gérard Debreu (4 July 1921 – 31 December 2004) was a French-born American economist and mathematician. He is best known for his work on general equilibrium theory, in particular the rigorous proof, with Kenneth Arrow, of the existence of a competitive equilibrium in a market economy. In 1983 he received the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences for his reformulation of the theory of general equilibrium.

Early life and education

Debreu was born in Calais and studied mathematics at the École Normale Supérieure in Paris, where his studies were interrupted by the Second World War. After the war he turned to economics, influenced by the work of Léon Walras and by Maurice Allais. He joined the Cowles Commission in the United States in 1950 and later held posts at Yale University and the University of California, Berkeley, where he spent most of his career.

Work

His monograph Theory of Value: An Axiomatic Analysis of Economic Equilibrium (1959) presented general equilibrium theory in a fully axiomatic form based on set theory and topology. With Kenneth Arrow he published the landmark 1954 paper "Existence of an Equilibrium for a Competitive Economy" in Econometrica. Later work with Herbert Scarf and Hugo Sonnenschein explored the limits of what equilibrium analysis can determine, culminating in results associated with the Sonnenschein–Mantel–Debreu theorem.

Honours

Debreu became a United States citizen in 1975. He was made a Chevalier of the French Legion of Honour and served as president of the Econometric Society and of the American Economic Association. He died in Paris on New Year's Eve 2004.
