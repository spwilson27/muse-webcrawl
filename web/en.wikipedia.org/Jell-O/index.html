Jell-O


Jell-O is a brand of powdered gelatin dessert mixes and pre-made gelatin snacks owned by the Kraft Heinz Company.

Jell-O is a variety of prepackaged powdered gelatin dessert (fruit-flavored gels) and related products owned by the Kraft Heinz Company and based in the United States. It is perhaps the best-known brand of gelatin desserts; variants include instant pudding, prepared cups, and no-bake cream pies.

The gelatin product was originally a trademark of the Genesee Pure Food Company in Le Roy, New York, which obtained the trademark in 1897. Jell-O was promoted heavily through the 20th century with advertising campaigns, recipe booklets, and sponsorships, making the name a genericized shorthand for gelatin desserts in the United States.

History

Gelatin had been known as a food ingredient since the 15th century, but it was slow and difficult to prepare in the home. In 1845, Peter Cooper obtained a patent for industrial manufacture of powdered gelatin. Half a century later, Pearle Bixby Wait, a carpenter and cough medicine manufacturer in Le Roy, New York, and his wife May added fruit flavouring to powdered gelatin and called the result "Jell-O." The trademark was registered in 1897.

Unable to market the product themselves, the Waits sold the business in 1899 to Orator Woodward's Genesee Pure Food Company for $450. Aggressive advertising — including free recipe booklets and door-to-door salespeople — turned Jell-O into a household staple. The brand became part of General Foods in 1925 through the merger of Postum and the Jell-O Company, and General Foods was later acquired by Philip Morris, which combined it with Kraft.

Products

Jell-O is sold as a powder that is dissolved in hot water, combined with cold water, and refrigerated to set. The original flavours included strawberry, raspberry, orange, and lemon; lime was added in 1930. Sugar-free versions are sweetened with aspartame. The brand also produces instant pudding mixes, prepared gelatin and pudding snack cups, no-bake pie mixes, and other desserts.

In popular culture

Jell-O became an icon of mid-20th-century American cuisine, appearing in moulded "Jell-O salads" combining gelatin with fruits, vegetables, or cottage cheese. The Jell-O Museum in Le Roy, New York, commemorates the brand, and Utah, where per-capita consumption was famously high, named Jell-O its official state snack food in 2001.
