Otto Dix

Wilhelm Heinrich Otto Dix (2 December 1891 – 25 July 1969) was a German painter and printmaker known for his ruthless, unsentimental depictions of Weimar society and the brutality of war. Associated with the New Objectivity (Neue Sachlichkeit) movement, he is one of the most important German artists of the 20th century.

War experience

Dix volunteered for the German Army in the First World War and served as a machine-gunner on the Western and Eastern fronts, an experience that marked him for life. His 1924 print cycle Der Krieg (The War), fifty etchings of corpses, craters and gas attacks, is compared with Goya's Disasters of War.

Weimar work

In the 1920s Dix painted the nightlife, street scenes and portraits of the Weimar Republic with a harsh, glazed old-master technique: works such as Portrait of the Journalist Sylvia von Harden (1926), the triptych Metropolis (1928) and The Skat Players. His great triptych The War (1929–32) is now in Dresden.

Later life

The Nazis dismissed Dix from his Dresden professorship in 1933 and condemned his work as "degenerate". He withdrew into "inner emigration" at Lake Constance, painting landscapes and religious allegories. Conscripted into the Volkssturm in 1945, he was briefly a French prisoner of war. He died in Singen in 1969.
