Crush syndrome

Crush syndrome (also known as traumatic rhabdomyolysis or Bywaters' syndrome) is a medical condition characterised by major shock and acute kidney injury after a crushing injury to skeletal muscle. It is the systemic manifestation of muscle cell breakdown — rhabdomyolysis — caused by prolonged compression, as occurs when casualties are trapped under collapsed buildings after earthquakes or bombings.

Pathophysiology

When the compressive force is released, damaged muscle cells release potassium, myoglobin, phosphate and acids into the circulation. The sudden potassium load can cause fatal cardiac arrhythmia, while myoglobin clogs the renal tubules and, together with hypovolaemia and acidosis, precipitates acute kidney failure — historically the main cause of death among rescued crush victims who initially appeared stable.

Treatment

Management centres on aggressive intravenous fluid resuscitation, begun where possible before or during extrication, to maintain urine output and dilute nephrotoxins; urine may be alkalinised and mannitol given. Hyperkalaemia is treated as an emergency, and dialysis may be required. The syndrome was described systematically by Eric Bywaters from casualties of the London Blitz in 1941.
