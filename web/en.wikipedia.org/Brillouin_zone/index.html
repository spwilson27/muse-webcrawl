Brillouin zone

The first Brillouin zone (red square) of a 2D square reciprocal lattice, with higher zones indicated.

In mathematics and solid state physics, the first Brillouin zone (pronounced [bʁijwɛ̃]) is a uniquely defined primitive cell in reciprocal space. In the same way the Wigner–Seitz cell is defined for the direct lattice, the Brillouin zone is the Wigner–Seitz cell of the reciprocal lattice: the set of points in reciprocal space that are closer to the origin than to any other reciprocal lattice point. The zones are named after the French physicist Léon Brillouin.

Definition and higher zones

The first Brillouin zone is bounded by planes that are the perpendicular bisectors of the reciprocal lattice vectors nearest the origin. The n-th Brillouin zone is defined analogously as the set of points in reciprocal space that are the n-th nearest to the origin — that is, points reached by crossing exactly n − 1 Bragg planes from the origin. All Brillouin zones of a given lattice have the same volume, equal to the volume of the reciprocal primitive cell.

Importance

The Brillouin zone is central to the theory of waves in periodic media. By Bloch's theorem, the wave vector k of an electron (or any wave) in a crystal can always be taken to lie within the first Brillouin zone, because adding any reciprocal lattice vector gives an equivalent state. Electronic band structures are therefore always plotted along paths inside the first Brillouin zone, connecting high-symmetry points conventionally labelled Γ (the zone centre), X, M, L and others depending on the lattice type. The shape of the zone is determined by the crystal symmetry: it is a hexagon for a triangular lattice in two dimensions, and a truncated octahedron for a body-centred cubic lattice and a rhombic dodecahedron for a face-centred cubic lattice in three dimensions.
