Acute kidney injury

Other nameAcute renal failure (older term)
SpecialtyNephrology
CausesReduced blood flow, sepsis, nephrotoxic drugs, urinary obstruction
DiagnosisRising creatinine; urine output < 0.5 mL/kg/h
TreatmentTreat cause, fluids, stop nephrotoxins, dialysis if severe

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a sudden episode of kidney failure or damage that develops within a few hours or days, causing waste products to accumulate in the blood and making it difficult for the kidneys to maintain fluid, electrolyte and acid–base balance. It replaced the older term "acute renal failure" to reflect a spectrum of severity. AKI is common in hospitalised patients, especially the critically ill, and is associated with increased mortality and later chronic kidney disease.

Causes and classification

Causes are traditionally grouped as prerenal (reduced blood flow to the kidneys from dehydration, blood loss, heart failure or sepsis), intrinsic (direct damage — acute tubular necrosis, glomerulonephritis, interstitial nephritis, or toxins such as aminoglycoside antibiotics, contrast agents and rhabdomyolysis), and postrenal (obstruction of urine flow, e.g. by an enlarged prostate or kidney stones). The KDIGO criteria stage AKI by the rise in serum creatinine and the fall in urine output.

Management

Treatment focuses on identifying and reversing the cause: careful intravenous fluids for volume depletion, stopping nephrotoxic and renally cleared drugs, treating infection, and relieving obstruction. Severe cases with refractory fluid overload, hyperkalaemia, acidosis or uraemic complications require renal replacement therapy (dialysis). Many patients recover kidney function, but AKI increases the long-term risk of chronic kidney disease.
