Midnight sun

The midnight sun is a natural phenomenon that occurs in the summer months in places north of the Arctic Circle or south of the Antarctic Circle, when the Sun remains visible at local midnight. Given good weather, the Sun is visible for a continuous 24 hours, mostly north of the Arctic Circle and south of the Antarctic Circle.

Cause

The phenomenon is caused by the axial tilt of the Earth (about 23.4°) relative to its orbital plane. During the summer of each hemisphere, the corresponding pole is tilted toward the Sun, so the Sun never fully sets over the polar cap; instead it circles the sky, dipping toward the horizon at night but never below it. The opposite pole experiences the polar night at the same time.

Duration and geography

At the poles themselves the Sun rises and sets only once per year, giving roughly six months of continuous daylight. At the Arctic Circle the midnight sun occurs for about a single night around the summer solstice, with the duration increasing toward the pole: roughly a month at 70° latitude and about 2.5 months at 78° (Svalbard). Countries and territories experiencing the midnight sun include Norway (which markets itself as the "Land of the Midnight Sun"), Sweden, Finland, Iceland, northern Russia, Canada's northern territories, Greenland and Alaska (notably Utqiaġvik, where the Sun does not set for about 84 days).

Effects

Continuous daylight disrupts sleep and circadian rhythms for many residents, blackout curtains being standard in northern homes, but it also enables extended outdoor activity, festivals such as the Midnight Sun Marathon in Tromsø, and round-the-clock agriculture during the short northern growing season.
