Mutual intelligibility

In linguistics, mutual intelligibility is a relationship between languages or dialects in which speakers of different but related varieties can readily understand each other without prior study or special effort. It is often used as a practical criterion for distinguishing languages from dialects, though politics and identity frequently override it.

Characteristics

Intelligibility can be asymmetric: speakers of one variety may understand another better than the reverse — Portuguese speakers, for example, generally understand Spanish more easily than Spanish speakers understand Portuguese. Intelligibility also varies between spoken and written forms: the Chinese "dialects" are largely mutually unintelligible when spoken but share a written standard, while Hindi and Urdu are colloquially near-identical yet use different scripts.

Examples

  • Norwegian, Danish, and Swedish form a mutually intelligible mainland Scandinavian group.
  • Serbian, Croatian, Bosnian, and Montenegrin are highly mutually intelligible but counted as separate languages.
  • Czech and Slovak retain a high degree of mutual intelligibility.
  • Dutch and Afrikaans are partially mutually intelligible.

Dialect continua

Across a dialect continuum, neighbouring varieties are intelligible to each other while distant ones are not, making any language boundary inherently fuzzy. Standardization, education, and media exposure can raise intelligibility between varieties over time.
