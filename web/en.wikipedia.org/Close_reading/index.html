Close reading

In literary criticism, close reading is the careful, sustained interpretation of a brief passage of a text in order to attend to the details of language, form and meaning. A truly close reading attends to individual words, syntax, imagery, rhythm and the order in which sentences and ideas unfold, treating the passage's particulars as the ground of interpretation.

Origins

The practice is associated with I. A. Richards, whose Practical Criticism (1929) presented students with unattributed poems to analyse without contextual information, and with the American New Critics of the 1930s–1950s — Cleanth Brooks, John Crowe Ransom, W. K. Wimsatt — who argued that a literary work should be understood primarily through its internal structure rather than through biography or history. Brooks's "heresy of paraphrase" held that a poem's meaning cannot be separated from its form.

Later developments

Although New Criticism fell from dominance, close reading survived as a core skill across later schools: structuralism, deconstruction (which pursued it to the point of exposing textual contradictions), feminist and postcolonial criticism, and legal and theological hermeneutics all employ versions of it. Critics of the method argue it can neglect historical context, readers and the social conditions of production; more recent movements such as "surface reading" and "distant reading" propose alternatives or complements.

In education

Close reading is widely taught in schools as a literacy strategy, involving repeated reading of a complex text with annotation and text-dependent questions.
