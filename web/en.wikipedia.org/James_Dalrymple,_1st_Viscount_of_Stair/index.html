James Dalrymple, 1st Viscount of Stair (May 1619 – 29 November 1695) was a Scottish lawyer, statesman and writer on law, regarded as the founding figure of Scots law as a systematic discipline. His Institutions of the Law of Scotland (1681) remains an authoritative institutional source of Scots law.

Life

Dalrymple was born at Drummurchie in Carrick, Ayrshire, and educated at the University of Glasgow, where he graduated in 1637 and lectured in philosophy. During the Civil War he served as secretary to the Earl of Glencairn's expedition in 1653–54 against the Commonwealth. After the Restoration of Charles II he was admitted as an advocate (1661), knighted, and rose rapidly: he became a Lord of Session in 1670 and Lord President of the Court of Session in 1671.

Stair resigned the presidency in 1681 rather than enforce Charles II's controversial Test Act against his Presbyterian convictions, and went into exile in the Netherlands, where his Institutions were published. Accused of complicity in the Rye House Plot, he returned to Scotland only after the Glorious Revolution of 1688. William III made him Lord Advocate and restored him as Lord President in 1689; he was created Viscount of Stair in 1690 and played a central role in the Revolution settlement in Scotland and in securing the Claim of Right.

The Institutions

The Institutions of the Law of Scotland, first published in Edinburgh in 1681, organized Scots law according to principles drawn from Roman law, feudal law and natural law theory, influenced by Grotius and continental jurisprudence. It is conventionally cited as an institutional writer's work, ranking as a formal source of law in Scotland, and later editions (notably 1693) remain in use. Stair's philosophical writings include Physiologia Nova Experimentalis (1686), a work of natural philosophy.

Family and legacy

Stair founded one of Scotland's great political dynasties: his son John Dalrymple, 1st Earl of Stair, was a principal architect of the Union of 1707, though the family's reputation was darkened by the Massacre of Glencoe (1692), for which the younger Stair bore political responsibility. The elder Stair died in Edinburgh in 1695.
