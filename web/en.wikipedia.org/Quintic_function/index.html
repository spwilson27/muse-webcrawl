In mathematics, a quintic function is a polynomial function of degree five, defined by g(x) = ax⁵ + bx⁴ + cx³ + dx² + ex + f, where a ≠ 0 and the coefficients belong to a specified field or ring, commonly the real or complex numbers. The corresponding equation g(x) = 0 is a quintic equation. Quintics occupy a famous place in the history of algebra: unlike quadratic, cubic, and quartic equations, the general quintic cannot be solved by radicals, a fact whose proof launched modern group theory.

Solvability

Formulas using only arithmetic operations and root extractions (radicals) exist for degrees up to four: the quadratic formula, Cardano's solution of the cubic (1545), and Ferrari's solution of the quartic. For two and a half centuries mathematicians searched for a quintic formula. Paolo Ruffini published an incomplete proof of impossibility in 1799; Niels Henrik Abel completed the proof in 1824, and the result is known as the Abel–Ruffini theorem. Évariste Galois then gave the definitive criterion: a polynomial equation is solvable by radicals if and only if its Galois group is a solvable group. For the general quintic the Galois group is the symmetric group S₅, which is not solvable because the alternating group A₅ is simple and non-abelian.

Solvable quintics

Particular quintics can still be solved by radicals. A quintic is solvable exactly when its Galois group is a subgroup of the Frobenius group of order 20 (the group of affine transformations x → ax + b over the field with five elements). Examples include de Moivre-type quintics such as x⁵ + 5ax⁴ − ... special families, and trivial cases like x⁵ − x = 0. John Stuart Glashan, George Paxton Young, and Carl Runge gave an explicit criterion in 1885: an irreducible quintic in Bring–Jerrard form is solvable precisely when it has a parametrised form with rational parameters satisfying a certain identity.

Bring–Jerrard form and elliptic functions

By Tschirnhaus transformations any quintic can be reduced to the Bring–Jerrard form x⁵ + px + q = 0. Although radicals do not suffice, Charles Hermite showed in 1858 that the quintic can be solved using elliptic modular functions, a method later simplified with the icosahedral symmetry of A₅ by Felix Klein in his Lectures on the Icosahedron (1884). In practice, roots are found numerically — every real quintic has at least one real root, and up to five — and special functions such as the Bring radical provide a closed form.

Applications

Quintic polynomials appear in computer-aided design and robotics: quintic splines give smooth trajectories with continuous position, velocity, and acceleration at the endpoints. In celestial mechanics and algebra, quintics arise in problems from the geometry of the icosahedron to the theory of elliptic curves.
