Algebraic group

AreaAlgebraic geometry, group theory
ExamplesGeneral linear group, elliptic curves, additive group

In mathematics, an algebraic group is an algebraic variety endowed with a group structure in which the operations of multiplication and inversion are given by regular functions on the variety.

Algebraic groups are the natural group objects in algebraic geometry and play a role analogous to Lie groups in differential geometry. Over the complex numbers, every algebraic group is a complex Lie group. The theory of algebraic groups was developed by, among others, Claude Chevalley, Armand Borel, and Jacques Tits, and is fundamental in modern number theory, representation theory, and geometry.

Definitions and examples

Formally, an algebraic group over a field k is a variety G over k together with morphisms m: G × G → G (multiplication) and i: G → G (inversion), and a distinguished k-point e (the identity), such that the group axioms are satisfied. Basic examples include:

  • The additive group Ga: the affine line with addition as the operation.
  • The multiplicative group Gm: the nonzero elements of the affine line under multiplication.
  • The general linear group GLn: invertible n × n matrices, an open subset of affine n²-space.
  • Elliptic curves: smooth projective curves of genus one with a group law defined geometrically by the chord-and-tangent construction.

Classification

A fundamental distinction is between linear algebraic groups, which can be embedded in some GLn, and abelian varieties, which are complete (projective) algebraic groups. Chevalley's structure theorem states that any algebraic group over a perfect field fits into a short exact sequence with an abelian variety quotient and a linear algebraic group subgroup.

Connected linear algebraic groups are classified further into tori, unipotent groups, and semisimple groups. Reductive groups — those with trivial unipotent radical — include GLn, SLn, and the orthogonal and symplectic groups. Chevalley showed that connected reductive groups over an algebraically closed field are classified by root data, a combinatorial object combining root and weight lattices.

Applications

Algebraic groups underpin the Langlands program, the theory of automorphic forms, and geometric invariant theory. Finite groups of Lie type — obtained by taking rational points of algebraic groups over finite fields — give rise to many of the finite simple groups.
