Toxin

Structure of a bacterial toxin

A toxin is a naturally occurring, poisonous substance produced within living cells or organisms. The term was first used by organic chemist Ludwig Brieger (1849–1919), derived from the word toxic. Toxins can be small molecules, peptides or proteins that are capable of causing disease on contact with or absorption by body tissues by interacting with biological macromolecules such as enzymes or cellular receptors.

Classification

Toxins vary greatly in their severity, ranging from usually minor (such as a bee sting) to potentially fatal (such as botulinum toxin). Bacterial toxins are divided into exotoxins, secreted by bacteria, and endotoxins, which are components of the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria released when the cell is disrupted. Plant toxins (phytotoxins) include ricin from castor beans and many alkaloids, while animal toxins underlie venoms delivered by snakes, spiders and marine organisms; those delivered by injection are often specifically termed venoms. Mycotoxins, produced by fungi, include aflatoxins contaminating stored grain.

Uses and countermeasures

In medicine, attenuated or inactivated toxins called toxoids are used as vaccines — the diphtheria and tetanus vaccines are prominent examples — and botulinum toxin has therapeutic and cosmetic applications in controlled microdoses. Antitoxins, antibodies raised against specific toxins, are used to treat envenomation and bacterial toxinoses. In biological research, toxins such as tetrodotoxin and saxitoxin are valuable tools for studying ion channels and receptors. Although synthetic poisons are sometimes loosely called toxins in popular usage, the term properly refers to substances of biological origin.
