Airship

An airship or dirigible is a type of aerostat that can navigate through the air under its own power. Airships gain lift from a large gas envelope filled with a lifting gas less dense than the surrounding air — historically hydrogen, today almost always helium. Unlike balloons, airships have engines, propellers, and control surfaces for steering.

Types

Rigid airships, such as the German Zeppelins, have an internal framework holding the envelope's shape. Semi-rigid airships combine a partial keel with pressure from the gas. Non-rigid airships, or blimps, rely entirely on internal pressure; the Goodyear blimps are famous examples used for advertising and aerial broadcasting.

History

Airships were the first aircraft capable of controlled, powered flight. Henri Giffard flew a steam-powered airship in 1852. In the early twentieth century, Count Ferdinand von Zeppelin's rigid ships pioneered passenger air travel, and German airships bombed Britain in the First World War. The 1920s and 1930s were the golden age of giant passenger airships, including the Graf Zeppelin and Hindenburg. The Hindenburg disaster of 1937, in which the hydrogen-filled ship burned at Lakehurst, New Jersey, effectively ended the passenger era, compounded by the rise of faster aeroplanes.

Modern use

Today airships serve in advertising, tourism, surveillance, and research, and hybrid designs combining buoyant and aerodynamic lift are under development for heavy cargo transport.
