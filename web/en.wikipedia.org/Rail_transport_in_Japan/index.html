Rail transport in Japan

Rail transport in Japan is a major means of passenger transport, especially for mass rapid transit between and within major cities. The network of about 30,000 kilometres is operated by the Japan Railways Group (JR Group), regional private railways, and municipal subways. Japan is famous for the punctuality of its services and for the Shinkansen high-speed network, opened in 1964, which has carried over ten billion passengers without a single fatal accident caused by derailment or collision.

A Shinkansen at Tokyo Station, the hub of Japan's high-speed network.

History

Japan's first railway opened between Shimbashi (Tokyo) and Yokohama in 1872. The network was nationalized in 1906–07, and after the Second World War Japanese National Railways (JNR) rebuilt and modernized the system, launching the Tōkaidō Shinkansen for the 1964 Tokyo Olympics. Mounting JNR debt led to its breakup and privatization in 1987 into six regional passenger companies and one freight company.

Network characteristics

Most conventional lines use 1,067 mm narrow gauge, while Shinkansen lines use standard gauge (1,435 mm). Urban areas such as Tokyo and Osaka are served by dense networks of JR lines, private railways (such as Tokyu, Hankyu, and Keio), and subway systems, integrated through IC smart cards such as Suica and Pasmo. Private railways traditionally combine rail operations with real-estate and retail development around stations.

Technology and future

JR Central is developing the Chūō Shinkansen maglev line between Tokyo and Nagoya using superconducting magnetic levitation, with test vehicles reaching 603 km/h, the world record for rail vehicles. Conventional services continue to emphasize energy efficiency, platform safety, and automation.
