Implicit-association test

The implicit-association test (IAT) is a computer-based measure in social psychology introduced by Anthony Greenwald, Debbie McGhee, and Jordan Schwartz in 1998. It aims to measure the strength of automatic associations between mental representations — for example, between racial categories and evaluations such as "good" or "bad" — and is widely used to study implicit bias.

Procedure

Respondents rapidly sort words and images into categories using two response keys. In a race IAT, faces (Black or White) and words (pleasant or unpleasant) are first sorted separately, then combined so that one key serves both "White" and "pleasant" while the other serves "Black" and "pleasant," with the pairings then reversed. Faster responses for one pairing than the other are interpreted as indicating a stronger implicit association; the effect is quantified with a D-score.

Uses and criticism

The test has been used in research and in corporate and institutional diversity training, and millions of tests have been taken through Harvard's Project Implicit website. Critics have questioned the IAT's test–retest reliability and its weak-to-modest ability to predict individual discriminatory behaviour, and its creators themselves caution against using it as a diagnostic of individual prejudice. Meta-analyses disagree on the size and significance of its predictive validity.
