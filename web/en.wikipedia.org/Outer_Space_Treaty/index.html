The Outer Space Treaty, formally the Treaty on Principles Governing the Activities of States in the Exploration and Use of Outer Space, including the Moon and Other Celestial Bodies, is a multilateral treaty that forms the basis of international space law. Negotiated and drafted under the auspices of the United Nations, it was opened for signature in the United Kingdom, the Soviet Union, and the United States on 27 January 1967 and entered into force on 10 October 1967.

Key provisions

The treaty bars states parties from placing nuclear weapons or any other weapons of mass destruction in Earth orbit, installing them on the Moon or any other celestial body, or otherwise stationing them in outer space. It exclusively limits the use of the Moon and other celestial bodies to peaceful purposes and expressly prohibits their use for testing weapons of any kind, conducting military manoeuvres, or establishing military bases, installations, and fortifications.

Article II provides that outer space, including the Moon and other celestial bodies, is not subject to national appropriation by claim of sovereignty, by means of use or occupation, or by any other means. Article I declares that the exploration and use of outer space shall be carried out for the benefit and in the interests of all states and that outer space shall be free for exploration and use by all.

Under Articles VI and VII, states parties bear international responsibility for national activities in outer space — including those of non-governmental entities, which require authorisation and continuing supervision — and are internationally liable for damage caused by their space objects. Article VIII provides that a state retains jurisdiction and control over objects it launches and their personnel, and that ownership of objects launched into outer space is not affected by their presence there or by their return to Earth.

History

The treaty was developed during the early Space Race, building on United Nations General Assembly resolutions of 1962 and 1963 that set out legal principles for space activities. The United States, the United Kingdom, and the Soviet Union were the original depositary governments. The treaty was in part inspired by the Antarctic Treaty of 1959, which similarly demilitarised and internationalised a frontier region.

Status and influence

The Outer Space Treaty has been ratified by more than 110 countries, including all major spacefaring nations. It is the foundational instrument of space law and has been supplemented by the Rescue Agreement (1968), the Liability Convention (1972), the Registration Convention (1975), and the Moon Agreement (1979), the last of which has attracted few parties. Questions the treaty leaves open — including the legality of asteroid mining and other forms of space resource extraction, and its application to private companies — remain subjects of international debate, addressed in part by national legislation and by instruments such as the Artemis Accords of 2020.
