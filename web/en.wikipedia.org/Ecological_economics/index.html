Ecological economics

Ecological economics is a transdisciplinary field that studies the interdependence of human economies and natural ecosystems, treating the economy as a subsystem of the larger, finite ecosphere. It emerged in the late 20th century from work by thinkers such as Nicholas Georgescu-Roegen, Kenneth Boulding and Herman Daly, and is institutionalized in the International Society for Ecological Economics (founded 1988) and its journal Ecological Economics.

Key concepts

Central ideas include: the laws of thermodynamics applied to economic processes — especially entropy, showing that production irreversibly degrades energy and materials; the distinction between growth (quantitative increase in throughput) and development (qualitative improvement); natural capital and ecosystem services as complements rather than substitutes for manufactured capital; and the goals of sustainable scale, just distribution and efficient allocation, in that order of priority. Daly's "steady-state economy" is a prominent policy vision, while valuation of ecosystem services (as in the Millennium Ecosystem Assessment and TEEB) attempts to make nature's contributions visible in decisions.

Relation to other fields

Ecological economics differs from mainstream environmental economics, which applies neoclassical tools (externality pricing, cost-benefit analysis) to environmental problems. Ecological economists criticize the assumption of unlimited substitutability and GDP growth, and draw instead on systems ecology, thermodynamics, ethics and social science. It overlaps with, but is distinct from, green economics and degrowth scholarship.

Methods and policy

Methods include material and energy flow accounting, input–output analysis, multi-criteria evaluation and ecological footprinting. Policy proposals include cap-and-trade or quotas on resource throughput, ecological tax reform, and alternative welfare indicators such as the Genuine Progress Indicator and the Index of Sustainable Economic Welfare.
