Plumage (Latin: pluma, "feather") is the layer of feathers that covers a bird and the pattern, colour and arrangement of those feathers. Plumage serves flight, insulation, waterproofing, camouflage and display. A bird's plumage changes through its life via successive moults, producing recognisable juvenile, basic (non-breeding) and alternate (breeding) plumages that are central to field identification of birds.

Colouration

Feather colours arise from pigments and from microscopic structure. Melanins give blacks, greys and browns and strengthen feathers against wear; carotenoids, obtained from food, produce reds, oranges and yellows; porphyrins add reds and greens in a few groups. Blues and iridescent greens are structural, produced by light scattering and interference in feather nanostructures — there is no blue pigment in birds. Plumage colour often differs between the sexes (sexual dichromatism), with males brighter in species where display matters most, while camouflage colours dominate in ground-nesting birds and seabirds' countershaded patterns.

Functions

Beyond flight, plumage insulates against heat and cold — down feathers trap air close to the skin — and semiplumes and filoplumes sense airflow and feather position. Display plumage underlies courtship in peacocks, birds of paradise and many ducks, while disruptive patterns hide incubating birds. Bristles around the eyes and bill protect and sense.

Moult and plumage cycles

Feathers wear out and are replaced in moults, typically once a year after breeding (a complete moult in many species) and sometimes with a partial pre-breeding moult that produces the brighter alternate plumage. Some species, such as ptarmigan, change from white winter plumage to mottled brown summer dress. Humphrey–Parkes terminology standardises the naming of these cycles.

Human use

Humans have long prized plumage for ornament — from egret plumes in 19th-century millinery, whose trade drove early conservation laws and the founding of bird-protection societies, to down insulation. Plumage trading is now restricted in many countries.
