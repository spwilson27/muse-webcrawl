Devised byWladimir Köppen (1900; revised with Rudolf Geiger)
Main groupsA (tropical), B (dry), C (temperate), D (continental), E (polar)

The Köppen climate classification is one of the most widely used systems for classifying the world's climates. It was first published by the German-Russian climatologist Wladimir Köppen in 1900, based on the idea that native vegetation is the best expression of climate, and was later modified by Köppen himself and by Rudolf Geiger, giving the related Köppen–Geiger system.

Overview

The scheme divides climates into five main groups, each subdivided by seasonal precipitation and temperature patterns, denoted by a code of two or three letters. Group A climates are tropical, with every month averaging above 18°C; group B are dry (arid and semi-arid); group C are temperate; group D are continental, found mainly in the interiors of large landmasses; and group E are polar and alpine climates.

Subtypes

A second letter describes precipitation seasonality — for example f for no dry season, s for a dry summer, w for a dry winter and m for monsoonal — while a third letter indicates temperature details, such as a for a hot summer. Well-known codes include Af (tropical rainforest), BSk (cold semi-arid/steppe), Cfb (temperate oceanic), Dfc (subarctic) and EF (ice cap).

Usage

Because it is based on easily observed temperature and precipitation thresholds, the Köppen system remains standard in geography, ecology and climate-change research, where shifting classification boundaries are used to visualise the movement of climate zones.
