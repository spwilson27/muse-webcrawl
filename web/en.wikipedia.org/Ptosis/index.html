Ptosis (from Greek ptōsis, "falling"), also called blepharoptosis, is a drooping or falling of the upper eyelid. It occurs when the levator palpebrae superioris muscle, which raises the lid, or its nerve supply is weakened. Ptosis may affect one or both eyes, may be present at birth (congenital) or acquired later, and in severe cases can obstruct vision; in children it can cause amblyopia ("lazy eye") if the visual axis is covered.

Causes

Acquired ptosis is classified by mechanism: aponeurotic ptosis, the commonest form, results from stretching or detachment of the levator tendon, usually with ageing; neurogenic ptosis follows damage to the oculomotor nerve or sympathetic supply (as in Horner's syndrome); myogenic ptosis arises from muscle diseases such as myasthenia gravis and chronic progressive external ophthalmoplegia; and mechanical ptosis from tumours or scarring. A drooping lid accompanied by a dilated pupil and "down-and-out" eye suggests third-nerve palsy, which can be an emergency sign of an aneurysm.

Treatment

Management begins with treating any underlying cause. Surgical correction is common, using levator resection or advancement when the muscle retains some function, or a frontalis sling — linking the lid to the brow muscle — when it does not. Temporary measures include ptosis crutches fitted to spectacles and, for myasthenic ptosis, medication for the underlying disease.
