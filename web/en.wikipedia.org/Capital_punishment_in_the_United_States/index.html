Capital punishment is a legal penalty in the United States at the federal level and in a majority of states, though its use has declined sharply since the late 1990s. The modern era of the American death penalty began in 1976, when the Supreme Court upheld revised statutes in Gregg v. Georgia after a four-year de facto moratorium following Furman v. Georgia (1972).

Status of the death penalty by US state.

Legal framework

Death is generally reserved for aggravated murder with statutory aggravating circumstances, determined in a bifurcated trial with a separate sentencing phase. The Supreme Court has barred execution of people with intellectual disability (Atkins v. Virginia, 2002), of juveniles (Roper v. Simmons, 2005), and for crimes against individuals not involving homicide (Kennedy v. Louisiana, 2008). Federal crimes carrying death include murder, treason, espionage, and large-scale drug trafficking.

Use and trends

Executions peaked at 98 in 1999 and new death sentences at over 300 in the mid-1990s; both have since fallen by roughly three-quarters or more, owing to exonerations, cost, lethal-injection drug shortages, and shifting public opinion. Texas has carried out the most executions by far, followed by Oklahoma, Virginia (which abolished the penalty in 2021), Florida, and Missouri. The federal government executed 13 people in 2020–21 after a 17-year hiatus, then reinstated a moratorium.

Debate

Since 1973, more than 190 death-row prisoners have been exonerated. Studies document racial disparities, particularly by the race of the victim. Support remains a majority position in national polling but near historic lows; several states have abolished the penalty in the twenty-first century, including New York (by court ruling), Illinois, Maryland, and Colorado.
