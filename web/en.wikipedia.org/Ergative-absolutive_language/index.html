Ergative–absolutive language

In linguistic typology, an ergative–absolutive language is a language in which the subject of an intransitive verb is morphologically treated the same way as the object of a transitive verb, distinct from the subject of a transitive verb. The case (or agreement pattern) shared by the intransitive subject and the transitive object is called the absolutive, while the case marking the transitive subject is the ergative.

Contrast with nominative–accusative alignment

Most familiar languages, including English and other Indo-European languages, follow nominative–accusative alignment: the subject of both transitive and intransitive verbs takes the nominative case, while the transitive object takes the accusative. Ergative languages invert part of this pattern. For example, in Basque, gizona etorri da ("the man has come") uses the absolutive form gizona ("the man"), and in the transitive sentence "the man saw the woman", "the man" would appear in the ergative case while "the woman" appears in the absolutive.

Distribution

Ergativity is found in Basque, most languages of the Caucasus, Mayan languages, Eskimo–Aleut languages, many Australian Aboriginal languages, Kurdish (in part), and numerous languages of the Himalayas and New Guinea. Many languages show split ergativity, using ergative alignment only in some tenses or aspects — Hindi-Urdu, for instance, is ergative in the perfective aspect but nominative–accusative elsewhere. Only a minority of languages are fully ergative in both case marking and syntax; syntactic ergativity, in which coordination and subordination also treat the intransitive subject and transitive object alike, is notably developed in Dyirbal.

Related concepts

The concept was developed in the early study of Caucasian languages and generalised in typological work by R. M. W. Dixon, who proposed the S, A, O primitives: in an ergative system, S and O are grouped together, whereas in an accusative system, S and A are.
