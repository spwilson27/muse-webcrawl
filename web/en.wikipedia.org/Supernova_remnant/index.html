Supernova remnant
NatureExpanding nebula of ejected stellar material
Typical lifetimeTens to hundreds of thousands of years

A supernova remnant (SNR) is the structure resulting from the explosion of a star in a supernova. The supernova remnant is bounded by an expanding shock wave, and consists of ejected material expanding from the explosion, and the interstellar material it sweeps up and shocks along the way. Remnants are extremely important for understanding the dynamics and chemistry of the interstellar medium, since they distribute heavy elements forged in the star and inject enormous energy into their surroundings, driving cosmic-ray acceleration.

Stages of evolution

  • Free expansion: ejecta travel ballistically at up to ~20,000 km/s, sweeping up their own mass in interstellar gas.
  • Sedov–Taylor phase: an adiabatic blast wave expands roughly as t2/5; the shell becomes bright in X-rays and radio.
  • Radiative (pressure-driven snowplough) phase: the shell cools, collapses into a thin dense shell, and becomes bright in visible light.
  • Dissipation: the remnant slows to the speed of ambient turbulence and merges with the interstellar medium after roughly 100,000 years.

Types

Remnants are classified morphologically as shell-type (e.g. Tycho's remnant), composite (a shell surrounding a central pulsar wind nebula), or filled-centre/plerionic (e.g. the Crab Nebula, powered by a central pulsar). Some, like Cassiopeia A, are bright across the electromagnetic spectrum and are among the strongest radio sources in the sky.
