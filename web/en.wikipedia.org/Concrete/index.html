Concrete

Concrete is a composite construction material composed of fine and coarse aggregate bonded together with a fluid cement (cement paste) that hardens (cures) over time. It is the second-most-used substance in the world after water and the most widely used man-made material, valued for its compressive strength, durability, fire resistance and the freedom it gives builders to cast almost any shape.

History

The Romans used a form of concrete based on lime and volcanic ash (pozzolana) to build structures such as the dome of the Pantheon in Rome, still the world's largest unreinforced concrete dome. The material was largely forgotten after Rome's fall and revived in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, notably with Joseph Aspdin's patent for Portland cement in 1824. Steel-reinforced concrete, patented in various forms from the 1850s, transformed modern construction.

Properties and production

Concrete's strength depends chiefly on the water-to-cement ratio and on proper curing. Typical structural concrete has a compressive strength of 20–40 MPa, but it is weak in tension, which is why steel reinforcement is embedded in beams, slabs and columns. Annual global production exceeds ten billion tonnes; cement manufacture accounts for roughly 8 percent of world carbon dioxide emissions, driving research into supplementary cementitious materials and low-carbon binders.
