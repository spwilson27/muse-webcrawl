Abdominal cavity

The abdominal cavity is the large body cavity in humans and many other animals that contains most of the internal organs of the abdomen. It is the upper part of the abdominopelvic cavity, separated from the thoracic cavity above by the diaphragm and continuous below with the pelvic cavity.

Structure

The cavity is bounded by the diaphragm superiorly, the abdominal muscles and lumbar vertebrae at the front, sides, and back, and the pelvic inlet below. It is lined by the peritoneum, a serous membrane whose parietal layer covers the cavity wall and whose visceral layer invests the organs. The peritoneum folds to form the mesentery and omenta, which suspend the intestines and carry blood vessels, lymphatics, and nerves.

Contents

The abdominal cavity contains the stomach, liver, gallbladder, spleen, pancreas, small intestine, most of the large intestine, kidneys, and adrenal glands, together with major vessels such as the abdominal aorta and inferior vena cava. Some organs, including the kidneys and pancreas, lie behind the peritoneum and are described as retroperitoneal. The cavity normally contains only a small amount of peritoneal fluid; accumulation of excess fluid is called ascites.

Clinical significance

For clinical examination the abdomen is divided into nine regions or four quadrants. Conditions affecting the cavity include peritonitis (inflammation of the peritoneum), internal bleeding, appendicitis, and abdominal compartment syndrome, in which raised intra-abdominal pressure compromises organ perfusion.
