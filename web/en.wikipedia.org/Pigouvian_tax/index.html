Pigouvian tax

A Pigouvian tax (also spelled Pigovian tax) is a tax on any market activity that generates negative externalities — costs borne by third parties not involved in the transaction. The tax is intended to correct an undesirable or inefficient market outcome by setting the tax equal to the external cost, so that the activity's price reflects its true social cost.

Origin

The concept is named after the English economist Arthur Cecil Pigou, who developed the theory of externalities in his 1920 book The Economics of Welfare. Pigou argued that taxes (and, symmetrically, subsidies for positive externalities) could align private and social costs.

Examples

Real-world examples include carbon taxes on greenhouse gas emissions, congestion charges for driving in city centres, and excise taxes on tobacco and alcohol. A corresponding Pigouvian subsidy may support activities with positive externalities, such as vaccination or education.

Criticism

Critics, notably Ronald Coase, note that in practice it is difficult to measure the true external cost, and that bargaining or property-rights assignment may sometimes achieve efficiency without taxation.
