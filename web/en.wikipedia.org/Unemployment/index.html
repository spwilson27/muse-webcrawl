Unemployment

Unemployment is the state of being unable to find a job despite being able to work and actively seeking employment. It is measured by the unemployment rate, the share of the labour force that is jobless. Under the standard International Labour Organization definition, a person is unemployed if they are without work, available for work, and have taken specific steps to find work in the recent past. Unemployment is a central indicator of economic health and a principal concern of fiscal and monetary policy.

Types

Economists distinguish several causes. Frictional unemployment arises from normal job search and transitions between jobs. Structural unemployment results from a mismatch between workers' skills or locations and available jobs, often from technological change or trade shifts. Cyclical unemployment rises in recessions when aggregate demand falls — in the Great Depression US unemployment reached about 25%. Seasonal unemployment follows predictable patterns in industries such as agriculture and tourism. Long-term unemployment (lasting 27 weeks or more in US statistics) can cause skill loss and labour-market scarring.

Measurement

National statistics offices measure unemployment through household surveys (such as the US Current Population Survey) rather than benefit claims. The headline US rate (U-3) excludes discouraged workers who have stopped searching; broader measures (such as U-6) include them plus involuntary part-time workers. Youth unemployment is typically much higher than the overall rate, and it exceeded 50% in some eurozone countries after the 2008 financial crisis.

Effects and policy

Unemployment wastes productive capacity (Okun's law relates output losses to rises in unemployment), worsens public finances, and harms individuals' income, health and future earnings. Governments respond with unemployment insurance, job training, public employment services and macroeconomic stabilisation; central banks weigh unemployment against inflation under the framework of the Phillips curve and target a "non-accelerating inflation rate of unemployment" (NAIRU). Theories of equilibrium unemployment include efficiency wages, insider–outsider dynamics and search-and-matching models, for which Diamond, Mortensen and Pissarides received the 2010 Nobel Memorial Prize.
