Whiplash (medicine)

Whiplash
Specialty: Orthopaedics; physical medicine
Symptoms: Neck pain and stiffness, headache, shoulder and arm pain
Usual cause: Rear-end vehicle collision
Prognosis: Most recover within months; some have chronic symptoms

Whiplash is a non-medical term describing a range of injuries to the neck most commonly caused by a sudden acceleration–deceleration, typically in rear-end car collisions, in which the head is thrown backward and then forward, straining the muscles, ligaments and other soft tissues of the neck. The clinical conditions are often called whiplash-associated disorders (WAD).

Symptoms and course

Symptoms include neck pain and stiffness, headache, shoulder and upper-back pain, and sometimes arm pain, dizziness, tinnitus and cognitive complaints. They often appear hours or a day after the accident rather than immediately. Most people recover within a few weeks to three months, but a significant minority develop chronic pain. The Quebec Task Force (1995) graded whiplash-associated disorders from 0 (no complaint) to IV (neck complaint with fracture or dislocation).

Treatment and prevention

Evidence favours early mobilisation, exercise and staying active over prolonged use of a soft collar, which may delay recovery; analgesics and physiotherapy are commonly used. Prevention focuses on vehicle design: properly adjusted head restraints and energy-absorbing seats reduce injury rates in rear impacts.

Controversy

Whiplash is a frequent subject of insurance claims and litigation, and the influence of compensation systems on symptoms has been debated; studies in countries without a compensation culture have found much lower rates of chronic complaints, while other research points to genuine physical injury visible in imaging and biomechanical studies.
