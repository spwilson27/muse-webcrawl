Bank of England

The Bank of England is the central bank of the United Kingdom and the model on which most modern central banks have been based. Established in 1694 to act as the English government's banker and debt manager, it is the world's second-oldest central bank. It is nicknamed the Old Lady of Threadneedle Street.

History

The Bank was founded by royal charter in 1694, following proposals by the Scotsman William Paterson, to raise £1.2 million to fund the war against France. It issued notes, managed the national debt, and gradually acquired a monopoly on note issue in England and Wales under the Bank Charter Act 1844. It was nationalized in 1946 and granted operational independence over monetary policy by the Bank of England Act 1998.

Functions

The Bank sets the United Kingdom's official interest rate (Bank Rate) through its Monetary Policy Committee to meet the government's inflation target of 2%. It issues banknotes, holds the UK's gold reserves, acts as lender of last resort, and supervises banks and insurers through the Prudential Regulation Authority. Its Financial Policy Committee monitors risks to the financial system as a whole.

Governance

The Bank is governed by a Court of Directors appointed by the Crown on the advice of the government. The Governor — who serves an eight-year term — is its most senior figure; past governors include Montagu Norman, Mervyn King, and the Canadian Mark Carney, the first non-Briton to hold the post.
