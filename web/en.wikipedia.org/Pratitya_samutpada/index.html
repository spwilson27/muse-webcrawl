Pratitya samutpada

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
The twelve nidanas are traditionally depicted around the rim of the bhavachakra, the wheel of life.

Pratitya samutpada (Sanskrit: प्रतीत्यसमुत्पाद; Pali: paṭiccasamuppāda), usually translated as dependent origination or dependent arising, is a central doctrine of Buddhism. It holds that all phenomena arise in dependence upon other phenomena: "this being, that becomes; from the arising of this, that arises; this not being, that does not become; from the ceasing of this, that ceases." Nothing exists independently or by its own power, and all things are conditioned and impermanent.

Pratitya samutpada

Translation: Dependent origination; dependent arising

Tradition: Buddhism (all schools)

Classic formulation: The twelve nidanas

Related: Śūnyatā (emptiness), anatta (non-self), saṃsāra

The twelve nidanas

The best-known formulation is a chain of twelve links (nidanas) explaining how suffering and rebirth in saṃsāra perpetuate themselves: (1) ignorance (avidyā), (2) volitional formations (saṃskāra), (3) consciousness (vijñāna), (4) name-and-form (nāmarūpa), (5) the six sense bases (ṣaḍāyatana), (6) contact (sparśa), (7) feeling (vedanā), (8) craving (tṛṣṇā), (9) clinging (upādāna), (10) becoming (bhava), (11) birth (jāti), and (12) ageing and death (jarāmaraṇa). Traditions differ on interpretation — whether the links unfold over three lifetimes or moment by moment in the mind — but agree that breaking the chain, beginning with ignorance, ends suffering.

Philosophical significance

Dependent origination is presented in the early texts as a middle way between eternalism (things exist by inherent essence) and nihilism (nothing exists at all). In Mahayana philosophy, Nagarjuna's Madhyamaka school equated pratitya samutpada with śūnyatā, emptiness: because things arise dependently, they are empty of independent, intrinsic nature (svabhāva). The doctrine also underlies the Buddhist analysis of the person as a bundle of conditioned aggregates rather than a permanent self (anatta).
