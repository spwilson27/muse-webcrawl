Cultural geography

Cultural geography is a subfield of human geography that studies the relationships between culture and place: how cultural practices, beliefs, languages, and identities vary across space, how they shape landscapes, and how places in turn shape cultures. It examines both material culture — buildings, fields, dress, food — and symbolic meanings attached to places.

History

The field descends from the Berkeley School of Carl O. Sauer, whose 1925 essay "The Morphology of Landscape" argued that cultural groups transform natural landscapes into cultural landscapes over time. Sauer and his students traced the diffusion of crops, house types, and agricultural systems, particularly in Latin America. Mid-century cultural geography also studied cultural regions, hearths of innovation, and the diffusion of traits.

New cultural geography

From the 1980s, a "new cultural geography" influenced by cultural studies, feminism, poststructuralism, and postcolonial theory shifted attention to representation, power, and identity. Geographers such as Denis Cosgrove, Peter Jackson, and Doreen Massey analyzed how landscapes encode ideology, how marginalized groups contest meanings of place, and how globalization creates cultural hybridity rather than simple homogenization. Research topics include language landscapes, religion and sacred space, geographies of race, gender and sexuality, popular culture, tourism, and memory.

Key concepts

Central concepts include the cultural landscape, place and sense of place, cultural diffusion, acculturation and assimilation, cultural regions, and topophilia (Yi-Fu Tuan's term for affective attachment to place). Contemporary work also engages with non-representational theory, mobility, and the cultural geographies of the digital world.
