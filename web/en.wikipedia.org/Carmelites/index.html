The Carmelites, formally the Order of the Brothers of the Blessed Virgin Mary of Mount Carmel, are a Roman Catholic mendicant religious order founded by hermits living on Mount Carmel in the Holy Land in the late 12th century. The order is known for its contemplative spirituality, devotion to the Virgin Mary under the title Our Lady of Mount Carmel, and the brown scapular. Its notable saints include Teresa of Ávila, John of the Cross and Thérèse of Lisieux.

History

Christian hermits settled in the caves of Mount Carmel, following the example of the prophet Elijah, and around 1206–1214 received a rule of life from Albert, Patriarch of Jerusalem. As conditions in the crusader states deteriorated, the hermits migrated to Europe, where in 1247 Pope Innocent IV adapted their rule, transforming them into a mendicant order of friars. Carmelite nuns were established from the 15th century. According to tradition, the Virgin Mary appeared to St Simon Stock in 1251 and gave him the brown scapular with promises for its wearers.

Teresian reform

In the 16th century, St Teresa of Ávila, together with St John of the Cross, reformed the order in Spain, seeking a return to stricter contemplative observance. Their branch became the Discalced Carmelites ("discalced" meaning barefoot or wearing sandals), formally separated in 1593, while the original branch continued as the Carmelites of the Ancient Observance. Both branches spread worldwide and remain active in parishes, missions, retreats and enclosed contemplative life. Carmelite spirituality, expressed in classics such as Teresa's Interior Castle and John's Dark Night of the Soul, emphasises prayer, interior recollection and union with God.
