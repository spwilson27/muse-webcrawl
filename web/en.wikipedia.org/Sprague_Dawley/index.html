The Sprague Dawley rat is an outbred, multipurpose stock of albino laboratory rat developed in 1925 in Madison, Wisconsin, by Robert W. Dawley of the Sprague-Dawley Company (named for Dawley's first wife's maiden name, Sprague). It is one of the most widely used rat stocks in biomedical research worldwide.

Characteristics

Sprague Dawley rats are calm, easy to handle, fast-growing and prolific breeders. Adult males typically weigh 450–600 g and females 250–350 g, with a lifespan of about 2.5–3.5 years. Because they are outbred, individuals are genetically heterogeneous, which is considered desirable for toxicology studies intended to model varied human responses; the trade-off is greater variability than inbred strains such as Fischer 344 or inbred models like the Wistar Kyoto rat.

Use in research

The stock is a standard model in toxicology, carcinogenicity testing, nutrition, endocrinology and surgery. Sprague Dawley rats are known for age-related spontaneous conditions, including pituitary and mammary tumours (especially in females), which long-term studies must account for. The stock was the subject of public controversy through the Séralini affair (2012), a retracted and later republished rat-feeding study on genetically modified maize and glyphosate, which drew scientific criticism partly concerning the strain's tumour-proneness and small group sizes.

Suppliers

Sprague Dawley colonies are maintained by major laboratory animal breeders including Charles River Laboratories, Envigo (now Inotiv) and Taconic Biosciences; the name is a registered trademark of Envigo. Genetic drift between colonies means rats sold under the same name from different suppliers are not genetically identical.
