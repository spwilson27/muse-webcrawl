Battle of Vimy Ridge

Date9–12 April 1917
LocationVimy, Pas-de-Calais, France
ResultCanadian victory
BelligerentsCanada (British Empire) vs. German Empire
CommandersJulian Byng; Arthur Currie vs. Ludwig von Falkenhausen

The Battle of Vimy Ridge was a military engagement fought from 9 to 12 April 1917 in northern France during the First World War. The four divisions of the Canadian Corps, fighting together for the first time, attacked and captured the German-held Vimy Ridge as the opening of the Battle of Arras. Meticulous preparation, extensive tunnelling, massive artillery support and innovative infantry tactics made the battle a landmark in Canadian military history and national identity.

Canadians advancing over the captured ridge, April 1917.

Background

Vimy Ridge, a commanding escarpment seven kilometres long, had been in German hands since 1914 and had resisted costly French attacks in 1915. For the 1917 Arras offensive the task was given to the Canadian Corps under Lieutenant-General Sir Julian Byng. Months of preparation followed: tunnels and light railways were dug, every soldier studied maps and models of the objective, and lessons from the Somme — notably platoon-level fire and movement — were built into the plan.

The battle

At 5:30 a.m. on Easter Monday, 9 April, some 1,000 guns opened fire and Canadian infantry advanced behind a precisely timed creeping barrage. By the end of the first day most of the ridge was in Canadian hands; the highest point, Hill 145, fell on 10 April, and the remaining position known as "the Pimple" on 12 April. Canadian casualties were 3,598 killed and about 7,000 wounded — the costliest days in Canadian military history — but the Corps had captured more ground, prisoners and guns than any previous British offensive.

Legacy

Vimy became a founding symbol of Canadian nationhood — the moment, it is often said, when Canada emerged from Britain's shadow as a nation. The ridge was granted to Canada by France, and the Canadian National Vimy Memorial, designed by Walter Allward and unveiled in 1936, stands on Hill 145 in memory of the more than 11,000 Canadians killed in France with no known grave.
