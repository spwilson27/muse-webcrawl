Life is a quality that distinguishes matter that has biological processes, such as signalling and self-sustaining processes, from matter that does not. It is defined descriptively by the capacity for homeostasis, organisation, metabolism, growth, adaptation, response to stimuli, and reproduction. All life shares the cellular form: living organisms are composed of one or more cells and use carbon- and water-based chemistry, with the genetic information of nearly all known organisms encoded in DNA.

Definitions

There is no universally accepted definition of life. A widely used working definition, adopted by NASA for astrobiology, describes life as "a self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution". Viruses, which reproduce only inside host cells and are not cellular, sit at the boundary of most definitions and are generally not classified as living. Alternative proposals emphasise metabolism, thermodynamics or information storage.

Origin and evolution

Life on Earth arose through abiogenesis — the gradual emergence of self-replicating chemistry — at least 3.7 billion years ago, and possibly earlier. All known species descend from a last universal common ancestor (LUCA) that lived roughly 3.5–4 billion years ago. Organisms are classified into three domains — Bacteria, Archaea and Eukarya — and evolution by natural selection, mutation, genetic drift and gene flow continually reshapes their diversity.

Range and survival

Life occupies nearly every environment on Earth, from deep-sea hydrothermal vents and subglacial lakes to the upper atmosphere. Extremophiles thrive in conditions of extreme heat, acidity, salinity and radiation, and many organisms endure dormancy — as spores, seeds or cryptobiotic states — for years or far longer. No life has yet been confirmed beyond Earth, though the search continues on Mars, icy moons such as Europa and Enceladus, and exoplanets.
