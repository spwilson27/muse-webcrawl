American Convention on Human Rights

The American Convention on Human Rights, also known as the Pact of San José, is an international human rights treaty adopted in San José, Costa Rica, on 22 November 1969, and in force since 1978. It is the principal treaty of the Inter-American human rights system, under the Organization of American States (OAS).

Content

The Convention obliges States Parties to respect and ensure the civil and political rights it proclaims — including rights to life, humane treatment, personal liberty, a fair trial, freedom of expression, assembly and association, freedom of movement and judicial protection — and contains an influential, strong formulation of the right to life "in general, from the moment of conception". The Protocol of San Salvador (1988) added economic, social and cultural rights, and a separate protocol addresses abolition of the death penalty. Most of Latin America and the Caribbean ratified the Convention; the United States signed in 1977 but never ratified, and Canada has not joined.

Institutions

Compliance is supervised by two bodies: the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights (Washington, D.C.), which receives petitions and issues reports, and the Inter-American Court of Human Rights (San José, Costa Rica), whose contentious judgments bind states that have accepted its jurisdiction. The Court's landmark early judgment, Velásquez Rodríguez v. Honduras (1988), established state responsibility for forced disappearances and shaped international human rights law on the duty to prevent, investigate and repair violations.
