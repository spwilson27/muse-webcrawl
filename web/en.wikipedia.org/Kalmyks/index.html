The Kalmyks (Kalmyk: Хальмгуд) are a Mongolic ethnic group living mainly in the Republic of Kalmykia in southwestern Russia, on the western shore of the Caspian Sea. Descended from the Oirat Mongols who migrated from western Mongolia (Dzungaria) to the Volga steppe in the early seventeenth century, they are the only traditionally Buddhist people native to Europe.

History

Oirat groups crossed the Kazakh steppe and settled the lower Volga region in the 1600s, establishing the Kalmyk Khanate (1630–1771), which acknowledged nominal Russian suzerainty while retaining wide autonomy, fielding cavalry for Russian campaigns and defending the southern frontier. In 1771, alarmed by encroachment on their lands and autonomy, the majority of the Kalmyks under Ubashi Khan undertook a disastrous return migration to Dzungaria; those who remained west of the Volga became the ancestors of the modern Kalmyks.

After the October Revolution, Kalmykia became an autonomous republic within the USSR. In December 1943, the entire Kalmyk population — over 90,000 people — was deported to Siberia and Central Asia on accusations of collaboration, and the republic was abolished; the exiles were permitted to return only in 1957, when Kalmykia was restored.

Culture

Kalmyks practise Tibetan (Gelug) Buddhism, adopted from the late sixteenth century; the Burkhan Bakshin Altan Sume in the capital Elista is the largest Buddhist temple in Europe. The Kalmyk language, written historically in the vertical Todo Bichig script and now in Cyrillic, belongs to the Mongolic family. Kalmyk culture preserves the epic of Jangar, nomadic pastoral traditions, horse culture, and a strong chess tradition promoted in the 1990s, when Elista hosted Chess City and international tournaments.

Diaspora

Small Kalmyk communities exist in Kyrgyzstan (the Sart Kalmyks of Issyk-Kul) and in the United States, where Kalmyk refugees admitted in 1951–52 settled mainly in New Jersey and Pennsylvania, among the first Tibetan Buddhist communities in America.
