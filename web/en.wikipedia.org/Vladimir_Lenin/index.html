Vladimir Ilyich Ulyanov, better known as Lenin (22 April 1870 – 21 January 1924), was a Russian revolutionary, politician and political theorist who founded the Soviet Union. He led the Bolsheviks in the October Revolution of 1917 and served as the first head of government of Soviet Russia and then the USSR.

Early life

Born in Simbirsk (later renamed Ulyanovsk) to a well-educated family, Lenin was radicalised after his elder brother Alexander was executed in 1887 for plotting to assassinate Tsar Alexander III. He studied law, embraced Marxism, was exiled to Siberia, and spent much of 1900–1917 in Western Europe, leading the Bolshevik faction after the Russian Social Democratic Labour Party split with the Mensheviks in 1903.

Revolution and rule

Returning to Petrograd in April 1917 after the February Revolution, Lenin issued the April Theses calling for “Peace, Land, and Bread” and all power to the soviets. The Bolsheviks seized power in October (November, New Style). Lenin's government withdrew Russia from the First World War at Brest-Litovsk, survived a civil war against the Whites through the Red Army and War Communism, suppressed opposition in the Red Terror, and in 1921 introduced the New Economic Policy, a partial retreat to market mechanisms. The USSR was formally founded in December 1922.

Death and legacy

After strokes from 1922, Lenin dictated a “Testament” criticising Stalin, but died in January 1924. His embalmed body was placed in a mausoleum on Red Square. Leninism — his adaptation of Marxism, centred on a vanguard party — shaped communist movements worldwide, and his legacy remains deeply contested.
