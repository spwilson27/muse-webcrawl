Heine–Borel theorem

In real analysis, the Heine–Borel theorem, named after Eduard Heine and Émile Borel, states that for a subset S of Euclidean space ℝn, the following two statements are equivalent:

  • S is compact — that is, every open cover of S has a finite subcover; and
  • S is closed and bounded.

For subsets of the real line, the theorem says that a set of real numbers is compact if and only if it is closed and bounded; in particular, every closed interval [a, b] is compact.

History and proof

The result grew out of work by Heine (1872) on uniform continuity and a covering lemma proved by Borel (1895) for countable coverings of an interval; the general equivalence was established by later authors including Lebesgue. Standard proofs of the direction "closed and bounded implies compact" proceed by bisection: if a bounded interval could not be covered finitely, repeated halving produces a point of the set belonging to some covering set, giving a contradiction. The converse follows because compact sets in Hausdorff spaces are closed and because the continuous image of a compact set is bounded.

Generalisations and role

The characterisation does not hold in arbitrary metric or topological spaces, where compactness need not imply "closed and bounded" in reverse; but in finite-dimensional normed spaces all norms give the same compact sets, and closed bounded sets are exactly the compact ones. The Heine–Borel theorem underlies fundamental results of analysis, such as the extreme value theorem and the uniform continuity of continuous functions on closed intervals, and it is closely related to the Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem.
