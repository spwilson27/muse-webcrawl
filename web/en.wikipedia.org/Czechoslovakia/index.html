Czechoslovakia (Czech and Slovak: Československo) was a landlocked country in Central Europe created in 1918 at the dissolution of the Austro-Hungarian Empire and dissolved peacefully on 1 January 1993 into the independent Czech Republic and Slovakia. Except for the years of German occupation during the Second World War and the communist period from 1948 to 1989, it was one of Central Europe's most industrialised and prosperous states.

Czechoslovakia between 1945 and 1993.

First Republic and dismemberment

Czechoslovakia declared independence on 28 October 1918 under the leadership of Tomáš Garrigue Masaryk, its first president, uniting the Bohemian crown lands (Bohemia, Moravia and Czech Silesia) with Slovakia and, until 1939, Subcarpathian Ruthenia. The First Republic was a functioning parliamentary democracy with a strong industrial base — by the 1930s it ranked among the world's top ten industrial economies — but it was strained by the Great Depression and by the grievances of its large German-speaking minority in the Sudetenland. The Munich Agreement of September 1938 forced it to cede the Sudetenland to Nazi Germany; in March 1939 Germany occupied the Czech lands as the Protectorate of Bohemia and Moravia while a client Slovak State was established.

War, restoration and communism

A government-in-exile under Edvard Beneš operated from London, and Czechoslovak forces fought with the Allies. Restored in 1945, the republic expelled some three million ethnic Germans. In February 1948 the Communist Party seized full power in a coup, aligning the country with the Soviet bloc. The reformist Prague Spring of 1968, led by Alexander Dubček under the banner of "socialism with a human face", was crushed by a Warsaw Pact invasion in August 1968, followed by two decades of rigid "normalisation". Dissidents such as the Charter 77 signatories, including playwright Václav Havel, kept protest alive.

Velvet Revolution and dissolution

In November 1989 mass protests — the Velvet Revolution — brought down the communist regime without bloodshed, and Havel became president of a democratic, federal Czechoslovakia. Diverging economic priorities and national politics between the Czech and Slovak governments led, after elections in 1992, to a negotiated split. On 1 January 1993 the federation dissolved peacefully — the "Velvet Divorce" — and both successor states went on to join NATO and, in 2004, the European Union.
