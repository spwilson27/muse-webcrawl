Serum therapy

Diphtheria antitoxin, the first great success of serum therapy

Serum therapy, also called serotherapy, is a form of treatment in which a patient is given serum containing antibodies (antitoxin or immunoglobulin) derived from the blood of a human or animal that has been immunized against a specific disease or toxin. It provides immediate but temporary passive immunity, in contrast to vaccination, which stimulates the recipient's own immune system.

History

Serum therapy began in 1890, when Emil von Behring and Shibasaburo Kitasato demonstrated that serum from animals immunized against diphtheria and tetanus toxins could cure infected animals. Diphtheria antitoxin, produced in horses, dramatically reduced the mortality of diphtheria in children and earned Behring the first Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1901. Sera were subsequently developed against tetanus, plague, meningococcal disease and pneumococcal pneumonia, among others.

Modern uses

With the arrival of antibiotics, serum therapy for bacterial infections largely fell from use, but antibody preparations remain essential for neutralizing toxins and venoms for which no other treatment exists. Modern applications include antivenoms for snake and scorpion envenomation, botulinum and tetanus antitoxins, rabies immunoglobulin, and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) for immunodeficiency and autoimmune disease. Convalescent plasma was also used experimentally during the Ebola epidemic and the COVID-19 pandemic.

Risks

Animal-derived sera can provoke allergic reactions, including anaphylaxis, and a delayed hypersensitivity illness known as serum sickness, first described in the early twentieth century. Modern purified and human-derived immunoglobulin preparations have reduced these risks.
