Claude Bernard (12 July 1813 – 10 February 1878) was a French physiologist and historian of science. He is regarded as one of the founders of experimental medicine. His book An Introduction to the Study of Experimental Medicine (1865) established the methodology of hypothesis-driven experimentation in biology, and his concept of the milieu intérieur — the constancy of the body's internal environment — became the foundation of the modern idea of homeostasis.

Biography

Born to humble parents in the village of Saint-Julien near Villefranche-sur-Saône, Bernard originally aspired to be a playwright, writing two plays and a comedy before moving to Paris in 1834 to study medicine. He became an assistant to the physiologist François Magendie at the Collège de France and later succeeded him. In 1854 he was appointed professor of physiology at the Sorbonne, and in 1868 he received France's first state-funded laboratory of experimental physiology at the Muséum d'Histoire Naturelle. He was elected to the French Academy of Sciences in 1854 and the Académie française in 1868.

Scientific contributions

  • Glycogen and liver function: Bernard discovered that the liver stores a carbohydrate he named glycogen and releases glucose into the blood, establishing the liver's role in maintaining blood-sugar levels.
  • Pancreatic digestion: he showed that pancreatic juice breaks down fats, starches and proteins — the foundation of understanding digestion.
  • Vasomotor control: his experiments on sympathetic nerves controlling blood vessels clarified how the nervous system regulates circulation and temperature.
  • Curare: he demonstrated that curare paralyses muscles by acting on the neuromuscular junction rather than on nerves or muscle fibres themselves.
  • Milieu intérieur: his culminating insight was that living organisms maintain a stable internal environment despite external change — the idea later formalised as homeostasis by Walter Cannon.

Legacy

Bernard's insistence on controlled experiments, on questioning accepted doctrine, and on quantitative measurement transformed physiology from a speculative into an experimental science. The Claude Bernard University in Lyon (Lyon 1) is named after him, as is the Claude Bernard Association, one of France's oldest patient-support organisations. He received the Copley Medal of the Royal Society in 1876.
