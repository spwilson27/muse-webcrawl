Cognitive miser

The cognitive miser is a concept in social psychology describing the tendency of human beings to conserve mental effort by solving problems using simple, economical strategies rather than thorough analysis. The metaphor, introduced by Susan Fiske and Shelley Taylor in 1984, portrays people as "misers" who hoard their limited cognitive resources.

Background

Because attention and working memory are limited, people rely on heuristics, schemas, stereotypes, and other shortcuts when forming impressions and making judgments. The cognitive miser model replaced an earlier "naïve scientist" picture, in which people were assumed to weigh evidence carefully like intuitive statisticians.

Developments

Later research refined the model into the "motivated tactician": people flexibly choose between effortful and effortless processing depending on motivation, accountability, and the importance of the outcome. Dual-process theories, such as the heuristic-systematic model and the elaboration likelihood model, formalize this choice between shallow and deep processing.

Implications

The concept helps explain cognitive biases, stereotyping, and susceptibility to persuasion, and it underlies practical interventions in education, health communication, and decision-making that either reduce cognitive load or motivate deeper thought.
