Pythagorean theorem

The Pythagorean theorem states that in a right triangle, the square of the length of the hypotenuse (the side opposite the right angle) equals the sum of the squares of the lengths of the other two sides. If the legs have lengths a and b and the hypotenuse has length c, the theorem is written:

a² + b² = c²

History

Knowledge of Pythagorean triples long predates Pythagoras: Babylonian tablets such as Plimpton 322 (c. 1800 BCE) list sets of integers satisfying the relation, and the theorem was used in ancient Egypt, India (in the Śulba Sūtras), and China (as the Gougu theorem in the Zhoubi Suanjing). The theorem is named for the Greek mathematician Pythagoras (c. 570–495 BCE), to whom tradition attributes the first proof, though no writings of his school survive. A rigorous proof appears as Proposition 47 of Book I of Euclid's Elements (c. 300 BCE).

Proofs

Hundreds of proofs are known — Elisha Scott Loomis's 1927 book The Pythagorean Proposition collected 371. They include proofs by rearrangement of areas, algebraic proofs using similarity, Euclid's "windmill" proof, a proof by U.S. President James A. Garfield (1876) using a trapezoid, and calculus and trigonometric proofs. In 2023, high-school students Ne'Kiya Jackson and Calcea Johnson presented a novel proof based on trigonometry and the law of sines.

Consequences and generalizations

The theorem yields the distance formula in coordinate geometry, the notion of Pythagorean triples, and the irrationality-related result that a unit square's diagonal is √2. Its converse is also true: a triangle whose sides satisfy the equation is necessarily right-angled. Generalizations include the law of cosines for arbitrary triangles, the extension to inner product spaces (the parallelogram law and Parseval's identity), and non-Euclidean analogues in spherical and hyperbolic geometry.
