Anaemia (also spelled anemia in American English) is a blood disorder in which the blood has a reduced ability to carry oxygen. This can be due to a lower than normal number of red blood cells, a reduction in the amount of haemoglobin or packed red blood cells. The World Health Organization defines anaemia by haemoglobin thresholds of below 13.0 g/dL in men, 12.0 g/dL in non-pregnant women, and 11.0 g/dL in pregnancy.

Signs and symptoms

Anaemia often develops slowly and may cause no symptoms at first. When present, symptoms include fatigue, weakness, pallor of the skin and conjunctivae, shortness of breath on exertion, rapid heartbeat, headache, and dizziness. Severe or rapidly developing anaemia can cause chest pain, fainting, and heart failure. Particular causes may add distinctive signs, such as pica (craving for non-food substances) in iron deficiency, or jaundice in haemolytic anaemias.

Causes

Causes are conventionally grouped into three mechanisms: blood loss (acute or chronic, e.g. gastrointestinal bleeding or menstruation); decreased red blood cell production (iron deficiency — the commonest cause worldwide — vitamin B12 or folate deficiency, bone marrow disorders such as aplastic anaemia, chronic kidney disease, and anaemia of chronic inflammation); and increased red blood cell destruction (haemolysis, as in sickle cell disease, thalassaemia, and autoimmune haemolytic anaemia). Anaemias are also classified by red cell size (mean corpuscular volume) as microcytic, normocytic, or macrocytic.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is by a full blood count; further tests such as ferritin, serum iron studies, vitamin B12, folate, and reticulocyte count identify the cause. Treatment addresses the underlying cause — oral or intravenous iron, vitamin replacement, erythropoiesis-stimulating agents, treatment of bleeding, or blood transfusion for severe cases.

Epidemiology

Anaemia affects roughly a quarter to a third of the world's population — about 1.6–2 billion people — disproportionately young children and women of reproductive age, and is a major contributor to maternal mortality and impaired childhood development in low-income countries.
