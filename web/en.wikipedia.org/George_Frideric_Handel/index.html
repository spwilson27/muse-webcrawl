George Frideric Handel

George Frideric Handel
Born: 5 March 1685, Halle, Duchy of Magdeburg
Died: 14 April 1759, London, England
Occupation: Composer
Notable works: Messiah, Water Music, Music for the Royal Fireworks

George Frideric Handel (born Georg Friedrich Händel; 5 March 1685 – 14 April 1759) was a German-British Baroque composer well known for his operas, oratorios, anthems, concerti grossi, and organ concertos. Handel received his training in Halle and worked as a composer in Hamburg and Italy before settling in London in 1712, where he spent the bulk of his career and became a naturalised British subject in 1727. His best-known work, the oratorio Messiah with its "Hallelujah" chorus, is among the most popular works in choral music.

Life

Handel was born in Halle in the same year as Johann Sebastian Bach and Domenico Scarlatti. His father, a barber-surgeon, discouraged music, but the boy practised in secret on a clavichord in the attic; Duke Johann Adolf I of Saxe-Weissenfels heard him play and persuaded his father to allow lessons. After working in Hamburg, where his opera Almira was staged in 1705, he spent four formative years in Italy composing sacred music and operas. In 1710 he became Kapellmeister to the Elector of Hanover, the future King George I of Great Britain, and soon moved to London, where Italian opera was in vogue.

London career

In London Handel composed for the Royal Academy of Music and later companies, writing more than forty operas including Giulio Cesare (1724), Rodelinda (1725), and Alcina (1735). As public taste turned against Italian opera, he reinvented himself with English oratorios — unstaged choral dramas such as Saul, Israel in Egypt, and Messiah, composed in about three weeks in 1741 and first performed in Dublin in 1742. For royal occasions he wrote the Water Music (1717), performed on the Thames for George I, the coronation anthem Zadok the Priest (1727), sung at every British coronation since, and the Music for the Royal Fireworks (1749).

Death and legacy

Handel's health failed in his last decade and he went blind, but he continued to direct performances. He died in London on 14 April 1759 and was buried in Westminster Abbey with state honours, attended by some 3,000 mourners. Revered by Haydn, Mozart, and Beethoven — who called him the master of them all — Handel has never left the repertory, and works such as the concerti grossi, Op. 6, remain staples of Baroque performance.
