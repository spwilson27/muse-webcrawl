Tautomer

Tautomers (from Greek tauto-, "the same", and -mer, "part") are structural isomers of chemical compounds that readily interconvert. The chemical reaction interconverting the two is called tautomerization. This conversion commonly results from the relocation of a hydrogen atom within the compound, accompanied by a switch of adjacent single and double bonds.

Keto–enol tautomerism

The best-known example is the equilibrium between a ketone or aldehyde (the keto form) and an enol. In most simple ketones, such as acetone, the keto form vastly predominates at equilibrium. In 1,3-dicarbonyl compounds such as acetylacetone, the enol form is stabilized by conjugation and intramolecular hydrogen bonding and may predominate. Tautomerism is central to the chemistry of sugars, where ring-chain tautomerism governs the open-chain and cyclic forms of glucose.

Other examples

Other common tautomeric pairs include imine–enamine, lactam–lactim, and amide–imidic acid systems. The nucleobases of DNA and RNA exist in tautomeric forms; rare tautomerization events change the hydrogen-bonding pattern of a base and can cause point mutations during replication, a mechanism proposed by Watson and Crick. Taurine chemistry and heterocyclic drugs such as warfarin also involve tautomeric equilibria.

Distinguishing features

Tautomers are distinct chemical species with different connectivity, unlike resonance structures, which are alternative depictions of a single species. Because interconversion is usually fast, tautomers are typically studied as an equilibrium mixture by spectroscopy rather than isolated individually.
