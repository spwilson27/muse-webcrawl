Trilobite

Trilobites are among the best-known fossil arthropods of the Palaeozoic seas.

Temporal rangeEarly Cambrian – Late Permian (521–251.9 million years ago)
PhylumArthropoda
Described speciesOver 22,000
Size rangeUnder 1 mm to over 70 cm

Trilobites (meaning "three lobes") are extinct marine arthropods. They are one of the earliest groups of arthropods to appear in the fossil record. Trilobites first evolved in the early Cambrian period and became widely distributed. They were the dominant marine fauna of the Palaeozoic era, and their last surviving members finally became extinct in the mass extinction at the end of the Permian period, around 251.9 million years ago.

Anatomy

The trilobite body is divided longitudinally into three lobes—a raised central (axial) lobe and two pleural lobes—and transversely into a head shield (cephalon), a segmented thorax, and a tail shield (pygidium). The calcified exoskeleton was shed repeatedly as the animal grew, which accounts for the abundance of trilobite fossils. Their compound eyes, built of calcite lenses, are among the earliest sophisticated visual systems known. Many trilobites could roll into a ball (enrollment) for protection.

Ecology and fossils

Trilobites occupied niches from deep water to shallow reefs: most crawled on or burrowed in the sea floor as deposit feeders or predators, while some swam or floated in the plankton. Because they were widespread, rapidly evolving and readily fossilised, trilobites serve as key index fossils for dating Palaeozoic rocks. Famous deposits include the Burgess Shale, the Wheeler Shale of Utah, and Moroccan Devonian localities.
