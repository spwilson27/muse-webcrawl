Monday demonstrations in East Germany

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Monday demonstrations (German: Montagsdemonstrationen) were a series of peaceful political protests against the government of the German Democratic Republic (East Germany) that took place every Monday evening, most famously in Leipzig, beginning on 4 September 1989 and continuing into 1990. They were a central element of the Peaceful Revolution that led to the fall of the Berlin Wall and German reunification.

Origins

The demonstrations grew out of Monday prayers for peace (Friedensgebete) held at the Nikolaikirche (St. Nicholas Church) in Leipzig since the early 1980s. As discontent with the regime grew in 1989 — fuelled by fraudulent local elections in May and a mass exodus of East Germans via Hungary — the prayer meetings spilled into street protests demanding free elections, freedom of travel, and freedom of the press.

Growth and the turning point of 9 October

Numbers grew from hundreds in September to tens of thousands by October. On 9 October 1989, some 70,000 people marched in Leipzig despite fears of a violent crackdown; security forces stood down, and the evening passed without bloodshed. This non-violent breakthrough is often seen as the decisive moment of the revolution. Demonstrations peaked on 6 November with around half a million participants in Leipzig alone, and similar protests spread to many other East German cities.

Slogans and legacy

The best-known chant was "Wir sind das Volk" ("We are the people"), asserting popular sovereignty against a state that claimed to rule in the people's name; after the Wall fell it sometimes changed to "Wir sind ein Volk" ("We are one people"), favouring reunification. The Monday demonstrations have since become a symbol of civil courage and non-violent resistance in Germany, commemorated annually in Leipzig.
