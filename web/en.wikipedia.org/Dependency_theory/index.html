Dependency theory

Dependency theory is a body of social science theories, developed principally in Latin America from the late 1950s through the 1970s, which hold that the poverty and underdevelopment of poorer nations (the "periphery") is a direct consequence of the way they have been integrated into the world economic system controlled by wealthy nations (the "core"). Rather than treating underdevelopment as an original condition to be overcome by modernization, dependency theorists argue that development and underdevelopment are two sides of the same historical process: the enrichment of the core through trade, investment and colonial extraction actively produced the underdevelopment of the periphery.

Origins

The theory emerged from the work of Raúl Prebisch and the economists of the United Nations Economic Commission for Latin America (ECLAC) in the late 1940s. Prebisch observed that the terms of trade for primary commodities exported by Latin America tended to deteriorate relative to manufactured goods imported from industrial countries, meaning peripheral economies had to export ever more raw material to buy the same quantity of manufactures. With Hans Singer, this became the Prebisch–Singer hypothesis. Prebisch advocated import substitution industrialization (ISI), in which peripheral states would protect infant industries behind tariffs and produce manufactured goods domestically rather than importing them.

Radical dependency

A more radical school — including André Gunder Frank, Theotônio dos Santos, Ruy Mauro Marini and Samir Amin — argued that ISI could not overcome underdevelopment because peripheral elites were themselves tied to foreign capital. Frank's famous formulation described a "development of underdevelopment": satellite economies were drawn into a chain of surplus extraction running from the remotest peasant community through provincial towns to national capitals and on to the metropolitan centres of Europe and the United States. Frank held that Latin America had been underdeveloped precisely since its incorporation into the world capitalist system in the sixteenth century, not because it remained feudal or isolated.

Key concepts

Central concepts include the core (industrialized, capital-exporting states), the periphery (exporters of primary products dependent on core markets and capital), and later the semi-periphery. Mechanisms of dependency include unequal exchange, profit repatriation by multinational corporations, technological dependence, financial dependence on foreign loans, and the political alliance between foreign capital and local elites — sometimes called the " comprador" class. Dos Santos defined dependence as a situation in which the economy of certain countries is conditioned by the development and expansion of another economy to which the former is subjected.

Influence and criticism

Dependency theory profoundly influenced world-systems theory, associated with Immanuel Wallerstein, and shaped the demands of developing countries for a New International Economic Order in the 1970s. A moderate variant, "associated-dependent development" developed by Fernando Henrique Cardoso and Enzo Faletto, accepted that growth could occur within dependence but argued it remained distorted and exclusionary. Critics objected that the theory was too deterministic, neglected internal class structures and state policy, and could not explain the rapid industrialization of the East Asian "tiger" economies within the capitalist world market. From the 1980s, with the debt crisis and the turn to neoliberal policies, dependency theory declined in academic fashion, though its core questions about global inequality continue to inform debates on globalization and development.
