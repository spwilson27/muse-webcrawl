Ant colony optimization algorithms

In computer science and operations research, the ant colony optimization algorithm (ACO) is a probabilistic technique for solving computational problems that can be reduced to finding good paths through graphs. It belongs to the swarm intelligence family of methods and was introduced by Marco Dorigo in his 1992 PhD thesis, inspired by the foraging behaviour of real ant colonies.

Biological inspiration

Ants deposit a chemical substance, pheromone, on the ground as they travel. Other ants are more likely to follow paths with stronger pheromone, so shorter routes to food accumulate pheromone faster and come to dominate — a positive-feedback process called stigmergy. Because pheromone also evaporates, neglected trails fade, allowing the colony to adapt when conditions change.

The algorithm

In ACO, a population of artificial "ants" constructs solutions step by step on a graph. Each ant chooses its next component probabilistically, favouring choices with high pheromone levels and good heuristic desirability. After solutions are completed, pheromone is updated: it evaporates at a fixed rate, and ants that found better solutions deposit more pheromone on the components they used. Iteration progressively concentrates the search on high-quality solutions.

Applications

ACO was first applied to the travelling salesman problem and has since been used for vehicle routing, scheduling, network routing, protein folding, and machine-learning tasks such as feature selection. Variants include the original Ant System, MAX–MIN Ant System and Ant Colony System, which differ in how pheromone updating and exploration are controlled.
