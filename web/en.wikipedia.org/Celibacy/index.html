Celibacy

Celibacy (from Latin caelibatus) is the state of voluntarily being unmarried, sexually abstinent, or both, usually for religious reasons. It is often associated with the role of a religious official or devotee.

Religious traditions

In Roman Catholicism, celibacy is mandatory for priests of the Latin Church and for bishops, and is a defining feature of monastic life. Eastern Orthodox, Anglican, and Oriental Orthodox rules vary, generally permitting married parish clergy but requiring celibate bishops. Celibacy is also central to Buddhist monasticism (bhikkhus and bhikkhunis), Hindu sannyasa, and Jain monastic orders, where it is understood as a means of spiritual discipline and detachment.

History

Although voluntary celibacy was practised in early Christianity, clerical celibacy was progressively mandated in the Latin West during the Gregorian Reform of the 11th century and confirmed at the Lateran Councils. The Protestant Reformation largely rejected mandatory clerical celibacy.

Other contexts

Outside religion, the term is applied to voluntary abstinence from marriage or sexual relations for personal, philosophical, or practical reasons, and is distinguished from chastity, which concerns conduct within one's state of life.
