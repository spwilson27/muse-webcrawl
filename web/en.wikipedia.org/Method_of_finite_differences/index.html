Method of finite differences

In numerical analysis, finite-difference methods (FDM) are a class of numerical techniques for solving differential equations by approximating derivatives with finite differences. The spatial domain and time intervals (if any) are discretised, most simply on a regular grid, and the unknown function values at grid points are found by solving algebraic equations containing the finite-difference approximations.

Derivation

From Taylor's theorem, the first derivative can be approximated by the forward difference (f(x+h) − f(x))/h, the backward difference, or the more accurate central difference (f(x+h) − f(x−h))/(2h), with truncation errors of order h, h and h² respectively. Combinations give approximations for second and higher derivatives; substituting them into an ordinary or partial differential equation turns it into a system of equations for the grid values.

Explicit and implicit schemes

For time-dependent problems, explicit schemes such as forward-time central-space compute each new value directly but are only conditionally stable (for diffusion, subject to a limit on the time step relative to the grid spacing). Implicit schemes such as backward Euler and the Crank-Nicolson method solve a linear system each step but can be unconditionally stable. Analysis of consistency, stability and convergence — linked by the Lax equivalence theorem — plus the CFL condition for wave problems, determines whether a scheme is trustworthy.

Applications

Finite differences are used for heat conduction, wave propagation, fluid flow, option pricing via the Black-Scholes equation, and many other problems, and are the basis of most finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) codes in electromagnetics.
