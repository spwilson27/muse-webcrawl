Community policing

StrategyProactive partnership between police and community
Emergence1970s–1980s, United States and United Kingdom
Key elementsCommunity partnership, organizational transformation, problem solving
RelatedProblem-oriented policing, broken windows theory

Community policing is a philosophy and organizational strategy of law enforcement that emphasizes building ties and working closely with members of the communities police serve. Rather than focusing solely on responding to calls and enforcing laws after crimes occur, community policing stresses proactive problem solving, permanent assignment of officers to neighborhoods, and partnerships with residents, businesses, and other agencies to address the conditions that contribute to crime and disorder.

An officer on foot patrol engaging with residents

Origins

The model emerged in reaction to the perceived failures of the mid-twentieth-century "professional" policing model, which emphasized motorized patrol, rapid response, and centralized command but often isolated officers from the public. Research in the 1970s — including the Kansas City Preventive Patrol Experiment — questioned the deterrent value of random patrol, while rising crime and strained police–community relations prompted reformers to seek alternatives. Foot patrol experiments, notably in Flint, Michigan, suggested that visible neighborhood officers improved residents' sense of safety.

Core components

  • Community partnerships — collaborative relationships with individuals, community groups,nonprofits, and other government agencies.
  • Organizational transformation — decentralization, flatter structures, and long-term geographic assignment of officers ("beat integrity").
  • Problem solving — systematic identification and analysis of recurring problems (often using the SARA model: scanning, analysis, response, assessment) rather than incident-by-incident reaction.

Adoption and evaluation

Community policing was widely adopted in the United States in the 1990s, supported by the federal Office of Community Oriented Policing Services (COPS) created by the 1994 Crime Act, which funded the hiring of many thousands of officers. Similar approaches spread internationally under labels such as neighborhood policing in England and Wales and the kōban system in Japan. Evaluations find consistent benefits for public satisfaction and perceived legitimacy, with more modest and mixed effects on measured crime; critics argue the term is often applied loosely to conventional programs and can mask expanded surveillance of communities.
