Indian Rebellion of 1857

The Indian Rebellion of 1857 was a major, though ultimately unsuccessful, uprising in India in 1857–58 against the rule of the British East India Company. It began as a mutiny of sepoys in the company's army at Meerut on 10 May 1857 and erupted into wider rebellions across northern and central India, drawing in peasants, dispossessed landowners and regional rulers. In Indian nationalist historiography it is often called the First War of Independence; in British accounts, the Sepoy Mutiny or Great Rebellion.

Causes

Underlying causes included resentment at Company expansion and annexation under the doctrine of lapse, land revenue demands, missionary activity and military grievances. The immediate spark was the introduction of the Pattern 1853 Enfield rifle, whose cartridges were rumoured to be greased with cow and pig fat, offending both Hindu and Muslim soldiers.

Course

Rebels seized Delhi and proclaimed the aged Mughal emperor Bahadur Shah II as their figurehead. Major centres of resistance included Delhi, Lucknow, Kanpur and Jhansi, where Rani Lakshmibai became a celebrated leader. British and loyal forces retook Delhi in September 1857 and suppressed the remaining resistance by mid-1858, with severe reprisals on both sides marking the conflict, including the massacres at Kanpur and the British retribution that followed.

Consequences

The rebellion led directly to the end of Company rule: the Government of India Act 1858 transferred authority to the Crown, beginning the British Raj. The Mughal Empire was formally abolished, the Indian Army was reorganised to reduce the proportion and autonomy of sepoy units, and British policy became more cautious about interfering in Indian religion and custom.
