Divan

Type: Council chamber; couch; poetry collection

Origin: Persian dīvān (register, office)

Ottoman form: Imperial Council (Dîvân-ı Hümâyûn)

A divan (Persian: دیوان, dīvān) is a term with several related meanings derived from Middle Eastern governmental practice. Originally denoting a register or account book, and then the office or council that kept it, the word came to refer to a council chamber, to the long cushioned seat lining its walls, and — by extension — in European languages, to a kind of sofa. In literature, a diwan is a collection of poems by a single author.

Government council

In the early Islamic caliphates, the diwan was the government register of soldiers and revenues, institutionalized under Caliph Umar. In the Ottoman Empire, the Divan (Imperial Council) was the empire's chief governing body, presided over by the grand vizier and meeting at the Topkapı Palace in Istanbul, where petitioners could present grievances. Provincial governors across the Middle East and Mughal India likewise held courts called diwans, and the title dewan or diwan came to denote a chief minister or finance officer.

Furniture

European visitors encountered the raised, mattress-covered platforms with cushions that lined Ottoman council rooms and reception halls, and borrowed the word for similar seating at home. By the 18th and 19th centuries, a divan in English and French meant a low couch without a back or arms, or a long upholstered seat. Divan beds — a base with storage drawers and a mattress — preserve the term in modern furniture retail.

Poetry

In Persian, Arabic, Turkish, and Urdu literature, a diwan is the collected poems of an author, typically arranged alphabetically by rhyme. The Diwan of Hafez is among the most celebrated in Persian literature. Goethe's West–östlicher Divan (1819) was inspired by Hafez and introduced the term to a broad German readership.
