Charles Maurice de Talleyrand-Périgord, 1st Prince of Benevento and Prince of Talleyrand (2 February 1754 – 17 May 1838), was a French statesman and diplomat who served under the regimes of Louis XVI, the French Revolution, Napoleon I, Louis XVIII, Charles X, and Louis Philippe I — a record of political survival that made him both admired as a supple negotiator and condemned as an opportunist. He is generally considered one of the most versatile and influential diplomats in French history.

Early career and Revolution

A childhood injury that left him with a club foot barred a military career, so the young aristocrat was directed into the Church, becoming Bishop of Autun in 1788 despite little religious vocation. In the Estates-General of 1789 he sided with the Third Estate, proposed the nationalization of Church lands, helped draft the Declaration of the Rights of Man, and took the oath to the Civil Constitution of the Clergy, for which he was excommunicated. Diplomatic missions to London failed to prevent war, and he fled the Terror to the United States in 1794 — an exile shadowed by his central role, as foreign minister from 1797, in the XYZ Affair with America.

Napoleon and the Restoration

Talleyrand helped bring Napoleon to power in the coup of 18 Brumaire and served as his foreign minister until 1807, negotiating treaties across Europe, but he grew disillusioned, resigned, and intrigued against his master, advising Alexander I at Erfurt. After Napoleon's fall he represented France at the Congress of Vienna (1814–1815), where, invoking the principle of legitimacy, he famously salvaged France's borders and status despite its defeat, splitting the victorious allies and signing a secret treaty with Austria and Britain against Russia and Prussia.

Later years

Briefly prime minister in 1815, Talleyrand spent the Restoration in opposition, assisted the July Revolution of 1830, and ended his career as ambassador to London, where he helped secure Belgian independence. He died in Paris in 1838, reconciled with the Church on his deathbed. His celebrated maxims — "Speech was given to man to conceal his thoughts" and "Above all, not too much zeal" (Surtout, pas trop de zèle) — epitomize his diplomatic style.
