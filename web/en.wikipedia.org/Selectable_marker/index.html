Selectable marker

A selectable marker is a gene introduced into a cell, especially a bacterium or a cultured eukaryotic cell, which confers a trait suitable for artificial selection. Selectable markers are a vital part of genetic engineering experiments, allowing researchers to identify and retain only the cells that have successfully taken up the introduced DNA.

Types

The most common markers confer antibiotic resistance: for example, the ampicillin resistance gene (bla) and kanamycin/neomycin resistance genes on bacterial plasmids, and the puromycin, hygromycin, and G418 resistance genes in mammalian cell culture. Auxotrophic markers, such as URA3 or LEU2 in yeast, instead restore the ability of a deficient host strain to synthesize an essential nutrient.

Selection

After transformation or transfection, cells are grown in selective conditions — for instance, medium containing the antibiotic — so that only marker-bearing cells survive or grow. Selectable markers differ from reporter genes, such as GFP or luciferase, which make cells detectable but do not provide a growth advantage.

Concerns

The use of antibiotic-resistance markers in genetically modified crops has attracted regulatory scrutiny over the theoretical risk of horizontal gene transfer, prompting interest in marker-free transformation systems.
