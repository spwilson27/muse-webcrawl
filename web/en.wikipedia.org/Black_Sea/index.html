The Black Sea is a marginal mediterranean sea lying between Europe and Asia, east of the Balkans, south of the East European Plain, west of the Caucasus, and north of Anatolia. It is bounded by Ukraine, Russia, Georgia, Turkey, Bulgaria, and Romania, and is connected to the Mediterranean through the Bosphorus, the Sea of Marmara, and the Dardanelles.

Hydrology

Great rivers — the Danube, Dnieper, Don, and Dniester — pour fresh water into the sea, making its surface layer relatively low in salinity and less dense than the saltier water flowing in from the Mediterranean along the Bosphorus depths. The two layers mix very little: below about 150–200 m the Black Sea is anoxic, saturated with hydrogen sulfide and largely devoid of life, the largest such water body on Earth. The stable stratification also preserves ancient shipwrecks remarkably well on the sea floor.

History

Greek colonists founded cities around its shores in antiquity (calling it the Pontos Euxeinos, the "Hospitable Sea"), and it later formed a frontier and trade highway for Byzantines, Genoese, Ottomans, and Russians. Control of the straits was regulated by the 1936 Montreux Convention, which governs warship passage. The sea assumed renewed strategic prominence after Russia's annexation of Crimea in 2014 and the full-scale Russo-Ukrainian war from 2022, including disputes over grain exports through its ports.

Ecology and economy

The surface waters support fisheries for anchovy, sprat, and horse mackerel, though overfishing and invasive species — notably the comb jelly Mnemiopsis — caused major collapses in the late 20th century. The sea's coasts host major ports (Odesa, Novorossiysk, Constanța, Varna, Trabzon) and resorts, and its floor holds significant oil and gas reserves.
