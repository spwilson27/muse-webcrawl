Kitsune

A nine-tailed kitsune, messenger of the god Inari.

Kitsune (Japanese: 狐, "fox") are fox spirits of Japanese folklore, intelligent beings possessed of magical abilities that increase with age and wisdom. According to legend, the older and more powerful a kitsune becomes, the more tails it grows — up to nine — and a nine-tailed fox (kyūbi no kitsune) may attain near-limitless insight. Kitsune are famed shapeshifters, most often taking the form of beautiful women, and feature in tales as faithful guardians, lovers and wives as well as tricksters and deceivers.

Types and beliefs

Folklore distinguishes the benevolent zenko ("good foxes"), celestial messengers of the Shinto deity Inari Ōkami, from the mischievous field foxes, nogitsune. Kitsune are said to fear dogs, to be betrayed by a reflection or shadow that shows their true form, and to love abura-age (fried tofu) — hence the dish kitsune udon. Belief in fox possession, kitsunetsuki, was widespread in Japan into the modern era and was treated as an illness or a supernatural affliction.

In stories

Famous kitsune tales include Kuzunoha, the fox-wife who was mother of the diviner Abe no Seimei, and Tamamo-no-Mae, the beautiful courtesan revealed as a nine-tailed fox at the court of Emperor Toba. Fox statues guard Inari shrines throughout Japan, most famously at Fushimi Inari-taisha in Kyoto, often holding a key, a jewel or a scroll in their mouths.
