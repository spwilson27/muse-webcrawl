Auto racing (also known as car racing, motor racing, or automobile racing) is a motor sport involving the racing of automobiles for competition. Auto racing has existed since the invention of the automobile. Races of various sorts were organised, with the first recorded as early as 1867. Many of the earliest events were effectively reliability trials, aimed at proving these new machines were a practical mode of transport, but soon became an opportunity for competing makers to demonstrate their machines.

History

The first organised competition is commonly considered to be the 1894 Paris–Rouen trial. In 1895 the first true race took place, from Paris to Bordeaux and back. In the United States, an early milestone was the Chicago Times-Herald race of 1895. City-to-city racing in France and elsewhere led to purpose-built circuits such as Brooklands (1907) and Indianapolis Motor Speedway (1909), and to the formation of governing bodies culminating in the international federation, the FIA (in its modern form, 1946), and NASCAR in the United States (1948).

Categories

Major categories include open-wheel single-seater racing (headed by Formula One, and in North America IndyCar), stock car racing (NASCAR), sports car and endurance racing (including the 24 Hours of Le Mans), touring car racing, rallying on closed public roads, drag racing over short straight-line distances, kart racing, and historic racing. Cars may be purpose-built prototypes or based on production models.

Flags and safety

Racing is controlled by marshals using flags: green to start or resume, yellow for caution, red to stop, chequered to finish. Safety has been transformed since the dangerous post-war decades by roll cages, fuel cells, the HANS device, deformable structures and circuit redesign, driven largely by campaigns in Formula One after fatal accidents such as those of 1994 at Imola.
