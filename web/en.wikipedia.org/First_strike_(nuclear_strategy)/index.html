First strike (nuclear strategy)

In nuclear strategy, a first strike is a preemptive surprise attack employing overwhelming force, designed to destroy an enemy's nuclear weapons and delivery systems before they can be used, thereby limiting the damage of any retaliatory "second strike."

Strategic theory

First-strike planning emerged in the 1950s as the United States and Soviet Union built large arsenals of bombers and later intercontinental ballistic missiles (ICBMs). A successful counterforce first strike would require destroying the adversary's silos, submarine bases, airfields and command centres within minutes. Analysts debated whether such a strike could ever be fully effective, given hidden missile submarines and mobile launchers.

Stability and deterrence

The possibility of a first strike is central to deterrence theory. Forces that are vulnerable invite preemption in a crisis — creating "use them or lose them" pressure — whereas survivable forces, especially ballistic missile submarines, guarantee a second-strike capability and underpin mutually assured destruction (MAD). Arms control measures and innovations such as MIRVs were evaluated partly by their effect on first-strike stability. Some states, notably China and India, have adopted declared no first use policies.

Related concepts

A launch on warning posture attempts to fire retaliatory missiles before incoming warheads arrive, while a "decapitation" strike targets leadership and command-and-control rather than weapons themselves.
