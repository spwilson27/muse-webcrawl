David Crockett (August 17, 1786 – March 6, 1836) was an American folk hero, frontiersman, soldier, and politician. He is commonly referred to in popular culture as the "King of the Wild Frontier." He served in the United States Congress representing Tennessee and died at the Battle of the Alamo during the Texas Revolution.

Early life

Crockett was born in what is now Greene County, Tennessee, near the Nolichucky River, to John and Rebecca Crockett. He was the fifth of nine children and worked as an hired hand and herdsman in his youth after little formal schooling. He married Polly Finley in 1806 and settled in Lincoln County, Tennessee, where he farmed and hunted.

Military career

Crockett served as a scout in the Creek War of 1813–1814 under Andrew Jackson, participating in campaigns in Alabama. He rose to the rank of colonel of the Lawrence County militia. His frontier experience made him a popular figure in Tennessee politics.

Congress

Crockett was elected to the Tennessee state legislature in 1821 and to the U.S. House of Representatives in 1827. Known for his colorful speech and opposition to President Jackson's Indian Removal Act, he lost re-election in 1831, won again in 1833, and lost finally in 1835. Afterward he famously told voters they could "go to hell" while he went to Texas.

Texas and death

Crockett traveled to Texas in late 1835, arriving in San Antonio in February 1836. He joined the Texian defenders of the Alamo and was killed when Mexican forces under Antonio López de Santa Anna stormed the mission on March 6, 1836. Accounts of his death vary; some traditions hold he died fighting, while the de la Peña narrative suggests he was captured and executed.

Legacy

Crockett's almanacs, published during and after his lifetime, spread tall tales about his exploits. Disney's 1950s television series "Davy Crockett" sparked a massive popular culture craze, including the hit song "The Ballad of Davy Crockett." He remains an enduring symbol of American frontier mythology.
