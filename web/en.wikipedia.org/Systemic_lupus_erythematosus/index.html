Systemic lupus erythematosus

The characteristic butterfly (malar) rash of lupus across the cheeks and nose.

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), commonly called lupus, is an autoimmune disease in which the immune system attacks the body's own tissues, causing widespread inflammation and damage. It can affect the skin, joints, kidneys, blood cells, brain, heart, and lungs, and its course is marked by flares and remissions. The name comes from the Latin lupus ("wolf"), from the historical belief that the facial rash resembled a wolf's bite.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms vary widely between patients and over time. Common features include fatigue, fever, joint pain and swelling, the classic "butterfly" malar rash across the cheeks and bridge of the nose, photosensitivity, hair loss, mouth ulcers, Raynaud phenomenon, and chest pain. Serious organ involvement includes lupus nephritis (kidney inflammation, potentially leading to kidney failure), serositis, blood disorders (anaemia, low platelets), and neuropsychiatric manifestations. SLE occurs predominantly in women of childbearing age (about nine women to one man) and is more common and often more severe in people of African, Asian, and Hispanic ancestry.

Causes and mechanism

The cause is multifactorial: genetic susceptibility (including complement deficiencies and many risk loci), hormonal factors, and environmental triggers such as ultraviolet light, infections (e.g. Epstein–Barr virus), and certain drugs. Defective clearance of apoptotic cells exposes nuclear antigens; loss of immune tolerance produces autoantibodies — notably antinuclear antibodies (ANA) and anti-double-stranded DNA — and immune complexes deposit in tissues, driving inflammation largely via type III hypersensitivity.

Diagnosis

There is no single diagnostic test. Classification criteria (such as the 2019 EULAR/ACR criteria) combine clinical domains with immunological tests: ANA positivity, anti-dsDNA and anti-Smith antibodies, low complement levels, and findings such as biopsy-proven nephritis.

Treatment

There is no cure; treatment aims to control flares and prevent organ damage. Hydroxychloroquine is recommended for nearly all patients. Other agents include NSAIDs, corticosteroids, immunosuppressants (methotrexate, azathioprine, mycophenolate, cyclophosphamide), and biologics such as belimumab and anifrolumab. Prognosis has improved greatly, with 10-year survival now exceeding 90% in many cohorts, though infection and cardiovascular disease remain leading causes of death.
