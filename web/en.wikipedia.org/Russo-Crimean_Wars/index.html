The Russo-Crimean Wars were a series of conflicts over about two and a half centuries between the Tsardom of Russia (later the Russian Empire) and the Crimean Khanate, a Turkic state and vassal of the Ottoman Empire. The wars were driven above all by Crimean Tatar slave raids into Russian and Ukrainian lands and by Russia's long-term drive to secure its southern steppe frontier and access to the Black Sea.

The slave raids and the defence lines

From the early 1500s, Tatar raiding parties crossed the steppe (Dikoe Pole, the "Wild Fields") to capture slaves for the markets of Kaffa and Istanbul; historians estimate that hundreds of thousands, possibly millions, of East Slavs were enslaved over the period. Moscow responded with fortified defence lines (zasechnaya cherta) of felled-timber barriers, forts and patrols, gradually pushing the frontier south. In the greatest Tatar triumph, Khan Devlet I Giray burned Moscow in 1571, but his army was destroyed the following year at the Battle of Molodi.

Turning of the tide

As Russian military power grew, the balance shifted. Russia's Crimean expeditions of 1687 and 1689 failed, but Peter the Great took Azov in 1696, and 18th-century wars brought Russian armies into Crimea itself. The Treaty of Küçük Kaynarca (1774) made the Khanate formally independent of the Ottomans, and in 1783 Catherine the Great annexed Crimea outright.

Legacy

The annexation ended the centuries-long slave-raiding frontier, opened the northern Black Sea steppe to agricultural settlement ("Novorossiya"), and founded the Black Sea Fleet at Sevastopol — beginning a strategic contest over Crimea that has continued into the present day.
