United States Department of Justice

The United States Department of Justice (DOJ) is an executive department of the United States federal government responsible for the enforcement of the law and administration of justice in the United States. It is equivalent to the justice or interior ministries of other countries. The department is administered by the U.S. attorney general, who reports directly to the president of the United States and is a member of the president's Cabinet.

History

The Office of the Attorney General was created by the Judiciary Act of 1789 as a part-time job for one person, but it grew with the bureaucracy. At one time, the attorney general gave legal advice to the U.S. Congress as well as the president. The Department of Justice itself was established by an act signed by President Ulysses S. Grant on June 22, 1870, taking effect July 1 of that year. Amos T. Akerman served as the first attorney general under the new department, prosecuting members of the Ku Klux Klan during the early Reconstruction era.

Organization

The department's primary responsibility is ensuring public safety against threats foreign and domestic, providing federal leadership in preventing and controlling crime, seeking just punishment for unlawful behavior, and ensuring fair and impartial administration of justice for all Americans. Its components include the Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI), the Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA), the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives (ATF), the United States Marshals Service, the Federal Bureau of Prisons, and the Executive Office for United States Attorneys, which oversees the 94 United States Attorneys' offices across the country.

Leadership

The attorney general is nominated by the president and confirmed by the Senate. Below the attorney general are the deputy attorney general and the associate attorney general, followed by the solicitor general, who represents the federal government before the Supreme Court of the United States.
