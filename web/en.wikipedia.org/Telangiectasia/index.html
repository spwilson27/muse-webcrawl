Telangiectasia

Telangiectasia (also spelled telangiectasis, plural telangiectasias or telangiectases) refers to small, widened blood vessels — capillaries, arterioles, and venules typically 0.5 to 1 millimetre in diameter — visible near the surface of the skin or mucous membranes. On the legs they are commonly called "spider veins" or thread veins. Facial telangiectasias often appear on the nose, cheeks, and chin.

Causes

Telangiectasias may be congenital or acquired. Acquired causes include sun exposure, ageing, rosacea, topical corticosteroid use, hormonal influences, alcohol use, and venous hypertension. They are also a feature of several systemic and hereditary conditions, notably hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia (Osler–Weber–Rendu syndrome), in which fragile dilated vessels cause recurrent nosebleeds and bleeding in the gut and other organs; ataxia-telangiectasia, a genetic disorder of DNA repair; scleroderma and the CREST syndrome; and liver disease, where "spider naevi" have a central arteriole with radiating branches.

Treatment

Isolated telangiectasias are usually harmless and require treatment only for cosmetic reasons or when they bleed. Options include sclerotherapy (injection of an irritant solution) for leg veins, and laser therapy or intense pulsed light for facial vessels. Electrocautery is sometimes used. Treatment of an underlying condition, such as rosacea or venous insufficiency, may be needed to prevent recurrence.
