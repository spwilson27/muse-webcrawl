CompStat

CompStat is a police management and crime-reduction strategy based on the collection, computerised mapping, and analysis of crime statistics, coupled with managerial accountability for local commanders. It was developed by the New York City Police Department (NYPD) in 1994 under Commissioner William Bratton and deputy commissioner Jack Maple; the name is short for "computer statistics" (or "compare statistics").

Principles

CompStat rests on four principles: accurate and timely intelligence, effective tactics, rapid deployment of personnel and resources, and relentless follow-up and assessment. Precinct commanders attend regular — often weekly — meetings at headquarters where they are questioned closely about crime patterns in their areas and their responses, with computerised maps and statistics displayed.

Influence and criticism

The model was widely adopted by police departments in the United States and abroad and is associated with the sharp fall in recorded crime in New York during the 1990s, though criminologists debate how much of the decline it caused. Critics have argued that pressure to show falling numbers can encourage manipulation of statistics, downgrading of offences, and aggressive enforcement tactics.
