Christian Wolff (composer)

Christian Wolff (born 8 March 1934, in Nice, France) is an American composer of experimental classical music and a classicist. He is associated with the so-called New York School of composers — John Cage, Earle Brown, Morton Feldman and David Tudor — and is known for works that pioneer indeterminacy, open forms and cueing systems which make performers listen and respond to one another in real time.

Life

Wolff was born in Nice to German literary publishers Kurt and Helen Wolff, who fled Europe and settled in New York in 1941. As a teenager he took piano lessons with Grete Sultan and briefly studied composition with John Cage, whose circle he joined around 1950 — making him, alongside Earle Brown, Morton Feldman and David Tudor, a central member of the New York School. He read classics at Harvard (BA 1955, PhD 1963) and taught Greek and Latin, later comparative literature, at Harvard and then at Dartmouth College, where he became Strauss Professor of Music, composing throughout his academic career.

Music

Wolff's early works use limited pitch material, simple rhythmic notation and unconventional scores (such as the graphic and verbal "prose pieces" of the late 1960s, and collections like Prose Pieces (1968–71) and For 1, 2 or 3 People (1964)). His cueing pieces require performers to coordinate by listening rather than by following a conductor or fixed score, an approach sometimes described as fostering a kind of musical democracy. From the 1970s his music engaged more explicitly with political themes, incorporating folk and protest songs (for example in Wobbly Music and collaborations with Frederic Rzewski and Cornelius Cardew's circle). Later works include the large-scale John Heartfield (Peace March 10), string quartets, and music for the Merce Cunningham Dance Company.
