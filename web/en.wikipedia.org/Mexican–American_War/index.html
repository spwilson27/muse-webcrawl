Mexican–American War

Date25 April 1846 – 2 February 1848
LocationTexas, northern and central Mexico, California, New Mexico
ResultAmerican victory; Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo
TerritoryMexican Cession: California, Nevada, Utah, most of Arizona and New Mexico, parts of Colorado and Wyoming

The Mexican–American War (1846–1848), known in Mexico as the Intervención estadounidense en México, was an armed conflict between the United States and Mexico fought over the American annexation of Texas and the disputed boundary between the two countries. It ended with a decisive American victory and the transfer of a vast northern territory from Mexico to the United States.

Causes

Mexico had never accepted the independence of Texas, declared in 1836, and broke relations when the United States annexed Texas in 1845. President James K. Polk, an expansionist committed to "Manifest Destiny", claimed the Rio Grande as the border, while Mexico insisted on the Nueces River further north. When Mexican troops clashed with a US patrol under General Zachary Taylor in the disputed strip in April 1846 (the Thornton Affair), Polk told Congress that Mexico had "shed American blood upon the American soil", and Congress declared war on 13 May 1846. The war was controversial from the start: Whigs and abolitionists, including Congressman Abraham Lincoln with his "spot resolutions", denounced it as a war to extend slavery.

Campaigns

Taylor won battles at Palo Alto, Resaca de la Palma and Monterrey, and in February 1847 repelled Santa Anna at Buena Vista. Meanwhile Colonel Stephen Kearny seized New Mexico and California, where the Bear Flag Revolt and naval forces had already acted. The decisive campaign was General Winfield Scott's: an amphibious landing at Veracruz in March 1847 — the largest in American history to that date — followed by victories at Cerro Gordo, Contreras, Churubusco and Molino del Rey, and the storming of Chapultepec Castle in September 1847, defended in Mexican memory by the boy cadets, the Niños Héroes. Mexico City fell on 14 September 1847.

Outcome

Under the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo (2 February 1848), Mexico recognised the Rio Grande boundary and ceded about 1.36 million km² — present-day California, Nevada, Utah, most of Arizona and New Mexico, and parts of Colorado and Wyoming — in exchange for $15 million and the assumption of American claims. The discovery of gold in California days before the treaty sparked the Gold Rush. The war trained many officers who later led both sides in the American Civil War (Grant called it "one of the most unjust ever waged by a stronger against a weaker nation"), and the new territories reignited the slavery debate that led to that war. The United States also remembered the discontent of the Saint Patrick's Battalion, Irish-American deserters who fought for Mexico.
