Farm

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A farm (also called an agricultural holding) is an area of land that is devoted primarily to agricultural processes with the primary objective of producing food and other crops; it is the basic facility in food production. The name is used for specialised units such as arable farms, vegetable farms, fruit farms, dairy, pig and poultry farms, and land used for the production of natural fibres, biofuel and other commodities. It includes the farmhouse and agricultural buildings, and usually the surrounding land and any farm workers' housing.

History

Farming dates to the Neolithic Revolution, when humans in the Fertile Crescent and elsewhere began domesticating plants and animals around 12,000 years ago, allowing settled communities to replace nomadic hunting and gathering. Farms remained small, family-worked units for millennia. Enclosures, mechanisation from the 19th century, synthetic fertilisers, pesticides and plant breeding in the 20th-century Green Revolution transformed farming into a capital-intensive industry, while the average size of farms grew and their number shrank across industrialised countries.

Types and modern farming

Farms are commonly classified by what they produce: subsistence farms, which feed a family with little surplus; commercial crop and livestock farms; ranches for grazing animals; orchards and vineyards; and specialised operations such as fish farms, fur farms and dairy farms. Modern farms range from smallholdings of under a hectare to industrial operations of thousands of hectares, employing GPS-guided machinery, drones and data-driven management ("precision agriculture"). Farming occupies roughly half of the world's habitable land, making farm practices central to debates over food security, climate change, water use and biodiversity. Alternatives and complements to conventional practice include organic farming, permaculture and agrivoltaics.
