Boiler

A boiler is a closed vessel in which fluid (generally water) is heated. The fluid does not necessarily boil; the heated or vaporized fluid exits the boiler for use in various processes or heating applications, including water heating, central heating, power generation, sanitation, and cooking.

Types

In fire-tube boilers, hot combustion gases pass through tubes surrounded by the water to be heated; these are common in older designs and small installations. In water-tube boilers, water circulates inside tubes heated externally by the combustion gases, permitting much higher pressures and capacities, as in utility power stations. Boilers are also classified by fuel (gas, oil, coal, biomass, electric), by purpose (steam versus hot water), and by configuration, including packaged and condensing domestic units that recover heat from flue gases.

Operation and safety

A boiler system includes a feedwater system, steam or hot-water distribution, fuel and combustion controls, and safety devices such as pressure relief valves, low-water cutoffs, and pressure gauges. Feedwater is treated to limit scaling and corrosion. Because a pressurized steam boiler stores enormous energy, its failure can be explosive; historic disasters drove the development of inspection regimes and construction codes, most notably the ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code first issued in 1914.

Efficiency

Efficiency is expressed as the ratio of useful heat delivered to fuel energy input. Condensing boilers, which cool exhaust below the dew point of water vapor in the flue gas, can exceed 90% efficiency on a lower-heating-value basis, while older non-condensing units are typically around 70–85%.
