Stately home

Type: Large country house
Region: United Kingdom and Ireland
Era: 17th–20th centuries

A stately home is a large and grand house in the British Isles, especially one built in the 17th to 20th centuries as a country seat by a wealthy landowning family. Stately homes are often architecturally distinguished, set in extensive landscaped grounds, and historically served as the centre of a landed estate.

History

The great age of the stately home began after the dissolution of the monasteries under Henry VIII redistributed land to the aristocracy and gentry. During the 18th century, houses such as Blenheim Palace, Kedleston Hall and Harewood House were built or rebuilt in Palladian and Neoclassical styles, surrounded by parkland landscaped by figures such as Capability Brown.

In the Victorian era, agricultural wealth and industrial fortunes produced a further wave of building. The agricultural depression of the late 19th century, followed by inheritance taxes and the social upheavals of the two world wars, led to the demolition of hundreds of country houses in the 20th century.

Survival and heritage

Many surviving stately homes are now owned or managed by conservation bodies such as the National Trust and English Heritage, or remain private homes opened to the public. They are significant repositories of architecture, art collections, and social history, and form a major part of Britain's heritage tourism.
