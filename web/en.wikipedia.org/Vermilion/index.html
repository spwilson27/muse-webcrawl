Vermilion

Vermilion is a brilliant red or scarlet pigment originally made from the powdered mineral cinnabar (mercuric sulfide, HgS), and the name of the resulting vivid red-orange colour. It was one of the most important red pigments of classical antiquity, the Middle Ages, and the Renaissance, prized for its opacity and intensity despite its toxicity and high cost.

History

Cinnabar was mined in antiquity at Almadén in Spain and used by the Romans as a pigment and cosmetic; Pliny the Elder recorded its use and price. In China, vermilion was used on oracle bones, lacquerware, and as the red of seals and calligraphy — giving rise to the phrase "vermilion ink". Medieval manuscript illuminators used it lavishly, and Renaissance painters including Titian prized it for draperies and flesh tones. A synthetic process for making vermilion, combining mercury and sulfur, was known in China by early centuries CE and in Europe by the 8th century; the Dutch process of the 17th century made it more widely available.

Properties and decline

Vermilion is dense and opaque with strong tinting power, but it can darken over time on exposure to light and air, turning a brownish or greyish tone as the surface converts to black metacinnabar — a degradation visible in many old paintings. Because it contains mercury, vermilion is toxic, and from the 19th and 20th centuries it was progressively replaced in artists' paints by cadmium red pigments. Genuine vermilion is still made for traditional uses, including Japanese and Chinese painting and the restoration of historic works.

Cultural use

In India, vermilion powder (sindoor) is worn by married Hindu women along the hair parting and used in religious offerings. In Japan, vermilion lacquer colours shrine gates (torii) and temples, such as the shrines of Nikko.
