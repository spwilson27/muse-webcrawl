Kibble–Zurek mechanism

The Kibble–Zurek mechanism (KZM) describes the formation of topological defects when a system is driven through a continuous phase transition at finite rate. It combines Tom Kibble's work (1976) on defect formation in the early universe with Wojciech Zurek's later proposal (1985) applying the same ideas to condensed matter systems.

Mechanism

Near a critical point, a system's relaxation time diverges (critical slowing down). When the transition is crossed at a finite rate, the system falls out of equilibrium at a "freeze-out" time; the correlation length at that moment sets the typical size of independently ordered domains. Where domains meet, topological defects — such as cosmic strings, vortices or dislocations — can be trapped. The mechanism predicts a power-law scaling of defect density with quench rate.

Tests

KZM scaling has been tested in liquid crystals, superfluid helium, superconductors, Bose–Einstein condensates, trapped ions and quantum simulators, and it underlies the analysis of errors in quantum annealing and adiabatic quantum computation.
