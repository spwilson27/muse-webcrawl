Habitus

Habitus is a concept in sociology, most famously developed by the French sociologist Pierre Bourdieu, referring to the deeply ingrained habits, skills, dispositions and ways of perceiving the world that individuals acquire through their life experiences, especially their upbringing and social position. Habitus shapes how people act, think and respond to their social environment, often without conscious reflection.

Origins of the concept

The term derives from Latin and has a long history in philosophy. It was used by Aristotle (as hexis), by medieval scholastics such as Thomas Aquinas, and later by sociologists including Émile Durkheim, Marcel Mauss — who described "techniques of the body" — Max Weber, Norbert Elias and Edmund Husserl. Bourdieu revived and systematised the concept in works such as Outline of a Theory of Practice (1972) and The Logic of Practice (1980).

Bourdieu's formulation

Bourdieu defined habitus as a system of durable, transposable dispositions: structured structures that function as structuring structures. In other words, habitus is produced by social conditions — family background, education, class position — and in turn generates practices, tastes and perceptions that tend to reproduce those conditions. It explains, for example, why members of different social classes often exhibit distinctive tastes in food, art, sport and speech.

Habitus operates below the level of conscious calculation. People raised in a particular milieu develop a practical "feel for the game" of social life: a sense of what is appropriate, possible or "for them". When a person's habitus encounters a social field for which it was not formed, the mismatch is called hysteresis, which can produce discomfort or maladaptation.

Relation to capital and field

Habitus is one element of Bourdieu's theoretical triad, alongside capital (economic, cultural, social and symbolic resources) and field (structured social arenas such as academia, art or politics). Practice, in Bourdieu's famous formula, results from the relation between habitus, capital and field.

Criticism

Critics have accused the concept of determinism, arguing that it leaves little room for agency and social change. Others find the definition elusive or circular. Defenders respond that habitus is generative and improvisational rather than mechanically determining, and that it can change, albeit slowly, in response to new experiences.
