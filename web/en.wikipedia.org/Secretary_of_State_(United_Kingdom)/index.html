Secretary of State (United Kingdom)


Whitehall, London

In the United Kingdom, a Secretary of State is a senior minister of the Crown who heads a government department. Secretaries of State are appointed by the monarch on the advice of the Prime Minister and are members of the Cabinet. Legally, there is in principle a single office of Secretary of State — "one of His Majesty's Principal Secretaries of State" — so that acts of any Secretary of State are formally interchangeable; in practice, each secretary has a defined portfolio such as Foreign Secretary, Home Secretary, or Secretary of State for Defence.

History

The office descends from the king's clerks and the principal secretary of Tudor times. By the late seventeenth century there were two secretaries, for the Northern and Southern Departments; the Foreign Office was created in 1782 and the Home Office assumed domestic business. Additional secretaryships followed as government expanded — for war, the colonies, India, and later the modern departments of the twentieth century.

Modern offices

Today there are more than a dozen secretaries of state, including the Secretary of State for Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Affairs, the Secretary of State for the Home Department, and the secretaries for Defence, Health and Social Care, Education, and the territorial offices for Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland. Each heads a department staffed by junior ministers and civil servants led by a permanent secretary.

Constitutional position

Secretaries of State are accountable to Parliament for their departments under the doctrine of ministerial responsibility. Most are Members of Parliament; occasionally a secretary sits in the House of Lords. The foreign secretary works closely with agencies such as the Secret Intelligence Service.
