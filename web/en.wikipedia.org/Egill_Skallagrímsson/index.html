Egill Skallagrímsson

Born: c. 904, Iceland

Died: c. 995, Mosfellsbær, Iceland

Occupation: Skald, warrior, farmer, chieftain

Known for: Egils saga; Höfuðlausn; Sonatorrek

Father: Skalla-Grímr Kveldúlfsson

Egill Skallagrímsson (Old Norse: [ˈeɣelː ˈskalːaˌɡriːmsˌson]; Modern Icelandic: [ˈeijɪtl̥ ˈskatlaˌkrimsˌsɔːn]; c. 904 – c. 995) was a Viking Age poet, warrior, and farmer. He is remembered as one of the greatest skalds of Iceland and is the protagonist of Egils saga, one of the most celebrated of the Icelandic family sagas, which recounts his life from childhood to old age and preserves many poems attributed to him.

Life

Egill was the son of Skalla-Grímr ("Bald Grímr"), one of the early settlers of Iceland, and grandson of Kveldúlfr ("Evening Wolf"). The saga portrays him as ugly, violent, and prodigiously talented: he is said to have composed his first verse at the age of three and killed his first man at seven. After conflicts with King Eiríkr Bloodaxe of Norway — in which Egill's brother Þórólfr was killed in the king's service and Egill outlawed — he travelled widely in Scandinavia and England.

His most famous exploit occurred at York, where, captured by his enemy Eiríkr Bloodaxe, he composed the praise poem Höfuðlausn ("Head-Ransom") overnight and recited it to the king, who freed him in exchange for the poem — literally buying back his head with verse. Egill also fought at the Battle of Brunanburh in the service of King Æthelstan of England.

Poetry

Three long poems are attributed to Egill: Höfuðlausn, Sonatorrek ("The Loss of Sons"), a wrenching lament composed after the deaths of his sons Böðvarr and Gunnarr, and Arinbjarnarkviða, in praise of his lifelong friend Arinbjörn Þórisson. Sonatorrek in particular is regarded as one of the supreme achievements of Old Norse poetry, blending pagan mythology with raw personal grief. Numerous individual stanzas (lausavísur) are also preserved in the saga.

Later years and legacy

Egill lived to a great age at Borg in western Iceland, becoming blind and infirm, and is said to have hidden his silver treasure — the saga relates that he rode out one night with two slaves and chests of silver and was never seen to return with them; the slaves were killed and the hoard never found. His remains, according to the saga, were later moved to the church at Mosfell. Egill remains a central figure of Icelandic national culture, embodying the union of the poet and the warrior.
