Folivore

In zoology, a folivore is a herbivore that specialises in eating leaves. Mature leaves contain a high proportion of hard-to-digest cellulose, less energy than most other foods, and often contain toxic compounds. For this reason, folivorous animals tend to have long digestive tracts and slow metabolisms. Many enlist the aid of symbiotic bacteria to release the nutrients in their diet, and they often devote long periods to rest and digestion.

Adaptations

Folivores cope with the low quality and chemical defences of leaves in several ways: enlarged hindgut or foregut fermentation chambers housing microbes, as in koalas, sloths, and colobus monkeys; detoxification by the liver; and selective feeding on young, less fibrous leaves. Leaves' low energy yield favours energy-conserving lifestyles — sloths and koalas sleep for much of the day — and relatively small brains in some lineages.

Examples

Mammalian folivores include koalas, which feed almost entirely on eucalyptus; sloths; many primates such as howler monkeys and colobines; and the okapi and some possums. Among other groups, the hoatzin is a folivorous bird with foregut fermentation, iguanas are largely folivorous reptiles, and many insects, such as caterpillars and leaf beetles, feed on leaves.
