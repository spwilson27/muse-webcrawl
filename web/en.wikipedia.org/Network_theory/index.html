In mathematics, computer science and network science, network theory is a part of graph theory: it studies graphs as a representation of either symmetric relations or asymmetric relations between discrete objects. In computer science and network science, network theory is part of graph theory: it studies networks as graphs of nodes connected by edges, and is concerned with the structure, behaviour and evolution of real-world networks.

History

The origins lie in Leonhard Euler's 1736 solution to the Seven Bridges of Königsberg problem, the founding result of graph theory. In the 20th century, sociologists developed social network analysis (Jacob Moreno's sociograms, Stanley Milgram's "six degrees" small-world experiment), and Paul Erdős and Alfréd Rényi formalised random graphs. The modern science of networks was transformed in the late 1990s by the Watts–Strogatz small-world model (1998) and the Barabási–Albert scale-free model (1999), which showed that many real networks — from the World Wide Web to protein interactions — share universal structural features such as hubs and heavy-tailed degree distributions.

Measures and concepts

Network analysis uses measures such as degree, betweenness, closeness and eigenvector centrality to find important nodes; clustering coefficients and path lengths to describe cohesion; and community detection to identify modules. Percolation, epidemic spreading (SIR-type models) and network resilience are central applied topics.

Applications

Network theory is applied across the sciences: mapping epidemics and information spread, designing robust infrastructure, analysing financial contagion, neuroscience (connectomes), ecology (food webs) and the organisation of the internet itself.
