Kumis


Kumis is a fermented drink made from mare's milk, traditional in Central Asia.

Kumis (also spelled koumiss, from Turkic qımız) is a fermented dairy product traditionally made from mare's milk by the nomadic peoples of the Central Asian steppes, including the Kyrgyz, Kazakhs, Mongols and Bashkirs. It is known as airag in Mongolian.

Production

Raw mare's milk is allowed to ferment over hours or days, traditionally in a leather sack or wooden churn and stirred frequently with a special paddle. Lactic acid bacteria and yeasts convert lactose into lactic acid, carbon dioxide and a small amount of alcohol (typically around 0.7–2.5%), giving kumis a mildly sour, fizzy character. During the milking season large quantities may be consumed daily, and the drink holds a ceremonial place in Kyrgyz and Kazakh culture; Bishkek's name derives from the paddle used to churn kumis.

History

Fermented mare's milk is attested in antiquity — Herodotus describes the Scythians processing mare's milk, and William of Rubruck gave a detailed account of kumis-making among the Mongols in the 13th century. In the 19th century kumis was used in "kumis cures" at sanatoria treating tuberculosis in Russia.
