Bulla (seal)

A bulla (plural bullae) is a lump of clay, or sometimes metal or wax, that was used in the ancient Near East and later administrations to seal documents, containers, or doors and to record transactions. The word is Latin for a round swelling or bubble, and the same term gives us the "papal bull", from the lead seal attached to papal documents.

Ancient Near East

In Mesopotamia and Iran, hollow clay balls enclosing small tokens were used from around 8000 BCE as an accounting device; their surfaces bore seal impressions identifying the parties. Later, solid bullae were pressed onto cords tying documents or fastening jar stoppers and were stamped with cylinder or stamp seals.

Biblical archaeology

Clay bullae bearing Hebrew inscriptions have been found in excavations in Jerusalem and elsewhere in Israel and Jordan. Famous examples name officials known from the Bible, including a bulla of Hezekiah, king of Judah, and bullae attributed to scribes in the circle of Jeremiah.

Later usage

In the medieval Byzantine and papal chanceries, lead bullae authenticated documents: the papal bulla was a lead disc stamped with the heads of Saints Peter and Paul on one side and the pope's name on the other. The practice gave its name to the most solemn form of papal decree.
