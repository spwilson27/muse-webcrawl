Hasse diagram

In order theory, a Hasse diagram is a type of mathematical diagram used to represent a finite partially ordered set, in the form of a drawing of its transitive reduction. Specifically, for a partially ordered set (S, ≤) one represents each element of S as a vertex in the plane and draws a line segment or curve upward from x to y whenever x < y and there is no z with x < z < y (that is, whenever x is covered by y). These curves may cross but may not touch vertices other than at their endpoints. Such a diagram, with labelled vertices, uniquely determines its partial order.

Properties and examples

The diagrams are named after Helmut Hasse (1898–1979); according to Garrett Birkhoff, they are so called because of the effective use Hasse made of them in his textbooks. Because Hasse diagrams are intended to represent only the covering relation, edges implied by transitivity and reflexivity are omitted, greatly simplifying the drawing of large orders such as subgroup lattices of groups.

Classic examples include the divisors of 60 ordered by divisibility, the power set of a set ordered by inclusion (giving the skeleton of a hypercube), and the partition lattice of a finite set ordered by refinement. Hasse diagrams are ubiquitous in lattice theory, in the depiction of weak orders in combinatorics, and in computer science — for example, in diagrams of class inheritance hierarchies and in the representation of precedence constraints in scheduling. A "good" drawing aims to minimize edge crossings and give the diagram symmetry where possible; deciding planarity of Hasse diagrams is a nontrivial problem, and many posets have no planar Hasse diagram.
