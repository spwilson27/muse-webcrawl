Philip Marlowe

Philip Marlowe is a fictional character created by the American novelist Raymond Chandler. A hardboiled but honourable private detective working in Los Angeles, Marlowe is the narrator-protagonist of Chandler's novels, beginning with The Big Sleep (1939), and has become the archetype of the wisecracking, incorruptible private eye in crime fiction and film noir.

Character

Marlowe is a native of Santa Rosa, college-educated, a veteran, and a former investigator for the Los Angeles district attorney's office, fired for insubordination. He works alone from a modest office, charges a standard fee (twenty-five dollars a day plus expenses in the early novels), plays chess against himself, drinks whisky, and narrates in a voice mixing tough slang with literary similes. Beneath the cynicism he is a romantic: in Chandler's essay "The Simple Art of Murder" (1944), the detective "must be ... a man of honor ... He must be the best man in his world and a good enough man for any world." Marlowe's exact age, like much of his past, shifts between books; Chandler was famously casual about continuity.

The novels

  • The Big Sleep (1939)
  • Farewell, My Lovely (1940)
  • The High Window (1942)
  • The Lady in the Lake (1943)
  • The Little Sister (1949)
  • The Long Goodbye (1953)
  • Playback (1958)
  • Poodle Springs (1959; unfinished, completed by Robert B. Parker, 1989)

Later authorised Marlowe novels by other writers include Parker's Perchance to Dream (1991), Benjamin Black's The Black-Eyed Blonde (2014) and Lawrence Osborne's Only to Sleep (2018).

Portrayals

Humphrey Bogart's performance in Howard Hawks's The Big Sleep (1946) fixed the screen image of Marlowe. Dick Powell played a lighter Marlowe in Murder, My Sweet (1944); Robert Montgomery directed and starred in the all-subjective-camera Lady in the Lake (1947); James Garner, Robert Mitchum (twice) and Elliott Gould — in Robert Altman's revisionist The Long Goodbye (1973) — followed, with Powers Boothe acclaimed in the 1980s television series.
