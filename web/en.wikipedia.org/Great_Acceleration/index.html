Great Acceleration

Coined/popularisedWill Steffen and colleagues, International Geosphere-Biosphere Programme (2004, 2015)
OnsetAround 1950 (mid-20th century)
Indicators12 socio-economic and 12 Earth-system trends
RelevanceKey concept in the Anthropocene debate

The Great Acceleration is the dramatic, continuous surge in human activity and its planetary impacts that began around the middle of the 20th century and continues today. In a famous set of 24 "hockey-stick" graphs published by Will Steffen and colleagues for the International Geosphere-Biosphere Programme, twelve socio-economic indicators (population, GDP, energy use, fertiliser consumption, water use, international tourism, and others) and twelve Earth-system indicators (atmospheric carbon dioxide, methane and nitrous oxide, ocean acidification, tropical forest loss, terrestrial biosphere degradation, and others) all bend sharply upward from about 1950.

Interpretation

The Great Acceleration is frequently invoked in the debate over the Anthropocene, the proposed geological epoch defined by human influence: Steffen and colleagues argued that the mid-20th century provides the clearest, most globally synchronous marker of humanity becoming a dominant force on the Earth system, visible in radionuclides from nuclear testing, plastics, concrete and other stratigraphic signals. The acceleration is geographically uneven — driven initially by the industrialised OECD countries and increasingly, since the 2000s, by rapid economic growth in Asia and other emerging economies.

Causes and responses

Drivers include post-war population growth, cheap fossil energy, industrialised agriculture, globalisation and mass consumption. The concept underlies frameworks such as planetary boundaries and "doughnut economics", which ask whether socio-economic development can be decoupled from the Earth-system trends, and it frames proposals for a "Great Transformation" or deliberate deceleration of material throughput.
