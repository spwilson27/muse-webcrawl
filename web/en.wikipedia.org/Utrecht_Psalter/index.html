The Utrecht Psalter (Utrecht, Universiteitsbibliotheek, MS Bibl. Rhenotraiectinae I Nr 32) is a 9th-century illuminated psalter which is a key masterpiece of Carolingian art, and is probably the most valuable manuscript in the Netherlands. It is famous for its 166 lively pen illustrations, drawn in the sketchy, animated style associated with the Reims school, each accompanying a psalm or canticle and interpreting its imagery with unusual literalism.

Description

The manuscript was produced near Reims between about 820 and 835, in the circle of Archbishop Ebbo. It contains the 150 Psalms in the Gallican version, together with canticles, the Te Deum, the Athanasian Creed and other texts — several of them added or bound in later. The text is written in rustic capitals, unusual for the period, and each psalm is headed by a drawing in brown ink spanning the width of the page.

The illustrations are famous for their literal depiction of the psalm texts: when the psalmist speaks of the wicked being blown away like chaff, or of God as a shield, sun or fortress, the artist draws exactly that, populating the scenes with crowds of small figures, angels, buildings and landscapes in rapid, sketch-like lines. The drawings are thought to copy a Late Antique model, perhaps of the 4th or 5th century, whose style the Carolingian artists imitated with extraordinary vivacity.

History

The psalter was in England by about 1000, probably at Christ Church, Canterbury, where it profoundly influenced Anglo-Saxon art. Three English copies were made directly from it: the Harley Psalter (c. 1000–1050), the Eadwine Psalter (c. 1155–1160) and the Anglo-Catalan Psalter (c. 1180–1200). It passed through the collection of Sir Robert Cotton and was later given to Utrecht University, where it has been since 1716. In 2015 the manuscript was added to UNESCO's Memory of the World Register.

Significance

Art historians regard the Utrecht Psalter as one of the most important surviving Carolingian manuscripts: it preserves the appearance of a lost late-antique picture cycle, demonstrates the "illusionistic" Reims style at its height, and served as a pattern book for generations of medieval artists in France and England.
