Morphism

[Image: Objects and morphisms in a category]

In mathematics, a morphism is a structure-preserving map between two mathematical structures of the same type. The notion is central to category theory, where a category consists of objects together with morphisms (also called arrows) between them, equipped with a composition operation that is associative and has identity morphisms. Familiar examples include functions between sets, group homomorphisms between groups, continuous maps between topological spaces, and linear maps between vector spaces.

In category theory

Formally, for objects X and Y in a category, a morphism f : X → Y has domain X and codomain Y. Morphisms can be composed when the codomain of one equals the domain of the next. Special classes of morphisms defined by categorical properties include monomorphisms (categorically injective), epimorphisms (categorically surjective) and isomorphisms, which have two-sided inverses. In concrete categories these often - but not always - coincide with the corresponding set-theoretic notions.

Examples and terminology

The word derives from the Greek morphē ("form"). Combined with prefixes it forms terms used throughout algebra: homomorphism (structure-preserving map in algebra), homeomorphism (bijective continuous map with continuous inverse in topology), diffeomorphism (smooth), isomorphism (bijective homomorphism), endomorphism (map of a structure to itself) and automorphism (an isomorphism of a structure with itself). In algebraic geometry, a morphism of schemes is a map that locally comes from ring homomorphisms.
