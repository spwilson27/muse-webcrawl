Jōmon period

The Jōmon period (縄文時代, Jōmon jidai) is the time in Japanese prehistory traditionally dated between about 14,000 and 300 BCE, characterised by a hunter-gatherer culture of considerable complexity and some of the world's earliest pottery. The name "cord-marked" (jōmon) was coined from the patterns impressed on the period's clay vessels with cords.

Phases and culture

Archaeologists divide the period into phases from Incipient through Final Jōmon. The Jōmon people lived in pit dwellings, exploited rich marine and forest resources — fish, shellfish, deer, nuts and chestnuts — and built large settlements such as Sannai-Maruyama in Aomori. They produced elaborate flame-rimmed pottery and the distinctive clay figurines known as dogū.

Society and subsistence

Although long described as simple foragers, the Jōmon practised forms of resource management, cultivating chestnut groves and possibly some plants, and engaged in long-distance trade in obsidian, jade and salt. Shell middens and lacquerware attest to a sophisticated material culture unusual for a non-agricultural society.

Transition

The Jōmon period ended with the arrival of wet-rice agriculture and new populations from the Asian mainland in the Yayoi period. Genetic studies show the modern Japanese population descends in part from Jōmon hunter-gatherers, with the Ainu of Hokkaido retaining the largest share of Jōmon ancestry.
