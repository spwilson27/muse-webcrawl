Basement membrane

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The basement membrane, also known as the base membrane, is a thin, fibrous sheet of extracellular matrix that underlies all epithelial and endothelial tissues in animals. It separates these cell layers from the underlying connective tissue, provides structural support, and acts as a platform for cell adhesion, migration, and signalling.

Structure

Under the electron microscope the basement membrane appears as two layers: the basal lamina, secreted by the epithelial cells themselves, and the underlying reticular lamina, produced by connective tissue cells. The basal lamina is further divided into an electron-lucent layer (lamina rara) and an electron-dense layer (lamina densa).

Its principal molecular components are:

  • Type IV collagen, which forms a sheet-like network in the lamina densa;
  • Laminins, large cross-shaped glycoproteins that bind cells via integrins and dystroglycan;
  • Nidogen (entactin), which links the collagen and laminin networks;
  • Perlecan and other heparan sulfate proteoglycans, which contribute charge-selective filtration properties.

Functions

Basement membranes anchor epithelia through hemidesmosomes, compartmentalise tissues, and regulate the passage of cells and macromolecules. The glomerular basement membrane of the kidney is a specialised filtration barrier. Basement membranes also influence cell polarity, differentiation, and repair after injury.

Clinical significance

Defects in basement membrane components cause several diseases. Mutations in type IV collagen genes produce Alport syndrome, affecting kidney, ear, and eye. In Goodpasture syndrome, autoantibodies attack type IV collagen in kidney and lung basement membranes. In epidermolysis bullosa, mutations in laminin or collagen VII cause severe skin blistering because the epidermis detaches from its basement membrane.
