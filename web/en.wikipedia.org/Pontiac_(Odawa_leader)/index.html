Pontiac (Odawa leader)

Pontiac (c. 1720 – April 20, 1769), known in Odawa as Obwaandi'eyaag, was an Odawa (Ottawa) war chief who became famous for his role in Pontiac's War (1763–1766), a widespread Native American uprising against the British military occupation of the Great Lakes region following the French and Indian War.

Early life

Pontiac was born around 1720, probably along the Maumee River in present-day Ohio or in the Detroit River region. His parents are believed to have been an Odawa father and an Ojibwe mother; he grew up within the Three Fires Confederacy of the Odawa, Ojibwe, and Potawatomi. By the 1750s he had become a prominent war leader among the Odawa of Detroit, allied with New France against the British.

Pontiac's War

After France ceded the Great Lakes country to Britain in 1763, British policies — sharply reduced gift-giving, restrictions on the sale of gunpowder and alcohol, and encroaching settlement — provoked resentment, amplified by the religious teachings of the Lenape prophet Neolin. In May 1763 Pontiac led an attempt to capture Fort Detroit by surprise; when the plan was betrayed, he besieged the fort for months. Inspired in part by his example, Native nations across the Great Lakes and Ohio Country captured eight British forts. The uprising wound down after Britain adjusted its policies and issued the Royal Proclamation of 1763; Pontiac formally made peace in 1766.

Death and legacy

Pontiac was assassinated on April 20, 1769, near Cahokia by a Peoria warrior, an act that triggered bitter retaliatory warfare. Remembered as one of the most influential Native leaders of the 18th century, his name was later given to the cities of Pontiac in Michigan and Illinois, and to General Motors' Pontiac automobile brand. No verified portrait of him exists.
