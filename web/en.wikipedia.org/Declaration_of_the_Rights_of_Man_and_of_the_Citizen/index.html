Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen


The Declaration, painted by Jean-Jacques-François Le Barbier

The Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen (French: Déclaration des droits de l'Homme et du citoyen de 1789) is a human civil rights document from the French Revolution. Adopted by the National Constituent Assembly on 26 August 1789, it sets out individual and collective rights held to be universal, influenced by Enlightenment thinkers such as Rousseau, Montesquieu, and Locke, and by the American Declaration of Independence.

Contents

The Declaration consists of a preamble and seventeen articles. It proclaims that "men are born and remain free and equal in rights," and defines liberty, property, security, and resistance to oppression as natural and imprescriptible rights. Other articles establish freedom of speech and religion, the presumption of innocence, popular sovereignty, and the separation of powers.

Significance

The document remains a cornerstone of French constitutional law; the Constitution of France's Fifth Republic (1958) affirms its principles in its preamble. Criticized in its own time for excluding women — prompting Olympe de Gouges to publish her Declaration of the Rights of Woman in 1791 — the Declaration influenced human rights instruments worldwide, including the Universal Declaration of Human Rights.
