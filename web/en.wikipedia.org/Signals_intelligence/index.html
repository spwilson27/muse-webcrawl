Signals intelligence


Intercept station radomes

Signals intelligence (SIGINT) is the gathering of intelligence by intercepting signals, whether communications between people (communications intelligence, COMINT) or electronic signals not directly used in communication (electronic intelligence, ELINT). SIGINT is a subset of intelligence collection management, involving the interception of transmissions from communications and information systems.

History

Modern SIGINT dates to the cable tapping and wireless interception of the First World War, when Britain's Room 40 decrypted German naval signals, including the Zimmermann Telegram. In the Second World War, Allied cryptanalysis at Bletchley Park of the German Enigma and the American breaking of Japanese codes (MAGIC) proved decisive. During the Cold War, the US National Security Agency (established 1952) and the UK Government Communications Headquarters built global interception networks, coordinated through the UKUSA Agreement and the "Five Eyes" alliance.

Methods

Collection methods include ground listening stations, reconnaissance aircraft and ships, satellites, and tapping of undersea cables and internet backbone infrastructure. Traffic analysis — the study of who communicates with whom, when, and how much — can yield intelligence even when message content cannot be decrypted. Disclosures by Edward Snowden in 2013 revealed the scale of bulk telephony metadata collection and programs such as PRISM.

Agencies

Major SIGINT agencies include the NSA (United States), GCHQ (United Kingdom), CSE (Canada), ASD (Australia), GCSB (New Zealand), FSB and GRU units (Russia), and China's PLA signals intelligence organizations.
