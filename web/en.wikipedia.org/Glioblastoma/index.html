Glioblastoma (GBM), also known as glioblastoma multiforme, is the most aggressive and most common malignant primary brain tumour in adults. Classified by the World Health Organization as a grade IV astrocytoma, it grows rapidly, infiltrates surrounding brain tissue, and is characterised microscopically by cellular pleomorphism, microvascular proliferation, and areas of necrosis.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms arise from the tumour's size and location and from raised intracranial pressure: headaches, seizures, nausea, weakness, and changes in personality, memory, or speech. Diagnosis rests on MRI imaging and histological and molecular examination of biopsy or resection tissue; most glioblastomas are IDH-wildtype.

Treatment and prognosis

Standard treatment combines maximal safe surgical resection with radiotherapy and concurrent and adjuvant temozolomide chemotherapy (the Stupp protocol), sometimes supplemented by tumour-treating fields. Despite treatment the prognosis is poor: median survival is roughly 12–15 months, and fewer than 10% of patients survive five years. The disease accounts for notable public cases, including US senators Edward Kennedy and John McCain. Research focuses on immunotherapy, targeted agents, and the blood–brain barrier, which limits drug delivery.
