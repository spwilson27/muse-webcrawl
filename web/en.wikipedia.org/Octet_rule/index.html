Octet rule

The octet rule is a chemical rule of thumb that reflects the theory that main-group elements tend to bond in such a way that each atom has eight electrons in its valence shell, giving it the same electronic configuration as a noble gas. The rule is especially applicable to the second-row elements carbon, nitrogen, oxygen and fluorine, and to ions such as oxide and nitride.

History

The octet pattern emerged from the work of Walther Kossel and Gilbert N. Lewis, who in 1916 independently developed theories of the chemical bond based on atoms sharing or transferring electrons to complete an outer shell of eight. Lewis's cubic atom model and dot structures became the standard pictorial device for applying the rule.

Application

In Lewis structures, electrons are arranged so that each atom is surrounded by eight (counting bonding pairs once for each atom they join). The rule explains the common valences of the main-group elements, the charges of simple ions, and the stoichiometry of many simple compounds.

Exceptions

Many exceptions are known. Hydrogen and helium follow a duet rule; radicals and molecules such as nitric oxide have odd electron counts; boron forms electron-deficient compounds; and elements from the third period onward form "expanded octet" species such as phosphorus pentachloride and sulfur hexafluoride. Transition-metal compounds instead often follow the 18-electron rule.
