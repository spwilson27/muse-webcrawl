Precipitation

In meteorology, precipitation is any product of the condensation of atmospheric water vapour that falls from clouds due to gravitational pull. The main forms of precipitation include drizzle, rain, sleet, snow, ice pellets, graupel and hail.

Precipitation occurs when a portion of the atmosphere becomes saturated with water vapour (reaching 100% relative humidity), so that the water condenses and "precipitates" or falls. Two processes, possibly acting together, can lead to air becoming saturated: cooling the air and adding water vapour.

Formation

Water droplets or ice crystals in clouds grow by condensation and collision until they are heavy enough to fall. In cold clouds the Bergeron process dominates: ice crystals grow at the expense of supercooled droplets because saturation vapour pressure is lower over ice than over water. In warm clouds, collision and coalescence of droplets of differing sizes produces rain. Precipitation falls from clouds but may evaporate before reaching the ground, forming virga.

Types and measurement

Precipitation is classified by its form (rain, snow, hail, freezing rain, drizzle), its mechanism (convectional, orographic, frontal/cyclonic) and its intensity. It is measured with rain gauges, weather radar and satellites, and is a key component of the water cycle and of climate classification systems such as the Köppen scheme. On global scales, average annual precipitation is about 990 mm, but it varies from near zero in deserts to over 11,000 mm at the wettest locations in India and Hawaii.

Effects

Precipitation sustains terrestrial life, replenishes groundwater and drives erosion. Excessive precipitation causes flooding and landslides; deficits lead to drought. Climate change is altering precipitation patterns, generally intensifying the hydrological cycle with heavier downpours in many regions.
