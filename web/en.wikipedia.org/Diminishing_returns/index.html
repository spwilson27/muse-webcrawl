Diminishing returns

The law of diminishing returns is a fundamental principle in economics that states: "If increasing amounts of a variable factor are applied to a fixed quantity of another factor, the marginal product of the variable factor will eventually decrease." In other words, adding more of one input while holding others constant eventually yields smaller per-unit increases in output, though total output may continue to rise at a decreasing rate.

History

The idea was developed by classical economists including Jacques Turgot, Johann Heinrich von Thünen, Thomas Malthus and David Ricardo, largely in the context of agriculture: successive applications of labour and capital to a fixed plot of land yield progressively smaller increments of crop. It became a cornerstone of marginalist theory and the theory of the firm.

Example and stages

Consider workers added to a fixed-size kitchen. Initially, extra workers raise output more than proportionally as tasks are divided (increasing returns). Eventually crowding causes each additional worker to add less than the previous (diminishing returns), and if still more workers are added, total output can fall (negative returns). The law applies in the short run, when at least one factor — capital, land — is fixed, and assumes constant technology and uniform units of the variable factor.

Applications and limits

Diminishing returns explains rising short-run marginal cost curves, informs fertiliser dosing in agriculture, staffing decisions, and study-effort analogies in everyday language. It is distinct from returns to scale, which concern proportional changes in all inputs in the long run, and it can be offset over time by technological progress, a point central to debates sparked by Malthusian population theory.
