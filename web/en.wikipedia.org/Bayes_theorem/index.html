Bayes theorem

Bayes theorem (alternatively Bayes law or Bayes rule), named after Thomas Bayes, describes the probability of an event based on prior knowledge of conditions that might be related to the event. It gives the posterior probability of a hypothesis A given evidence B:

P(A|B) = P(B|A) · P(A) / P(B)

where P(A) is the prior probability of A, P(B|A) is the likelihood of observing B if A is true, and P(B) is the marginal probability of the evidence.

History

The theorem was first set out in Bayes's "An Essay towards solving a Problem in the Doctrine of Chances", published posthumously in 1763 by his friend Richard Price. Pierre-Simon Laplace later developed and popularised the result, which became a foundation of the Bayesian interpretation of probability.

Applications

Bayes theorem underlies Bayesian inference throughout science and technology: medical diagnosis (updating disease probability from test results), spam filtering, machine learning (naive Bayes classifiers), forensic science and the evaluation of evidence. A famous practical consequence is that even accurate tests yield mostly false positives when the condition tested for is very rare — the base rate fallacy.
