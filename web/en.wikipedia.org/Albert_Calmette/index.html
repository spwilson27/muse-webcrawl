Léon Charles Albert Calmette (12 July 1863 – 29 October 1933) was a French physician, bacteriologist and immunologist, and an important officer of the Pasteur Institute. He is best known, with Camille Guérin, for developing the Bacillus Calmette–Guérin (BCG) vaccine against tuberculosis, and for producing the first antivenom against snake venom.

Early career and antivenom

Calmette trained as a naval doctor and served in the Far East and West Africa. Working at the Pasteur Institute's outpost in Saigon, which he founded in 1891, he studied snake venom after floods drove cobras into villages, and by 1894 had developed "Calmette's serum", the first commercial antivenom, prepared by immunising horses. He also worked on bubonic plague in Saigon and later directed the Pasteur Institute of Lille, where he campaigned against tuberculosis, alcoholism and poor sanitation in industrial northern France.

BCG vaccine

With veterinarian Camille Guérin, Calmette spent over a decade attenuating a virulent strain of Mycobacterium bovis by repeated subculture on a glycerin–bile–potato medium — some 230 passages between 1908 and 1921. The resulting BCG strain was first given to a newborn infant in Paris in 1921. Despite controversy, including the Lübeck disaster of 1930 (caused by contamination of a locally prepared batch with virulent bacilli, not by BCG itself), the vaccine came into worldwide use and remains the only licensed vaccine against tuberculosis, administered to more than 100 million children annually.

Legacy

Calmette served as deputy director of the Pasteur Institute in Paris from 1917. Streets, hospitals and schools across France and the Francophone world bear his name, as does the bacillus he tamed.
