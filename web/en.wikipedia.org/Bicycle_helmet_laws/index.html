Bicycle helmet laws

Bicycle helmet laws are regulations that require cyclists to wear helmets, usually with penalties for non-compliance. Laws vary widely: some countries mandate helmets for all riders, others only for children, and many have no mandate at all. The laws are the subject of continuing debate among safety researchers, cycling advocates, and public health officials.

History

Australia was the first country to introduce compulsory helmet wearing for all cyclists, with legislation enacted state by state between 1990 and 1992, beginning with Victoria in 1990. New Zealand followed in 1994. In the United States there is no federal law, but many states and cities require helmets for minors. In most of Europe, helmets are encouraged but not compulsory for adults.

Evidence and debate

Case-control studies generally find that helmets reduce the risk of serious head injury in a crash. Critics of mandatory laws argue that they discourage cycling, reducing the health benefits of exercise and the "safety in numbers" effect, and that population-level injury rates did not fall as expected in some jurisdictions. Supporters respond that the injury-prevention benefits outweigh these effects and point to increases in helmet use after laws were introduced.

By country

Mandatory all-ages laws exist in Australia, New Zealand, and a number of other jurisdictions including parts of Canada and Spain (outside urban areas). Several countries, including Mexico City and Israel, have repealed or relaxed earlier mandates. Denmark and the Netherlands, with the world's highest cycling rates, have no helmet mandate and very low rates of helmet use.
