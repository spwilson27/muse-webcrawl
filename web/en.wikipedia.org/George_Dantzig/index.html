George Dantzig

George Bernard Dantzig
Born: 8 November 1914, Portland, Oregon, U.S.
Died: 13 May 2005, Stanford, California, U.S.
Field: Mathematical optimization, operations research
Known for: Simplex algorithm; linear programming

George Bernard Dantzig (November 8, 1914 – May 13, 2005) was an American mathematical scientist who made important contributions to industrial engineering, operations research, computer science, economics, and statistics. Dantzig is known for his development of the simplex algorithm, an algorithm for solving linear programming problems, and for his other work with linear programming. In statistics, Dantzig solved two open problems in statistical theory, which he had mistaken for homework after arriving late to a lecture.

The homework problems

In 1939, as a graduate student at the University of California, Berkeley, Dantzig arrived late to a statistics class taught by Jerzy Neyman and copied down two problems written on the blackboard, assuming they were a homework assignment. They were in fact two famous unproved theorems of statistics. Working over several days, Dantzig produced complete solutions, which Neyman later helped him publish. The story became a famous inspirational anecdote and was retold in sermons, motivational literature, and the opening of the film Good Will Hunting.

Linear programming and the simplex algorithm

During the Second World War Dantzig worked for the U.S. Air Force Office of Statistical Control, and after the war he took up a post at the Pentagon, where he was charged with mechanizing military planning. In 1947 he formulated the problem as linear programming — optimizing a linear objective subject to linear constraints — and devised the simplex method, which solves such problems by moving along the edges of a convex polytope from vertex to vertex. The simplex algorithm proved extraordinarily effective in practice and became a cornerstone of operations research, logistics, and economics; Dantzig's 1963 book Linear Programming and Extensions remains a classic of the field.

Later career

Dantzig joined the RAND Corporation in 1952, became a professor at Berkeley in 1960, and moved to Stanford University in 1966, where he remained until his death. He received the National Medal of Science in 1975. He died in Stanford, California, on 13 May 2005, aged 90, of complications from diabetes and cardiovascular disease.
