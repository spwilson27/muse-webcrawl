Water conservation

Water conservation
GoalReduce freshwater use and waste; protect water resources
SectorsAgriculture (about 70% of global withdrawals), industry, households
MethodsEfficient fixtures, drip irrigation, reuse and recycling, pricing, watershed protection

Drip irrigation delivers water directly to plant roots, reducing losses.

Water conservation includes all the policies, strategies and activities to sustainably manage the natural resource of fresh water, to protect the hydrosphere, and to meet current and future human demand. It aims to reduce unnecessary water use and waste, improve the efficiency of use in homes, industry and agriculture, and ensure supplies remain available during droughts.

Households and cities

Domestic measures include low-flow showerheads and taps, dual-flush and low-flush toilets, efficient washing machines and dishwashers, fixing leaks — which can waste thousands of litres per household per year — and water-wise landscaping (xeriscaping) with native or drought-tolerant plants in place of lawns. Utilities use metering, tiered pricing, drought restrictions and public education; many cities also harvest rainwater and reuse treated wastewater ("greywater" and recycled water) for irrigation and groundwater recharge.

Agriculture and industry

Agriculture accounts for roughly 70% of global freshwater withdrawals, so irrigation efficiency yields the largest savings: drip and micro-sprinkler systems, laser levelling, soil-moisture monitoring, drought-resistant crops and scheduling irrigation to need can substantially cut use compared with flood irrigation. Industry conserves water through recycling and closed-loop cooling, process redesign and water audits. At the landscape scale, protecting wetlands and watersheds, reducing pollution and managing aquifers sustainably are considered conservation, as is reducing the "virtual water" embedded in traded goods. Programmes such as the U.S. EPA's WaterSense label certify water-efficient products and homes.

Rationale

Only about 2.5% of Earth's water is fresh, and much of that is locked in ice or deep groundwater. Population growth, urbanisation and climate change are intensifying water stress; the United Nations estimates that billions of people experience severe water scarcity during at least part of the year. Conservation is generally cheaper and less environmentally damaging than building new supply infrastructure such as dams and desalination plants.
