House of Commons of the United Kingdom

The House of Commons is the lower house of the Parliament of the United Kingdom, which also comprises the monarch and the upper house, the House of Lords. It is a democratically elected body of 650 members known as Members of Parliament (MPs), each elected by the first-past-the-post system to represent a single constituency. The Commons meets in the Palace of Westminster in London, in a chamber furnished with green benches arranged in an adversarial two-sided layout, presided over by the Speaker.

Powers and functions

Governments are formed from, and accountable to, the Commons: by convention the prime minister must command its confidence, and a government that loses a confidence vote must resign or call an election. Under the Parliament Acts of 1911 and 1949, the Commons can ultimately legislate without the consent of the Lords, and the Lords may not block money bills. The Commons scrutinises the executive through question times — including Prime Minister's Questions — select committees, and debates, while its members also undertake constituency work.

History

The Commons developed from the "commons" — knights and burgesses summoned to medieval parliaments — and met separately from 1341. Its power grew through the Civil War, the Glorious Revolution of 1688, and the nineteenth-century Reform Acts, which extended the franchise and curbed rotten boroughs. Universal adult suffrage arrived in 1928, and the maximum parliamentary term is now five years under the Dissolution and Calling of Parliament Act 2022.
