Non-cooperation movement (1919–22)

The non-cooperation movement was a political campaign launched by Mahatma Gandhi on 1 August 1920 in response to the Jallianwala Bagh massacre of 1919 and the British handling of the Khilafat issue. It was the first nationwide satyagraha campaign in the Indian independence movement, aiming to induce the British government to grant self-governance (swaraj) by withdrawing Indian cooperation from the colonial state.

Programme

Supporters surrendered government honours, boycotted British courts, schools, councils and foreign cloth, resigned from government service, and promoted hand-spun khadi and national schools. The Indian National Congress endorsed the programme at its Calcutta and Nagpur sessions in 1920, and the movement drew in peasants, students, workers and, through the Khilafat alliance, large numbers of Indian Muslims.

Suspension

On 5 February 1922, at Chauri Chaura, a crowd killed 22 policemen. Gandhi, fearing the movement was turning violent, called it off over the objections of many Congress leaders. He was arrested shortly afterwards and sentenced to six years' imprisonment. Although it did not achieve swaraj, the movement transformed Congress into a mass organisation and established non-cooperation as the template for later campaigns such as the civil disobedience movement of 1930.
