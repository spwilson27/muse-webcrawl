The Atlantic blue crab (Callinectes sapidus), also known as the Chesapeake blue crab, is a species of crab native to the waters of the western Atlantic Ocean and the Gulf of Mexico, and introduced internationally. Its species name, sapidus, means "savory" in Latin, and the genus name means "beautiful swimmer"—apt for a highly prized seafood.

Description

The carapace can grow up to about 23 cm (9 in) wide and bears a prominent sharp spine at each side. Males have brilliant blue claws and a narrow, T-shaped abdomen; females have orange-tipped claws and a broad, rounded abdomen. Like other swimming crabs (Portunidae), the fifth pair of legs is flattened into paddles. Blue crabs are strong swimmers and opportunistic bottom feeders, eating clams, oysters, mussels, small fish, plants, and detritus.

Life cycle

Females mate only once, shortly after their final molt, storing sperm for later use. A female may produce several sponges of eggs—each containing up to two million eggs—over her lifetime. Larvae (zoeae) develop in high-salinity ocean waters before megalopae settle in estuaries such as Chesapeake Bay and grow through a series of molts. Lifespan is typically one to three years.

Fishery

Blue crabs support one of the most valuable fisheries on the U.S. East Coast and Gulf Coast. They are caught in crab pots, trotlines, and winter dredges, and sold live, as picked meat, or as soft-shell crabs (recently molted individuals). Maryland and Virginia jointly manage the Chesapeake Bay stock, which has fluctuated widely with harvest pressure, winter mortality, and loss of seagrass habitat.
