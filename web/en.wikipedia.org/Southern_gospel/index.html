Southern gospel music is a genre of Christian music that originated in the southeastern United States. Its roots lie in nineteenth-century shape-note singing schools, camp meetings and the publishing houses — such as the Stamps-Baxter and James D. Vaughan companies — that sold songbooks and sponsored travelling quartets. The classic sound is a male four-part harmony quartet (tenor, lead, baritone and a deep bass) accompanied by piano, performing songs about salvation, heaven and the Christian life.

History

James D. Vaughan formed the first professional Southern gospel quartet in 1910 to promote his songbooks, and the Vaughan and Stamps-Baxter companies built an industry of singing schools, conventions and radio broadcasts. Groups such as the Blackwood Brothers and the Statesmen Quartet popularised the style after the Second World War; the Statesmen's showmanship strongly influenced the young Elvis Presley, who idolised them and recorded gospel throughout his career. The Gospel Music Association (founded 1964) and its Dove Awards, and later Bill Gaither's "Homecoming" tours and videos, carried the genre to large audiences.

Characteristics

Southern gospel is distinguished from Black gospel and contemporary Christian music by its close four-part harmony, conventional song structures drawn from hymnody, and themes centred on conversion, assurance and heaven. "Convention songs" with fast, interlocking vocal parts remain a staple of all-night singings in the South.
