Particle therapy

Particle therapy is a form of external beam radiotherapy that uses beams of energetic charged particles — most often protons, and in some centres heavier ions such as carbon — instead of the X-rays used in conventional radiotherapy. Because charged particles deposit the bulk of their energy in a sharp maximum called the Bragg peak just before they stop, the dose can be conformed closely to a tumour while sparing healthy tissue beyond it, with essentially no exit dose.

Principle and technique

A particle accelerator (cyclotron or synchrotron) accelerates protons to energies of roughly 70–250 MeV, enough to reach deep-seated tumours. The beam is shaped by scattering systems or, in modern centres, by pencil-beam scanning, in which magnets steer a narrow beam spot-by-spot through the tumour volume. By superimposing Bragg peaks of different energies, clinicians build a "spread-out Bragg peak" covering the whole target. Rotating gantries allow treatment from multiple angles.

Clinical use

Particle therapy is particularly valued for tumours near critical structures — the base of skull, spine and eyes (ocular melanoma) — and for children, in whom reducing integral dose lowers the lifetime risk of second cancers. Carbon-ion therapy, offered at a small number of centres in Japan, Germany, Italy, Austria and China, adds a higher biological effectiveness against radioresistant tumours. The main drawbacks are cost: a proton centre requires a large accelerator facility costing far more than a conventional linac bunker, and clinical evidence of superiority over modern photon techniques remains an active research question.

History

The method was proposed by Robert R. Wilson in 1946 and first used on patients at physics laboratories — Berkeley (1954) and Harvard (1961). The first hospital-based proton facility opened at Loma Linda, California, in 1990; by the 2020s well over one hundred proton centres were operating or under construction worldwide, and more than 300,000 patients had been treated with particle beams.
