Ra (expeditions)

Thor Heyerdahl's papyrus boats Ra and Ra II tested whether ancient Egyptians could have crossed the Atlantic.

The Ra expeditions were two voyages undertaken by the Norwegian explorer and ethnographer Thor Heyerdahl in 1969 and 1970 aboard papyrus reed boats, Ra and Ra II. The expeditions were intended to demonstrate that ancient Egyptians could, in principle, have crossed the Atlantic Ocean in vessels made of papyrus, and thus that transoceanic contact between the Old and New Worlds was technologically possible in antiquity.

The boats were named after Ra, the ancient Egyptian sun god. They were built in Egypt using techniques depicted in ancient tomb paintings, with the help of boat builders from Lake Chad and, for the second vessel, Aymara reed-boat builders from Lake Titicaca.

Ra (1969)

The first Ra departed Safi, Morocco, on 25 May 1969 with a seven-man international crew. Design errors — in particular the omission of a rope shown in the ancient depictions, which kept the stern raised — caused the stern to sag and the papyrus to become waterlogged. After sailing some 5,000 km, the boat began to break up and the crew abandoned it roughly a week short of Barbados.

Ra II (1970)

Learning from the failure, Heyerdahl had Ra II built shorter, with the stern rope properly rigged. The vessel left Safi on 17 May 1970 and reached Barbados on 12 July, after 57 days. The crossing proved that a papyrus boat could survive an Atlantic passage, although it did not prove that such a voyage actually occurred in antiquity. The crew also documented oil pollution in the Atlantic, and their report contributed to growing international attention on marine pollution.

Legacy

The surviving Ra II is displayed at the Kon-Tiki Museum in Oslo. The expeditions, following Heyerdahl's earlier Kon-Tiki voyage of 1947, popularised experimental archaeology, though his diffusionist interpretations remain controversial among scholars.
