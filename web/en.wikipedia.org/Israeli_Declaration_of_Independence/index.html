Israeli Declaration of Independence

Ben-Gurion reads the Declaration, 14 May 1948

The Israeli Declaration of Independence, formally the Declaration of the Establishment of the State of Israel, was proclaimed on 14 May 1948 (5 Iyar 5708) by David Ben-Gurion, head of the Jewish Agency, in Tel Aviv, on the day the British Mandate for Palestine expired.

Background

The United Nations General Assembly had adopted Resolution 181 (the Partition Plan) on 29 November 1947, recommending the creation of independent Jewish and Arab states. The Jewish leadership accepted the plan; Arab leaders rejected it, and civil war escalated in Mandatory Palestine. As the Mandate's termination approached, the People's Council (the proto-parliament of the Yishuv) voted to declare statehood.

Contents

The Declaration recounts the historical connection of the Jewish people to the Land of Israel, the revival of the national movement, the catastrophe of the Holocaust, and the UN resolution as legal grounding. It proclaims the establishment of a Jewish state, to be called Israel, commits it to freedom, justice and peace, promises complete equality of social and political rights to all inhabitants irrespective of religion, race or sex, and appeals to the Arab inhabitants to participate in building the state.

Significance

The document was signed by 37 members of the People's Council. Within hours the United States extended de facto recognition, and the Soviet Union followed with de jure recognition. The following day, neighbouring Arab states invaded, beginning the 1948 Arab–Israeli War. The Declaration remains a foundational document of Israel, and Israel's Supreme Court has treated its principles as interpretive guidance for the Basic Laws in the absence of a formal constitution.
