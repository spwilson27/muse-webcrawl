Geoffrey Chaucer

Geoffrey Chaucer
Born: c. 1343, London, England
Died: 25 October 1400 (aged about 57)
Occupation: Poet, author, courtier, diplomat
Notable works: The Canterbury Tales, Troilus and Criseyde

Geoffrey Chaucer (c. 1343 – 25 October 1400) was an English poet and civil servant widely considered the greatest English writer of the Middle Ages. He is best known for The Canterbury Tales, a collection of stories told by pilgrims travelling to Canterbury Cathedral, left unfinished at his death.

Life

Born into a family of London wine merchants, Chaucer served as a page, soldier, diplomat, and royal official under Edward III and Richard II. Captured in France during the Hundred Years' War, he was ransomed in 1360. He travelled to Italy on royal business, encounters that likely exposed him to Boccaccio and Petrarch. Chaucer held posts including comptroller of customs and clerk of the king's works, and was the first poet buried in what became Poets' Corner in Westminster Abbey.

Works

His major works include The Book of the Duchess, The House of Fame, The Parliament of Fowls, the tragedy Troilus and Criseyde, and The Canterbury Tales, whose General Prologue offers a vivid cross-section of medieval society. Chaucer wrote in Middle English at a time when French and Latin dominated literature, helping to establish English as a literary language.
