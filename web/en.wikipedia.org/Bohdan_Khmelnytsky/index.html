Bohdan Khmelnytsky

Bohdan Zynoviy Mykhailovych Khmelnytsky (c. 1595 – 6 August 1657) was a Ruthenian nobleman and military commander of the Zaporozhian Cossacks. As hetman of the Zaporizhian Host, he led an uprising against the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth in 1648 that resulted in the creation of an independent Cossack state, the Cossack Hetmanate. In Ukraine he is regarded as a national hero; in Polish and Jewish historical memory he is a far more controversial figure, because the uprising was accompanied by massacres of Poles and Jews.

Early life

Khmelnytsky was probably born in Subotiv, the estate of his father Mykhailo, a Cossack officer. Educated by Jesuits at Lviv or Jarosław, he fought against the Ottomans and was captured at the Battle of Cecora in 1620, spending two years as a prisoner in Constantinople. He later served in Cossack campaigns and became a captain of the Chyhyryn regiment. A personal grievance — the seizure of his estate and the flogging of his son by a Polish official — helped precipitate his revolt.

The uprising

In 1648 Khmelnytsky allied with the Crimean Tatars and won victories over Polish forces at Zhovti Vody and Korsun. The uprising spread across Ukraine; Cossack and peasant forces massacred Polish nobles, Roman Catholic clergy and Jewish communities, with Jewish deaths estimated in the tens of thousands. Khmelnytsky entered Kyiv in triumph in December 1648.

By the Treaty of Zboriv (1649) Poland recognized a Cossack Hetmanate in three palatinates. War resumed, however, and after defeat at Berestechko in 1651 — caused by the desertion of his Tatar allies — Khmelnytsky turned to Moscow. By the Pereiaslav Agreement of 1654, the Hetmanate accepted the suzerainty of the Tsar of Russia in exchange for military support against Poland, a step whose meaning Ukrainians and Russians have disputed ever since.

Death and legacy

Khmelnytsky died in 1657 at Chyhyryn, leaving the Hetmanate to his young son Yurii amid the ruinous civil strife known as "the Ruin." In modern Ukraine he appears on the five-hryvnia banknote and in the name of the Order of Bohdan Khmelnytsky; cities, streets and a nuclear power plant bear his name. In Jewish tradition, by contrast, the "Khmelnytsky massacres" (gezerot takh) are remembered as one of the great catastrophes before the modern era.
