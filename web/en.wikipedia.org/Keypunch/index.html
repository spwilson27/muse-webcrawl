Keypunch

An operator at an IBM 029 card punch.

A keypunch is a device for precisely punching holes into stiff paper cards at specific locations, encoding data or computer instructions for punched-card systems. Keypunches were the primary data-entry machines of the computing and tabulating industries from the early 20th century until the 1970s.

History

Herman Hollerith's tabulating machines, used in the 1890 United States census, relied on manually operated pantograph punches. As unit record equipment spread, electric keypunches with typewriter-like keyboards became standard office machinery. IBM dominated the market with models such as the IBM 026 (introduced 1949) and the IBM 029 (1964), which printed the punched characters along the top of the card as well as encoding them in Hollerith code.

Operation

Keypunch operators — a job held disproportionately by women and often called "keypunching" — transcribed source documents onto blank cards, typically using the standard 80-column card format. Accuracy was checked with a related machine, a verifier, on which a second operator re-keyed the same data. Finished decks were fed to card readers connected to tabulators or computers; a dropped deck had to be reassembled from sequence numbers punched in its last columns.

Decline

Key-to-tape and key-to-disk systems, followed by interactive terminals, displaced keypunches in the 1970s. The machines survive in museums and in cultural memory as emblems of early computing.
