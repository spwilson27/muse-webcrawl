The Sultanate of Women (Turkish: Kadınlar Saltanatı) was a period in Ottoman history, lasting roughly from 1534 to 1656, during which the imperial harem — in particular the mothers of sultans (valide sultans) and chief consorts (haseki sultans) — exercised extraordinary political influence over the affairs of the Ottoman Empire.

Background

The period began with the rise of Hürrem Sultan, legal wife of Suleiman the Magnificent, who broke with tradition by marrying the sultan and remaining at court for life, corresponding with foreign rulers and founding major charitable complexes. The later practice of keeping princes confined in the palace (the kafes) rather than sending them to govern provinces meant sultans often ascended the throne young or inexperienced, and their mothers — who managed the harem, controlled vast revenues, and maintained diplomatic correspondence — became power brokers and sometimes regents.

Principal figures

  • Hürrem Sultan (d. 1558) — wife of Suleiman I, originator of the haseki's political role.
  • Nurbanu Sultan (d. 1583) — valide sultan under Murad III.
  • Safiye Sultan (d. 1619) — correspondent of Elizabeth I of England.
  • Kösem Sultan (d. 1651) — regent for two sons and a grandson; one of the most powerful women in Ottoman history.
  • Turhan Sultan (d. 1683) — whose regency and appointment of Köprülü Mehmed Pasha in 1656 conventionally marks the period's end.

Historiography

Traditional Ottoman and early modern historians often blamed the era for imperial "decline", attributing corruption to harem influence. Modern scholarship, notably Leslie Peirce's The Imperial Harem (1993), has reframed the period: the harem was a formal political institution, and the valide sultan's authority was a legitimate, dynastic form of sovereignty in an empire where reproduction and succession were matters of state.
