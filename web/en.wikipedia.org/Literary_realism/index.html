Literary realism

Literary realism is a literary movement and mode of writing that attempts to represent reality faithfully, depicting everyday experiences and ordinary people without romantic idealisation. Emerging in mid-19th-century France as a reaction against Romanticism, realism became the dominant mode of the European and American novel, emphasising detailed description of social environments, plausible characters drawn from the middle and lower classes, and narratives shaped by social and economic forces.

Origins and development

Honoré de Balzac's La Comédie humaine (1829–1850) is often cited as a foundation of realism for its panoramic depiction of French society. Gustave Flaubert's Madame Bovary (1857) perfected the realist style through meticulous prose and free indirect discourse. In Russia, Leo Tolstoy and Fyodor Dostoevsky produced vast realist (and psychological) novels; in England, George Eliot, Charles Dickens and Thomas Hardy combined social observation with moral seriousness; and in Spain, Benito Pérez Galdós and Leopoldo Alas chronicled provincial and urban life.

Naturalism

In the late 19th century, Émile Zola and others pushed realism toward naturalism, applying quasi-scientific ideas of heredity and environment to fiction and treating characters as products of deterministic forces. American writers such as Stephen Crane, Theodore Dreiser and Frank Norris adopted naturalistic methods, while William Dean Howells and Mark Twain represented a gentler American realism.

Legacy

Although modernism reacted against realist conventions, realism remains the default mode of much contemporary fiction and of prestige television drama. Its techniques — verisimilitude, social detail, and an interest in the texture of daily life — continue to shape narrative art.
