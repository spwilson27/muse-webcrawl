Juneteenth (officially Juneteenth National Independence Day) is a federal holiday in the United States commemorating the emancipation of enslaved African Americans. Its name is a portmanteau of "June" and "nineteenth". It is celebrated on 19 June, the anniversary of the day in 1865 when Union Army general Gordon Granger arrived in Galveston, Texas, and proclaimed freedom for enslaved people there — more than two years after President Abraham Lincoln's Emancipation Proclamation took effect.

History

The Emancipation Proclamation of 1 January 1863 declared freedom for enslaved people in the rebelling Confederate states, but enforcement depended on the advance of Union troops, and Texas — the most remote Confederate state, with few Union soldiers — saw little change. On 19 June 1865, Granger landed at Galveston with about 2,000 federal troops and issued General Order No. 3, announcing that "all slaves are free" in accordance with the proclamation. Some 250,000 enslaved people in Texas were affected. Slavery was abolished throughout the United States by the Thirteenth Amendment, ratified later that year.

Celebrations

Freedmen in Texas began celebrating the date the following year, 1866, with church services, readings of the Emancipation Proclamation, prayer meetings, food, games and rodeos. The observance spread with the Great Migration across the United States. Traditional elements include red foods and drinks such as red velvet cake and hibiscus drinks, symbolising resilience and sacrifice.

Official recognition

Texas made Juneteenth a state holiday in 1980, the first state to do so. Other states gradually followed with official observances. In June 2021, after renewed national attention, Congress passed the Juneteenth National Independence Day Act nearly unanimously, and President Joe Biden signed it into law on 17 June 2021, making Juneteenth the first new federal holiday since Martin Luther King Jr. Day in 1983.
