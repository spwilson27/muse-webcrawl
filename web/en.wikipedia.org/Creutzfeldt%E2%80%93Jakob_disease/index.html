Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease

SpecialtyNeurology
TypePrion disease (transmissible spongiform encephalopathy)
Named afterHans Gerhard Creutzfeldt, Alfons Maria Jakob

Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease (CJD) is a rare, rapidly progressive and invariably fatal neurodegenerative disorder caused by prions — misfolded proteins that induce abnormal folding of normal prion protein in the brain. It belongs to the family of transmissible spongiform encephalopathies, which also includes kuru, fatal familial insomnia, and variant CJD.

Forms and causes

About 85% of cases are sporadic, arising spontaneously, typically in people around 60–70 years old. Five to fifteen percent are familial, caused by mutations in the PRNP gene. A small number are iatrogenic, acquired through contaminated surgical instruments, corneal or dura mater grafts, or human growth hormone. Variant CJD (vCJD), identified in the United Kingdom in 1996, is linked to eating meat from cattle with bovine spongiform encephalopathy ("mad cow disease") and affects younger patients.

Signs and diagnosis

Early symptoms include memory loss, personality change, and problems with coordination; these progress rapidly to dementia, myoclonus (involuntary jerking), blindness, and akinetic mutism. Diagnosis is supported by characteristic MRI changes, periodic sharp wave complexes on EEG, and the RT-QuIC assay on cerebrospinal fluid; definitive diagnosis requires brain biopsy or autopsy, which shows spongiform change.

Treatment and history

There is no effective treatment; care is supportive, and most patients die within a year of onset. The disease is named for German neurologists Hans Gerhard Creutzfeldt and Alfons Maria Jakob, who described cases in the early 1920s. Prion theory, developed by Stanley Prusiner, earned the Nobel Prize in 1997.
