The Indus is a transboundary river of Asia and a trans-Himalayan river of South and Central Asia. Rising in the Tibetan Plateau near Lake Mansarovar, it flows northwest through Ladakh, then turns south through Pakistan — whose economic lifeline it forms — to empty into the Arabian Sea near Karachi. At 3,180 km it is one of the longest rivers in Asia and gave India its name.

Course

Fed by glaciers of the Karakoram, Hindu Kush, and Himalaya, the Indus is joined by the Kabul River and then by the famous "five rivers" of the Punjab — Jhelum, Chenab, Ravi, Beas, and Sutlej. In its upper course it carves one of the deepest gorges on Earth near Nanga Parbat. The lower course crosses the plains of Punjab and Sindh before ending in a large delta.

History

The Indus Valley Civilisation (c. 3300–1300 BCE), one of the world's earliest urban cultures, flourished along the river at cities such as Mohenjo-daro and Harappa. Alexander the Great reached the Indus in 326 BCE. The 1960 Indus Waters Treaty between India and Pakistan allocates the basin's rivers and has survived several wars. Catastrophic floods, notably in 2010 and 2022, inundated vast areas of Pakistan.

Economy and ecology

The Indus system irrigates the largest contiguous irrigation network in the world and supports most of Pakistan's agriculture and much of its hydroelectricity (including the Tarbela and Diamer-Bhasha dams). The endangered Indus river dolphin survives only in this system.
