Rift lake

A rift lake is a lake formed as a result of subsidence related to movement on faults within a rift zone, an area of extensional tectonics in the continental crust. Rift lakes are typically long, narrow and very deep, with steep sides, because they occupy the floor of a subsiding graben between fault scarps.

Lake Baikal in Siberia, the world's deepest lake, occupies a continental rift.

Examples

The largest and most famous rift lakes are the African Great Lakes of the East African Rift, including Lake Tanganyika and Lake Malawi, which are among the deepest lakes on Earth. Lake Baikal in Siberia, lying in an active continental rift zone, is the deepest and oldest lake in the world and holds roughly a fifth of the world's fresh surface water. Other examples include the Dead Sea, Lake Vostok (subglacial), and numerous lakes of the Basin and Range Province of North America.

Ecology

Because many rift lakes are ancient and isolated, they are celebrated hotspots of endemism. The cichlid fishes of the African rift lakes have undergone spectacular adaptive radiations, producing hundreds of species found nowhere else. Lake Baikal similarly hosts a unique fauna including the Baikal seal and numerous endemic invertebrates.

Geology

Rift lakes fill depressions created as the crust is pulled apart. Continued extension may eventually split a continent and open a new ocean basin, as is beginning in the Afar region; the sedimentary records preserved in rift-lake basins are important archives of past climate and are also prospective source rocks for petroleum.
