Cantillation

Cantillation is the ritual chanting of readings from the Hebrew Bible in synagogue services. The texts are chanted according to a system of accents — the te'amim in Hebrew, often called trope in Yiddish and English — which appear in printed editions of the Bible as marks above and below the consonantal text. The accents serve three functions at once: they indicate the stress of words, they punctuate and parse the syntax of each verse, and they prescribe the musical motifs to which the words are sung.

History

Jewish tradition attributes public Torah reading to Moses and its regular weekly cycle to Ezra. The notation of the accents was developed by the Masoretes of Tiberias between roughly the sixth and tenth centuries CE, alongside the vowel points (niqqud) of the Masoretic Text. The signs themselves do not fix exact melodies: Ashkenazi, Sephardi, Yemenite, and other communities each preserve distinct traditional melodies for the Torah, for the haftarah (prophetic readings), and for scrolls such as Esther, Lamentations, Ruth, Ecclesiastes, and Song of Songs, which have their own cantillation systems and tunes.

Practice

The reader (ba'al korei) prepares the reading from an unvocalised Torah scroll, having memorised the accents from a printed tikkun, and chants from the scroll in the synagogue. Traditionally the reader (or an assistant following the text) used a silver pointer, the yad, to follow the words. Cantillation is typically taught to children preparing for bar and bat mitzvah, who chant the haftarah and sometimes the Torah portion. Analogous systems of chant exist in other traditions — Syriac and Byzantine chant and Qur'anic recitation (tajwid) are often compared — but the te'amim are distinctive in fusing grammatical, semantic, and musical notation in a single set of signs.
