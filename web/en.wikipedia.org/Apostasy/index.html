Apostasy

Apostasy
EtymologyFrom Greek apostasía, "a defection or revolt"
DefinitionThe formal disaffiliation from, abandonment of, or renunciation of a religion
Related termsHeresy, blasphemy, excommunication, deconversion

Apostasy (from Ancient Greek ἀποστασία, apostasía, "a defection or revolt") is the formal disaffiliation from, abandonment of, or renunciation of a religion by a person. It can also be defined within a broader context as embracing an opinion contrary to one's previous religious beliefs. One who undertakes apostasy is known as an apostate. The term is used by sociologists to mean the renunciation and criticism of, or opposition to, a person's former religion, though it carries a pejorative connotation in many religious communities, where apostasy has historically been punished, sometimes by death.

Historical treatment

In the ancient world, apostasy from state cults could be treated as a political crime. The Roman emperor Julian (r. 361–363) was dubbed "the Apostate" by Christian writers for abandoning Christianity and attempting to restore traditional Roman and Hellenistic religion. In medieval Christendom, apostasy by clergy or monks was an ecclesiastical offence; baptised Christians who converted to Judaism or Islam faced severe penalties, and during the Spanish Inquisition, alleged apostasy among converted Jews (conversos) was a principal target of prosecution.

In major religions

Christianity

Christian texts such as the Epistle to the Hebrews warn against apostasy, and theologians have long debated whether true believers can fall away. Historically, churches imposed penalties up to excommunication; in modern times, most Christian bodies treat leaving the faith as a matter of conscience, though some high-demand groups practise shunning of former members.

Islam

In classical Islamic jurisprudence, apostasy (ridda) was traditionally punishable by death for adult male apostates, based on certain hadith, though the Quran itself prescribes no worldly penalty and emphasises "no compulsion in religion." Scholars and Muslim-majority states differ sharply today; a number of countries still criminalise apostasy or blasphemy, while many Muslim thinkers argue the classical rulings reflected treason in particular historical circumstances.

Judaism and others

In Judaism, an apostate (meshumad) historically faced communal exclusion, though conversion out of Judaism is not a capital offence under modern Jewish practice. Hinduism and Buddhism generally lack a formal concept of apostasy, as belonging is defined less by creedal adherence.

Human rights perspective

Article 18 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights and the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights guarantee the freedom to change one's religion or belief. Human rights organisations criticise laws that criminalise apostasy as violations of freedom of religion; nevertheless, apostasy remains a capital offence in a small number of states, and former believers in many societies face family and social sanctions.
