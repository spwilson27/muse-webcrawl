Anchoring (cognitive bias)

An initial "anchor" value pulls subsequent numerical judgments toward itself.

Anchoring or focalism is a cognitive bias in which an individual relies too heavily on an initial piece of information (the "anchor") when making decisions. Once the anchor is set, subsequent judgments are made by adjusting away from it, and the adjustment is typically insufficient — leaving estimates biased toward the anchor even when it is arbitrary or explicitly random.

Experimental evidence

The effect was famously demonstrated by Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman in 1974: participants who watched a wheel of fortune land on 10 or 65, and were then asked what percentage of UN member states were African, gave median estimates of 25% and 45% respectively, though the wheel was obviously irrelevant. Anchoring has since been shown in pricing (a list price anchors negotiation), sentencing decisions by experienced judges, real-estate valuation, and medical diagnosis.

Explanations and mitigation

Two main mechanisms have been proposed: anchor-and-adjust, in which people move from the anchor until they reach a plausible value and stop too early, and selective accessibility, in which the anchor primes anchor-consistent information that then dominates judgment. The bias is difficult to eliminate; warning people about it and offering incentives has limited effect, though considering the opposite ("consider-the-opposite" strategies) can reduce it somewhat.
