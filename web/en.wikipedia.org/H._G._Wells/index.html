H. G. Wells

BornHerbert George Wells, 21 September 1866, Bromley, England
Died13 August 1946 (aged 79), London, England
OccupationWriter, historian, futurist
GenreScience fiction, social realism, history

Herbert George Wells (21 September 1866 – 13 August 1946) was an English writer, prolific in many genres. He is best remembered for his science fiction novels and is regarded, along with Jules Verne, as one of the "fathers of science fiction". His early "scientific romances" — The Time Machine (1895), The Island of Doctor Moreau (1896), The Invisible Man (1897) and The War of the Worlds (1898) — established many of the genre's enduring themes: time travel, alien invasion, invisibility and genetic engineering.

Wells trained in biology under Thomas Henry Huxley at the Normal School of Science in London, and evolutionary ideas shaped his fiction and his politics throughout his life. He later wrote successful social novels such as Kipps (1905), Tono-Bungay (1909) and The History of Mr Polly (1910), and the feminist novel Ann Veronica (1909).

Later work and ideas

A committed Fabian socialist and pacifist-turned-supporter of a world state, Wells wrote influential works of non-fiction, notably The Outline of History (1920), one of the best-selling history books of its time, The Open Conspiracy (1928) and The Rights of Man (1940), the latter influencing the Universal Declaration of Human Rights. He predicted aerial warfare, tanks, atomic bombs ("atomic bombs" is his coinage, in The World Set Free, 1914) and a form of the internet he called the "World Brain".

Wells was nominated for the Nobel Prize in Literature four times. He died at his home in Hanover Terrace, London, in 1946.
