Clément Marot

Born1496, Cahors, France
Died1544, Turin, Savoy
OccupationPoet, courtier
Notable workL'Adolescence clémentine; verse Psalms

Clément Marot (1496–1544) was a French Renaissance poet, one of the most celebrated vernacular poets of the reign of Francis I. The son of the poet Jean Marot, he served at court as valet de chambre to the king and to the king's sister, Marguerite de Navarre, whose evangelical circle shaped his religious sympathies.

Life and career

Marot's witty, elegant verse—epistles, epigrams, rondeaux, and ballades—made him famous, but his association with Reformation ideas repeatedly brought him into danger. He was imprisoned in 1526 on suspicion of heresy after allegedly eating meat during Lent, an episode he turned into the allegorical poem L'Enfer. After the Affair of the Placards in 1534 he fled France, taking refuge at the court of Ferrara and in Venice before being pardoned and returning.

The Psalms

Marot's most enduring work was his verse translation of the Psalms into French. Set to music, the fifty psalms he completed became the foundation of the Genevan Psalter and were sung by Huguenot congregations for centuries; after his final exile he spent his last months in Geneva with John Calvin before dying in Turin in 1544. His psalm translations were completed by Théodore de Bèze.

Style and legacy

Known for the light, musical form later called the style marotique, Marot modernized medieval fixed forms and helped shape the French lyric tradition. His influence extended to later poets and, through the psalter, to Protestant worship across Europe.
