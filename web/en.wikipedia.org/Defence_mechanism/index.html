In psychoanalytic theory, a defence mechanism (American spelling: defense mechanism) is an unconscious psychological operation that functions to protect a person from anxiety-producing thoughts and feelings related to internal conflicts and external stressors. According to this theory, healthy people normally use a range of defences throughout life, and a defence becomes pathological only when its persistent use leads to maladaptive behaviour that harms physical or mental health.

History

Sigmund Freud introduced the concept in his 1894 paper "The Neuro-Psychoses of Defence," and used it throughout his work to describe the ego's struggles against painful or unacceptable ideas and affects. His daughter Anna Freud systematised the theory in The Ego and the Mechanisms of Defence (1936), describing ten principal mechanisms. Later theorists, notably George Vaillant, arranged defences in a hierarchy from psychotic and immature defences, through neurotic defences, to mature defences such as humour, altruism, and sublimation.

Common mechanisms

  • Repression — excluding distressing memories or impulses from conscious awareness.
  • Denial — refusing to accept external facts or experiences that are too threatening.
  • Projection — attributing one's own unacceptable feelings or impulses to another person.
  • Rationalisation — constructing plausible but false explanations for one's behaviour.
  • Displacement — redirecting an emotion from its original target to a safer one.
  • Reaction formation — behaving in the opposite way to an unacceptable impulse.
  • Sublimation — channelling unacceptable impulses into socially valued activities, considered a mature defence.

Assessment and criticism

Clinicians assess defensive functioning in psychotherapy and in instruments such as the Defense Style Questionnaire, and DSM-IV included a proposed Defensive Functioning Scale. Empirical psychologists have debated the scientific status of individual mechanisms; repression in particular remains contested, although experimental work supports some mechanisms, such as projection and reaction formation, in modified form. The concept remains influential in psychodynamic therapy and in everyday language.
