Anatomy

The cheeks (Latin: buccae) constitute the area of the face below the eyes and between the nose and the left or right ear. "Buccal" means relating to the cheeks. The cheek is bounded above by the infraorbital region and the zygomatic arch (cheekbone), and forms the lateral wall of the oral cavity. Internally it is lined by buccal mucosa, a non-keratinised stratified squamous epithelium, and opens to the parotid duct opposite the upper second molar.

Structure and function

The principal muscle of the cheek is the buccinator, which compresses the cheek against the teeth during chewing, keeps food on the occlusal surfaces of the molars, and is used in blowing and whistling. Between the buccinator and the overlying skin lies the buccal fat pad, especially prominent in infants, where it aids suckling. Blood supply comes chiefly from the buccal artery, a branch of the maxillary artery, and sensation from the buccal nerve, a branch of the trigeminal nerve.

Society and culture

Cheeks are important in facial expression, nonverbal communication and assessments of health and emotion: blushing occurs in the cheeks, and dimples — small indentations often caused by a bifid zygomaticus major muscle — are widely considered an attractive trait. A kiss on the cheek is a common social greeting in many cultures.
