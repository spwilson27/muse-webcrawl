Racketeering

Racketeering prosecutions in the United States are most often brought under the federal RICO statute.

Racketeering is a type of organized crime in which the perpetrators set up a coercive, fraudulent, extortionary, or otherwise illegal coordinated scheme or operation (a "racket") to repeatedly or consistently collect a profit. The term is closely associated with the American Mafia and other criminal organisations, and was coined by the Employers' Association of Chicago in June 1927 in a statement about the influence of organised crime in the Teamsters union.

Originally and often still specifically, racketeering may refer to an organized criminal act in which the perpetrators offer a service that will not be put into effect, offer a service to solve a nonexistent problem, or offer a service that solves a problem that would not exist without the enterprise — the classic protection racket. In many other cases, rackets involve fraud, extortion, loansharking, illegal gambling, labour corruption and other continuing criminal enterprises.

Legal framework

In the United States, racketeering is criminalised most prominently by the Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations Act (RICO), enacted in 1970 as part of the Organized Crime Control Act. RICO makes it illegal to participate in the affairs of an enterprise through a "pattern of racketeering activity" — at least two acts from a list of state and federal predicate offences (including extortion, bribery, wire fraud, drug trafficking and murder) within ten years. Conviction carries up to 20 years' imprisonment per count, forfeiture of illegal gains, and the statute also provides a civil cause of action allowing private plaintiffs to recover treble damages.

Notable cases

RICO was used extensively from the 1980s against the American Mafia, including the Mafia Commission Trial of 1985–86, which convicted the heads of New York's Five Families. The statute has since been applied — sometimes controversially — to organisations far removed from the Mafia, including corrupt police departments, financial firms, protest groups and, in 2020s civil litigation, a variety of business defendants. Most US states have enacted analogous "little RICO" statutes.
