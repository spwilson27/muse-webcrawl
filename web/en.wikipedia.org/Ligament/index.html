Ligament

A ligament is a band or sheet of dense fibrous connective tissue that connects bones to other bones across a joint, stabilising the joint and limiting excessive movement. The term is also used for folds of peritoneum and other structures that support organs.

Structure and function

Ligaments consist largely of parallel type I collagen fibres produced by fibroblasts, giving high tensile strength with slight elasticity. They contain mechanoreceptors contributing to proprioception — the sense of joint position. Well-known examples include the anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) and other ligaments of the knee, the ligaments of the ankle and wrist, and the ligamenta flava of the spine.

Injuries

Overstretching causes sprains, graded from mild fibre damage to complete rupture. ACL tears are common in sport and may require surgical reconstruction using tendon grafts. Ligaments heal slowly because of their relatively poor blood supply.

Other uses of the term

Peritoneal ligaments (e.g. the falciform ligament of the liver) are double layers of peritoneum connecting organs; the periodontal ligament anchors teeth in their sockets; and remnants of fetal vessels, such as the ligamentum arteriosum, are also called ligaments. Ligaments differ from tendons, which connect muscle to bone, and from aponeuroses and fasciae.
