The Terracotta Army is a collection of terracotta sculptures depicting the armies of Qin Shi Huang, the first emperor of China. It is a form of funerary art buried with the emperor in 210–209 BC with the purpose of protecting him in his afterlife.

Discovery

The figures were discovered on 29 March 1974 by local farmers — including Yang Zhifa and his brothers — digging a well for water near the village of Xiyang, east of Xi'an. They found fragments of pottery figures and weapons at a depth of about four metres. The find was reported to the authorities and became one of the most significant archaeological discoveries of the twentieth century.

Construction

Construction of the mausoleum began shortly after Qin Shi Huang ascended the throne of Qin in 246 BC, and according to the historian Sima Qian involved some 700,000 workers. The warriors, averaging about 1.8 metres tall, vary in height, uniform and hairstyle according to rank. Their bodies were mass-produced in workshops from modular parts, but each face was individually finished, giving every soldier a distinct appearance. The figures were originally painted in bright pigments, most of which flaked off rapidly on exposure to air after excavation.

The pits

Pit 1, the largest, contains an estimated 6,000 figures in battle formation. Pit 2 contains cavalry, infantry and chariots, and Pit 3 is interpreted as the army's command post. A fourth pit was found empty, suggesting the project was abandoned amid the rebellions that followed the emperor's death in 210 BC.

The mausoleum

The emperor's tomb mound itself remains unexcavated. Sima Qian's account describes a vast underground palace with rivers of mercury — a claim supported by anomalously high mercury levels detected in soil samples over the mound.

Exhibition

A museum on the site opened in 1979. Selected warriors tour internationally, and the site was listed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1987 as part of the Mausoleum of the First Qin Emperor.
