The Poetic Edda, also known as the Elder Edda or Sæmund's Edda, is the modern name for an untitled collection of Old Norse poems, principally preserved in the Icelandic manuscript Codex Regius, written in the 13th century. The poems are thought to have circulated orally for centuries before being committed to writing; many may date from the Viking Age.

Contents

The Codex Regius contains 29 poems. The mythological lays include Völuspá, the prophecy of a seeress that recounts the creation and prophesied destruction (Ragnarök) of the world; Hávamál, the gnomic sayings of Odin; and tales of the gods such as Þrymskviða, in which Thor must dress as a bride to recover his hammer. The heroic lays turn to the Völsung cycle — Sigurd the dragon-slayer, the Nibelung treasure and the fall of the Burgundians — material shared with the German Nibelungenlied.

Influence

Together with Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda, the Poetic Edda is the principal source for Norse mythology. Its rediscovery in the 17th century fed Scandinavian Romanticism and influenced writers including J. R. R. Tolkien, whose legendarium drew on its names, motifs and verse forms. The Codex Regius was returned from Denmark to Iceland in 1971 amid great national celebration.
