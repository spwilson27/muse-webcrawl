Modernisme

Casa Batlló in Barcelona, remodelled by Antoni Gaudí, is a landmark of Modernisme

Modernisme (Catalan for "modernism") was a cultural movement centred in Catalonia, above all in Barcelona, roughly between 1888 — the year of the Barcelona Universal Exposition — and 1911. Part of the wider European Art Nouveau current, it was distinguished by its fusion with Catalan national revival (the Renaixença), an embrace of nature-inspired curving forms, rich ornament, and the integration of architecture with the decorative arts of ironwork, ceramics, stained glass, and mosaic (trencadís).

Architecture

The movement's great architects were Lluís Domènech i Montaner, Josep Puig i Cadafalch, and Antoni Gaudí. Domènech's Palau de la Música Catalana (1908) and Hospital de Sant Pau are masterpieces of brick, tile, and glass; Gaudí's Casa Batlló, Casa Milà ("La Pedrera"), Park Güell, and the still-unfinished Sagrada Família basilica carried organic form furthest. Industrial patrons such as Eusebi Güell financed many of the works, and nine modernista buildings in Barcelona are inscribed on UNESCO's World Heritage list.

Literature, painting, and music

In literature, Modernisme sought to modernise Catalan culture: the poet Joan Maragall, novelist Víctor Català (Caterina Albert), and the playwright Santiago Rusiñol — also a noted painter — were central figures, many gathering at the Barcelona café Els Quatre Gats, where the young Pablo Picasso exhibited. Painters Ramon Casas and Isidre Nonell brought Parisian influences into Catalan art. The movement gradually gave way after 1906 to Noucentisme, a classicising reaction promoted by Eugeni d'Ors.

Legacy

Modernisme remains the defining visual identity of Barcelona, drawing millions of visitors annually, and a symbol of Catalan cultural identity.
