Dirichlet L-function

In mathematics, a Dirichlet L-series is a function of the form

L(s, χ) = Σn=1∞ χ(n) / ns,

where χ is a Dirichlet character and s a complex variable with real part greater than 1. By analytic continuation this function can be extended to a meromorphic function on the whole complex plane, and is then called a Dirichlet L-function and also denoted L(s, χ). These functions are named after Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet, who introduced them in 1837 to prove his theorem on primes in arithmetic progressions.

Properties

For the principal character χ₀ (which is 1 on numbers coprime to the modulus and 0 otherwise), L(s, χ₀) differs from the Riemann zeta function only by finitely many Euler factors, and has a simple pole at s = 1 with residue φ(q)/q. For non-principal characters, L(s, χ) is entire. Dirichlet L-functions satisfy a functional equation relating L(s, χ) to L(1 − s, χ̄), involving the gamma function, the Gauss sum of the character, and a power of π. They factor as Euler products over primes, reflecting the complete multiplicativity of χ.

Dirichlet's theorem

Dirichlet proved that if a and q are coprime, there are infinitely many primes congruent to a mod q, by showing that L(1, χ) ≠ 0 for every non-principal character χ. The non-vanishing at s = 1 is the analytic heart of the proof and is closely related to Dirichlet's class number formula for quadratic fields, which expresses L(1, χd) in terms of the class number, regulator and discriminant of ℚ(√d).

Zeros

The generalised Riemann hypothesis asserts that all nontrivial zeros of every Dirichlet L-function lie on the critical line Re(s) = 1/2. Of particular concern are real zeros close to s = 1 — so-called Siegel zeros — whose possible existence complicates effective results on primes in arithmetic progressions; Siegel's theorem bounds them, but ineffectively. Zero-free regions for L-functions underpin the prime number theorem for arithmetic progressions and estimates for the least prime in an arithmetic progression (Linnik's theorem).
