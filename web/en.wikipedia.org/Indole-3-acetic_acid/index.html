Indole-3-acetic acid

Indole-3-acetic acid (IAA, 3-IAA) is the most common naturally occurring plant hormone of the auxin class. It is a white crystalline solid with the molecular formula C10H9NO2, derived biosynthetically from the amino acid tryptophan. IAA regulates many aspects of plant growth and development, including cell elongation and division, tissue differentiation, apical dominance, fruit development, and tropic responses to light and gravity.

Discovery

The existence of a growth-promoting signal produced in coleoptile tips was demonstrated in the experiments of Charles and Francis Darwin (1880) and Frits Went (1928), whose oat coleoptile curvature assay led to the isolation of "auxin." Kenneth Thimann and others identified the active compound as indole-3-acetic acid in the 1930s.

Synthesis and transport

Plants synthesize IAA mainly in young leaves and shoot apical meristems, principally via the indole-3-pyruvic acid pathway involving TAA aminotransferases and YUCCA flavin monooxygenases. IAA is redistributed through polar auxin transport, mediated by PIN efflux carrier proteins, creating the concentration gradients that pattern development. The hormone acts through the TIR1/AFB receptor pathway, triggering degradation of Aux/IAA repressor proteins and activating gene expression.

Uses

Synthetic IAA and more stable auxin analogues are used in horticulture to promote rooting of cuttings, set fruit, and — in the case of synthetic auxin herbicides such as 2,4-D — to kill broadleaf weeds.
