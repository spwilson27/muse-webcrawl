French-based creole languages

A French creole, or French-based creole language, is a creole language for which French is the lexifier. Most often this lexifier consists of a 17th- or 18th-century koiné of French from Paris, the Île-de-France, and the French Atlantic ports, with influence from other dialects and substrate languages spoken by enslaved Africans and others in French colonies.

Classification

French-based creoles are usually grouped geographically. In the Americas they include Haitian Creole, the most widely spoken creole language in the world with roughly ten million speakers; Antillean Creole, spoken in Martinique, Guadeloupe, Dominica, and Saint Lucia; Louisiana Creole; and French Guianese Creole. In the Indian Ocean they include Mauritian Creole, Réunion Creole, and Seychellois Creole. Pacific varieties include Tayo of New Caledonia.

Features

These languages typically combine French-derived vocabulary with simplified inflection, preverbal tense–mood–aspect particles, and grammar influenced by West African substrate languages. In most communities the creole coexists with standard French in a diglossic relationship, though Haitian Creole is an official language of Haiti alongside French and has a standardised orthography.

Status

Historically stigmatised as corrupted French, French-based creoles are increasingly used in education, broadcasting, and literature, and are recognised in linguistics as full languages in their own right.
