Microeconomics is a branch of mainstream economics that studies the behaviour of individuals and firms in making decisions regarding the allocation of scarce resources and the interactions among these individuals and firms. Whereas macroeconomics studies the economy as a whole, microeconomics focuses on particular markets, industries, and decision-makers such as consumers, workers, and firms.

Supply and demand

The foundational model of microeconomics describes how prices are determined in a market by the interaction of supply and demand. Demand reflects the quantity of a good buyers are willing to purchase at each price, typically decreasing as price rises; supply reflects the quantity sellers will offer, typically increasing with price. The intersection of the curves defines the market equilibrium price and quantity. Changes in income, input costs, technology, or expectations shift the curves and move the equilibrium.

Elasticity

Elasticity measures the responsiveness of quantity demanded or supplied to a change in price or income. Price elasticity of demand equals the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price. Goods with close substitutes, luxuries, and goods that take a large share of the budget tend to have more elastic demand; necessities such as insulin have very inelastic demand.

Consumer and producer theory

Consumer theory models individuals as maximising utility subject to a budget constraint; its geometry of indifference curves and budget lines yields demand curves and concepts such as the marginal rate of substitution. Producer theory models firms as maximising profit, choosing inputs and outputs subject to a production function and cost structure; the marginal cost curve forms the firm's supply curve in competitive markets.

Market failure

Microeconomics also analyses market failures — situations in which unregulated markets allocate resources inefficiently. These include externalities (such as pollution), public goods subject to free-riding, information asymmetries (adverse selection and moral hazard), and market power. Proposed remedies include Pigouvian taxes, subsidies, regulation, and the assignment of property rights, as examined in welfare economics and the theory of the second best.
