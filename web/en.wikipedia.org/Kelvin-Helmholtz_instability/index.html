Kelvin–Helmholtz instability

The Kelvin–Helmholtz instability (after Lord Kelvin and Hermann von Helmholtz) is a fluid instability that occurs when there is a velocity difference across the interface between two fluids of different densities. The resulting shear produces characteristic rolling wave patterns that grow and break down into turbulence.

Occurrence

The instability is visible in wave-like fluctus clouds formed when wind blows over them, in the banded appearance and Great Red Spot dynamics of Jupiter, in ocean surface waves generated by wind, and in astrophysical flows and fusion plasmas.

Theory

Linear stability analysis shows that small perturbations of the interface grow exponentially when the relative velocity exceeds a threshold set by gravity, surface tension and magnetic fields, which stabilise the flow at short wavelengths.
