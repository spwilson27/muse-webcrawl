Medium-density fibreboard

Medium-density fibreboard (MDF) is an engineered wood product made by breaking down hardwood or softwood residuals into wood fibres, often in a defibrator, combining these fibres with wax and a resin binder, and forming them into panels by applying high temperature and pressure. MDF is generally denser than plywood and is made of separated fibres, but it can be used as a building material similar in application to plywood.

Properties

MDF has a smooth, uniform surface with no visible grain, which makes it easy to paint and veneer. Its density is typically between 600 and 800 kg/m³, compared with about 500 kg/m³ for particleboard. Because the fibres are fine and evenly distributed, MDF can be machined to detailed profiles without splintering, and edges can be shaped and finished directly.

Manufacture

Wood chips are steamed and ground into fibres, blended with resin and wax, then formed into a mat. The mat is hot-pressed to bond the resin and compress the fibres to the target thickness, after which the panels are cooled, trimmed and sanded. Most MDF uses urea-formaldehyde resin, though lower-emission and no-added-formaldehyde binders are also produced.

Uses

MDF is widely used for flat-pack furniture, kitchen and bathroom cabinets, interior doors, skirting boards and decorative mouldings. Its smooth face makes it a common substrate for melamine laminates and wood veneers. It is not generally suitable for exterior or load-bearing structural use because it absorbs moisture and swells when wet.

Health and safety

Cutting MDF generates fine dust, and formaldehyde may be released from the resin during manufacture and machining. Occupational exposure is controlled with extraction ventilation and respiratory protection, and regulations in many countries set formaldehyde emission limits for finished boards.
