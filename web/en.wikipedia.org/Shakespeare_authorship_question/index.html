Shakespeare authorship question

Alternative candidates proposed by anti-Stratfordians include Francis Bacon, Edward de Vere, and Christopher Marlowe.

The Shakespeare authorship question is the argument that someone other than William Shakespeare of Stratford-upon-Avon wrote the works attributed to him. Anti-Stratfordians—a collective term for proponents of the various alternative-authorship theories—believe that Shakespeare of Stratford was a front to shield the identity of the real author or authors, who, for reasons such as social rank, state security, or gender, could not be publicly acknowledged.

Doubts about Shakespeare's authorship were first raised in the mid-19th century, when the plays and poems came to be regarded as the supreme expression of English literature. The most frequently proposed candidates include Francis Bacon, philosopher and statesman; Edward de Vere, 17th Earl of Oxford, the leading "Oxfordian" candidate since the 1920s; Christopher Marlowe, the rival playwright; and Mary Sidney, Countess of Pembroke. Group theories propose collaboration among several writers.

Mainstream view

The overwhelming majority of Shakespeare scholars and literary historians consider the authorship question a fringe theory, holding that the documentary evidence—title pages, contemporary references, the First Folio of 1623 assembled by Shakespeare's fellow actors John Heminges and Henry Condell, and the testimony of contemporaries such as Ben Jonson—establishes Shakespeare of Stratford's authorship beyond reasonable doubt. Critics of alternative theories note the absence of contemporary doubt about Shakespeare's authorship and the anachronisms in several candidates' timelines, such as de Vere's death in 1604, before several plays were written.

History of the debate

Early scepticism appeared in print in the 1850s in works by Joseph C. Hart and Delia Bacon, who proposed Francis Bacon as the principal author of a group theory. The Baconian theory flourished in the late 19th century, spawning elaborate claims of ciphers hidden in the texts. In 1920, schoolteacher J. Thomas Looney proposed Edward de Vere, founding the Oxfordian movement, which remains the most popular anti-Stratfordian position today.
