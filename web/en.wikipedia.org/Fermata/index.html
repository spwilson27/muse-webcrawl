Fermata

A fermata (from Italian fermare, "to stop"; also called a hold or pause) is a symbol in musical notation indicating that a note, chord, or rest should be prolonged beyond its normal duration, at the discretion of the performer or conductor. It is written as a dot beneath a semicircle (or "bird's eye") placed above or below the note.

Usage

The length of a fermata is not fixed; the performer decides how long to sustain the sound, often roughly doubling the written value, though in ensemble performance the conductor controls the release. A fermata over a barline or rest indicates a pause in the music. In concertos, a fermata over a chord often signals the start of a cadenza, an improvised or written-out solo passage.

Historical practice

In Baroque and Renaissance music, a fermata could simply mark the end of a phrase, as in the chorales of J. S. Bach, where fermatas indicate phrase endings for breathing rather than dramatic prolongation. In chorale harmonisations the symbol helps singers coordinate breaths.

Types

Modern notation sometimes distinguishes square, triangular, and curved fermatas to suggest short, medium, and long holds respectively, a convention introduced in the 20th century and used by composers such as Francis Poulenc. The opposite instruction — to play strictly in tempo without pause — may be given where a fermata might otherwise be assumed by convention.
