Twinkling

Twinkling, also called scintillation, is a generic term for variations in apparent brightness, colour or position of a distant luminous object viewed through a medium, most commonly the atmosphere. When the object is a star, the effect is known as stellar scintillation; the familiar nursery rhyme "Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star" describes it. The term astronomical scintillation refers specifically to variations caused by the Earth's atmosphere.

Cause

Starlight passing through the atmosphere traverses many turbulent cells of air at different temperatures and densities, each refracting the light slightly differently. Because the cells move with the wind, the amount of light reaching the eye fluctuates rapidly, and the star appears to flicker and change colour. Stars near the horizon twinkle more, since their light passes through a greater thickness of air. Planets generally twinkle much less because, unlike stars, they present a visible disc; the scintillation from different points of the disc averages out.

Astronomical significance

Scintillation limits the precision of ground-based photometry and contributes to the blurring known as astronomical seeing. Astronomers mitigate it by observing from high, dry sites, using large apertures, or employing adaptive optics. Rapid radio scintillation of pulsars similarly probes the interstellar medium.
