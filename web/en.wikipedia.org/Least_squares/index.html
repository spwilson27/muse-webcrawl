Least squares

The method of least squares is a standard approach in regression analysis to approximate the solution of overdetermined systems — sets of equations with more equations than unknowns — by minimising the sum of the squares of the residuals, the differences between observed and fitted values.

History

Adrien-Marie Legendre published the method in 1805 in work on comet orbits, while Carl Friedrich Gauss claimed to have used it since 1795; the resulting priority dispute is famous. Gauss connected the method to probability theory through the normal distribution, and Andrey Markov later established the Gauss–Markov theorem, showing that least squares gives the best linear unbiased estimator under weak assumptions.

Linear least squares

For a model y = Xβ + ε, the least-squares estimate of β solves the normal equations XᵀXβ = Xᵀy, equivalently minimising ‖y − Xβ‖². Numerically, solutions are computed by QR factorisation or the singular value decomposition rather than by forming the normal equations directly.

Extensions

Non-linear least squares problems are solved iteratively, e.g. by the Gauss–Newton algorithm. Related variants include weighted least squares, total least squares, and regularised forms such as ridge regression. Ordinary least squares (OLS) remains the workhorse of linear regression in statistics and econometrics.
