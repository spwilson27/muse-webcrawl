Obergefell v. Hodges, 576 U.S. 644 (2015), is a landmark United States Supreme Court decision holding that the fundamental right to marry is guaranteed to same-sex couples by both the Due Process Clause and the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. The ruling required all fifty states to perform and recognize marriages of same-sex couples on the same terms as marriages of opposite-sex couples.

Background

The case consolidated challenges to bans in Michigan, Ohio, Kentucky, and Tennessee, after the Sixth Circuit had upheld those bans, creating a circuit split. The lead plaintiff, Jim Obergefell, had sued Ohio to be recognized as the surviving spouse of his terminally ill husband, John Arthur, whom he had married in Maryland. The defendant, Richard Hodges, was the director of the Ohio Department of Health.

Decision

Justice Anthony Kennedy's majority opinion identified four principles supporting marriage as a fundamental right that applies equally to same-sex couples: individual autonomy, the committed two-person union, safeguards for children and families, and marriage as a keystone of social order. Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Scalia, Thomas, and Alito each wrote dissents arguing the question should be left to democratic processes and state legislatures.

Impact

The decision legalized same-sex marriage nationwide and is regarded as the culmination of the American LGBT rights movement's marriage campaign. Its principles were later reinforced by the Respect for Marriage Act of 2022, which required federal and interstate recognition of valid marriages. June 26 — also the date of the earlier Lawrence v. Texas (2003) and United States v. Windsor (2013) rulings — became a celebrated anniversary in LGBTQ culture.
