Henry Joseph Darger Jr. (12 April 1892 – 13 April 1973) was an American writer, novelist, and artist who worked as a hospital custodian in Chicago, Illinois. He has become famous for his posthumously discovered 15,145-page fantasy novel manuscript, The Story of the Vivian Girls, in What Is Known as the Realms of the Unreal, of the Glandeco-Angelinian War Storm, Caused by the Child Slave Rebellion, along with several hundred drawings and watercolor paintings illustrating the story. Darger is regarded as one of the most significant figures in outsider art.

Life

Darger was born in Chicago; his mother died when he was four, and at age eight, judged "feeble-minded" after behavioural incidents, he was placed in the Illinois Asylum for Feeble-Minded Children in Lincoln, from which he escaped in 1908. Returning to Chicago, he found work as a janitor and dishwasher in Catholic hospitals, attended Mass daily, and lived in a single rented room in Lincoln Park. Apart from a brief period of U.S. Army service in 1917–18, he lived in near-total obscurity, writing and painting in secret for over four decades.

Work

In the Realms of the Unreal narrates the war between the Christian nation of Abbieannia and the child-enslaving Glandelinians, led on the Christian side by the seven Vivian Girls, princesses who rescue enslaved children. Darger's illustrations — large, often double-sided watercolour panels — combine traced and collaged imagery from magazines, colouring books, and advertisements with his own drawing. He also wrote a sequel of comparable length, an autobiography, weather journals, and other manuscripts. When his landlord Nathan Lerner discovered the work as Darger, dying, was moved to a nursing home in 1972, Darger's reported response about publication was: "Too late now."

Legacy

Darger's room has been partly reconstructed as the Henry Darger Study Center, whose collection is now at the American Folk Art Museum in New York. His art and story have inspired the 2004 documentary In the Realms of the Unreal, works by numerous artists and writers, and sustained critical debate about outsider art, childhood, violence, and faith.
