Prague uprising

The Prague uprising (Czech: Pražské povstání) was a partially successful attempt by the Czech resistance movement to liberate the city of Prague from German occupation during World War II. The uprising began on 5 May 1945, in the last moments of the war in Europe. It was largely fought by unorganized resistance fighters with small arms, supported by the Czech police and gendarmerie, and by the Russian Liberation Army, which turned against the Germans. After more than three days of fighting, the German forces in the city agreed to withdraw, and Soviet troops of the Red Army entered Prague on 9 May; an estimated 1,694 Czechs and 1,000 Germans were killed during the uprising.

Background

Prague had been occupied since 1939 as the capital of the Protectorate of Bohemia and Moravia. By spring 1945, the Wehrmacht was collapsing; American forces were advancing into western Bohemia, and the Red Army was fighting through Moravia. Resistance groups and the Czech National Council prepared for an uprising rather than allow the Germans to destroy the city or fight street by street with Soviet troops.

The uprising

On 5 May, protests and attacks on German personnel escalated into open fighting after Czech radio broadcast appeals for help. Insurgents seized the radio station, police stations, and key buildings, and Praguers built over 1,600 barricades across the city. German forces, including Waffen-SS units, counterattacked with tanks and aircraft. The Russian Liberation Army under General Bunyachenko, seeking goodwill from the Western Allies, intervened on the Czech side on 6–7 May before withdrawing westward. On 8 May, with Germany's surrender imminent and Soviet forces approaching, the Czech National Council negotiated a ceasefire allowing German units to withdraw from Prague. The Old Town Hall was heavily damaged by German fire during the fighting. Soviet troops entered the largely liberated city on 9 May 1945.

Legacy

The uprising is commemorated annually in Prague, with memorials at the radio building, the barricades, and the Old Town Hall. Under the Communist regime, its history was reframed to emphasize the Soviet liberation and minimize the roles of the domestic resistance and the Russian Liberation Army; since 1989, these aspects have been more fully acknowledged.
