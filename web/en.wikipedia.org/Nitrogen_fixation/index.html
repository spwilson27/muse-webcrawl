Nitrogen fixation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Nitrogen fixation is a chemical and biological process in which molecular nitrogen (N2) in the air is converted into ammonia (NH3) or related nitrogenous compounds available to living organisms. Because the triple bond of N2 is exceptionally strong, fixation is energetically costly and, in nature, is carried out almost exclusively by certain bacteria and archaea (diazotrophs) possessing the enzyme complex nitrogenase. Nitrogen fixation is essential to life, since fixed nitrogen is required to build amino acids, proteins, and nucleic acids.

Biological fixation

Diazotrophs include free-living soil and aquatic organisms (such as Azotobacter and many cyanobacteria, e.g. Trichodesmium and Anabaena) and symbiotic bacteria. The best-known symbiosis is between leguminous plants (family Fabaceae) and rhizobia (Rhizobium, Bradyrhizobium, and others), which inhabit root nodules, receive sugars from the plant, and supply it with fixed nitrogen in return. Other symbioses involve the actinobacterium Frankia with alder and other actinorhizal plants, and cyanobacteria with cycads, the water fern Azolla, and lichens. The nitrogenase reaction consumes large amounts of ATP and is irreversibly inhibited by oxygen, which the nodule counters with the oxygen-carrying protein leghemoglobin.

Industrial and abiotic fixation

The Haber–Bosch process, developed in the early 20th century, fixes nitrogen industrially by reacting N2 with hydrogen over an iron catalyst at high temperature and pressure; its ammonia output underpins synthetic fertilisers that support roughly half of the world's population. Lightning also fixes small amounts of nitrogen by producing nitrogen oxides that rain out as nitrate, and combustion engines generate nitrogen oxides from air.

Ecological importance

In most terrestrial and many marine ecosystems, the supply of fixed nitrogen limits plant growth — the reason legume rotation and green manures were central to pre-industrial agriculture. Researchers have long sought to transfer nitrogen-fixing ability to cereals or to design synthetic symbioses, which would reduce dependence on fertiliser. Excess anthropogenic fixation, however, has doubled the flow of reactive nitrogen through ecosystems, with consequences including eutrophication and nitrous oxide emissions.
