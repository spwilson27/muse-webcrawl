The Highland Clearances (Scottish Gaelic: Fuadaichean nan Gàidheal) were the evictions of a significant number of tenants in the Scottish Highlands and Islands, mostly between 1750 and 1860, as landlords replaced traditional mixed farming and the clan social order with large-scale sheep farming under the banner of agricultural improvement.

Background

After the Jacobite rising of 1745, the clan system was dismantled by legislation and the commercialisation of estates. Chiefs, once bound by kinship obligations, became commercial landlords seeking higher rents. Cheviot sheep, introduced from the 1760s, offered far greater returns than the rents of numerous small tenants, and whole glens were cleared — most notoriously the Sutherland Clearances of 1807–1821, overseen by factors such as Patrick Sellar, who was tried and acquitted for the burning of a cottage in 1816.

Second phase and aftermath

A second phase followed the collapse of the kelp industry and the potato famine of the 1840s, when landlords — sometimes with assisted-passage schemes — pressed cottars and crofters to emigrate, above all to Canada, the United States, and Australia. Evictions provoked resistance, notably the "Battle of the Braes" (1882), and eventually legislation: the Crofters' Holdings (Scotland) Act 1886 granted security of tenure. The Clearances remain a deeply emotive subject in Scottish and diaspora memory, transforming Highland demography, the Gaelic language, and landscapes still patterned with ruined townships.
