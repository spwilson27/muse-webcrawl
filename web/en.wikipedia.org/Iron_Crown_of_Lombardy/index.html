Iron Crown of Lombardy

The Iron Crown of Lombardy (Italian: Corona Ferrea di Lombardia; Latin: Corona Ferrea Langobardiae) is a reliquary and votive crown that was used for the coronation of the Kings of Italy. It consists of a broad band of gold decorated with enamels and precious stones, enclosing a thin inner band of iron which tradition says was beaten from a nail of the True Cross. It is kept in the treasury of Monza Cathedral in Lombardy.

Description

The crown is made of six hinged gold plates joined into a circlet about 53 cm in circumference and 8 cm wide. It is set with garnets, amethysts, sapphires, rubies and coloured glass, and decorated with rosettes and cloisonné enamel plaques depicting figures, commonly interpreted as Christ between apostles. Scientific examination has shown the inner band to be silver-coloured rather than pure iron, prompting debate about when the "iron" element entered the tradition.

History

According to legend the nail came from the Empress Helena, mother of Constantine, and was fashioned into a crown for the Lombard queen Theodelinda, who presented it to Monza in the late sixth century. In the Middle Ages the Kings of Italy — including emperors such as Charlemagne, Otto I, Henry IV and Frederick Barbarossa — were traditionally crowned with it, often by the Archbishop of Milan. Charles V was crowned with it at Bologna in 1530. Use of the crown lapsed, but Napoleon Bonaparte revived the ceremony, crowning himself King of Italy with it in Milan Cathedral in 1805 and reportedly declaring, "God has given it to me; let him beware who touches it." Ferdinand I of Austria was crowned with it in 1838. During the Italian Wars of Independence the crown was taken to Vienna for safekeeping and returned to Monza in 1866, after which it ceased to be used for coronations.

Symbolism

The Iron Crown became an enduring symbol of Italian kingship and national identity, referenced in art, literature and the chivalric Order of the Iron Crown founded by Napoleon in 1805.
