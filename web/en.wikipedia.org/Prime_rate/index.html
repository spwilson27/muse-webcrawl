Prime rate

Also calledPrime lending rate
TypeBenchmark interest rate
Set byCommercial banks
Related toFederal funds rate

The prime rate, or prime lending rate, is an interest rate used by banks, usually the interest rate at which banks lend to their most creditworthy customers. Some variable interest rates may be expressed as a percentage above or below the prime rate.

Use in the United States

In the United States, the prime rate is the rate at which banks charge their most creditworthy customers for loans. The modern U.S. prime rate is closely tied to the federal funds rate set by the Federal Reserve; since the mid-1990s it has generally been three percentage points above the federal funds rate. It is published in newspapers and financial media, with the Wall Street Journal prime rate — based on a survey of the largest U.S. banks — serving as the most widely quoted figure.

Determination

Individual banks set their own prime rates, but most move in lockstep with changes in the federal funds target rate. When the Federal Open Market Committee raises or lowers its target, banks typically adjust their prime rate by the same amount within days. The rate is an index widely used for setting rates on home equity lines of credit, credit cards, and small business loans.

History

The prime rate emerged as a formal benchmark in the United States during the 1930s, when major banks began posting a standard rate for their best borrowers. Before that era, lending rates varied widely. During the era of high inflation in the late 1970s and early 1980s, the U.S. prime rate reached its all-time high of 21.5 percent in December 1980.

International usage

Other countries use analogous benchmarks. In Canada, the prime rate is set by each bank and follows the Bank of Canada overnight rate. Many economies instead use interbank offered rates or central-bank policy rates as their principal lending benchmarks.
