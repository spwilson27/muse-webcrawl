Unibody is the informal term for unitary body construction in automobiles, in which the body and frame are merged into a single structural unit. Rather than mounting a non-structural body on a separate ladder frame (body-on-frame), the vehicle's floor pan, roof, pillars, and bulkheads are welded together so that the entire shell carries loads from the suspension, engine, and passengers.

Advantages and disadvantages

Unitary construction is generally lighter and stiffer than body-on-frame for a given size, improving handling, fuel economy, and crash safety: engineers can design crumple zones and a rigid safety cell precisely into the shell. The trade-offs are more difficult and expensive repair after collision damage, more road noise transmission, and lower towing and off-road durability, which is why most pickup trucks and heavy SUVs remain body-on-frame.

History

Early unit-construction cars included the 1934 Citroën Traction Avant and Chrysler Airflow, and the 1936 Lincoln-Zephyr. Nash championed the approach in the United States with the 1941 Nash 600. After the Second World War, unitary construction spread through the European industry and became the standard for passenger cars worldwide by the 1970s–80s; today's electric "skateboard" platforms once again separate a flat battery chassis from varied upper bodies, an evolution of the same idea.
