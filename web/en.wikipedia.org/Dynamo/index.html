Dynamo

A dynamo is an electrical generator that produces direct current using a commutator. Dynamos were the first electrical generators capable of delivering power for industry and the foundation on which many later electric-power conversion devices were based.

Principle

A dynamo converts mechanical rotation into electrical energy through electromagnetic induction: a coil of wire rotates within a magnetic field, inducing an alternating voltage, which the commutator — a rotating split-ring switch — rectifies into direct current at the brushes.

History

The first dynamo, based on Michael Faraday's principles, was built by Hippolyte Pixii in 1832. In the 1860s and 1870s, self-exciting designs by Werner von Siemens, Charles Wheatstone and Zénobe Gramme made dynamos practical for arc lighting and industrial power, powering early factories and electroplating works.

Decline and legacy

From the 1890s, alternators displaced dynamos for large-scale power generation because alternating current could be transformed and transmitted efficiently. Small dynamos survived for decades in automotive charging systems and bicycle lighting; the word also survives in dynamo theory, explaining how celestial bodies generate magnetic fields.
