Rubella

Rubella, also known as German measles or three-day measles, is an infection caused by the rubella virus. It is usually mild, with symptoms including a red rash, low-grade fever, sore throat and swollen lymph nodes behind the ears; many infections cause no symptoms at all. Its seriousness lies in infection during pregnancy, which can cause miscarriage or congenital rubella syndrome in the baby.

TransmissionRespiratory droplets

Signs and symptoms

After an incubation period of about two to three weeks, a pink rash spreads from the face over the body, typically lasting three days, accompanied by tender post-auricular and occipital lymphadenopathy. Adults, especially women, may develop joint pain and arthritis. Complications are uncommon but include thrombocytopenia and encephalitis.

Congenital rubella syndrome

Infection in the first trimester of pregnancy carries a high risk of congenital rubella syndrome (CRS), characterised by the classic triad of congenital cataracts, sensorineural deafness and congenital heart disease (especially patent ductus arteriosus), along with growth restriction and developmental disability. The link was first recognised by Australian ophthalmologist Norman Gregg in 1941 after an epidemic. The rubella vaccine, developed by Stanley Plotkin in the 1960s and delivered as part of the MMR vaccine, has eliminated endemic rubella from the Americas (declared in 2015) and greatly reduced it elsewhere; the World Health Organization targets global elimination. Rubella is spread through respiratory droplets, and people are infectious from about a week before to a week after the rash appears.
