B-tree

In computer science, a B-tree is a self-balancing tree data structure that maintains sorted data and allows searches, sequential access, insertions, and deletions in logarithmic time. The B-tree generalizes the binary search tree, allowing nodes to have many children, and is optimized for systems that read and write large blocks of data, such as disks.

History

B-trees were invented by Rudolf Bayer and Edward M. McCreight while working at Boeing Research Labs in 1970, and described in their 1972 paper "Organization and maintenance of large ordered indexes". The meaning of the letter "B" has never been officially explained; it may stand for Boeing, balanced, or Bayer.

Properties

In a B-tree of order m, every node has at most m children; every internal node holds a sorted list of keys that act as separators dividing its subtrees into ranges. All leaves are at the same depth, keeping the tree balanced and shallow: even with millions of keys, the height of the tree is only a handful of levels, minimizing disk accesses.

Applications

B-trees and their variants, especially the B+ tree, are the standard indexing structure in databases and filesystems. They are used in the storage engines of relational databases, in filesystems such as NTFS, ext4 (via extents trees), and btrfs, and in many key-value stores.
