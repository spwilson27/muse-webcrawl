Electrical resistance and conductance

Electrical resistance is a measure of the opposition to the flow of electric current through a conductor. The corresponding reciprocal quantity is electrical conductance, which measures how readily current passes.

Ohm's law

For many materials, the current through a conductor is proportional to the voltage across it, a relationship known as Ohm's law: V = IR, where V is voltage, I is current, and R is resistance, measured in ohms (symbol Ω).

Conductance

Conductance G is the reciprocal of resistance: G = 1/R. Its SI unit is the siemens (S), formerly called the mho. Conductance depends on the same factors as resistance: material, length, and cross-sectional area.

Resistance of conductors

The resistance of a uniform conductor is R = ρL/A, where ρ is the electrical resistivity of the material, L its length, and A its cross-sectional area. Metals such as copper and aluminium have low resistivity and are used for wiring, while insulators such as rubber have extremely high resistance.

Temperature dependence

The resistance of most conductors increases with temperature, whereas that of semiconductors generally decreases. At very low temperatures, some materials become superconductors with zero resistance.
