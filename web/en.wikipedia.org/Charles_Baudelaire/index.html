Charles Baudelaire


Charles Baudelaire (1821–1867), French poet, author of Les Fleurs du mal.

Charles Pierre Baudelaire (9 April 1821 – 31 August 1867) was a French poet who also worked as an essayist, art critic and pioneering translator of Edgar Allan Poe. His most famous work, the collection of lyric poetry Les Fleurs du mal (The Flowers of Evil), expresses the changing nature of beauty in the rapidly industrialising Paris of the mid-19th century. Baudelaire is regarded as a foundational figure of Symbolism and of literary modernism.

Life

Born in Paris, Baudelaire lost his father at six and was sent reluctantly on a sea voyage to India in 1841 in an attempt to break his bohemian habits; the journey instead furnished him with exotic imagery. Living on an inheritance, he moved among Parisian literary and artistic circles, became a noted dandy, ran through much of his fortune and was placed under a financial guardian in 1844. His relationship with Jeanne Duval, the "Black Venus" of many of his poems, lasted two decades.

Les Fleurs du mal

Published in 1857, Les Fleurs du mal was prosecuted for offences against public morality; Baudelaire was fined and six poems were banned (the ban in France lasted until 1949). The collection's sections move from "Spleen et Idéal" through Parisian scenes to revolt, wine and death, seeking beauty in traditionally ugly or forbidden subjects. Baudelaire also coined the concept of the flâneur and wrote the influential essay The Painter of Modern Life (1863), praising Constantin Guys and articulating a programme of modernity.

Later years and influence

In 1864 Baudelaire left for Brussels, where his health collapsed; he suffered a stroke in 1866 and died in Paris the following year, aged 46. His prose poems, Le Spleen de Paris, appeared posthumously in 1869. Verlaine, Rimbaud, Mallarmé and later T. S. Eliot all acknowledged his influence on modern poetry.
