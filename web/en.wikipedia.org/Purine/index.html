Purine

FormulaC5H4N4
StructureFused pyrimidine and imidazole rings
Important derivativesAdenine, guanine, caffeine, uric acid

Purine is a heterocyclic aromatic organic compound consisting of a pyrimidine ring fused to an imidazole ring, giving a two-ringed nitrogenous base structure. It is the parent compound of the purines, a class of molecules of central importance in biochemistry.

Biological role

The most important purine derivatives are the nucleobases adenine and guanine, which are two of the four bases that encode genetic information in DNA and RNA. Purine nucleotides also serve as energy carriers (ATP, GTP), signalling molecules (cyclic AMP), and components of coenzymes such as NAD and FAD. Caffeine and theobromine are methylated purine alkaloids found in coffee and cocoa.

Metabolism

Animals synthesise purines de novo and salvage them by recycling. In humans, purine catabolism ends with uric acid, excreted in the urine; excessive uric acid can crystallise in joints, causing gout. Rich dietary sources of purines include organ meats, certain seafood, and legumes. The name "purine" was coined by the German chemist Emil Fischer in 1884.
