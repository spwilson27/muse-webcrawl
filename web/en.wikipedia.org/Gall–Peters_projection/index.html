The Gall–Peters projection is an equal-area cylindrical map projection of the world. It was first described in 1855 by the Scottish clergyman and geographer James Gall, who presented it together with two other projections; it was largely forgotten until the German historian and journalist Arno Peters promoted it at a press conference in Bonn in 1973 as a fairer alternative to the widely used Mercator projection.

Because the projection preserves relative areas, landmasses appear in their correct proportions to one another, in contrast to the Mercator projection, which greatly exaggerates the size of regions near the poles. This property comes at the cost of considerable distortion of shapes, especially near the equator and the poles, where continents appear stretched vertically.

Formula

The projection is a special case of the cylindrical equal-area projection with standard parallels at 45°. Points are mapped according to x = Rλ cos 45° and y = R sin φ / cos 45°, where λ is longitude, φ is latitude, and R is the radius of the model globe. The result is a rectangular map in which areas are everywhere proportional to areas on the Earth.

The Peters controversy

Peters presented the projection as a wholly new invention and criticised existing maps as Eurocentric and distorted. Cartographers responded that the projection was not new — Gall had published it more than a century earlier — and that all flat maps necessarily distort something. Nevertheless, the ensuing "Peters controversy" drew wide attention to the political and pedagogical implications of map choice. Development agencies and some schools adopted Peters maps, and organisations such as UNESCO distributed them.

Use

The Gall–Peters projection remains a well-known example of an equal-area world map. Other equal-area projections, such as the Mollweide, Eckert IV and Hobo–Dyer projections, achieve similar area fidelity with less extreme shape distortion.
