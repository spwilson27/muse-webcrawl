Taq polymerase

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Taq polymerase is a thermostable DNA polymerase I named after the thermophilic eubacterial microorganism Thermus aquaticus, from which it was originally isolated by Chien et al. in 1976. Its name is often abbreviated to Taq or Taq pol. It is frequently used in the polymerase chain reaction (PCR), a method for greatly amplifying the quantity of short segments of DNA.

Properties

T. aquaticus is a bacterium that lives in hot springs and hydrothermal vents, and Taq polymerase was identified as an enzyme able to withstand the protein-denaturing conditions (high temperature) required during PCR. Consequently, it replaced the DNA polymerase from E. coli that had previously been used, which had to be replenished after every heating cycle. Taq's optimum temperature for activity is 75–80 °C, with a half-life of more than two hours at 92.5 °C. It synthesizes DNA at roughly 1,000 bases per minute.

Limitations and use

Taq polymerase lacks a 3′ to 5′ exonuclease proofreading activity, so it has a relatively high error rate of about one mistake per 9,000 nucleotides. It also adds a single deoxyadenosine (A) overhang to the 3′ ends of PCR products, a feature exploited in TA cloning. The enzyme was a key enabling technology for PCR, whose inventor Kary Mullis received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1993; the patent history of Taq itself was long contested in the courts.
