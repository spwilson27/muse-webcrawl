Robert Southey

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Robert Southey (12 August 1774 – 21 March 1843) was an English poet of the Romantic school and Poet Laureate from 1813 until his death. One of the so-called "Lake Poets" alongside William Wordsworth and Samuel Taylor Coleridge, he was also a prolific letter writer, historian, and biographer, and is remembered today for his biography of Horatio Nelson and for short poems such as "After Blenheim" and "The Inchcape Rock".

Life

Southey was educated at Westminster School and Balliol College, Oxford, which he left without a degree. In his youth he was a radical admirer of the French Revolution and collaborated with Coleridge on the utopian "Pantisocracy" scheme and the play The Fall of Robespierre (1794). His long eastern epics Thalaba the Destroyer (1801) and The Curse of Kehama (1810) brought fame. Settling at Greta Hall, Keswick, in the Lake District, he supported a large extended household by ceaseless literary labour, producing histories of Brazil and of the Peninsular War, reviews for the Quarterly Review, and the celebrated Life of Nelson (1813).

Reputation

Southey's drift from youthful radicalism to Tory orthodoxy made him a target for younger Romantics: Lord Byron famously mocked him in The Vision of Judgment (1822), a parody of Southey's own poem celebrating George III. His narrative poems and "After Blenheim" — with its refrain "But what good came of it at last?" — long remained schoolroom staples, and his prose style was admired by contemporaries.
