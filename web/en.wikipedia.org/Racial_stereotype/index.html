Racial stereotype

Minstrel-show and advertising imagery of the 19th and early 20th centuries spread many racial caricatures that are now widely recognised as stereotypes.

A racial stereotype is a fixed, oversimplified and widely held belief about the characteristics, behaviour or abilities of members of a particular racial or ethnic group. Like other stereotypes, racial stereotypes assign traits to individuals based on group membership rather than personal qualities, and they may be positive, negative or mixed in content while remaining inaccurate as generalisations.

Social psychologists study racial stereotypes as part of the interlocking set of attitudes that includes prejudice (affective reactions) and discrimination (behaviour). Stereotypes can influence perception, memory and judgement: people tend to notice and remember information that confirms an existing stereotype, a phenomenon known as confirmation bias.

Formation and transmission

Racial stereotypes arise from a combination of cognitive shortcuts, socialisation, and the representations circulated in a society. Historically, they were propagated through slavery and colonialism, scientific racism, and mass media — including minstrel shows, early film, advertising mascots and children's literature — which repeatedly paired groups with caricatured traits. Modern research finds stereotypes are also absorbed from patterns of social roles: when a group is concentrated in particular occupations or social positions, observers may wrongly attribute the behaviours associated with those roles to inherent group traits.

Effects

Being the target of a negative stereotype can itself affect performance, a phenomenon called stereotype threat, first demonstrated by Claude Steele and Joshua Aronson in 1995. Stereotypes can also shape institutional outcomes when they influence decisions in employment, policing, housing and education. Conversely, supposedly "positive" stereotypes, such as the model minority stereotype applied to Asian Americans, can impose pressure, obscure discrimination and mental-health needs, and mask diversity within the group.

Change

Public stereotypes have shifted over the decades in response to social movements, demographic change and media representation. Research on stereotype change suggests that counter-stereotypical examples, intergroup contact under equal-status conditions, and structural change in roles can each weaken stereotype endorsement, though implicit associations often change more slowly than explicitly reported beliefs.
