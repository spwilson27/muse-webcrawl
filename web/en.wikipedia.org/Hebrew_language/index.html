Hebrew (עִבְרִית, Ivrit) is a Northwest Semitic language of the Afro-Asiatic family. Historically the language of the Israelites and their ancestors, it is the only Canaanite language still spoken and the only truly successful example of a dead language revived as a fully vernacular tongue: after ceasing to be an everyday spoken language around the 3rd century CE, it survived as a liturgical and literary language for some 1,700 years before being revived in the 19th and 20th centuries. Modern Hebrew is the official language of the State of Israel and is spoken by more than nine million people.

History

Ancient Hebrew is attested from the 10th century BCE in inscriptions such as the Gezer calendar. Biblical Hebrew (c. 10th–6th centuries BCE) is the classical language of most of the Hebrew Bible (Tanakh). After the Babylonian exile, Aramaic increasingly displaced Hebrew in daily life; by the Mishnaic period (c. 1st–3rd centuries CE) Hebrew remained in scholarly and liturgical use, codified in the Mishnah. Medieval Hebrew flourished as a language of liturgy, poetry, philosophy and science in both Islamic and Christian lands. In the 19th century, the Haskalah (Jewish Enlightenment) broadened its secular literature, and Eliezer Ben-Yehuda led its revival as a spoken vernacular in Ottoman Palestine, founding institutions and coining thousands of words; the language's academy continues this work today.

Grammar and writing

Hebrew morphology is built on (usually triliteral) roots interwoven with vowel patterns (binyanim for verbs). The script is an abjad of 22 consonantal letters, written right to left, with five letters having special final forms; vowels are ordinarily unwritten but can be marked with the niqqud pointing system devised by the Masoretes. Modern Hebrew phonology, influenced by the European languages of its revivalists, has fewer consonantal distinctions than the biblical language, and its syntax shows a fixed SVO order in most registers.

Vocabulary and modern usage

The modern lexicon draws on biblical, Mishnaic and medieval strata, supplemented by coinages from native roots and loanwords from Aramaic historically and from Arabic, English, Russian, Yiddish and other languages in modern times. Hebrew is the language of government, education and media in Israel, and remains the primary liturgical language of Judaism worldwide.
