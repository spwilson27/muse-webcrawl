Black lung disease, formally coal workers' pneumoconiosis (CWP), is an occupational lung disease caused by long-term inhalation and accumulation of coal dust in the lungs. Inhaled dust provokes inflammation and fibrosis; in its severe form, progressive massive fibrosis, large areas of lung are replaced by blackened scar tissue, causing severe breathlessness, disability and premature death. Miners sometimes cough up black sputum (melanoptysis).

Course and diagnosis

Simple CWP may produce few symptoms but is visible on chest X-rays as small nodules, classified under the ILO system. Complicated CWP, or progressive massive fibrosis, involves lesions larger than 2 cm and steadily worsening lung function even after exposure ends. The disease is distinct from, though often combined with, silicosis from silica dust in rock — many miners have mixed-dust disease. There is no cure; management focuses on stopping exposure, treating complications such as infections and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and, in end-stage cases, lung transplantation.

Prevention and compensation

In the United States, the Coal Mine Health and Safety Act of 1969 set dust limits and created a federal benefits programme, following disasters and activism such as the Farmington mine disaster of 1968. The Black Lung Disability Trust Fund pays benefits where no operator can be held responsible. Despite regulation, US health surveillance has documented a resurgence of severe disease since the late 1990s among miners in central Appalachia, linked to longer shifts, thinner seams and greater silica exposure. Similar diseases and compensation schemes exist in other coal-producing countries, including the United Kingdom, Australia and China.
