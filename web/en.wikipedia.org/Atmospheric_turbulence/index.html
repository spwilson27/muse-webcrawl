Atmospheric turbulence
Field: Atmospheric science, fluid dynamics
Types: mechanical, thermal (convective), clear-air turbulence
Key parameter: Richardson number

Atmospheric turbulence is the irregular, chaotic motion of air in the atmosphere, occurring as eddies over scales from millimetres to hundreds of metres. It is the dominant mechanism for mixing heat, moisture, momentum, and pollutants in the planetary boundary layer, and it is a familiar hazard to aviation in the form of bumps and jolts in flight.

Causes

Turbulence is generated mechanically when wind shears over rough terrain, buildings, or mountains (producing mountain waves and rotors), and thermally when solar heating creates buoyant thermals. In the free troposphere, clear-air turbulence arises near jet streams and fronts where strong vertical wind shear destabilizes the flow into Kelvin–Helmholtz billows. Turbulence intensity relative to stability is measured by the Richardson number.

Effects

Turbulence disperses air pollution, drives evaporation and the exchange of carbon dioxide between land and atmosphere, degrades astronomical seeing, and shakes aircraft. Pilots receive forecasts and reports grading turbulence as light, moderate, severe, or extreme; severe encounters can injure occupants, which is why passengers are advised to keep seatbelts fastened.
