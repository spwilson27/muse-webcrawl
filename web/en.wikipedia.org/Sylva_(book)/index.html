Sylva (book)

Sylva, or A Discourse of Forest-Trees
AuthorJohn Evelyn
Published1664 (first edition); further editions 1670, 1679, 1706
SubjectForestry, arboriculture
PublisherRoyal Society, London

Sylva, or A Discourse of Forest-Trees and the Propagation of Timber in His Majesty's Dominions is a work on forestry by the English writer and diarist John Evelyn, first published in 1664. It was the first book published by the Royal Society and is regarded as one of the most influential texts in the history of forestry.

Background and content

Evelyn wrote Sylva in response to a query from the commissioners of the Royal Navy, who were alarmed by the depletion of England's oak forests, the essential raw material for the wooden walls of the fleet. Prompted also by enclosure and by the demand of glassworks and ironworks for charcoal, the book surveys the species of forest trees, their soils and propagation, the management of coppices and pollards, and the felling and seasoning of timber. A companion treatise, Pomona, on fruit trees and cider, was appended.

Influence

Sylva went through numerous editions — including a celebrated illustrated edition edited by Alexander Hunter in 1776 — and encouraged generations of landowners to plant trees, shaping the wooded character of the English landscape. Evelyn's famous injunction that men should plant trees for posterity, thinking of what they owe to the future, has made the book a founding text of conservation and arboriculture. The journal of the modern forestry profession in Britain, and the Sylva Foundation, take their names from it.
