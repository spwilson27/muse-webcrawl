Shishuo Xinyu (Chinese: 世說新語, "New Tales of the World") is a Chinese collection of anecdotes, sketches, and witticisms about some 600 historical figures of the later Han, Wei, and Jin dynasties, compiled in the 5th century under the direction of Liu Yiqing (403–444), a prince of the Liu Song dynasty. One of the masterpieces of the zhiji ("records of persons") tradition, it is the richest single source for the culture of the Wei–Jin literati — their "pure conversation" (qingtan), character appraisal, and cultivated eccentricity — and has been beloved and imitated in China for over 1,500 years.

Structure and contents

The work contains about 1,130 entries arranged in 36 thematic chapters whose headings became proverbial — "Virtuous Conduct", "Speech and Conversation", "Letters and Scholarship", "The Square and the Round", "Stinginess", "Slander and Treachery" — many borrowed from the vocabulary of character appraisal in the Analects tradition. Its famous subjects include the Seven Sages of the Bamboo Grove, the calligrapher Wang Xizhi, the statesmen Xie An and Xie Lingyun, and the strange, witty exchanges of the salons of Jiankang. Liu Xiaobiao's commentary, nearly as famous as the text, quotes hundreds of now-lost works and verifies or corrects the anecdotes.

Themes

The book captures the Wei–Jin era's turn from Confucian public duty toward Daoist-inflected appreciation of personality, spontaneity, and wit: Cao Pi's circle debating character grades, officials ruined by a quip, grief expressed in howls, drunkenness as authenticity. Anecdotes range from the sublime — Xie An calmly finishing a game of go while news of the great victory at the Fei River arrives — to the comic, as in stories of misers and pedants. Its laconic prose, often only a few lines per entry, perfected an aesthetic of suggestive brevity.

Influence

Imitations and sequels (such as the Tang-dynasty Yulin and later "new Shishuo" collections) appeared for centuries, and the work remains a standard source for the social history of early medieval China and a treasure-house of allusions in later Chinese literature. It has been translated into English by Richard B. Mather as Shih-shuo Hsin-yü: A New Account of Tales of the World and into several other languages.
