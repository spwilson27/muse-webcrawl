Claisen condensation

Claisen condensation
Type: Coupling reaction (condensation)
Reactants: Two esters (or ester + carbonyl compound)
Product: β-keto ester or β-diketone
Named after: Rainer Ludwig Claisen

The Claisen condensation is a carbon–carbon bond forming reaction in organic chemistry that occurs between two esters or one ester and another carbonyl compound in the presence of a strong base. The product is a β-keto ester or a β-diketone. It is named after Rainer Ludwig Claisen, who first described the reaction in 1887.

Mechanism

A strong base such as sodium ethoxide removes an α-hydrogen from one ester to form an enolate. The enolate attacks the carbonyl carbon of a second ester molecule as a nucleophile, and expulsion of the alkoxide leaving group (tetrahedral intermediate collapse) yields a β-keto ester. The product is more acidic than the starting ester and is deprotonated by the base, so a stoichiometric amount of base is required and the product is obtained after acidic workup. Traditionally the alkoxide base is chosen to match the ester's alcohol component to avoid transesterification.

Variants

The Dieckmann condensation is the intramolecular version of the reaction, used to form cyclic β-keto esters (five- and six-membered rings are favoured). Mixed (crossed) Claisen condensations between two different esters are most useful when one partner has no α-hydrogens, such as ethyl formate or ethyl benzoate. The Claisen condensation is related to the aldol reaction, which involves aldehydes and ketones instead of esters.

Applications

The classic example is the self-condensation of ethyl acetate to give ethyl acetoacetate, an industrially important reagent in organic synthesis.
