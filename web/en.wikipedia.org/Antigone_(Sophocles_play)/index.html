Antigone (Ancient Greek: Ἀντιγόνη) is a tragedy by the Athenian playwright Sophocles, written in or before 441 BC. It is chronologically the third of the three Theban plays but was written first. The play expands on the Theban legend of Antigone, daughter of Oedipus, and presents the conflict between divine/unwritten law and the edicts of the state — one of the most influential themes in Western literature and philosophy.

Plot

After Oedipus's sons Eteocles and Polynices kill each other in battle over the throne of Thebes, the new king Creon decrees that Eteocles be honoured with burial while Polynices, who attacked the city, be left unburied on pain of death. Antigone defies the edict and performs burial rites for her brother, holding that the gods' laws override human decrees. Caught and condemned, she is sealed alive in a tomb. The prophet Tiresias warns Creon of divine anger; Creon relents too late — Antigone has hanged herself, his son Haemon (Antigone's fiancé) kills himself, and his wife Eurydice follows, leaving Creon broken.

Themes and influence

Hegel famously read the play as a collision of two equally justified ethical powers — family and divine law versus state and human law. The play has inspired many adaptations, notably Jean Anouilh's Antigone (1944), performed in Nazi-occupied Paris, and has become a touchstone for discussions of civil disobedience and conscientious resistance.
