Old Norse, also called Old Scandinavian, is a stage of development of North Germanic dialects before their final divergence into separate Nordic languages. Old Norse was spoken by inhabitants of Scandinavia and their overseas settlements and chronologically coincides with the Viking Age, the Christianization of Scandinavia, and the consolidation of Scandinavian kingdoms from about the 8th to the 15th centuries.

By the 8th century, Proto-Norse had developed into Old Norse, itself dividing into Old West Norse (Norway, Iceland, Faroe Islands) and Old East Norse (Denmark, Sweden). Because the great corpus of saga and Eddic literature was preserved in Iceland, "Old Norse" in practice usually means Old Icelandic/Old West Norse — the language of Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda, the Poetic Edda, and the Icelandic family sagas.

Features

Old Norse retained a rich inflectional system inherited from Proto-Germanic: four noun cases (nominative, accusative, genitive, dative), three genders, strong and weak verb conjugations, and a suffixed definite article — a Scandinavian innovation. Its literature, written first in the Younger Futhark runes and after Christianisation in the Latin alphabet, includes skaldic and eddic poetry, prose sagas, laws, and translations of European romances. Through Viking settlement, Old Norse left a deep imprint on English vocabulary (words such as sky, egg, knife, they, law) and on the place-names of the Danelaw, Normandy, Scotland, and Ireland.

Descendants

Old Norse developed into the modern North Germanic languages: Icelandic (the most conservative, still close enough that Icelanders can read the sagas), Faroese, Norwegian, Danish, and Swedish.
