Bradykinin

Sequence: Arg-Pro-Pro-Gly-Phe-Ser-Pro-Phe-Arg
Formula: C50H73N15O11
Class: Kinin peptide

Bradykinin is an inflammatory mediator and a peptide composed of nine amino acids (a nonapeptide) with the sequence Arg-Pro-Pro-Gly-Phe-Ser-Pro-Phe-Arg. It is part of the kallikrein-kinin system and is one of the most potent known vasodilators. Bradykinin causes blood vessels to dilate, lowers blood pressure, increases vascular permeability, and contributes to pain and inflammation.

Production and metabolism

Bradykinin is released from the precursor protein high-molecular-weight kininogen by the enzyme plasma kallikrein. It is rapidly broken down, within seconds to minutes, by enzymes including angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE), which is why ACE inhibitors raise bradykinin levels — an effect believed responsible for the dry cough and, rarely, angioedema associated with those drugs.

Receptors and effects

Bradykinin acts mainly on two G-protein-coupled receptors, B1 and B2. The B2 receptor is constitutively expressed and mediates most acute effects: vasodilation via nitric oxide and prostacyclin release, increased permeability, and stimulation of pain fibers. The B1 receptor is induced during inflammation and tissue injury and is activated by bradykinin's metabolite des-Arg9-bradykinin.

Clinical significance

Excess bradykinin is central to hereditary angioedema, a disorder of recurrent swelling caused by deficiency or dysfunction of C1 esterase inhibitor. The bradykinin B2 receptor antagonist icatibant is used to treat acute attacks. Bradykinin was discovered in 1948 by Brazilian physiologists Maurício Rocha e Silva, Wilson Beraldo, and Gastão Rosenfeld, who observed that plasma incubated with the venom of the snake Bothrops jararaca released a substance that slowly contracted smooth muscle — hence the name, from the Greek bradys (slow) and kinein (to move).
