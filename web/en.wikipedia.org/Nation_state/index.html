Nation state

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A nation state is a political unit in which the territorial boundaries of a state and the community of a nation coincide — where the state derives its political legitimacy from serving as the sovereign expression of a particular cultural or ethnic group. In practice, few states are perfectly homogeneous, and the term is often used loosely for any sovereign state.

Origins

The nation state is conventionally traced to the Peace of Westphalia of 1648, which established principles of territorial sovereignty and non-interference. The French Revolution fused the idea of popular sovereignty with national identity, and in the 19th century the unification of Germany and Italy, and the independence movements in Latin America, spread the model. After 1945, decolonisation made the nation state the universal form of political organisation, institutionalised in the United Nations.

Characteristics

Nation states typically possess a defined territory, a permanent population, a centralised government, sovereignty in external relations, a common legal system, and shared national symbols such as a flag, anthem, and currency. Many promote a common language and culture through public education and media.

Multinational and minority questions

Many states contain multiple nations or national minorities — for example, the United Kingdom comprises England, Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland. Tensions between nation-state ideals and internal diversity have produced separatist movements, federal arrangements, and, in the worst cases, ethnic conflict. Critics also argue that globalisation and supranational bodies such as the European Union have eroded the classic nation-state model.
