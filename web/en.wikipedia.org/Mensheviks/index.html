The Mensheviks (from Russian men'shevik, "one of the minority") were a faction of the Russian Social Democratic Labour Party (RSDLP) that split with Vladimir Lenin's Bolsheviks ("majority") at the party's Second Congress in 1903 over the definition of party membership. Led by Julius Martov, the Mensheviks favoured a broad, democratic party organisation and a more orthodox Marxist strategy.

Beliefs

Mensheviks held that backward, agrarian Russia must first pass through a stage of bourgeois-democratic revolution and capitalist development before socialism was possible; the workers' party should therefore cooperate tactically with liberal forces, build trade unions and a mass party, and avoid premature attempts to seize power. Lenin's faction advocated a tight, disciplined party of professional revolutionaries.

Revolutions of 1917

After the February Revolution the Mensheviks were a leading force in the soviets and joined the coalition Provisional Government — Tsereteli and Skobelev became ministers — supporting continued participation in the First World War ("revolutionary defencism"). Their popularity collapsed as the war and economic crisis continued; in the October Revolution the Bolsheviks seized power, and the Menshevik delegates walked out of the Congress of Soviets.

Suppression and exile

Mensheviks initially opposed Bolshevik rule and won support in unions and soviets in 1918–21, but they were progressively banned, arrested and driven into exile; the party organisation inside Russia was destroyed by the mid-1920s. In Georgia, however, Mensheviks under Noe Zhordania governed the independent Democratic Republic of Georgia (1918–1921) until the Red Army invasion. Émigré Mensheviks, centred on the journal Sotsialisticheskii vestnik in Berlin, Paris and New York, remained critics of Stalinism for decades.
