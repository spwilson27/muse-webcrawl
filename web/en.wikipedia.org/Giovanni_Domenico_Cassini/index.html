Giovanni Domenico Cassini, also known as Jean-Dominique Cassini (8 June 1625 – 14 September 1712), was an Italian (later French) mathematician, astronomer and engineer, the founding director of the Paris Observatory.

Discoveries

Cassini discovered four satellites of Saturn — Iapetus (1671), Rhea (1672), Tethys and Dione (1684) — and the dark gap in Saturn's rings now called the Cassini Division (1675). He observed Jupiter's Great Red Spot and, with Robert Hooke, its differential rotation. With Jean Richer in Cayenne, he measured the parallax of Mars in 1672, yielding the first reasonably accurate estimate of the astronomical unit. He also catalogued the zodiacal light phenomenon.

Career

Cassini began his career in Bologna, where he constructed a famous meridian line in the Basilica of San Petronio. Invited by Louis XIV, he moved to Paris in 1669, became a French citizen, and directed the Paris Observatory for four decades. He founded a dynasty: his son Jacques, grandson César-François and great-grandson Jean-Dominique all directed the observatory in succession and produced the first modern topographic map of France.

Legacy

The NASA/ESA Cassini–Huygens mission to Saturn (1997–2017) was named in his honour.
