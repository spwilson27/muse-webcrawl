Vitaly Ginzburg

Vitaly Lazarevich Ginzburg (Russian: Вита́лий Ла́заревич Ги́нзбург; 4 October 1916 – 8 November 2009) was a Russian physicist and one of the fathers of the Soviet hydrogen bomb. He was the head of the theory division of the Lebedev Institute of Physics and later became a successor to Igor Tamm. He shared the 2003 Nobel Prize in Physics with Alexei Abrikosov and Anthony James Leggett for pioneering contributions to the theory of superconductors and superfluids.

Scientific work

In 1950, together with Lev Landau, Ginzburg formulated the phenomenological Ginzburg–Landau theory of superconductivity, which describes the behaviour of superconductors near the critical temperature and underlies the distinction between type-I and type-II superconductors. His research spanned condensed matter physics, radio astronomy, cosmic rays — he championed their galactic origin — plasma physics, and crystallography. With Tamm and others he contributed to the design of the Soviet thermonuclear weapon in the early 1950s, proposing the use of lithium deuteride ("Sloika" design work), though he was later removed from the project.

Public life

Ginzburg was a prominent public intellectual in post-Soviet Russia, writing on science, religion, and society. An outspoken atheist, he opposed the growing influence of the Russian Orthodox Church in state affairs and in 2007 co-signed an open letter to President Vladimir Putin warning against the clericalisation of Russian society. He was a member of the Russian Academy of Sciences and received numerous honours, including the UNESCO Niels Bohr Medal and the Lomonosov Gold Medal.
