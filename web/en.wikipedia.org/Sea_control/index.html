Sea control

Sea control is a naval concept describing the degree to which a force can use a sea area for its own purposes — moving shipping, projecting power ashore, and conducting operations — while denying that use to an adversary. It is closely related to the older term command of the sea, and is contrasted with sea denial.

Concept

Theorists such as Alfred Thayer Mahan emphasised command of the sea as a prerequisite for national power, while Julian Corbett stressed that control is normally limited in time and space and exists for the purpose of securing communications. Modern usage treats sea control as relative and local: a navy may hold control in one area for one operation without commanding the sea generally.

Means

Sea control is exercised through surface fleets, submarines, naval aviation, minesweeping and mine warfare, land-based aircraft and missiles, and control of chokepoints. It supports amphibious operations, blockade, protection of merchant shipping and sea lines of communication.

Sea denial

A weaker navy may pursue sea denial — preventing the enemy's use of the sea without itself being able to use it — using submarines, mines, fast attack craft and anti-ship missiles.
