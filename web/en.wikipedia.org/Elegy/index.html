Elegy

Form: Poem of lamentation and reflection

Classical metre: Elegiac couplet

An elegy is a poem of serious reflection and lament for the dead. However, similar to a eulogy, an elegy may also reflect something that has been lost and longed for. In Greek literature, the elegy was originally written in the elegiac couplet — alternating hexameter and pentameter lines — and covered subjects from grief and love to war and politics.

History

Ancient Greek and Latin poets including Callimachus, Propertius, Tibullus, and Ovid used the elegiac form for a wide range of themes. In English literature the term narrowed to laments for the dead and meditations on mortality, while the pastoral elegy — in which the mourner is a shepherd — became a major subgenre from Theocritus through Milton's "Lycidas" (1638) and Shelley's "Adonais" (1821).

Famous examples

Thomas Gray's "Elegy Written in a Country Churchyard" (1751) is among the best-known poems in English. Other celebrated elegies include John Milton's "Lycidas", Alfred Tennyson's "In Memoriam A.H.H.", Walt Whitman's "When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom'd" on the death of Abraham Lincoln, and W. H. Auden's "In Memory of W. B. Yeats".

Characteristics

The pastoral elegy conventionally invokes the muse, praises the deceased, expresses grief and anger at fate, imagines nature mourning in sympathy, and moves toward consolation, often through an affirmation of renewal or the survival of the dead in memory or fame.
