Abel–Ruffini theorem

The Abel–Ruffini theorem (also known as the Abel impossibility theorem) states that there is no solution in radicals to general polynomial equations of degree five or higher with arbitrary coefficients. In other words, unlike quadratic, cubic and quartic equations, which can all be solved by formulas using only arithmetic operations and root extractions, no such formula exists for the general quintic, sextic, or any higher-degree equation.

Interpretation

The theorem does not say that quintic equations have no solutions — every polynomial equation has complex roots by the fundamental theorem of algebra — nor that no quintic can be solved in radicals: many specific quintics, such as x5 − 1 = 0, are solvable. It says there is no general formula in radicals that works for all quintics. Certain unsolvable quintics can still be solved using other functions, such as elliptic or theta functions, as shown by Hermite and Kronecker.

History

Paolo Ruffini published the first proof in 1799, but it was incomplete: he assumed that the radicals in a supposed solution could be expressed within the field of the roots. Niels Henrik Abel supplied a complete proof in 1824, in a self-published pamphlet, at the age of twenty-one. Évariste Galois later gave the deeper explanation: a polynomial equation is solvable in radicals if and only if its Galois group is a solvable group, and the symmetric group S5 is not solvable. Galois theory thus both subsumes the theorem and provides a criterion for deciding whether any particular equation can be solved in radicals.
