Sashimi

OriginJapan
TypeRaw sliced seafood (occasionally meat)
Typical speciesTuna, salmon, yellowtail, sea bream, squid, octopus
Served withSoy sauce, wasabi, grated ginger, shiso, daikon
Related dishesSushi, tataki, crudo, poke, hoe

Sashimi (Japanese: 刺身, "pierced body") is a Japanese delicacy of fresh raw fish or meat sliced into thin pieces and typically eaten with soy sauce. It is one of the signature dishes of Japanese cuisine and is prized for the purity of its flavours, the quality and freshness of its ingredients, and the knife skills of the chef who prepares it.

Preparation and serving

Sashimi is usually the first course in a formal Japanese meal and is served early, before strong flavours dull the palate. The fish — commonly tuna (maguro), salmon (sake), yellowtail (hamachi), sea bream (tai), mackerel, squid and octopus — is cut with a long, thin knife (yanagiba) using distinct techniques: flat cuts (hira-zukuri), thin slices (usu-zukuri) and cubes (kaku-zukuri). The slices are arranged on a platter over shredded daikon radish with shiso leaves, and eaten dipped in soy sauce mixed with wasabi or grated ginger. Sashimi differs from sushi in that it is served without vinegared rice.

Freshness and safety

Because it is eaten raw, sashimi demands impeccable freshness and handling. Fish may be killed by ikejime, a technique of pithing and bleeding that delays rigor mortis and improves texture. To control parasites such as Anisakis, food-safety regulations in the European Union and the United States require most fish intended for raw consumption to have been frozen at specified temperatures before serving; certain species, such as some tunas, are exempt. Freshwater fish are rarely served as sashimi because of parasite risk. Meat sashimi, such as horse (basashi) and chicken (torisashi), is eaten in some regions of Japan.

History

The practice of eating raw fish in Japan dates back centuries, developing from preserved and fermented preparations such as narezushi. The word "sashimi" is said to derive from the practice of pinning the fish's fin or tail to the slices so that the species being eaten could be identified. The dish took its modern form in the Edo period, as soy sauce production and urban food culture expanded, and spread worldwide with the globalisation of Japanese cuisine in the late twentieth century.
