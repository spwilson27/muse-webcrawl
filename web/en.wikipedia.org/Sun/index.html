The Sun is the star at the centre of the Solar System. It is a nearly perfect sphere of hot plasma heated to incandescence by nuclear fusion reactions in its core. It accounts for about 99.86% of the total mass of the Solar System, and its light and heat are the primary source of energy for life on Earth.

Structure and fusion

Roughly three-quarters of the Sun's mass is hydrogen and most of the rest is helium, with small quantities of oxygen, carbon, neon, iron, and other elements. In the core, at temperatures around 15 million kelvin and densities some 150 times that of water, hydrogen nuclei fuse into helium through the proton–proton chain, converting about 4 million tonnes of matter into energy every second. This energy works its way outward through the radiative zone and the convection zone before being radiated from the visible surface, the photosphere. Above the photosphere lie the chromosphere and the corona, the Sun's extended outer atmosphere visible during total solar eclipses.

Life cycle

The Sun formed about 4.6 billion years ago from the gravitational collapse of part of a giant molecular cloud. It is currently in the main-sequence phase of its life, fusing hydrogen steadily. In about 5 billion years it will exhaust its core hydrogen, expand into a red giant that engulfs the inner planets, shed its outer layers as a planetary nebula, and leave behind a white dwarf that will cool over trillions of years.

Activity

The Sun's magnetic field drives an approximately 11-year solar cycle of sunspots, solar flares, and coronal mass ejections. Its outermost layers emit the solar wind, a stream of charged particles that fills the heliosphere and interacts with planetary magnetospheres to produce auroras.
