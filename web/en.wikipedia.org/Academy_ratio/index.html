Academy ratio

The Academy ratio of 1.375:1 (often shortened to 1.37:1) was the standard aspect ratio of 35 mm film used in the motion picture industry between 1932 and the early 1950s. It was defined by the Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences in 1932, following the transition from silent film to sound film.

When sound-on-film processes were introduced in the late 1920s, part of the film frame had to be surrendered to the optical soundtrack. The Academy standardised a frame of 22 mm by 16 mm, giving an aspect ratio of 1.375:1, slightly taller than the 1.33:1 ratio of the silent era. For decades nearly all Hollywood productions were photographed and projected in this format.

History

During the silent era, the standard frame used almost the full width of 35 mm film between the perforations, yielding a ratio of about 1.33:1. The addition of a soundtrack strip along one edge reduced the available width, and studios initially adopted a variety of cropped formats. In 1932 the Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences settled the matter by prescribing the 1.375:1 aperture, restoring much of the lost height by reducing the frame line between images.

The ratio remained dominant until television competition in the 1950s pushed studios toward widescreen formats such as CinemaScope (2.35:1) and the matted ratios of 1.66:1 and 1.85:1, which became the new standards.

Legacy

Many classic films, including Citizen Kane and Casablanca, were composed for the Academy ratio. Some modern directors have revived it for artistic effect; the ratio remains a recognised stylistic choice in contemporary cinema. On 4:3 television screens, Academy-ratio films could be shown nearly full-frame, which is why the format is sometimes conflated with the "fullscreen" ratio of early television.
