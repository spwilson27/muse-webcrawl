The Napoleonic Code (French: Code Napoléon), officially the Civil Code of the French (Code civil des Français; simply referred as Code civil), is the French civil code established during the French Consulate in 1804 and still in force, although frequently amended. It was drafted by a commission of four eminent jurists and entered into force on 21 March 1804. The code, with its stress on clearly written and accessible law, spread the principles of legal equality and property rights across Europe during the Napoleonic Wars, and was a major milestone in replacing the fragmented feudal laws of the ancien régime with a unified national code.

Contents

The Code is organised in the "institutional" scheme derived from Roman law: Book I treats persons (civil rights, marriage, family); Book II treats property and the different modifications of ownership; Book III treats the modes of acquiring property (succession, contracts, obligations, matrimonial regimes). It abolished birth-based privilege, established equality of (male) citizens before the law, secured private property and freedom of contract, and made civil marriage and divorce matters of the state rather than the Church. At the same time it was patriarchal: wives owed obedience to husbands and could not transact without their consent, provisions only gradually dismantled by reforms in the 20th century (1938, 1965, 1970 and after).

Drafting

Revolutionary assemblies had promised a civil code but failed to enact one. After the coup of 18 Brumaire, Napoleon appointed a four-man commission — Portalis, Tronchet, Bigot de Préameneu and Maleville — which produced a draft in four months. It was debated in the Conseil d'État, over whose sessions Napoleon frequently presided, and enacted in stages in 1803–1804. Portalis's preliminary discourse famously described the code as a compromise between written Roman law of the south and the customary law of the north, moderated by revolutionary principles.

Influence

Imposed or adopted across Napoleon's empire — Belgium, Luxembourg, the Netherlands, Italy, the Rhineland, Poland and beyond — the Code's structure and concepts became the foundation of the civil law tradition in much of continental Europe, and, through colonisation and imitation, in Louisiana, Quebec, Latin America, Egypt, Japan (in part), and many other jurisdictions. Napoleon, exiled on Saint Helena, judged it his most enduring achievement: "My true glory is not to have won forty battles... What will live forever is my Civil Code."
