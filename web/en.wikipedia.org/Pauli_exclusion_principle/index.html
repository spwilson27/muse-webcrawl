Pauli exclusion principle

The Pauli exclusion principle is a quantum mechanical principle which states that two or more identical half-integer-spin particles (that is, fermions) cannot simultaneously occupy the same quantum state within a system that obeys the laws of quantum mechanics. The principle was formulated by Austrian physicist Wolfgang Pauli in 1925 for electrons, and later extended to all fermions.

History

In the early 1920s, attempts to explain the anomalous Zeeman effect and the structure of atomic spectra led Pauli to postulate a "two-valuedness" in electron behaviour not described by the existing quantum numbers. In January 1925 he published the exclusion principle, initially as an empirical rule for electrons in atoms: no two electrons in an atom could have the same values for all four quantum numbers (n, l, ml, and ms). Pauli received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1945, in part for this work.

Statement and consequences

The principle is a consequence of the antisymmetry of the fermionic wavefunction: swapping two identical fermions changes the sign of the total wavefunction, so a state in which two fermions occupy identical single-particle states cancels itself to zero probability. It underlies the shell structure of atoms, the periodic table, the stability and incompressibility of ordinary matter, degeneracy pressure in white dwarf stars and neutron stars, and Pauli paramagnetism in metals. Bosons, by contrast, have integer spin and can share quantum states.

Applications

Beyond atomic physics, the exclusion principle explains chemical bonding behaviour, the rigidity of solids, the heat capacity of electron gases, and limits on the density of neutron stars before collapse to black holes. It also plays a foundational role in the spin–statistics theorem of relativistic quantum field theory.
