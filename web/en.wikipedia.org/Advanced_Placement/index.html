Advanced Placement

Students take AP examinations each May at schools around the world.

Advanced Placement (AP) is a program in the United States and Canada created by the College Board which offers college-level curricula and examinations to high school students. American colleges and universities may grant placement and course credit to students who obtain high scores on the examinations. The AP curriculum for each subject is created for the College Board by a panel of experts and college-level educators in that academic discipline.

History

The program began in the 1950s, amid Cold War-era concern that American high school students were not being sufficiently challenged. A pilot program with a small number of schools offered advanced courses in eleven subjects; the College Board took over administration of the program in 1955. Participation grew enormously over the following decades, and today millions of students take AP exams annually across dozens of subjects.

Courses and exams

AP courses are offered in categories including arts, English, history and social sciences, mathematics and computer science, sciences, and world languages and cultures. Popular subjects include AP English Language and Composition, AP United States History, AP Calculus AB and BC, AP Biology, AP Psychology and AP Computer Science Principles.

Each course culminates in a standardized exam administered in May, generally lasting about three hours and combining multiple-choice questions with free-response questions. Exams are scored on a scale of 1 to 5, with 5 the highest; scores of 3 or higher are generally considered passing, and many colleges grant credit or advanced placement for scores of 4 or 5, though policies vary by institution.

Scoring and credit

Free-response sections are graded by college faculty and experienced AP teachers at annual readings held each June. Because credit policies differ widely, some selective institutions limit or decline AP credit, while others allow students to graduate early or skip introductory courses. Research on the program's effects and criticism regarding cost, access and equity of outcomes have accompanied its growth.
