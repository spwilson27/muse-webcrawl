Gdynia

Gdynia (Polish: [ˈɡdɨɲa]; German: Gdingen) is a city in northern Poland on the Baltic Sea, in the Pomeranian Voivodeship. With a population of about 244,000, it forms, together with Gdańsk and Sopot, the Tricity (Trójmiasto) metropolitan area of over a million inhabitants.

History

Gdynia was a small Kashubian fishing village until, after the First World War, it lay within the Polish Corridor giving the reborn Polish state access to the sea. Because the nearby Free City of Danzig was outside Polish control, in 1921 Poland began building a major new seaport at Gdynia under engineer Tadeusz Wenda and minister Eugeniusz Kwiatkowski. By the late 1930s it was the largest and most modern port on the Baltic, and the village had grown into a city of over 100,000, celebrated as a symbol of Poland's maritime ambitions and built largely in the modernist style.

During the Second World War the Germans renamed it Gotenhafen and used it as a naval base; the refugee liner Wilhelm Gustloff sailed from here in January 1945 and was sunk with enormous loss of life. In December 1970, shipyard and transport workers in Gdynia were among the protesters shot by the communist authorities, an event commemorated by the Monument to the Victims of December 1970.

The city today

Gdynia remains a major port and naval base. Its attractions include the museum ships ORP Błyskawica and Dar Pomorza, the Gdynia Aquarium, the Emigration Museum, and the annual Open'er music festival at Gdynia-Kosakowo airport. The city is also known for its well-preserved interwar modernist architecture.
