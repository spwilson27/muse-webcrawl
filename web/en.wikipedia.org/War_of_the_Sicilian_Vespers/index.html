War of the Sicilian Vespers
Date1282 – 1302
LocationSicily, southern Italy, the western Mediterranean, Catalonia
BelligerentsCrown of Aragon and Sicilian rebels vs. Angevin Naples, France and the papacy
ResultPeace of Caltabellotta (1302): Sicily divided from Naples

The War of the Sicilian Vespers was a conflict that began with the insurrection known as the Sicilian Vespers in Palermo on Easter Monday, 30 March 1282, against the rule of the French-born king Charles I of Anjou, and ended with the Peace of Caltabellotta in 1302. It split the old Norman–Hohenstaufen Kingdom of Sicily into an island kingdom under Aragonese kings and a mainland Kingdom of Naples under the Angevins — a division that lasted centuries.

The uprising

According to tradition, the revolt began at the hour of vespers (evening prayer) outside the church of Santo Spirito in Palermo, when a French soldier insulted or assaulted a Sicilian woman, and her husband killed him. The cry went up and the crowd massacred the French of the city; the rising spread across Sicily, with thousands of French soldiers and officials killed. The conspirator John of Procida, a Salernitan physician in Aragonese service, had long prepared the ground with Byzantine and Aragonese support.

The war

The Sicilians offered their crown to Peter III of Aragon, whose wife Constance was the daughter and heir of Manfred of Sicily. Peter landed at Trapani in August 1282 and was crowned at Palermo. Pope Martin IV excommunicated Peter, declared his Aragonese crown forfeit, and preached a crusade: France invaded Catalonia in 1285 (the Aragonese Crusade) but failed disastrously. At sea the Aragonese admiral Roger of Lauria repeatedly destroyed Angevin and French fleets, notably at Malta (1283) and in the Bay of Naples (1284), where he captured Charles of Salerno, the Angevin heir.

Settlement

Charles I died in 1285 and Peter III the same year, but the war dragged on under their sons. The Peace of Caltabellotta (1302) recognised Frederick, Peter's younger son, as king of the island (styled "King of Trinacria") for his lifetime, in return for which the Angevins kept Naples. In the event the Aragonese dynasty remained in Sicily permanently.

Legacy

The Vespers became a founding myth of Sicilian identity and a byword for popular revolt; it inspired Verdi's opera Les vêpres siciliennes (1855) and was invoked by Italian nationalists during the Risorgimento.
