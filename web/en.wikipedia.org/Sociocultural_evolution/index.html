Sociocultural evolution

Sociocultural evolution is the study of how societies and cultures change over time — in their technology, institutions, beliefs, and scale of organization. Nineteenth-century theorists such as Herbert Spencer, Lewis Henry Morgan, and Edward Burnett Tylor proposed unilineal schemes in which all societies pass through the same stages (Morgan's savagery, barbarism, civilization), an idea bound up with the era's assumptions of progress and later criticized as ethnocentric.

Twentieth-century approaches

Franz Boas and his students rejected unilineal evolution in favor of historical particularism, emphasizing diffusion and each culture's unique history. Mid-century anthropologists revived evolutionary questions in chastened forms: Julian Steward's multilineal evolution examined parallel adaptations to similar environments and his concept of levels of sociocultural integration; Leslie White made energy capture per capita the measure of cultural advance; and Marshall Sahlins and Elman Service distinguished specific from general evolution. Service's typology of band, tribe, chiefdom, and state remains a standard vocabulary for political evolution, used and refined by archaeologists studying the origins of agriculture, urbanism, and states (as in the work of V. Gordon Childe, who coined "Neolithic Revolution" and "Urban Revolution").

Modern research

Contemporary work includes dual inheritance theory (gene-culture coevolution), associated with Robert Boyd, Peter Richerson, and Luigi Luca Cavalli-Sforza, which models cultural transmission with the tools of evolutionary biology; cultural group selection debates; and quantitative comparative history using databases such as Seshat to test hypotheses about social complexity, religion, and warfare. Critics continue to warn against progressivist readings — evolution describes diversification and adaptation, not inevitable improvement.
