Jürgen Stroop (born Josef Stroop; 26 September 1895 – 6 March 1952) was a German SS commander during the Nazi era, best known for commanding the suppression of the Warsaw Ghetto Uprising in 1943 and authoring the report that documented it. He was convicted of war crimes and crimes against humanity after the Second World War and executed in Poland.

Early life and career

Stroop was born in Detmold in the Principality of Lippe, the son of a police officer. He served in the Imperial German Army in the First World War and joined the Nazi Party and the SS in 1932. He rose through SS and police posts in Münster, Hamburg, and later in occupied Greece, and by 1943 held the rank of SS-Brigadeführer; he was later promoted to SS-Gruppenführer and lieutenant general of police.

Warsaw Ghetto Uprising

In April 1943 Heinrich Himmler ordered Stroop to take over the liquidation of the Warsaw Ghetto after the first German attempt was repelled by Jewish fighters. Stroop's forces burned the ghetto block by block over nearly a month; about 13,000 Jews were killed during the operation and most of the roughly 50,000 remaining inhabitants were deported to extermination or labour camps. Stroop reported the destruction to Himmler in a leather-bound, photograph-illustrated document — the Stroop Report — concluding "The former Jewish quarter of Warsaw is no longer in existence." The report later served as evidence at the Nuremberg trials.

Trial and execution

Captured by American forces in 1945, Stroop was convicted by a U.S. military court at Dachau for the murder of American airmen and sentenced to death, but he was extradited to Poland before the sentence was carried out. Tried in Warsaw, he was convicted of crimes connected with the ghetto's destruction and hanged on 6 March 1952 at the site of the former ghetto. His prison conversations with a fellow inmate were later published as the basis of Kazimierz Moczarski's book Conversations with an Executioner.
