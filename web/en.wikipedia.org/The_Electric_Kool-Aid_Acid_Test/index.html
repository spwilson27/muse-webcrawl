The Electric Kool-Aid Acid Test


First edition (1968).

The Electric Kool-Aid Acid Test is a 1968 nonfiction book by Tom Wolfe. It is a landmark of the New Journalism movement and an account of novelist Ken Kesey, his communal group the Merry Pranksters, and the early psychedelic movement in the United States. Wolfe tells the story largely in the voices and perceptions of his subjects, using stream-of-consciousness narration, onomatopoeia, and lavish typographical effects.

Summary

The book follows Kesey — author of One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest — after his experiences as a paid volunteer in LSD experiments at Menlo Park. Kesey and the Pranksters purchase a 1939 International Harvester school bus, paint it in swirling Day-Glo colours, name it Furthur, and drive it across America in 1964 toward the New York World's Fair, with Beat icon Neal Cassady at the wheel. Back in California, the Pranksters stage the "Acid Tests", public happenings at which participants took LSD (sometimes in the Kool-Aid) amid light shows and music by the newly formed Grateful Dead. The narrative continues through Kesey's arrests for marijuana possession, his faked suicide and flight to Mexico, his return and trial, and the movement's decline — concluding with the Pranksters' disillusioning 1966 "Acid Test Graduation".

Style and significance

Wolfe, who spent time with the group and worked from their films, tapes, and letters, sought to render the subjective experience of the psychedelic scene. The book helped define the popular image of the 1960s counterculture and is regularly listed among the greatest works of literary journalism. Its title entered common usage in the phrase "drinking the Kool-Aid" — although the Jonestown massacre later gave that expression a darker connotation.
