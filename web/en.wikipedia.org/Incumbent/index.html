Incumbent

The incumbent is the current holder of an office or position, usually in relation to an election. In an election for that position, the incumbent is said to be "running for re-election"; the race may be described as an "open seat" contest when no incumbent seeks another term, often because of retirement or term limits.

Incumbency advantage

Incumbents in many political systems enjoy significant advantages over challengers: greater name recognition, established fundraising networks, access to media coverage, experience, and — in legislative systems — the ability to claim credit for government services and "pork barrel" spending directed to their districts, as well as franking privileges in some countries. In the United States Congress, re-election rates for House incumbents who seek another term have typically exceeded 90 percent in recent decades. Redistricting that protects incumbents (gerrymandering) can reinforce the advantage.

Anti-incumbency

Conversely, anti-incumbency describes a sentiment among voters to vote against officeholders, often during economic downturns or scandals, which can produce wave elections sweeping incumbents from office. In some countries, such as India, anti-incumbency is a recurring feature of state and national elections.

Other uses

In business, "incumbent" refers to the dominant existing firm or technology in a market, contrasted with new entrants or disruptors. In ecclesiastical contexts, an incumbent is the holder of a benefice.
