Traditional Chinese medicine


Acupuncture and herbal medicine are the best-known TCM practices.

Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) is an alternative medical practice drawn from traditional medicine in China. It encompasses a range of practices, including acupuncture, herbal medicine (zhōngyào), massage (tui na), exercise (qigong), and dietary therapy. Its theoretical framework centres on concepts such as qi (vital energy) flowing through meridians, the balance of yin and yang, and the five phases (wuxing). TCM has a documented history of more than two thousand years, with foundational texts such as the Huangdi Neijing (Yellow Emperor's Inner Canon), compiled by the 2nd century BCE.

History

Chinese medical thought developed through texts including the Shennong Bencao Jing (herbal pharmacopoeia), Zhang Zhongjing's Shanghan Lun (Treatise on Cold Damage, c. 200 CE), and Li Shizhen's great Bencao Gangmu (Compendium of Materia Medica, 1593), which catalogued nearly 1,900 substances. In the 1950s the People's Republic of China systematised diverse regional practices into a standardised "TCM," promoted it as a complement to Western medicine, and institutionalised training in combined universities. The famous "barefoot doctors" of the Cultural Revolution carried a simplified version into rural areas.

Practice and diagnosis

Diagnosis traditionally relies on inspection (including of the tongue), listening and smelling, inquiry, and palpation — above all reading the pulse at both wrists. Conditions are described as patterns of disharmony rather than diseases in the biomedical sense. Treatment individualises herbal formulas, acupuncture point selection, and lifestyle advice to the pattern. TCM is widely used in China alongside biomedicine, is licensed in many countries, and in 2019 the World Health Organization included a traditional medicine chapter in ICD-11.

Scientific evaluation

The core theoretical entities of TCM — qi, meridians, and the five phases — have no counterpart in anatomy or physiology and are regarded by the scientific community as pre-scientific. Evidence for specific practices is mixed: acupuncture shows modest effects for some chronic pain conditions, though trials controlling for placebo effects are debated; some herbal compounds have yielded important drugs, most famously artemisinin, isolated from Artemisia annua by Tu Youyou's team (Nobel Prize, 2015) by mining classical texts. Concerns include contamination and adulteration of herbal products, hepatotoxicity, and the use of endangered animal parts such as rhino horn and tiger bone, which China banned in trade in 1993 (a proposed 2018 relaxation was postponed).
