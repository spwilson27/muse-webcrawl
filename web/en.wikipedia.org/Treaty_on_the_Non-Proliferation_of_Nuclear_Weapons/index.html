Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons

The Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons, commonly known as the Non-Proliferation Treaty or NPT, is an international treaty whose objective is to prevent the spread of nuclear weapons and weapons technology, to promote cooperation in the peaceful uses of nuclear energy, and to further nuclear disarmament. Opened for signature in 1968, the treaty entered into force on 5 March 1970 and was extended indefinitely in 1995. With 191 states parties, it is among the most widely adhered-to arms control agreements.

The three pillars

The treaty rests on three pillars: non-proliferation, under which the five recognized nuclear-weapon states (the United States, Russia/the Soviet Union, the United Kingdom, France, and China) pledge not to transfer nuclear weapons, and non-nuclear-weapon states pledge not to acquire them, accepting International Atomic Energy Agency safeguards; disarmament, under Article VI, committing all parties to pursue negotiations toward nuclear disarmament; and peaceful use, affirming the right of all parties to develop nuclear energy for peaceful purposes.

Issues

India, Pakistan, Israel, and South Sudan have never signed; North Korea acceded but announced withdrawal in 2003 and has since tested nuclear weapons. Critics argue the nuclear-weapon states have been slow to honour Article VI, while enforcement against clandestine programmes has been contested. Review conferences are held every five years.
