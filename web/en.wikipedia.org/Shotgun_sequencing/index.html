Shotgun sequencing


Assembling reads into a full sequence

In genetics, shotgun sequencing is a method used for sequencing random DNA strands. It is named by analogy with the rapidly expanding, quasi-random shot grouping of a shotgun. The chain-termination method of DNA sequencing ("Sanger sequencing") can only be used for short DNA strands of 100 to 1000 base pairs; longer sequences are subdivided into smaller fragments that are sequenced and reassembled to give the overall sequence.

Procedure

DNA is fragmented randomly — by physical shearing or by enzymes — and the fragments are cloned or amplified, sequenced from both ends ("paired-end" reads), and assembled by computer programs that look for overlapping regions. Multiple overlapping reads for a region, called coverage or depth, resolve ambiguities and correct errors. Genomes are often first separated into large pieces cloned in bacterial artificial chromosomes (hierarchical shotgun) or sequenced whole (whole-genome shotgun).

History

The approach was pioneered for small genomes in the early 1980s and scaled up in the 1990s: in 1995 J. Craig Venter's institute sequenced the first free-living organism, Haemophilus influenzae, by whole-genome shotgun. Celera Genomics controversially applied the method to the human genome, publishing its assembly in 2001 alongside the public Human Genome Project. Modern high-throughput ("next-generation") platforms are essentially massively parallel shotgun sequencing systems.
