Caesar Cipher

In cryptography, a Caesar cipher, also known as Caesar's cipher, the shift cipher, Caesar's code, or Caesar shift, is one of the simplest and most widely known encryption techniques. It is a type of substitution cipher in which each letter in the plaintext is replaced by a letter some fixed number of positions down the alphabet.

Example

With a left shift of 3, D becomes A, E becomes B, and so on, wrapping around so X becomes U. The method is named after Julius Caesar, who used it in his private correspondence with a shift of three.

History

Suetonius reports Caesar used a shift of three to protect messages of military significance. His nephew Augustus used a shift of one, without wrapping, so A became B and Z became AA.

Breaking the cipher

The cipher offers minimal security: only 25 possible keys exist in English, and frequency analysis readily reveals the shift even without trying all keys. Even so it survives in puzzles and as the ROT13 system used to obscure spoilers online.

Mathematics

Encryption of letter x by shift n can be written as En(x) = (x + n) mod 26, and decryption as Dn(x) = (x − n) mod 26.
