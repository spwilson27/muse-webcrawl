Treaty of Ancón

Peru ceded Tarapacá outright; Tacna and Arica were to be decided by plebiscite.

Signed20 October 1883, Ancón, Peru
PartiesChile and Peru
ContextEnd of the War of the Pacific
Main effectPeru ceded Tarapacá to Chile; Tacna and Arica held by Chile pending a plebiscite

The Treaty of Ancón (Spanish: Tratado de Ancón) was signed by Chile and Peru on 20 October 1883 in the district of Ancón near Lima. It formally ended the War of the Pacific (1879–1884) between the two countries, following Chile's occupation of the Peruvian capital and the collapse of organised Peruvian resistance under the government of Miguel Iglesias.

Terms

Under the treaty, Peru ceded the coastal province of Tarapacá, rich in nitrate deposits, to Chile in perpetuity. The provinces of Tacna and Arica were to remain under Chilean administration for ten years, after which a plebiscite would decide their fate. Chile also undertook to withdraw its forces from the rest of occupied Peru once the treaty was ratified, which occurred in 1884.

The Tacna–Arica question

The promised plebiscite was never held. Disagreement over who should be allowed to vote, together with Chile's policy of "Chileanisation" in the occupied provinces, deadlocked the question for more than four decades. United States mediation in the 1920s, culminating in proposals by Presidents Coolidge and Hoover, produced the Treaty of Lima in 1929: Tacna was returned to Peru and Arica remained with Chile, with Chile paying compensation and granting Peru port facilities in Arica. The settlement remains the basis of the Peru–Chile border, and the episode is still remembered in Peru, where the period is known as the cautiverio (captivity) of Tacna and Arica.
