Cajun French (French: français cadien) is the variety of French spoken in Louisiana by the Cajuns, descendants of the Acadian exiles who were expelled from Canada by the British in the mid-18th century (the Grand Dérangement) and resettled in the bayous and prairies of South Louisiana. It is closely related to, but distinct from, Louisiana Creole and the colonial French formerly spoken in New Orleans.

History

The Acadians arrived in Louisiana from 1764 onwards, preserving features of the 17th- and 18th-century French of western France alongside innovations developed in Acadia and Louisiana, and borrowing vocabulary from Spanish, Native American languages, African languages and English. French remained the dominant language of much of South Louisiana into the 20th century.

Decline and revival

Louisiana's 1921 constitution mandated English-only public schooling, and children were punished for speaking French, driving a steep decline in intergenerational transmission. From the late 1960s, the Council for the Development of French in Louisiana (CODOFIL) led a revival, promoting French immersion schools, scholarships and media. Speaker numbers remain far below their early-20th-century peak, but immersion programmes have produced a new generation of younger speakers.

Features

Cajun French retains some archaic pronunciations and vocabulary lost in standard European French, uses distinctive pronouns such as eusse for "they", and includes Louisiana-specific words for local flora, fauna and food. It is mutually intelligible with other varieties of French, though its accent and lexicon mark it clearly.
