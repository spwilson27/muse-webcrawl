Histories (Polybius)

The Histories (Greek: Ἱστορίαι, Historiai) of Polybius (c. 200 – c. 118 BCE) is a universal history in forty books covering the years 264–146 BCE, from the First Punic War to the Roman destruction of Carthage and Corinth. Its central question is how, in less than fifty-three years, Rome came to dominate the entire Mediterranean world.

Scope and survival

Polybius, a Greek statesman from Megalopolis, was deported to Rome as a hostage after the Battle of Pydna and became a friend and adviser of Scipio Aemilianus, witnessing the sack of Carthage in 146 BCE. Only the first five books of the Histories survive complete; the remainder are preserved in fragments and excerpts, including the famous sixth book analysing the Roman constitution.

Ideas

In Book VI Polybius sets out his theory of anacyclosis, the cycle by which constitutions decay from monarchy through aristocracy and democracy into their corrupt forms, and argues that Rome's mixed constitution — combining consuls, Senate, and people — explains its stability and success. His insistence on causal explanation, eyewitness evidence, and the criticism of sources made the Histories a founding work of pragmatic historiography, later read by Machiavelli and the framers of modern republics.
