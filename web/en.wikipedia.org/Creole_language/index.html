Creole language

A creole language is a stable natural language that develops from the process of different languages simplifying and mixing into a new form (often a pidgin), and then that form expanding and elaborating into a full-fledged language with native speakers, a system known as nativization. Pidgins are simplified languages that develop as a means of communication between two or more groups that do not have a language in common. When a pidgin becomes the first language of a community's children, it acquires the grammatical complexity and expanded vocabulary of a full language, becoming a creole.

Creole languages are spoken around the Caribbean, the Atlantic and Indian Ocean coasts, and the Pacific

Overview

Most creole languages formed in colonial settings, particularly in the Atlantic and Indian Ocean worlds, where enslaved Africans and other labourers from many language backgrounds were thrown together on plantations and in ports. The vocabulary of a creole generally derives mainly from one language, called the lexifier or superstrate — often a European colonial language such as English, French, Portuguese, Spanish, or Dutch — while its grammar is generally held to derive in part from the substrate languages of its first speakers and in part from universal processes of language creation. Well-known examples include Haitian Creole (French-based), Jamaican Patois and Tok Pisin (English-based), Papiamento (Portuguese/Spanish-based), and Mauritian Creole (French-based).

Development

The classical account of creole genesis holds that a pidgin first develops as a makeshift means of communication among adults; children exposed to the pidgin as their primary input regularise and expand it into a creole in a single generation. Derek Bickerton's language bioprogram theory proposed that this rapid expansion draws on an innate human capacity for grammar. Other linguists argue that creoles can develop gradually over several generations and that their grammars substantially continue substrate patterns.

Features

Common structural features attributed to creoles include preverbal tense–mood–aspect particles, the use of reduplication for emphasis or plurality, invariant word forms with little inflection, and serial verb constructions. However, linguists continue to debate whether creoles form a distinct typological class or simply occupy one end of a continuum of contact-induced change. Many creole-speaking communities exhibit a post-creole continuum, in which the creole coexists with, and is influenced by, its lexifier in a range of intermediate varieties.

Status

Creoles were long stigmatised as corrupt or broken forms of their lexifiers, but since the mid-20th century they have increasingly gained recognition, official status, and use in education and media. Haitian Creole is an official language of Haiti, Seychellois Creole of Seychelles, and Tok Pisin of Papua New Guinea.
