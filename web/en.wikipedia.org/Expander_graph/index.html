Expander graph

An expander graph is a sparse graph that has strong connectivity properties, quantified by vertex, edge, or spectral expansion. Intuitively, a finite graph is an expander if every sufficiently small subset of vertices has a relatively large boundary — many neighbors outside the set — so there are no bottlenecks. Expanders are simultaneously sparse (few edges per vertex) and robustly connected, a combination that makes them valuable in computer science and mathematics.

Definitions and spectral theory

Expansion can be measured by the Cheeger constant (edge expansion) or, equivalently in a precise sense (via the Cheeger inequality), by the spectral gap: for a d-regular graph, the difference between d and the second-largest eigenvalue of the adjacency matrix in absolute value. A family of d-regular graphs is a family of expanders if this gap stays bounded away from zero as the graphs grow. The Alon–Boppana bound shows eigenvalues cannot be smaller in magnitude than about 2√(d−1); graphs attaining this bound are called Ramanujan graphs, first constructed by Lubotzky, Phillips, and Sarnak using deep number theory.

Constructions and applications

Early constructions came from Margulis (1973) and Gabber–Galil, and explicit zig-zag product constructions by Reingold, Vadhan, and Wigderson led to Reingold's proof that undirected connectivity is in logarithmic space (SL = L). Applications include error-correcting codes, derandomization of algorithms, pseudorandom generators, network design, and proofs in complexity theory such as the PCP theorem. Random regular graphs are expanders with high probability, a fact that guided the search for explicit constructions.
