Barbary Coast

The Barbary Coast (also known as the Barbary, or the Berber Coast) was the name given to the coastal regions of central and western North Africa — roughly present-day Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia, and Libya — from the 16th to the 19th century. The name derives from the Berber peoples who lived there.

History

The region was divided among the Ottoman regencies of Algiers, Tunis, and Tripoli, and the independent sultanate of Morocco. Its ports were notorious in Europe as bases for the Barbary corsairs, who raided Mediterranean shipping and European coasts and captured sailors and coastal villagers for enslavement or ransom. European states and the young United States paid tribute or fought wars to suppress the piracy: the United States fought the First and Second Barbary Wars (1801–1805 and 1815) against the Barbary states, and the 1816 bombardment of Algiers by an Anglo-Dutch fleet marked a turning point. The French conquest of Algiers in 1830 began the colonial period and effectively ended the corsair era.

The name "Barbary Coast" was later applied, by analogy, to a notorious red-light district of San Francisco during the Gold Rush era.
