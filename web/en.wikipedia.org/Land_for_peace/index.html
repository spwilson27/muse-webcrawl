Land for peace

Land for peace
ConceptDiplomatic formula in the Arab–Israeli conflict
Legal basisUN Security Council Resolution 242 (1967)
Key applicationEgypt–Israel peace treaty (1979)

Land for peace is a diplomatic formula underpinning efforts to resolve the Arab–Israeli conflict: in exchange for a peace treaty and recognition, Israel would withdraw from territories it occupied during the Six-Day War of 1967. The formula derives from United Nations Security Council Resolution 242, adopted in November 1967, which called for the "withdrawal of Israel armed forces from territories occupied in the recent conflict" together with the right of every state in the area to live in peace within secure and recognised boundaries.

Applications

The clearest successful application was the Egypt–Israel peace treaty of 1979, following the Camp David Accords: Israel returned the entire Sinai Peninsula, including dismantling settlements such as Yamit, and Egypt became the first Arab state to recognise Israel. The 1994 Israel–Jordan peace treaty settled borders with minor land swaps rather than a major withdrawal. The formula also framed the Oslo Accords with the Palestinians and the Madrid Conference, and the 2002 Arab Peace Initiative offered full normalisation in return for complete withdrawal to the 1967 lines.

Debate

The principle remains contested. Ambiguity in Resolution 242 — the English text omits the definite article before "territories", unlike the French version — has fuelled decades of argument over whether full or partial withdrawal was required. Israeli critics argue the formula failed where land was returned without durable peace, pointing to the unilateral withdrawals from southern Lebanon (2000) and Gaza (2005), while supporters maintain it remains the only viable basis for a negotiated settlement.
