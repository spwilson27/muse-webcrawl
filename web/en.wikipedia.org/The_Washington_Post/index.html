The Washington Post, locally known as the Post and abbreviated as WaPo, is an American daily newspaper published in Washington, D.C. Founded in 1877, it is the most widely circulated newspaper within the Washington metropolitan area and has a large international audience. The paper has won 76 Pulitzer Prizes, second only to The New York Times.

History

The Post was founded by Stilson Hutchins in 1877. Its most consequential era began in 1946 when Eugene Meyer, who had bought the paper at a bankruptcy auction in 1933, was succeeded by his son-in-law Philip Graham, and then by Graham's widow Katharine Graham after 1963. Under Katharine Graham and editor Ben Bradlee, the Post published the Pentagon Papers in 1971 and led the reporting on the Watergate scandal — by reporters Bob Woodward and Carl Bernstein — which led to the resignation of President Richard Nixon in 1974.

Bezos ownership

In 2013 the Graham family sold the paper to Jeff Bezos's Nash Holdings for $250 million. Under Bezos, the Post invested heavily in digital subscriptions and technology, achieving profitability and greatly expanding its online readership, with the slogan "Democracy Dies in Darkness" adopted in 2017. In 2024–2025 the paper saw turmoil over Bezos's decision to block a planned presidential endorsement and to refocus the opinion section on "personal liberties and free markets," prompting resignations and subscription losses.

Circulation and influence

Published for the capital region, the Post is especially influential in American politics, known for investigative reporting, the Style section, film critic Ann Hornaday, and guitarist of features such as the Date Lab column. It is one of the United States' principal newspapers of record.
