The praetorian prefect (Latin: praefectus praetorio) was a high office in the Roman Empire. Originally the commander of the Praetorian Guard, the emperor's personal bodyguard, the prefecture grew into one of the most powerful positions in the state — in effect the emperor's chief deputy, combining military command with judicial, financial and administrative authority. In the later empire the title survived in transformed form as the civil governor of one of the great praetorian prefectures.

The Principate

Augustus created the post in 2 BC, appointing two equestrian prefects to command the Praetorian cohorts based in Italy. Under Tiberius, the prefect Lucius Aelius Sejanus concentrated the Guard in a single camp at Rome (the Castra Praetoria) in AD 23 and amassed such influence that he effectively governed while Tiberius withdrew to Capri, before falling in a coup in AD 31. Thereafter the prefect was normally the emperor's most trusted subordinate: Seneca's ally Sextus Afranius Burrus served Nero, while Gaius Fulvius Plautianus under Septimius Severus came close to co-rulership. Because the Guard could make and unmake emperors — it famously auctioned the empire to Didius Julianus in AD 193 — prefects were central figures in imperial politics. The prefect also acquired extensive judicial powers, hearing appeals from across the empire, so that several of Rome's greatest jurists, including Papinian and Ulpian, held the office.

The later empire

Constantine I disbanded the Praetorian Guard after the Battle of the Milvian Bridge in 312, and the prefecture was recast as a purely civilian office. Under Constantine and his successors the empire was divided into great territorial prefectures — the Gauls, Italy, Illyricum and the East — each governed by a praetorian prefect who was the senior civilian administrator and judge within it, supervising taxation, the grain supply, public works and the diocesan vicars. The eastern prefecture's prefect remained one of the leading officials of the Byzantine state into the seventh century.
