Reserve currency

A reserve currency is a foreign currency held in significant quantities by central banks and other monetary authorities as part of their foreign-exchange reserves. Reserve currencies are also widely used to price commodities, settle international trade, issue debt, and conduct financial transactions.

Characteristics

A currency is attractive as a reserve asset when its issuing economy is large, its financial markets are deep and liquid, exchange controls are limited, property rights and monetary policy are credible, and safe assets such as government bonds are available in quantity. Network effects reinforce incumbents: because many traders and borrowers already use a currency, others find it cheaper and safer to do so.

The U.S. dollar remains the dominant reserve currency by share of disclosed reserves, international payments, foreign-exchange turnover, and trade invoicing. The euro is second. Exact reserve shares change with exchange rates and central-bank diversification.

Advantages and burdens

The issuer may enjoy lower borrowing costs, greater ability to borrow in its own currency, seigniorage, and influence over payment systems. Demand can also appreciate the currency, encourage external deficits, and expose the financial system to global shocks. The Triffin dilemma describes a tension between supplying the world with safe liquid assets and maintaining confidence in the issuer's long-term liabilities.

History

Sterling was the leading international currency during the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, supported by British trade, finance, and the gold standard. The Bretton Woods system after the Second World War formally centred reserves on the dollar, then convertible into gold. Dollar centrality survived the end of convertibility in 1971 because of the scale and liquidity of U.S. markets and the absence of an equally complete alternative.
