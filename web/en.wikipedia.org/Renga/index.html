Renga

Renga (連歌, "linked poem") is a genre of Japanese collaborative poetry in which two or more poets alternate composing stanzas, each linked to the preceding one, to form a long poem. A renga consists of sequences of alternating tanka-like verses of 5-7-5 and 7-7 syllables. The opening verse, the hokku, later developed into the independent form known as haiku.

Renga sessions brought poets together to compose linked verse collaboratively.

History

Short linked verses appeared as early as the Heian period, in exchanges recorded in the Man'yōshū and Kokinshū. By the Kamakura and Muromachi periods, renga had become a major literary art, with formal rules codified by poets such as Nijō Yoshimoto in the fourteenth century. The standard long form, the hyakuin, contained one hundred verses. The poet Sōgi (1421–1502) is considered the classical master of the genre; his Minase Sangin Hyakuin (1488), composed with two companions, is among the most celebrated renga sequences.

Form and rules

Each new verse must link to the immediately preceding verse — by association of imagery, season, or phrase — while shifting away from the verse before that, so the poem moves continually forward without returning to earlier topics. Codified rules governed the placement of conventional topics such as moon and flower verses, and sessions were guided by a master who judged contributions. Under Matsuo Bashō in the seventeenth century, a lighter, more playful style called haikai no renga emerged, from which haiku eventually separated.
