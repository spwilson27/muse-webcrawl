Homicide Act 1957

The Homicide Act 1957 (5 & 6 Eliz. 2. c. 11) is an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom. It was enacted as a partial reform of the law of murder in England and Wales, then punishable by mandatory capital punishment. The Act restricted the death penalty to certain categories of "capital murder", introduced the defence of diminished responsibility, and reformed the doctrines of provocation and constructive malice.

Provisions

Part I of the Act abolished "constructive malice" — the rule that a killing in the course of another crime could automatically amount to murder — and required the prosecution to prove the mental element of murder. It also modified the law of provocation, providing that words alone could amount to provocation and that the question of whether provocation was enough to make a reasonable person act as the defendant did was for the jury. Section 2 created the partial defence of diminished responsibility, under which a person suffering from an "abnormality of mind" that substantially impaired their mental responsibility for a killing could be convicted of manslaughter instead of murder. Section 3 reformed provocation into its modern form.

Part II restricted capital punishment: the death penalty remained only for specified categories of capital murder, including murder in the course of theft, murder by shooting or explosion, murder in the course of resisting arrest, and the second murder committed by someone already convicted of murder. The death penalty for murder was suspended in 1965 and abolished permanently in 1969, rendering these provisions obsolete, though the diminished responsibility and provocation reforms endured.
