Murus gallicus

Reconstruction of a murus gallicus rampart

A murus gallicus or Gallic wall is a method of construction of defensive walls used by the Celts of La Tène Europe, particularly the Gauls, to protect hillforts and oppida. The term was coined from Julius Caesar's description of such walls in his Commentarii de Bello Gallico.

Construction

The wall was built as a timber framework: long horizontal beams were laid lengthwise and crosswise at intervals of about two feet, fastened together with iron spikes, and the interior was filled with earth and rubble. The front face was a revetment of dressed stone. Typically a murus gallicus stood about 6 metres high and 4 metres wide.

Advantages

The timber lacing gave the wall strength and elasticity that made it highly resistant to battering rams, while the stone facing made it difficult to set on fire or to undermine — Caesar noted both qualities during his siege of Avaricum. The main weaknesses were cost and the eventual rotting of the timbers.
