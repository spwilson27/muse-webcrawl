The Capitoline Hill (Latin: Mons Capitolinus; Italian: Campidoglio), between the Roman Forum and the Campus Martius, is one of the Seven Hills of Rome. Though the smallest of the seven, it was the religious and political citadel of the city: the great Temple of Jupiter Optimus Maximus Capitolinus stood on its southern summit, and the hill gave its name — through the word capitol — to legislative buildings around the world.

Ancient history

According to tradition, when foundation trenches were dug for the temple a human head (Latin caput) was found intact, which augurs interpreted as a sign that Rome would be the head of the world — one traditional explanation of the hill's name. The Temple of Jupiter, dedicated in 509 BCE in the first year of the Republic, housed the Capitoline Triad of Jupiter, Juno and Minerva and was the destination of triumphal processions, whose generals laid their laurel at the statue's feet. The northern summit, the Arx, held the Temple of Juno Moneta, adjoining the Roman mint — the origin of the word "money".

The hill was Rome's citadel: in 390 BCE its sacred geese famously warned of a night attack by the Gauls. The steep Tarpeian Rock on its southern face was the execution site from which traitors were thrown. The Tabularium, the great state archive built in 78 BCE, formed a monumental backdrop to the Forum and still underlies the later palace on the hill.

Medieval and Renaissance

In 1143 the revived Senate of Rome established itself on the Capitoline, making it once again the seat of civic government — a role it retains, as the Palazzo Senatorio houses the office of the Mayor of Rome. In 1536–1546 Pope Paul III commissioned Michelangelo to redesign the summit. Michelangelo reoriented the square toward St Peter's and the modern city, created the trapezoidal Piazza del Campidoglio with its oval pavement pattern, gave the Palazzo Senatorio and Palazzo dei Conservatori new façades, and added the Palazzo Nuovo; the ancient equestrian statue of Marcus Aurelius was moved to the centre of the piazza (a copy now stands there, the original preserved inside the museum).

The Capitoline today

The hill is reached by Michelangelo's broad stepped ramp, the Cordonata, flanked by statues of the Dioscuri. The Capitoline Museums, opened to the public in 1734 and among the oldest public museums in the world, occupy the two palazzi facing the piazza; the church of Santa Maria in Aracoeli stands on the Arx summit beside the vast white Vittoriano monument (1885–1935) on the hill's eastern slope.
