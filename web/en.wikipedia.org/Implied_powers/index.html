Implied powers

Implied powers
[Image: The United States Capitol, seat of the Congress that exercises implied powers]
FieldConstitutional law
Source (US)Necessary and Proper Clause, Art. I, §8
Landmark caseMcCulloch v. Maryland (1819)

Implied powers are political powers granted to a government body that are not explicitly stated in its founding document but are inferred to exist because they are necessary to carry out the powers that are expressly granted. They contrast with enumerated (expressed) powers and with inherent powers.

United States

In the United States the concept rests chiefly on the Necessary and Proper Clause of Article I, Section 8 of the Constitution, which empowers Congress "to make all Laws which shall be necessary and proper for carrying into Execution the foregoing Powers". The classic application came in McCulloch v. Maryland (1819), where Chief Justice John Marshall upheld Congress's power to charter a national bank — nowhere expressly authorised — as implied by its fiscal powers, famously writing: "Let the end be legitimate, let it be within the scope of the constitution, and all means which are appropriate, which are plainly adapted to that end, which are not prohibited, but consistent with the letter and spirit of the constitution, are constitutional."

Examples and debates

Implied powers have supported such federal actions as creating the Air Force under the power to raise armies, regulating intrastate activity that substantially affects interstate commerce, and establishing much of the administrative state. The breadth of the doctrine has been contested since the founding — Thomas Jefferson's strict-constructionist objection to the First Bank of the United States against Alexander Hamilton's broad reading — and remains central to disputes over federalism and the scope of congressional power. Analogous doctrines exist in other federations and in the European Union's principle of implied external powers.
