The pion (from the Greek letter pi, the symbol π used for it) is a type of meson discovered in cosmic rays in 1947. Pions come in three varieties distinguished by electric charge: π⁺, π⁰ and π⁻. Each is a bound state of a first-generation quark and antiquark—up and down quarks—making pions the lightest mesons and among the lightest of all hadrons.

Why pions matter

Pions mediate the strong interaction between nucleons at distances beyond a femtometre or so, in the way Hideki Yukawa envisaged in 1935 when he predicted a particle of about 100–200 times the electron's mass. Their unusual lightness compared with other hadrons is explained in quantum chromodynamics by chiral symmetry: pions would be exactly massless Nambu–Goldstone bosons if up and down quarks were massless, and their small real masses reflect the quarks' small masses.

Production and decay

Pions are produced abundantly in collisions of high-energy particles—in cosmic-ray showers and at accelerators, where pion beams are used in experiments and even in medical hadron therapy research. The charged pion's dominant decay, π⁺ → μ⁺ + νμ, violates parity in the weak interaction and famously helped establish the two-component neutrino theory and the V−A structure of the weak force. The neutral pion decays electromagnetically, π⁰ → γ + γ, with a lifetime of only about 84 attoseconds, a decay governed by the chiral anomaly of QCD.
