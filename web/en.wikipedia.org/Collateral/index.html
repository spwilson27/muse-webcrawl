Collateral

In lending, collateral is an asset that a borrower pledges to a lender to secure a loan. If the borrower defaults, the lender may seize and sell the collateral to recover the amount owed. Loans backed by collateral are called secured loans and generally carry lower interest rates than unsecured loans because the lender's risk is reduced.

Types

Common forms of collateral include real estate (in mortgages), vehicles (in auto loans), inventory and receivables (in business lending), securities (in margin loans), and cash deposits. In financial markets, collateral is central to repurchase agreements, derivatives margining, and secured funding.

Key concepts

The loan-to-value ratio compares the loan amount with the collateral's value; lenders often lend only a fraction of the value, applying a "haircut" to allow for price fluctuations. Rehypothecation — the reuse of pledged collateral by the lender — became a focus of concern after the 2008 financial crisis because long collateral chains amplified systemic risk.

Legal aspects

Collateral arrangements create a security interest, perfected by registration or possession depending on jurisdiction. On default, foreclosure or repossession transfers the asset to the lender, subject to consumer-protection rules.
