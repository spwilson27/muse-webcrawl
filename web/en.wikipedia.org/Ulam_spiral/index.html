Ulam spiral

The Ulam spiral or prime spiral is a graphical depiction of the set of prime numbers, devised by mathematician Stanisław Ulam in 1963 and popularized in Martin Gardner's Scientific American column the following year. It is constructed by writing the positive integers in a square spiral and specially marking the prime numbers.

Discovery

Ulam constructed the spiral while doodling during the presentation of a paper at a scientific meeting, reportedly finding the arrangement "boring" talks conducive to doodling. To his surprise, the primes tended to concentrate along certain diagonal lines. In the 200×200 Ulam spiral shown in many reproductions, diagonal lines are clearly visible, confirming the phenomenon. Horizontal and vertical lines, while less prominent, are also evident.

Structure

The numbers are arranged in a square spiral beginning with 1 at the centre. Primes appear to lie preferentially on diagonals because numbers on a given diagonal are generated by a quadratic polynomial. Certain polynomials, such as Euler's famous n² − n + 41, are rich in primes and correspond to conspicuous diagonal streaks in the spiral. Although the patterns are real, no diagonal can consist entirely of primes, and heuristics such as the Hardy–Littlewood conjectures predict the degree of prime richness along each quadratic sequence.

Variants

Related constructions include the Sacks spiral, which uses an Archimedean spiral, and triangular arrangements of integers in which primes also form striking patterns.
