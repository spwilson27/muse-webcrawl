Plea bargain

Most criminal cases in the United States are resolved by plea bargaining rather than trial

A plea bargain (also plea agreement or plea deal) is an agreement in criminal law proceedings, whereby the prosecutor provides a concession to the defendant in exchange for a plea of guilt or nolo contendere. This may mean that the defendant will plead guilty to a less serious charge, or to one of several charges, in return for the dismissal of other charges; or it may mean that the defendant will plead guilty to the original criminal charge in return for a more lenient sentence.

Types and prevalence

Plea bargaining generally takes three forms: charge bargaining (pleading to a reduced charge), sentence bargaining (agreement on a recommended sentence) and fact bargaining (stipulating facts to limit exposure). In the United States, plea bargains account for the overwhelming majority — commonly estimated at around 90–95 per cent — of criminal convictions in both federal and state courts, a practice upheld by the Supreme Court in cases such as Brady v. United States (1970) and Santobello v. New York (1971). Many other common-law jurisdictions, and increasingly civil-law countries such as Italy and Germany, permit forms of negotiated pleas, while some systems prohibit or restrict them.

Criticism

Defenders argue that bargaining spares victims testimony, guarantees conviction, and keeps courts functioning. Critics contend that it can coerce innocent defendants — especially those facing a severe "trial penalty" or unable to make bail — into pleading guilty, that it bypasses constitutional trial rights, and that it produces sentencing disparities. Innocence projects have documented numerous cases in which defendants who pleaded guilty were later exonerated, notably by DNA evidence. Reform proposals range from greater judicial scrutiny to restricting charge reductions and recording all negotiations.
