Stinging nettle

Stinging nettle (Urtica dioica) is a herbaceous perennial flowering plant in the family Urticaceae. Originally native to Europe, much of temperate Asia, and western North Africa, it is now found worldwide. The plant is well known for its stinging hairs (trichomes), which inject histamine and other chemicals that produce a stinging sensation upon contact with the skin.

Description

The plant grows 1 to 2 metres tall in summer and dies back to the ground in winter. It has widely spreading rhizomes and stolons, bright yellow in colour. The soft green leaves are 3 to 15 cm long, borne oppositely on an erect stem, with a strongly serrated margin. The leaves and stems are covered with hollow stinging hairs that act like hypodermic needles, injecting histamine, acetylcholine, and formic acid.

Distribution and habitat

Stinging nettle is abundant in northern Europe and much of Asia, and is widespread in North America as an introduced species. It thrives in nitrogen-rich soils, in meadows, riverbanks, hedgerows, and disturbed ground near human habitation.

Uses

Nettle has a long history of use as a source of food, fibre, and medicine. The young leaves are cooked and eaten like spinach; cooking destroys the stinging chemicals. Nettle fibre was used for textiles into the 20th century. In herbal medicine, nettle is used for a variety of conditions, and it is an important food plant for the caterpillars of several butterfly species, including the small tortoiseshell and peacock.

Ecology

Nettles support a wide range of insects and are an indicator of fertile, phosphate-rich soil. See also Urticaceae and histamine.
