Freezing rain is rain that falls as supercooled liquid droplets and freezes on impact with the ground or other surfaces, forming a smooth, transparent coating of ice called glaze. It is among the most hazardous forms of winter weather: even a few millimetres can make roads impassable, and heavier accumulations snap trees and power lines, causing prolonged outages.

Formation

Freezing rain occurs when precipitation falls through a layered atmosphere: snow melts completely in a warm layer aloft (above 0 °C), then passes into a shallow layer of sub-freezing air near the surface. If that cold layer is too thin to refreeze the drops into ice pellets, they become supercooled — liquid below freezing — and freeze instantly on contact. This temperature inversion profile is common ahead of winter warm fronts. Freezing drizzle is the same process with smaller droplets.

Effects

Glaze ice adds enormous weight — a centimetre of ice can add hundreds of kilograms to the span of a power line — and coats roads in invisible "black ice". The January 1998 North American ice storm, driven by days of freezing rain over Quebec, eastern Ontario, and New England, left millions without power, some for weeks, and caused billions of dollars in damage. Freezing rain is also a serious aviation hazard, as ice accretes on wings and airframes faster than de-icing systems may remove it; aircraft avoid forecast freezing rain because it often indicates severe icing conditions.

Observation and forecasting

Weather services issue freezing rain and ice storm warnings based on expected accretion; in the United States, an ice storm warning is issued for accumulations of at least 6 mm (0.25 in). Automated stations report freezing rain with the METAR code FZRA, though distinguishing it from ice pellets requires radar profiles and surface reports.
