Block grant

A block grant is a large sum of money granted by a national government to a regional government with only general provisions as to the way it is to be spent, in contrast to a categorical grant, which comes with strict conditions on how the money may be used. Block grants are a central instrument of fiscal federalism in countries such as the United States, the United Kingdom and Canada.

United States

In the United States, block grants have been distributed by the federal government to state governments since the 1960s. Major examples include the Community Development Block Grant (CDBG) programme, created in 1974, and Temporary Assistance for Needy Families (TANF), created in 1996 when the previous categorical welfare programme was converted into a block grant. Supporters argue that block grants give states flexibility to tailor programmes to local needs; critics respond that funding often fails to keep pace with inflation and that accountability is weakened.

United Kingdom

In the United Kingdom, the block grant refers to the funding provided by the central government to the devolved administrations of Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland. Since 1999 the size of each devolved block grant has generally been determined by the Barnett formula, which allocates changes in funding in proportion to population and the degree to which a service is devolved.
