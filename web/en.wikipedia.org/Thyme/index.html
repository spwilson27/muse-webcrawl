Thyme

Thyme is the herb (dried leaves or flowering tops) of several species of the genus Thymus in the mint family, Lamiaceae. The most commonly used culinary species is Thymus vulgaris, common or garden thyme, a small evergreen subshrub native to the Mediterranean region. Thyme has a pungent, minty and slightly bitter flavour and is a staple of Mediterranean, French and Middle Eastern cooking.

History

Thyme has been used since antiquity. The ancient Egyptians used it in embalming, and the ancient Greeks burned it as incense and associated it with courage — the Greek word thymos means courage or spirit. Roman soldiers reputedly bathed in thyme water before battle, and in the Middle Ages sprigs of thyme were given to knights as tokens of bravery. The plant spread across Europe with the Romans and was widely grown in monastery gardens.

Cultivation and varieties

Thyme thrives in hot, sunny positions with well-drained, even poor soil, and is drought-tolerant once established. Varieties include French, English and German thyme, lemon thyme (Thymus citriodorus) with a citrus scent, caraway thyme, and creeping thyme, which is used mainly as groundcover. The leaves are harvested as the plants come into flower, when the concentration of aromatic oils is highest.

Culinary use

Thyme is a component of the French bouquet garni and herbes de Provence, the Egyptian spice mixture dukkah, and the Levantine blend za'atar. It seasons soups, stews, roasts, tomato sauces and beans, and pairs well with other Mediterranean herbs. Because its flavour survives long cooking, sprigs are often added whole and removed before serving.

Constituents

The essential oil of thyme contains thymol, a phenol with antiseptic properties long used in mouthwashes and traditional medicine, along with carvacrol, linalool and p-cymene.
