Easterlin paradox

The Easterlin paradox is a finding in happiness economics formulated by economist Richard Easterlin in his 1974 paper "Does Economic Growth Improve the Human Lot? Some Empirical Evidence." The paradox arises from the observation that, within a country at a given point in time, richer people report being happier than poorer people, yet comparisons over time and between countries do not clearly show that rising national income is associated with rising average happiness.

The original findings

Easterlin examined survey data from the United States and a number of other countries. He found that happiness varies directly with income within a society's cross-section, but that in the United States average reported happiness did not increase between 1946 and 1970 despite substantial growth in real income, and that richer countries were not clearly happier than poorer ones once basic needs were met.

Explanations

Easterlin explained the paradox through relative income and adaptation: people judge their material circumstances against social norms and their own past experience, so when everyone's income rises together, the benchmarks rise too, leaving average happiness unchanged — an instance of the hedonic treadmill. Material aspirations grow in step with income, so additional income never closes the gap between wants and means.

Criticism and later research

The paradox has been extensively debated. Economists Betsey Stevenson and Justin Wolfers, among others, have argued with larger datasets that life satisfaction rises with the logarithm of income both within and between countries and over time, with no clear satiation point, challenging the paradox. Easterlin and co-authors responded with studies, including of China during its rapid growth, in which life satisfaction failed to rise despite steep income increases. The debate continues, with attention to the distinction between evaluative life satisfaction and day-to-day emotional well-being, and to measures such as the Cantril ladder.

Implications

The paradox is frequently invoked in arguments that public policy should target well-being directly rather than gross domestic product alone, and it underlies interest in national well-being accounts, the capability approach, and alternative development indicators such as Bhutan's Gross National Happiness.
