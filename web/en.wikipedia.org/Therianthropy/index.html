Therianthropy

Therianthropy is the mythological ability or affiliation of humans to metamorphose into animals or to possess combined human and animal characteristics. The term comes from Greek thēríon (θηρίον, "wild animal, beast") and ánthrōpos (ἄνθρωπος, "human being"). It is used in folklore studies for shape-shifting traditions worldwide, and in modern contexts for people, called therians, who identify spiritually or psychologically as non-human animals.

In myth and folklore

The most familiar form in European folklore is lycanthropy, transformation into a wolf. Other traditions include berserkers and the Norse hamrammr, the were-tigers of India and Southeast Asia, the were-jaguars of Mesoamerican nagual belief, Japanese hengeyōkai such as foxes (kitsune) and tanuki that take human form, and the skin-walkers of Navajo tradition. Some of the earliest candidate depictions are in cave art, such as the "Lion Man" figurine of the Upper Paleolithic and the "Sorcerer" figure at Trois-Frères, suggesting the antiquity of human–animal transformation ideas in shamanic contexts.

Clinical and modern senses

In psychiatry, clinical lycanthropy is a rare syndrome in which a patient believes they can transform, or have transformed, into an animal; it is the clinical sense in which "therianthropy" sometimes appears in medical literature. Separately, since the 1990s an online subculture of "therians" has grown up, whose members understand themselves as partly or wholly non-human animals in a psychological or spiritual sense, experiencing their animal nature (their "theriotype") through shifts, dreams, and identity — a usage unrelated to delusion or to folkloric transformation.

Scholarly study

Folklorists and anthropologists study therianthropy in relation to totemism, shamanism, hunting ritual, and the porous boundary between human and animal in pre-modern worldviews, as well as in literary werewolf traditions from Petronius and Ovid's story of Lycaon to medieval romances such as Bisclavret.
