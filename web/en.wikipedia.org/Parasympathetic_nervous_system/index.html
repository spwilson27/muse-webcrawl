Parasympathetic nervous system

Parasympathetic nervous system Part of: Autonomic nervous system
Outflow: Craniosacral (CN III, VII, IX, X; S2–S4)
Main nerve: Vagus nerve (CN X)
Neurotransmitter: Acetylcholine
Function: "Rest and digest"

The parasympathetic nervous system (PSNS) is one of the three divisions of the autonomic nervous system, the others being the sympathetic nervous system and the enteric nervous system. The parasympathetic nervous system is responsible for stimulation of "rest-and-digest" or "feed and breed" activities that occur when the body is at rest, in contrast to the sympathetic system's "fight-or-flight" response.

Anatomy

Parasympathetic nerve fibres arise from the central nervous system at two levels — the brainstem and the sacral spinal cord (segments S2–S4) — a pattern called craniosacral outflow. Cranial nerves III (oculomotor), VII (facial), and IX (glossopharyngeal) carry parasympathetic fibres to the pupils, lacrimal glands, salivary glands, and parotid gland, respectively. The vagus nerve (CN X) supplies the great majority of parasympathetic innervation to the viscera, reaching the neck, thorax, and abdomen as far as the distal transverse colon; pelvic splanchnic nerves from S2–S4 supply the hindgut, bladder, and reproductive organs.

Unlike the sympathetic system, whose ganglia lie near the spinal cord, parasympathetic ganglia sit near or within the target organs, so preganglionic fibres are long and postganglionic fibres short. Both pre- and postganglionic neurons use acetylcholine as their primary neurotransmitter, acting on nicotinic receptors in the ganglia and muscarinic receptors in the effector organs.

Function

Parasympathetic activation constricts the pupil (miosis), stimulates salivation and tear production, slows the heart rate via the vagus nerve, constricts the bronchi, increases gastrointestinal motility and secretion, promotes bladder contraction for urination, and mediates sexual arousal. The system conserves energy and maintains routine bodily functions; its activity predominates during sleep and digestion.

Clinical significance

Drugs that mimic parasympathetic action (cholinomimetics) and those that block it (anticholinergics such as atropine) are widely used in medicine. Excessive vagal activity can cause fainting (vasovagal syncope), while damage to pelvic parasympathetic nerves can impair bladder, bowel, and sexual function.
