River delta

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A river delta is a landform created by deposition of sediment that is carried by a river as the flow leaves its mouth and enters slower-moving or standing water. This occurs in a body of water such as a lake, sea, or ocean, into which the river flows. The name comes from the Greek letter delta (Δ), whose triangular shape resembles that of the Nile Delta, the example described by Herodotus.

Deltas form where the sediment supply of a river exceeds the ability of waves and tidal currents to redistribute it. The river splits into distributary channels, building land outward in lobes; when a channel is abandoned, a new lobe grows elsewhere, giving many deltas their shifting, fan-like patterns.

Formation

As river water decelerates on entering standing water, it can no longer carry its suspended load, and sediment settles out. Coarse material drops first near the mouth, forming mouth bars that split the flow; finer silt and clay travel farther. Over time, levees, crevasse splays, marshes, and tidal flats build a broad deltaic plain.

Types

Geologists classify deltas by the dominant process shaping them: river-dominated deltas such as the bird's-foot Mississippi; wave-dominated deltas such as the arcuate Nile; and tide-dominated deltas such as the Ganges–Brahmaputra, the largest delta system on Earth.

Human significance

Delta soils are fertile and have supported dense populations and ancient civilisations since the Neolithic, but deltas are vulnerable to flooding, subsidence, sea-level rise, and upstream dams that starve them of sediment. Major cities on deltas include Alexandria, Dhaka, Ho Chi Minh City, Rotterdam, and New Orleans.
