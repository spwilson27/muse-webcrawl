Annales school

[Image placeholder]

The Annales school is a group of historians named after the French academic journal Annales. Histoire, Sciences Sociales, founded in 1929 by Lucien Febvre and Marc Bloch at the University of Strasbourg as the Annales d'histoire économique et sociale. The school pioneered a new kind of history — often called la nouvelle histoire — that rejected the primacy of political, diplomatic and military narrative in favour of social, economic and cultural structures studied over long periods, drawing on geography, demography, economics, sociology and anthropology.

Generations

The first generation (1929–1945) was led by Bloch, author of Feudal Society, and Febvre. The second generation (1945–1968) was dominated by Fernand Braudel, Febvre's successor, whose The Mediterranean and the Mediterranean World in the Age of Philip II (1949) organised history into three time scales: the near-motionless time of geography (longue durée), the slower rhythms of social and economic structures (conjonctures), and the rapid time of events (événements). Braudel institutionalised the school at the VIe Section of the École Pratique des Hautes Études and the Maison des Sciences de l'Homme. The third generation (from 1968) fragmented into many directions — historical demography, quantitative history, and the history of mentalités represented by Emmanuel Le Roy Ladurie (Montaillou), Jacques Le Goff and Georges Duby. A fourth generation, associated with Roger Chartier and the journal's 1989 "critical turn," moved toward cultural history and a more reflexive stance.

Influence

The Annales approach reshaped historical practice worldwide, influencing British social history, the Bielefeld school in Germany, microhistory in Italy, and world-systems analysis; Immanuel Wallerstein's centre at Binghamton was named for Braudel. Critics have charged the school with neglecting politics, biography and the role of chance and contingency.
