Microcode

Microcode
LayerBetween hardware and machine code
Invented byMaurice Wilkes (1951 concept)
StorageROM, PLA, or updateable RAM/control store

Microcode is a layer of low-level instructions that controls a computer's central processing unit, translating the machine-code instructions of the instruction set architecture into sequences of detailed circuit-level operations. In many processors, each machine instruction is implemented as a short program — a microprogram — stored in a control store within the CPU.

History

The concept of microprogramming was proposed by Maurice Wilkes in 1951 and popularised through IBM's System/360 family in the 1960s, which used microcode to offer a range of compatible machines at different speeds and prices, and to emulate earlier models. Microcode simplified processor design and debugging because control logic could be altered by changing the microprogram rather than rewiring hardware.

Modern use

Simpler RISC processors largely execute instructions directly in hardware, but complex instructions in CISC designs such as x86 are still commonly decomposed into micro-operations, sometimes with the aid of stored microcode. In modern Intel and AMD processors, microcode can be updated after manufacture — loaded by the BIOS or operating system at boot — to fix bugs or mitigate security vulnerabilities such as Spectre, making updateable microcode a critical part of platform security.

Microinstructions

Individual microinstructions typically specify operations on internal registers, arithmetic logic unit functions, and the address of the next microinstruction. Horizontal microcode packs many control signals into wide words, while vertical microcode encodes them more compactly, trading speed for density.
