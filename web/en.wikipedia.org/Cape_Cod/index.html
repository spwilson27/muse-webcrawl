Cape Cod

Cape Cod is a hook-shaped peninsula in southeastern Massachusetts extending into the Atlantic Ocean. Comprising all of Barnstable County, it is divided into the Upper, Mid, Lower, and Outer Cape and includes the towns of Bourne, Sandwich, Barnstable, Hyannis, Chatham, Provincetown, and fifteen others. It is one of New England's best-known summer resort areas.

Dunes and a lighthouse on the Outer Cape.

Geography and geology

The Cape is a glacial moraine and outwash plain formed at the end of the last ice age. Its arm is bounded by Cape Cod Bay to the north, Buzzards Bay to the west, and Nantucket and Vineyard Sounds to the south. The Cape Cod Canal, completed in 1914, cut the peninsula off from the mainland and made it technically an island. Much of the Atlantic-facing shore is protected as the Cape Cod National Seashore, established in 1961.

History

Explorer Bartholomew Gosnold named the cape in 1602 for the codfish taken in its waters. The Pilgrims first landed at Provincetown in 1620 and signed the Mayflower Compact in its harbor before settling at Plymouth. In the nineteenth century the Cape thrived on whaling, salt works, and cranberry cultivation — the cranberry bogs remain an agricultural fixture. The writer Henry David Thoreau chronicled his walks along the great beach in Cape Cod (1865).

Tourism

Summer tourism dominates the modern economy; the seasonal population roughly triples. Provincetown is a noted art colony and LGBTQ resort; Hyannis is associated with the Kennedy family compound. Ferry services connect the Cape with the islands of Martha's Vineyard and Nantucket.
