Status Quo (Jerusalem and Bethlehem)

The Status Quo is a historical set of agreements governing the shared religious sites in Jerusalem and Bethlehem — principally the Church of the Holy Sepulchre, the Church of the Nativity, and the Tomb of the Virgin Mary — among the Christian communities that claim rights in them. It freezes the rights, privileges, and practices of each denomination as they stood in the mid-nineteenth century, so that "no change whatsoever" may be made without the consent of all parties.

Origins

Conflicts over precedence and possession at the holy places, particularly between Roman Catholic (Latin) and Greek Orthodox custodians, were a recurring source of friction under Ottoman rule. Sultan Osman III issued a firman in 1757 that divided the sites among the communities; a later firman of Sultan Abdülmecid I in 1852 confirmed the arrangement in detail — specifying which community could clean which step, hang which lamp, and officiate at which hour. Disputes over these rights, and over French and Russian protection of the Catholic and Orthodox communities respectively, contributed to the outbreak of the Crimean War (1853–1856).

International recognition

The Status Quo was confirmed by the Treaty of Paris (1856) and the Treaty of Berlin (1878), administered under the British Mandate, and is still respected by the authorities in Israel and Palestine today. The British Mandate official L. G. A. Cust summarized the arrangement in his 1929 memorandum The Status Quo in the Holy Places, which remains a standard reference.

The immovable ladder

The most famous symbol of the Status Quo is the "immovable ladder," a wooden ladder resting on a ledge beneath a window above the entrance of the Church of the Holy Sepulchre. Because the ledge and window are subject to the agreement, the ladder — depicted in engravings as early as 1728 — has remained in place for centuries, since moving it would require the agreement of all six Christian communities sharing the church.
