Modality (natural language)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In linguistics, modality is the phenomenon by which language expresses the speaker's attitude toward a proposition — whether it is possible, necessary, permitted, obligatory, believed, desired or hypothetical — rather than presenting it as a plain fact. Modality is expressed by modal verbs (such as English can, must, may), adverbs (possibly, certainly), grammatical mood, particles and constructions in the world's languages.

Types of modality

  • Epistemic modality concerns knowledge and belief: "She must be home" (I infer it), "He may be late" (it is possible).
  • Deontic modality concerns permission and obligation: "You may leave", "You must stop".
  • Dynamic modality concerns ability and volition: "She can swim", "He will help".
  • Alethic modality, studied in modal logic, concerns truth itself — necessity and possibility independent of knowledge or rules.

Many modal expressions are polysemous across these categories, a central topic in semantic research. Cross-linguistic studies, such as those drawing on the framework of F. R. Palmer, show recurring patterns — for instance epistemic meanings frequently developing historically from deontic ones.

Realisation across languages

Languages grammaticalise modality differently: English relies heavily on a small class of modal auxiliary verbs; German has a richer set of modal verbs and modal particles; many languages use dedicated affixes, inflections of mood (subjunctive, optative, conditional), or serial constructions. Modality interacts with tense, aspect and negation — for example, English must not (prohibition) contrasts with need not (absence of necessity), a classic scope puzzle in formal semantics.
