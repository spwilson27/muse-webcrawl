French nobility

The French nobility (noblesse française) was a privileged social class in France from the Middle Ages until its legal privileges were abolished during the French Revolution. Along with the clergy (the First Estate), the nobility constituted the Second Estate of the realm; nobles were largely exempt from the taille (the principal direct tax), held exclusive rights such as hunting and carrying a sword, and staffed the upper ranks of the army, church, and judiciary. Estimates of the nobility's size on the eve of the Revolution range from about 110,000 to 300,000 people, roughly 0.5–1.5 percent of the population.

Categories

The nobility was divided between the noblesse d'épée ("nobility of the sword"), old families whose status derived from military service and feudal landholding, and the noblesse de robe ("nobility of the robe"), families ennobled through high judicial and administrative offices, especially the parlements. Nobility could be inherited, granted by royal letters patent, or acquired by purchasing certain venal offices. The upper ranks bore titles — duke, marquis, count, viscount, baron — though title and nobility were legally distinct.

Decline and abolition

Under Louis XIV, the great nobles were drawn to the court at Versailles and domesticated into courtiers, while real power shifted to royal intendants and ministers. On the Night of August 4, 1789, the National Assembly abolished feudal privileges, and in June 1790 hereditary nobility and noble titles were suppressed altogether. Napoleon created a new imperial nobility in 1808; the Bourbon Restoration and the July Monarchy retained titles, but noble legal privilege was never restored. The Third Republic ended official recognition, though titles continue to be borne socially, and today associations such as the ANF (Association d'entraide de la noblesse française) certify the authenticity of noble descent.
