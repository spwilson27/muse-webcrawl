A nebula (Latin for "mist" or "cloud"; pl. nebulae) is an interstellar cloud of dust, hydrogen, helium and other ionised gases. Originally, the term was used to describe any diffuse astronomical object, including galaxies beyond the Milky Way. The Andromeda Galaxy, for instance, was once referred to as the Andromeda Nebula before its true nature was confirmed in the 20th century.

Most nebulae are of vast size; some are hundreds of light-years in diameter. A nebula that is barely visible to the human eye from Earth would be larger than most if brought closer. The Orion Nebula, at about 1,344 light-years away, spans 24 light-years across and is visible to the naked eye as a fuzzy patch in Orion's sword.

Formation and types

Nebulae form from collapsing clouds of interstellar medium, from gas expelled by stars, or from the debris of supernova explosions. Many serve as stellar nurseries where new stars are born. Astronomers classify nebulae by how they shine: emission nebulae glow from ionised gas heated by nearby hot stars; reflection nebulae scatter starlight (usually appearing blue); dark nebulae block background light; planetary nebulae are shells ejected by dying Sun-like stars; and supernova remnants, such as the Crab Nebula, expand from stellar explosions.

Historical observations

The Orion Nebula was catalogued by Messier as M42 and observed by astronomers since the early 17th century. Charles Messier's catalogue of "nebulae" was created to avoid confusion with comets. In the 1920s, Edwin Hubble's identification of Cepheid variables in the Andromeda Nebula established that spiral "nebulae" are separate galaxies.
