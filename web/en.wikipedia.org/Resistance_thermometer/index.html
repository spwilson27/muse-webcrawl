Resistance thermometer

Resistance thermometers, also called resistance temperature detectors (RTDs), are sensors used to measure temperature. Many RTD elements consist of a length of fine wire wrapped around a heat-resistant ceramic or glass core but other constructions are also used. The RTD wire is a pure material, typically platinum, nickel, or copper. The material has an accurate resistance/temperature relationship which is used to provide an indication of temperature. As RTD elements are fragile, they are often housed in protective probes.

RTDs, which have higher accuracy and repeatability, are slowly replacing thermocouples in industrial applications below 600 °C.

Resistance/temperature relationship of metals

The electrical resistance of most metals increases approximately linearly with temperature. For platinum, the relationship is well characterized and highly repeatable, which is why platinum RTDs (such as the common Pt100, with a resistance of 100 ohms at 0 °C) are the most widely used type. The variation of resistance with temperature is described by the Callendar–Van Dusen equation, which extends the simple linear approximation with higher-order terms to cover wide temperature ranges:

R(T) = R0 [1 + A T + B T2 + C (T − 100) T3]

where R0 is the resistance at 0 °C, T is the temperature in degrees Celsius, and A, B, C are coefficients determined by the purity of the platinum. Standard industrial platinum RTDs conform to standards such as IEC 60751, with a temperature coefficient of about 0.00385 per °C.

History

The application of the tendency of electrical conductors to increase their electrical resistance with temperature was first described by William Siemens in 1871; the platinum resistance thermometer was developed further by H. L. Callendar in the 1880s, whose equations for interpolating temperature from resistance measurements were used for decades. Platinum was chosen because it can withstand high temperatures while maintaining good stability, and because it resists oxidation and corrosion.

Construction

Common types of RTD sensing elements include wire-wound elements, in which a coil of fine platinum wire is wound on a ceramic or glass former, and thin-film elements, in which a platinum film is deposited on a ceramic substrate and laser-trimmed to the desired resistance. Thin-film elements are cheaper and smaller but somewhat less stable. The element is usually enclosed in a protective sheath of stainless steel or Inconel, often with mineral insulation.

Wiring configurations

Because the resistance of the connecting leads adds to the measured resistance, RTDs are wired in two-, three-, or four-wire configurations. Two-wire connections are acceptable only for short leads or low accuracy. Three-wire bridges, common in industry, compensate for lead resistance assuming matched leads. Four-wire (Kelvin) connections eliminate lead resistance errors almost entirely and are used for precision measurements.

Advantages and limitations

RTDs offer excellent accuracy, stability, and repeatability over wide ranges (approximately −200 to +850 °C for platinum). Their disadvantages include higher cost than thermocouples, fragility, slower response in some constructions, and self-heating: the measurement current dissipates power in the element and can raise its temperature slightly above that of its surroundings, an effect that must be minimized by using low excitation currents.
