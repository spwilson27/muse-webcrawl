Sermon on the Mount


Jesus delivering the Sermon on the Mount, a traditional depiction.

The Sermon on the Mount (anglicised from the Matthean Vulgate Latin section title: Sermo in monte) is a collection of sayings spoken by Jesus of Nazareth found in the Gospel of Matthew, chapters 5, 6 and 7. It is the longest continuous discourse of Jesus in the New Testament and is generally regarded as encapsulating the central teachings of his ministry. The setting is a mountainside near the Sea of Galilee, traditionally identified with the Mount of Beatitudes.

Contents

The sermon opens with the Beatitudes ("Blessed are the poor in spirit..."), a series of blessings pronounced on the meek, the merciful, the peacemakers and the persecuted. It continues with the metaphors of salt and light; teaching on the Law, in which Jesus internalises the commandments (against murder, adultery, and swearing oaths) in a series of antitheses ("You have heard that it was said... but I say to you..."); instruction on almsgiving, prayer — including the Lord's Prayer — and fasting; the sayings on treasures in heaven, the Golden Rule ("Do to others what you would have them do to you"), the narrow gate, and false prophets; and it closes with the parable of the wise and foolish builders.

Relationship to the Sermon on the Plain

A shorter parallel discourse, the Sermon on the Plain, appears in the Gospel of Luke (chapter 6), sharing much of the same material, including several beatitudes and the Golden Rule. Scholars debate whether both derive from a common source (such as the hypothetical Q source) or whether one evangelist redacted the other tradition.

Interpretation

The sermon's demands — non-retaliation, love of enemies, perfection — have generated many readings: as an attainable programme for disciples; as an "impossible ideal" intended to drive hearers to grace (a Lutheran reading); as interim ethics for an expected imminent end; or, in the Anabaptist and pacifist traditions exemplified by Leo Tolstoy, as a literal charter for Christian nonviolence. Mahatma Gandhi famously admired the sermon and drew on it in developing his philosophy of nonviolent resistance. It remains one of the most widely quoted and influential texts of Christianity.
