Clearing house (finance)

Clearing house
Role: Intermediary between buyers and sellers
Functions: Clearing, settlement, netting, risk management
Examples: LCH, CME Clearing, DTCC, Eurex Clearing

A clearing house is a financial institution that provides clearing and settlement services for financial transactions, acting as an intermediary between buyers and sellers. In derivatives and securities markets, the clearing house typically becomes the central counterparty (CCP): through novation it inserts itself between the two parties to a trade, becoming the buyer to every seller and the seller to every buyer, thereby guaranteeing performance of the contracts even if one party defaults.

Functions

Clearing houses perform netting, offsetting each member's many trades into a single net obligation, which greatly reduces the volume of payments and deliveries. They manage risk by collecting margin (collateral) from members, marking positions to market daily, and maintaining default funds. Clearing covers exchange-traded derivatives, securities, and — increasingly since the 2008 financial crisis — standardized over-the-counter derivatives, which reforms such as the Dodd–Frank Act in the United States and EMIR in Europe required to be centrally cleared.

History

Clearing arrangements date to the 18th- and 19th-century exchanges and to bank clearing houses such as the London Bankers' Clearing House and the New York Clearing House (1853), which settled cheques between banks. Major modern clearing houses include LCH in London, CME Clearing in Chicago, the Depository Trust & Clearing Corporation (DTCC), and Eurex Clearing. Because central counterparties concentrate risk, they are themselves systemically important and subject to stringent regulation and "skin in the game" requirements.
