Draco (lawgiver)

Draco (Ancient Greek: Δράκων, Drákōn; fl. c. 621 BC) was the first recorded legislator of Athens in Ancient Greece. He was commissioned to write down the city's laws, replacing a system of oral law and blood feud with a written legal code. His laws are known as the Draconian constitution.

The code

Draco's code, published around 621 BC, distinguished between intentional and unintentional homicide and established courts to try such cases, transferring the right of vengeance from families to the state. However, the code was notoriously harsh: the death penalty was prescribed for a wide range of offences, including theft of a cabbage or an apple, according to later writers. The severity of the code gave rise to the adjective "draconian", meaning excessively harsh.

Most of Draco's laws were repealed by Solon in the early 6th century BC, but his homicide law remained in force, with modifications, for centuries.

Legacy

Draco's code was significant not because of its severity but because it was written down and publicly displayed, making the law knowable to all citizens rather than the exclusive knowledge of aristocratic judges. This principle — that laws should be public and applied equally — became a foundation of later Athenian democracy.
