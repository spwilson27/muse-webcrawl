Squaring the circle

Squaring the circle is a problem in geometry first proposed in Greek mathematics. It is the challenge of constructing a square with the area of a given circle by using only a finite number of steps with a compass and straightedge. In 1882, the task was proven impossible, as a consequence of the Lindemann–Weierstrass theorem, which shows that π is a transcendental number.

History

Greek geometers such as Anaxagoras, Hippocrates of Chios and Antiphon attempted the problem in the 5th century BCE. Because compass-and-straightedge constructions can only produce algebraic numbers constructed from rational operations and square roots, the construction would require π to be algebraic. Ferdinand von Lindemann proved in 1882 that π is transcendental, settling the question after more than two thousand years.

Idiom

The phrase "squaring the circle" has entered common usage as a metaphor for attempting an impossible or hopeless task. Approximate constructions, which can achieve any desired practical accuracy, have also been known since antiquity, including an Egyptian method recorded in the Rhind papyrus.
