Analytical Engine

DesignerCharles Babbage
Designed1837 (from)
TypeMechanical general-purpose computer
InputPunched cards
Memory"Store": 1,000 numbers of 50 decimal digits

The Analytical Engine was a proposed mechanical general-purpose computer designed by the English mathematician and inventor Charles Babbage. It was first described in 1837 as the successor to his difference engine, a design for a simpler mechanical calculator. The Analytical Engine incorporated an arithmetic logic unit (the "mill"), control flow in the form of conditional branching and loops, and integrated memory (the "store"), making it the first design for a general-purpose, Turing-complete computer.

Design

Programs were to be supplied on punched cards, borrowed from the Jacquard loom. The store was designed to hold 1,000 numbers of 50 decimal digits each, and the mill would perform arithmetic on numbers fetched from the store, returning results to it. Output devices included a printer, a curve plotter, and a bell.

Ada Lovelace

In 1842–43 Ada Lovelace translated and expanded a memoir on the engine by Luigi Menabrea, adding notes three times longer than the original. Her Note G describes an algorithm for computing Bernoulli numbers on the engine — widely considered the first computer program ever published — and she famously foresaw computers manipulating symbols and music, not just numbers. See analog computer for the contrasting continuous approach to computation.

Construction

Babbage refined the design for the rest of his life but none of his engines was completed in his lifetime, owing to cost, manufacturing limits, and disputes with his engineer. In 1991 the Science Museum, London, built his Difference Engine No. 2 to period tolerances, proving the designs workable.
