Ediacaran biota

The Ediacaran biota is the assemblage of largely soft-bodied, mostly sessile organisms that inhabited Earth's oceans during the Ediacaran Period (c. 635–538.8 million years ago), the final period of the Proterozoic Eon. They represent some of the earliest known complex multicellular life and appear in the fossil record shortly after the Marinoan glaciation ended.

Discovery

The first Ediacaran fossil, Aspidella, was found in Newfoundland in 1868 but was long dismissed as inorganic. Systematic study began in the 1940s–50s, when Reg Sprigg described fossils from the Ediacara Hills of South Australia and Martin Glaessner recognised their Precambrian age — overturning the assumption that complex life began in the Cambrian.

Assemblages

Palaeontologists recognise three main assemblages: the Avalon assemblage (deep-water frond-like rangeomorphs, c. 571–560 Ma), the White Sea assemblage (the most diverse, including the mobile Dickinsonia and the mollusc-like Kimberella), and the Nama assemblage, which includes early calcifying animals such as Cloudina.

Interpretation

The affinities of many Ediacaran organisms remain debated. Some, like Kimberella and Dickinsonia (confirmed as an animal by cholesterol biomarkers in 2018), are considered early animals; others may represent extinct lineages of multicellular life — Adolf Seilacher proposed grouping the quilted forms as "Vendobionta". The biota largely disappears at the Ediacaran–Cambrian boundary, amid the rise of burrowing animals and the Cambrian explosion.
