Reciprocity law

Gauss called quadratic reciprocity the "golden theorem" and published eight proofs of it.

In number theory, a reciprocity law is a theorem that relates the solvability of a Diophantine equation modulo one prime to its solvability modulo another prime. The prototype is the law of quadratic reciprocity, conjectured by Euler and Legendre and first proved by Carl Friedrich Gauss in 1801, which connects the Legendre symbols (p/q) and (q/p) for distinct odd primes p and q.

Quadratic reciprocity

The law states that (p/q)(q/p) = (−1)^((p−1)(q−1)/4), together with supplementary laws for the symbols (−1/p) and (2/p). In words, except when both primes are congruent to 3 modulo 4 — in which case exactly one of the two symbols is −1 — p is a quadratic residue modulo q if and only if q is a quadratic residue modulo p. Gauss regarded the theorem so highly that he returned to it throughout his life, publishing eight distinct proofs; more than two hundred proofs are now known.

Higher reciprocity laws

Nineteenth-century mathematicians sought analogues for higher powers. Gotthold Eisenstein and Ernst Kummer developed cubic and biquadratic reciprocity laws in the rings of Eisenstein integers and Gaussian integers. Generalising these results motivated Kummer's theory of ideal numbers and, ultimately, class field theory, in which reciprocity laws are subsumed by the Artin reciprocity law of Emil Artin (1927), relating Galois groups of abelian extensions to arithmetic data of the base field.

Modern formulation

In modern language, reciprocity laws govern the splitting of primes in abelian extensions and connect local and global fields through the Artin map. They form a central part of the Langlands program, which generalises reciprocity to non-abelian extensions via automorphic forms. The importance of these laws is such that David Hilbert listed the search for the most general reciprocity law among his famous problems of 1900 (the ninth problem), a quest answered in large part by class field theory.
