Stein's example

Shrinkage estimation: pulling individual estimates toward a common centre can reduce total error.

Stein's example (or Stein's phenomenon) is a result in decision theory and statistics showing that, when three or more parameters are estimated simultaneously, there exist combined estimators that are more accurate in expectation than any method that treats each parameter separately. The result was discovered by Charles Stein in 1956 and developed by Stein and Willard James in the James–Stein estimator of 1961.

Statement

Suppose observations are drawn from a multivariate normal distribution with unknown mean vector and known variance. The natural estimate of the mean is the observation itself, and in one or two dimensions this estimator is admissible: it cannot be uniformly improved. Stein showed that in three or more dimensions it is inadmissible — an estimator that shrinks each observation toward a chosen target (such as zero or the grand mean) has strictly smaller expected squared error, whatever the true mean.

Intuition

The improvement arises because the coordinates are judged jointly: extreme values are probably extreme partly by chance, so shrinking them toward the centre reduces total error. The surprising consequence is that information from unrelated quantities can improve the estimate of each — estimating baseball players' batting averages together, for example, beats estimating each player's average from his own record alone.

Applications

Stein's example underlies modern shrinkage and hierarchical modelling, including empirical Bayes methods, ridge-type regularisation, and the batting-average analyses popularised by Bradley Efron and Carl Morris. It remains a standard illustration that admissibility intuitions from one dimension fail in higher dimensions.
