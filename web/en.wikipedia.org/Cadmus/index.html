Cadmus

Cadmus (Greek: Κάδμος, Kadmos) was a Phoenician prince in Greek mythology, the son of King Agenor of Tyre and Queen Telephassa, and the brother of Europa. He is credited with founding the city of Thebes in Boeotia and with introducing the Phoenician alphabet to Greece.

The search for Europa

After Zeus, in the form of a bull, carried off Europa, Agenor sent his sons to search for her, forbidding them to return without her. Cadmus wandered widely, accompanied by his mother Telephassa, who died in Thrace. Unable to find his sister, Cadmus consulted the oracle at Delphi, which told him to abandon the search, follow a cow, and found a city where the animal lay down.

Founding of Thebes

Cadmus followed a cow through Phocis into Boeotia, where it sank to the ground; there he founded the city that would become Thebes, first building its citadel, the Cadmeia. Intending to sacrifice the cow to Athena, he sent companions to fetch water from a nearby spring guarded by a dragon, offspring of Ares, which killed them. Cadmus slew the dragon and, on Athena's advice, sowed its teeth in the earth. From the teeth sprang armed men, the Spartoi ("sown men"), who fought one another until only five survived; these five became the ancestors of the noble families of Thebes. To atone for killing Ares' dragon, Cadmus served the god for eight years.

Marriage and descendants

Cadmus married Harmonia, daughter of Ares and Aphrodite, in a wedding attended by the gods. Their children included Semele (mother of Dionysus), Ino, Autonoë, Agave, and Polydorus. Late in life Cadmus and Harmonia left Thebes for Illyria, where, according to legend, they were transformed into serpents. The phrase "Cadmean victory" entered Greek usage for a victory won at ruinous cost to the victor.

The alphabet

Ancient sources, including Herodotus, credited Cadmus and the Phoenicians who came with him with bringing letters (phoinikeia grammata) to Greece — the basis of the Greek alphabet.
