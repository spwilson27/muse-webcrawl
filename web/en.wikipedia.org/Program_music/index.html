Program music

Program music or programme music is a type of instrumental art music that attempts to render an extra-musical narrative musically. The narrative itself might be offered to the audience through the piece's title, or in the concert program in the form of introductory notes. The term is almost exclusively applied to works in the Romantic tradition, particularly those of the 19th century; it contrasts with absolute music, which is conceived as "purely musical" and not about anything outside the music itself.

History

Although descriptive music existed earlier — for example in Vivaldi's The Four Seasons and in Beethoven's Pastoral Symphony — program music flourished in the Romantic era. Hector Berlioz's Symphonie fantastique (1830), accompanied by a detailed written program, became a landmark. Franz Liszt developed the one-movement symphonic poem, and composers such as Richard Strauss (with Don Juan, Also sprach Zarathustra, and Ein Heldenleben) and the Russian nationalists pushed musical depiction to vivid extremes.

Debate

The value and legitimacy of program music was a central topic of 19th-century musical aesthetics. Critic Eduard Hanslick argued that music cannot represent definite ideas or narratives, championing absolute music, while Liszt, Wagner, and the "New German School" defended programmatic composition. The dispute formed part of the so-called "War of the Romantics".

Examples

  • Berlioz — Symphonie fantastique
  • Smetana — Má vlast
  • Mussorgsky — Pictures at an Exhibition
  • Richard Strauss — Till Eulenspiegel's Merry Pranks
  • Debussy — La mer
