Claudio Monteverdi

Claudio Giovanni Antonio Monteverdi (baptised 15 May 1567 – 29 November 1643) was an Italian composer, choirmaster and string player. A composer of both secular and sacred music, and a pioneer in the development of opera, he is considered a crucial transitional figure between the Renaissance and Baroque periods of music history.

Life

Born in Cremona, Monteverdi studied with Marc'Antonio Ingegneri and published his first book of madrigals as a teenager. In about 1590 he entered the service of Vincenzo I Gonzaga, Duke of Mantua, first as a viol player and singer and eventually as maestro di cappella in 1601. After the Duke's death he was dismissed in 1612, and in 1613 became maestro di cappella at St Mark's Basilica in Venice, where he remained for the rest of his life, revitalising the basilica's music. He was ordained a priest in 1632 and died in Venice in 1643.

Music

Monteverdi's nine books of madrigals chart the transition from Renaissance polyphony to the expressive Baroque style of the seconda pratica, in which the music serves the text. His opera L'Orfeo (1607), to a libretto by Alessandro Striggio, is the earliest opera still regularly performed. Of his other stage works, Il ritorno d'Ulisse in patria (1640) and L'incoronazione di Poppea (1643) survive, while the music of L'Arianna (1608) is mostly lost apart from its famous lament. His sacred works include the Vespro della Beata Vergine (1610) and the posthumous Selva morale e spirituale (1641).

Legacy

Neglected after his death, Monteverdi's music was revived in the late 19th and 20th centuries; today he is regarded as one of the most important composers in the Western tradition, and his operas anchor the early Baroque repertoire.
