The Brontës were a nineteenth-century literary family associated with the village of Haworth in the West Riding of Yorkshire, England. The best known members are the three sisters Charlotte (1816–1855), Emily (1818–1848) and Anne (1820–1849), who are famous as poets and novelists. They originally published under the pen names Currer, Ellis and Acton Bell.

Background

Their father, Patrick Brontë (1777–1861), was an Irish-born Anglican clergyman who became perpetual curate of Haworth. Their mother, Maria Branwell (1783–1821), died of cancer when the children were young, and the children were raised largely by their aunt Elizabeth Branwell. Of six children — Maria, Elizabeth, Charlotte, Patrick Branwell, Emily and Anne — the two eldest died in childhood following an outbreak of tuberculosis at the Clergy Daughters' School at Cowan Bridge, an experience Charlotte later drew upon in Jane Eyre.

Literary work

The surviving children created elaborate imaginary worlds — Angria and Gondal — in miniature handwritten books. In 1846 the sisters published a volume of poems at their own expense under male pseudonyms. In 1847 Charlotte's Jane Eyre and Emily's Wuthering Heights were published, followed by Anne's Agnes Grey and, in 1848, The Tenant of Wildfell Hall.

Deaths and legacy

Branwell, a painter who had struggled with alcohol and opium, died in September 1848; Emily died of tuberculosis that December, and Anne in May 1849. Charlotte, the last surviving sibling, married her father's curate, Arthur Bell Nicholls, in 1854 and died in 1855. The Brontë Parsonage Museum in Haworth preserves the family's home and artefacts, and the sisters' works are regarded as classics of English literature.
