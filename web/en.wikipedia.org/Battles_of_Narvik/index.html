The Battles of Narvik were fought from 9 April to 8 June 1940 as a naval battle in Ofotfjord and as a land battle in the mountains surrounding the town of Narvik in northern Norway, during the Norwegian campaign of the Second World War. The fight for the strategic iron-ore port saw the destruction of ten German destroyers — half the Kriegsmarine's destroyer force — and the first Allied land victory of the war, before the Allied evacuation forced by the collapse of France.

Background

Narvik was the ice-free port from which Swedish iron ore from Kiruna reached Germany in winter. On 9 April 1940, as part of Operation Weserübung, ten German destroyers under Commodore Friedrich Bonte carried General Eduard Dietl's 2,000 mountain troops into Ofotfjord and seized Narvik, quickly sinking the elderly Norwegian coastal defence ships Eidsvold and Norge.

Naval battles

On 10 April, five British destroyers of the 2nd Destroyer Flotilla under Captain Bernard Warburton-Lee made a surprise dawn attack in a snowstorm, sinking two German destroyers and several supply ships, but were ambushed on the way out: Warburton-Lee's flagship Hardy and the destroyer Hunter were lost, and Warburton-Lee was killed, receiving a posthumous Victoria Cross. Three days later, on 13 April, a British force built around the battleship HMS Warspite and nine destroyers returned and destroyed the remaining eight German destroyers in the Second Battle of Narvik. Some 2,600 German sailors joined Dietl's troops ashore as improvised infantry.

Land battle and evacuation

An Allied expeditionary force of British, French (including Foreign Legion and Chasseurs Alpins), Polish and Norwegian troops, some 24,500 men, gradually pressed Dietl's outnumbered force back toward the Swedish border. Supported by naval gunfire, French and Norwegian troops captured Narvik on 28 May 1940 — the first major German land defeat of the war. But the Allied position in France was collapsing; under Operation Alphabet the Allies evacuated Narvik by 8 June, demolishing the port facilities, and Dietl reoccupied the town. The evacuation convoys suffered further loss when the aircraft carrier HMS Glorious was sunk by Scharnhorst and Gneisenau on 8 June.

Legacy

The battles cost the Kriegsmarine ten destroyers and numerous other vessels, losses that hampered later operations, while France, Poland and Norway remember the campaign in cemeteries and memorials around Narvik and the Narvik War Museum.
