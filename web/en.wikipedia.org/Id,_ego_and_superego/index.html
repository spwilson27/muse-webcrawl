Id, ego and superego

In psychoanalytic theory, the id, ego and superego are the three agencies of the human personality according to the structural model developed by Sigmund Freud in his essay Beyond the Pleasure Principle (1920) and fully set out in The Ego and the Id (1923). The three agencies are not physical structures but theoretical constructs describing the functions and conflicts of the mind.

The id

The id (German: das Es, "the It") is the wholly unconscious reservoir of instinctual drives and libido, present from birth. It operates on the pleasure principle, demanding immediate gratification of hunger, thirst, sex and aggression without regard for reality or morality. Freud described it as "a chaos, a cauldron full of seething excitations".

The ego

The ego (German: das Ich, "the I") develops out of the id under the pressure of the external world. It operates on the reality principle, mediating between the id's demands, the superego's prohibitions and the constraints of reality. Partly conscious and partly unconscious, the ego employs defence mechanisms — such as repression, denial and rationalisation — to manage anxiety arising from these conflicts.

The superego

The superego (German: das Über-Ich, "the Over-I") forms last, through the internalisation of parental and social values, typically via the resolution of the Oedipus complex. It comprises the conscience, which punishes transgression with guilt, and the ego ideal, which rewards conformity with pride. It strives for perfection rather than pleasure or reality.

Influence and criticism

The structural model became central to psychoanalytic practice and passed into everyday language, where "ego" in particular is used loosely for self-importance. Freud's daughter Anna Freud systematised the ego's defence mechanisms. Within psychology the model has been criticised as unfalsifiable and lacking empirical support, though its concepts influenced ego psychology, literary criticism and popular culture.
