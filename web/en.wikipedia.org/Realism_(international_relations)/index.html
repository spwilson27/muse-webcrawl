Realism (international relations)

Realism, also known as political realism, is a view of international politics that stresses its competitive and conflictual side. It is the dominant theoretical paradigm in international relations. Realists assume that states are the principal actors, that the international system is anarchic — lacking any authority above the state — and that states therefore pursue power and security in a self-help system.

Assumptions and varieties

Core assumptions include: states are unitary, rational actors; survival is their primary goal; military capability and the distribution of power are the key variables. Classical realism, exemplified by Hans Morgenthau's Politics Among Nations (1948), roots conflict in human nature. Neorealism or structural realism, founded by Kenneth Waltz's Theory of International Politics (1979), derives state behaviour from the anarchic structure and distribution of capabilities. Within neorealism, defensive realists (Waltz) argue states seek an appropriate amount of power, while offensive realists (John Mearsheimer) argue states maximise power and seek hegemony.

Intellectual lineage and policy

Realists claim antecedents in Thucydides' account of the Peloponnesian War, Machiavelli and Hobbes. Realist concepts include the balance of power, the security dilemma and relative gains. Realism has shaped diplomacy and statecraft, from Bismarck to Kissinger, and stands in debate with liberalism (emphasising institutions and interdependence) and constructivism (emphasising ideas and identities).

Criticisms

Critics charge realism with underestimating economic interdependence, international institutions, democratic peace, transnational actors and the possibility of peaceful change, and note its difficulty explaining the end of the Cold War.
