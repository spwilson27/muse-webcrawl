Assistance dog

An assistance dog, known as a service dog in North America, is a dog trained to perform specific tasks that mitigate an individual handler's disability. Assistance dogs are distinguished from emotional support animals and therapy dogs by their task training and, in many jurisdictions, by legal rights of access to public places. Common types include guide dogs for blind people, hearing dogs for deaf people, mobility assistance dogs, medical alert and response dogs (for example for seizures or diabetic hypoglycaemia), and psychiatric service dogs.

Training

Assistance dogs undergo extensive socialization and obedience training followed by specialized task training, which can take one to two years. Typical tasks include guiding around obstacles, retrieving dropped items, opening doors, alerting to sounds such as alarms and doorbells, providing balance support, and interrupting repetitive behaviours. Commonly used breeds include Labrador Retrievers, Golden Retrievers and Standard Poodles, selected for temperament, health and trainability. Training may be provided by charities and accredited schools — many members of Assistance Dogs International — or by the handler with professional support.

Legal status

Laws differ by country. In the United States, the Americans with Disabilities Act grants public access to service dogs individually trained to do work or perform tasks for a person with a disability; no certification or registry is required. In the United Kingdom, assistance dogs trained by member charities of Assistance Dogs UK are recognized under the Equality Act 2010. Access disputes, fraudulent service-dog claims, and the welfare of working dogs are ongoing policy issues.
