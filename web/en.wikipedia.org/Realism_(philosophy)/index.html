Realism (philosophy)

Realism holds that entities of a given kind exist independently of our perceptions and theories about them.

Realism, in philosophy, is the viewpoint which accords to things which are known or perceived an existence or nature which is independent of whether anyone is thinking about or perceiving them. It may be contrasted with anti-realism, the view that the existence of certain objects depends on minds or subjective experience, and with idealism, which holds that reality is fundamentally mental in nature.

Realist positions are found in many domains of philosophy. Metaphysical realism concerns the existence of the external world; moral realism holds that there are objective moral facts; scientific realism holds that the theoretical entities posited by mature sciences genuinely exist and that scientific theories are approximately true; and mathematical realism, in its Platonist form, holds that abstract objects such as numbers exist independently of human thought.

History

The roots of philosophical realism lie in ancient Greek philosophy. In medieval philosophy, the problem of universals set realists, who held that universals such as redness or humanity exist in some sense independently of particular things, against nominalists, who held that only particulars exist and universals are mere names. The scholastic realists drew on Plato and Aristotle, while figures such as William of Ockham championed nominalism.

In the modern period, the debate shifted toward the status of the external world. Direct or naïve realism holds that perception gives us immediate access to mind-independent objects, while indirect realism, associated with John Locke and René Descartes, holds that we perceive the world by way of sense data or ideas that represent it. Immanuel Kant combined empirical realism about appearances with transcendental idealism about things in themselves.

Forms of realism

Scientific realism is the view that the universe described by science is real regardless of how it may be interpreted. Scientific realists argue that the predictive success of science would be miraculous if its theories were not approximately true — an argument associated with Hilary Putnam. Opponents point to the pessimistic meta-induction: many once-successful theories turned out to be false.

Moral realism holds that ethical sentences express propositions, some of which are true in virtue of objective features of the world. Aesthetic realism applies the same structure to judgements of beauty. In each domain, realism faces a characteristic challenge: explaining how the entities in question could be both independent of us and knowable by us.

Criticism

Anti-realists such as Michael Dummett argued that a speaker's understanding of a sentence must be manifestable in their use of it, which tells against realist accounts of meaning for statements that transcend all possible evidence. Constructive empiricism, associated with Bas van Fraassen, urges that science aims at empirical adequacy rather than truth about unobservables.
