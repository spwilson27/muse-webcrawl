The Romance languages are the modern languages that evolved from Vulgar Latin, the everyday spoken Latin of the Roman Empire. About 45 Romance languages and dialects survive today, spoken natively by roughly 800 million people in Europe, the Americas, Africa and Asia. (The name refers to Rome, not to "romantic music" — see Romantic music, a separate topic.)

Origins and spread

As Roman soldiers, settlers and administrators spread Latin across the empire — the same expansion carried out by the Roman legion — local speech diverged. After the fall of the Western Empire, regional varieties of Latin developed in isolation into distinct languages, first clearly attested in documents such as the Strasbourg Oaths (AD 842, early French) and the Placiti Capuani (AD 960, early Italian). Through later European colonisation from the 15th century onward, Spanish and Portuguese became dominant across the Americas, and French across parts of Africa and the Caribbean.

Classification

Linguists commonly divide the family into Western Romance (Spanish, Portuguese, Catalan, French, Occitan, and the Rhaeto-Romance languages) and Eastern Romance (Italian and Romanian, with the extinct Dalmatian), with Sardinian — famously conservative — often classed separately. The languages share much core vocabulary, a two-gender system (Romanian has three), loss of Latin's case system except in Romanian, and the development of definite articles from Latin demonstratives.

Major languages today

Spanish is the largest Romance language by native speakers (about 480 million), followed by Portuguese, French, Italian and Romanian. All five are official languages of the European Union except in number — four of them are — and French, Spanish and Portuguese are working languages of international organisations worldwide. Their medieval prestige forms also shaped regional revivals of older building styles, including the Romanesque Revival in architecture.
