The Westminster Assembly of Divines was a council of divines (theologians) and members of the English Parliament appointed from 1643 to 1649 to restructure the Church of England during the English Civil War. It was summoned by the Long Parliament to reform the English church along Reformed lines, and its work produced the Westminster Standards — documents that remain the doctrinal foundation of Presbyterian churches worldwide.

Background

Parliament's quarrel with Charles I over the government and worship of the Church of England was a principal cause of the Civil War. Having allied with the Scots under the Solemn League and Covenant (1643), Parliament resolved to replace episcopacy with a Presbyterian settlement. The Assembly first met on 1 July 1643 in the Henry VII Chapel of Westminster Abbey; it later moved to the Jerusalem Chamber, sitting for 1,163 sessions over five and a half years.

Composition

Parliament named 121 divines and 30 lay assessors (20 from the Commons and 10 from the Lords), though attendance varied and the lay members rarely participated in theological debate. Most divines favoured Presbyterian church government; a small but influential "Independents" faction (the "dissenting brethren", including Thomas Goodwin and Jeremiah Burroughs) argued for congregational autonomy, while a handful of Erastians held that the church should be governed by the civil magistrate. Six Scottish commissioners, among them Alexander Henderson, George Gillespie and Samuel Rutherford, attended without a vote but exercised great influence.

Work

The Assembly first revised the Thirty-nine Articles, then turned to new documents at Parliament's direction: the Directory for Public Worship (1645), which replaced the Book of Common Prayer; the Westminster Confession of Faith (completed December 1646), a systematic statement of Calvinist doctrine; a plan of Presbyterian church government; and the Larger Catechism and Shorter Catechism (1647–48). The Church of Scotland adopted the Confession (with minor changes) in 1647, and the standards became foundational for Presbyterianism in Scotland, Ireland, North America and beyond.

Legacy

Although the Restoration of Charles II in 1660 reversed the Assembly's settlement in England, its documents — especially the Shorter Catechism, famous for its opening question, "What is the chief end of man?" — have shaped Reformed and Presbyterian theology for nearly four centuries.
