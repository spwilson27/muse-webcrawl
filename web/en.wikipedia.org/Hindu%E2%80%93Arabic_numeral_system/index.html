The Hindu–Arabic numeral system, also called the Hindu numeral system or Arabic numeral system, is a positional decimal numeral system for representing numbers. It uses ten digits (0–9) and is the most common system for the symbolic representation of numbers in the world today. The system was invented in India, adopted by Persian and Arab mathematicians in the Islamic world, and transmitted to Europe during the Middle Ages.

History

Positional notation with a symbol for zero developed in India; the mathematician Brahmagupta gave rules for computing with zero in the 7th century. The system spread westward through works such as al-Khwarizmi's On the Calculation with Hindu Numerals (c. 825), and was popularised in Europe by Leonardo Fibonacci's Liber Abaci (1202). By the 15th century it had largely displaced Roman numerals in Western commerce and science.

Positional notation

The value of a digit depends on its position: each place represents a power of ten. Combined with a genuine zero, this allows compact representation of arbitrarily large numbers and straightforward algorithms for arithmetic, advantages that drove the system's adoption over additive systems.

Variants

Several regional variants exist, including the Eastern Arabic numerals used in much of the Middle East and the many Indic scripts' digit forms used across South and Southeast Asia.
