Middle Passage

The Middle Passage was the stage of the Atlantic triangular trade in which millions of enslaved Africans were transported across the Atlantic Ocean to the Americas. It was the middle leg of the three-part voyage: ships departed Europe with manufactured goods, exchanged them for captives on the African coast, carried the enslaved across the Atlantic, and returned to Europe with plantation produce. Between the 16th and 19th centuries, an estimated 12.5 million Africans were embarked on slave ships; about 10.7 million survived the crossing.

Conditions on board

Captives were packed below decks in spaces often less than a metre high, chained in pairs, and given minimal food and water. Disease — especially dysentery — spread rapidly in the unsanitary holds, and mortality averaged roughly 10–20 per cent over the history of the trade, varying with voyage length and conditions. Crews forced captives on deck for exercise ("dancing the slaves") and used violence to suppress resistance.

Resistance

Shipboard revolts were a constant risk; historians estimate insurrections occurred on perhaps one in ten voyages. The most famous include the revolt aboard La Amistad in 1839, in which Mende captives seized the ship and were ultimately freed by a United States Supreme Court decision, and the 1763 revolt on the Dutch ship that survivors used to reach freedom in Suriname. Hunger strikes and suicide by jumping overboard were also forms of resistance.

Abolition

Britain and the United States outlawed the transatlantic slave trade in 1807–1808, and British naval patrols (the West Africa Squadron) intercepted slavers for decades. Illegal trading continued, particularly to Brazil and Cuba, until the 1860s; the last known slave ship to reach the United States, the Clotilda, arrived in 1860.

Legacy

The Middle Passage is central to the historical memory of the African diaspora. It has been commemorated in literature, including narratives by survivors such as Olaudah Equiano, whose 1789 autobiography described the crossing, and in memorials such as the Door of No Return on Gorée Island, Senegal.
