Supersonic speed is the speed of an object that exceeds the speed of sound (Mach 1). At sea level in dry air at 20 °C, the speed of sound is about 343 metres per second (1,235 km/h; 767 mph), though it varies with temperature and altitude. Speeds greater than five times the speed of sound (Mach 5) are generally termed hypersonic.

Supersonic flight

As an aircraft approaches Mach 1 it enters the transonic regime, where shock waves form over parts of the airframe and drag rises sharply — the so-called "sound barrier". On 14 October 1947, Chuck Yeager flew the rocket-powered Bell X-1 to Mach 1.06, the first controlled supersonic flight. Military jets routinely operate supersonically; the only supersonic airliners to enter service were Concorde and the Tupolev Tu-144.

Sonic boom

A supersonic object trails a conical shock wave — a Mach cone — whose passage over the ground is heard as a sonic boom, a sharp double crack caused by the bow and tail shocks. The intensity of the boom led to restrictions on overland supersonic flight by civil aircraft in many countries.

Other examples

Many bullets, the tip of a bullwhip, and the exhaust of rockets travel supersonically. The crack of a whip is a miniature sonic boom — the first man-made device to break the sound barrier. Objects re-entering the atmosphere from space do so at hypersonic speeds many times Mach 5.
