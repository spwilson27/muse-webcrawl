Missile Defense Agency

The Missile Defense Agency (MDA) is an agency of the United States Department of Defense responsible for developing a comprehensive defense against ballistic missiles. It originated in the Strategic Defense Initiative (SDI) announced by President Ronald Reagan in 1983 and was reorganized from the Ballistic Missile Defense Organization in 2002, when it received its current name under President George W. Bush.

History

The agency traces its history to the Strategic Defense Initiative Organization (SDIO), created in 1984 to manage the research program popularly known as "Star Wars." In 1993, under President Bill Clinton, SDIO was renamed the Ballistic Missile Defense Organization (BMDO) and its emphasis shifted from a space-based shield against a massive Soviet attack toward theater missile defense and protection against limited strikes by rogue states. In January 2002 Secretary of Defense Donald Rumsfeld renamed it the Missile Defense Agency and elevated its status, granting it special acquisition authorities.

Mission and programs

The MDA develops, tests, and fields the Ballistic Missile Defense System (BMDS), a layered architecture intended to intercept ballistic missiles in all phases of flight — boost, midcourse, and terminal. Its major programs include:

  • Ground-based Midcourse Defense (GMD), with Ground-Based Interceptors based at Fort Greely, Alaska, and Vandenberg Space Force Base, California.
  • Aegis Ballistic Missile Defense, using ship-based and shore-based (Aegis Ashore) SM-3 and SM-6 interceptors.
  • THAAD (Terminal High Altitude Area Defense) batteries for terminal-phase interception.
  • Patriot (PAC-3) systems for point defense against shorter-range threats.
  • Sensor networks including the Sea-Based X-Band Radar, AN/TPY-2 radars, and upgraded early-warning radars, along with space-based tracking programs.

Testing and criticism

The agency conducts regular flight tests of its systems. Critics have questioned the realism of test conditions and the system's effectiveness against sophisticated countermeasures such as decoys, while supporters argue the capability deters adversaries and provides insurance against limited attacks from states such as North Korea and Iran.
