Programmed cell death

Programmed cell death
TypesApoptosis, autophagy-associated death, necroptosis, pyroptosis, ferroptosis
Key mediatorsCaspases, Bcl-2 family, death receptors, gasdermins
RoleDevelopment, tissue homeostasis, immunity, removal of damaged cells
ContrastNecrosis (uncontrolled, injury-related death)

Programmed cell death (PCD) is the death of a cell as a result of events inside the cell, such as apoptosis or autophagy, carried out in a regulated biological process that confers advantages during an organism's life cycle. Billions of cells die by programmed pathways in the human body each day, balancing cell proliferation to maintain tissue homeostasis, sculpting organs during development, and eliminating infected, damaged or potentially cancerous cells.

Forms

Apoptosis is the best-studied form: the cell shrinks, its chromatin condenses, the membrane blebs, and the cell breaks into membrane-bound apoptotic bodies that are engulfed by neighbours or macrophages without provoking inflammation. It is executed by proteases called caspases, activated either through the intrinsic (mitochondrial) pathway — controlled by the Bcl-2 protein family and triggered by cell stress or DNA damage, often via p53 — or the extrinsic pathway through cell-surface death receptors such as Fas. Other regulated forms include necroptosis and pyroptosis, which are inflammatory and help fight infection; ferroptosis, driven by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation; and autophagy-dependent cell death.

Biological significance

Programmed death shapes the developing embryo — separating the fingers and toes, pruning excess neurons, and hollowing out tubes and cavities — and maintains the adult body, for example by removing neutrophils after infection and self-reactive lymphocytes in the immune system. It is evolutionarily ancient: apoptosis-like processes occur in plants, fungi and even bacteria.

Disorders

Too little programmed death contributes to cancer and autoimmune disease (for example, mutations in Fas cause autoimmune lymphoproliferative syndrome); too much contributes to neurodegenerative diseases, immunodeficiency and tissue injury after stroke or heart attack. Many chemotherapy drugs work by triggering apoptosis, and targeted drugs such as the Bcl-2 inhibitor venetoclax deliberately restore it in cancer cells.
