Motion capture

Motion capture (sometimes abbreviated mo-cap or mocap) is the process of recording the movement of objects or people and using that information to animate digital character models in 2D or 3D computer animation. When it includes face and fingers or captures subtle expressions, it is often referred to as performance capture. In films and video games, motion capture records the actions of human actors, whose movements are then applied to digital characters.

Method

In a typical optical system, a performer wears a suit fitted with reflective markers at the joints. An array of infrared cameras surrounding the capture volume tracks the markers' positions many times per second; software triangulates their three-dimensional locations and reconstructs a skeletal motion, which is cleaned and retargeted onto a character rig. Other systems use inertial sensors (IMUs), magnetic sensors, or markerless tracking based on computer vision and machine learning, which can work outside a studio volume.

Applications

Motion capture is widely used in entertainment: films such as the The Lord of the Rings trilogy (Andy Serkis's portrayal of Gollum), Avatar and the modern Planet of the Apes series relied on performance capture, as do most major video games for character animation. Beyond entertainment it is used in biomechanics and sports science to analyse gait and athletic movement, in medicine for clinical gait analysis, in robotics, and in military training and simulation.

History

Precursors include the chronophotography of Eadweard Muybridge and Étienne-Jules Marey in the nineteenth century and the rotoscoping technique of Max Fleischer, in which animators traced filmed actors. Photogrammetric research in the 1970s and 1980s, notably at Simon Fraser University and the MIT Media Lab, established modern optical tracking. Commercial systems became practical in the 1990s, and the success of Gollum in The Two Towers (2002) established performance capture as a central technique in film-making.

Advantages and limitations

Motion capture produces realistic, nuanced movement quickly and records complex interactions between actors and props. Limitations include the cost of studio systems, difficulty capturing loose clothing, water or close contact between performers, occlusion of markers, and the need for post-processing and animator cleanup. Ethical and credit questions about the respective contributions of the captured performer and the animators remain a subject of debate.
