Symbolic capital

Symbolic capital is a concept developed by the French sociologist Pierre Bourdieu for the resources available to an individual or group on the basis of honour, prestige, recognition, and reputation. It is one of the forms of capital in Bourdieu's theory, alongside economic capital, cultural capital, and social capital.

In Bourdieu's theory

For Bourdieu, capital is accumulated labour that can be appropriated and converted between forms. Symbolic capital arises when another form of capital — wealth, knowledge, networks — is perceived and recognised as legitimate rather than arbitrary. A large fortune becomes symbolic capital when it is recognised as tasteful philanthropy; academic credentials become symbolic capital when they confer authority. Crucially, symbolic capital works through misrecognition: it is effective precisely because it is not seen as power or self-interest, which is why Bourdieu calls the gentle, disguised exercise of power symbolic violence.

Examples and use

Institutions such as the state, universities, and churches act as “banks” of symbolic capital, issuing titles, honours, and credentials. Political leaders, celebrities, and professionals all draw on accumulated prestige. The concept has been widely applied in sociology, anthropology, education research, and media studies, though critics note that it can be difficult to measure empirically.
