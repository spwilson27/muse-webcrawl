Park ranger

A park ranger is a person entrusted with protecting and preserving parklands — national, state, provincial, county or local parks. Rangers' duties vary widely between jurisdictions but commonly combine resource protection, law enforcement, environmental education and interpretation, visitor services, search and rescue, and fire management. The image of the ranger in a broad flat-brimmed hat — popularised by the U.S. National Park Service — is recognised worldwide.

History

The term "ranger" descends from medieval English forest wardens who "ranged" the king's forests. In the United States, Harry Yount — hired in 1880 to police Yellowstone — is often called the first national park ranger, and Stephen Mather, first director of the National Park Service (founded 1916), professionalised the corps, choosing its distinctive uniform with the campaign hat derived from military and Royal Canadian Mounted Police dress. Similar services developed in Canada, Australia, Africa and elsewhere, often modelled on the American example; in many African parks, rangers (or game scouts) are front-line defenders against poaching, sometimes in paramilitary roles.

Duties

  • Interpretation and education: guided walks, talks, visitor-centre programmes explaining natural and cultural history.
  • Protection and law enforcement: patrolling, enforcing park regulations, anti-poaching work, and protecting wildlife and archaeological sites.
  • Emergency services: search and rescue, emergency medical response, and wildland firefighting.
  • Resource management: wildlife monitoring, invasive species control, trail and facility maintenance, scientific data collection.

Around the world

In the United States, rangers work for the National Park Service, Forest Service, Bureau of Land Management and state park systems; many positions are seasonal. Canada's Parks Canada wardens, Australia's state park rangers, New Zealand's Department of Conservation rangers, and the wardens of British national parks perform similar mixes of conservation and visitor work. The International Ranger Federation, founded in 1992, links ranger associations globally and promotes World Ranger Day on 31 July, which also commemorates rangers killed in the line of duty — in some regions the job is among the most dangerous in conservation.

Training

Rangers typically hold degrees in natural sciences, recreation management, history or law enforcement; U.S. federal protection rangers complete law-enforcement training at the Federal Law Enforcement Training Centers, and many rangers hold emergency medical and wildland fire qualifications.
