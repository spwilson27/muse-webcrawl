Succession to Muhammad

Medina, where the question of succession arose after Muhammad's death in 632 CE.

The issue of the succession to Muhammad is the central question that divides the major branches of Islam — Sunni and Shia. The Islamic prophet Muhammad died in 632 CE in Medina, and a dispute arose over who should lead the Muslim community (ummah) after him.

The events of 632

As Muhammad lay dying, and immediately after his death, a group of the Ansar (Medinan helpers) gathered at the Saqifa of the Banu Sa'ida to choose a leader. There, Abu Bakr, a close companion and father-in-law of Muhammad, was acclaimed as the first caliph (khalifa, "successor"), with the support of Umar and Abu Ubayda. Ali ibn Abi Talib, Muhammad's cousin and son-in-law, and the Banu Hashim were occupied with Muhammad's burial and initially withheld allegiance, though Ali later pledged it.

Sunni and Shia views

Sunni Muslims hold that Muhammad did not appoint a successor and that the community rightly chose Abu Bakr, followed by Umar, Uthman and Ali — the four "Rightly Guided" (Rashidun) caliphs. Shia Muslims hold that Muhammad designated Ali as his successor, pointing to the event of Ghadir Khumm, where the prophet is reported to have said, "For whomever I am his mawla, Ali is his mawla"; they regard Ali and his descendants, the Imams, as the rightful leaders. The disagreement, sharpened by the later killing of Ali's son Husayn at Karbala in 680, developed over centuries into distinct theologies, legal schools and communal identities.
