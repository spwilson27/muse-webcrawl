Queen Victoria

Victoria (Alexandrina Victoria; 24 May 1819 – 22 January 1901) was Queen of the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland from 20 June 1837 until her death in 1901. Her reign of 63 years and 216 days — the "Victorian era" — was longer than that of any previous British monarch, and was surpassed only in 2015 by Elizabeth II.

Early life and accession

Victoria was the only child of Prince Edward, Duke of Kent and Strathearn, the fourth son of George III. Her father died in 1820, less than a year after her birth, and she was raised under the close supervision of her German-born mother, Princess Victoria of Saxe-Coburg-Saalfeld, under the restrictive "Kensington System". She inherited the throne at 18 on the death of her uncle William IV in 1837.

Marriage and family

In 1840 she married her first cousin Prince Albert of Saxe-Coburg and Gotha. They had nine children, who married into royal and noble families across the continent, earning Victoria the sobriquet "grandmother of Europe". Albert died of typhoid fever in December 1861, and Victoria withdrew into deep mourning and seclusion for years, wearing black for the rest of her life — a withdrawal that damaged the monarchy's popularity mid-reign.

Reign

The Victorian era saw the high point of the Industrial Revolution and the vast expansion of the British Empire. In 1876 Victoria was proclaimed Empress of India under the Royal Titles Act. Her Golden Jubilee (1887) and Diamond Jubilee (1897) were celebrated across the Empire. Politically, the monarchy under Victoria settled into its modern constitutional role, while her relations with prime ministers from Lord Melbourne to Gladstone and Disraeli shaped the period.

Death and legacy

Victoria died at Osborne House on the Isle of Wight on 22 January 1901 and was buried beside Albert in the Royal Mausoleum at Frogmore. She was succeeded by her eldest son, Edward VII. The era named after her is associated with industrial progress, scientific advancement, strict moral codes, and imperial expansion.
