Lambertian reflectance

Lambertian reflectance is the property that defines an ideal "matte" or diffusely reflecting surface. The apparent brightness of a Lambertian surface to an observer is the same regardless of the observer's angle of view. More technically, the surface's luminance is isotropic, and the luminous intensity obeys Lambert's cosine law.

Lambert's cosine law

Johann Heinrich Lambert, in his 1760 Photometria, showed that the intensity of light radiated from a diffuse surface in a given direction is proportional to the cosine of the angle between that direction and the surface normal. For an illuminated surface, the irradiance also falls with the cosine of the angle of incidence, so a Lambertian surface appears equally bright from all viewing angles.

Use in computer graphics

Lambertian shading is a basic lighting model in 3D computer graphics: a surface's brightness is computed as the dot product of the surface normal and the normalized light direction, multiplied by the surface colour (albedo) and light intensity. It models rough surfaces such as matte paint or unpolished stone, but not specular highlights; models such as Phong, Blinn–Phong and Oren–Nayar extend it.

Examples

Perfectly diffuse surfaces are idealizations, but matte paper, snow and the Moon's surface show approximately Lambertian behaviour over some ranges — although the Moon famously departs from it, appearing roughly uniformly bright across its disk partly due to other effects such as the opposition surge.
