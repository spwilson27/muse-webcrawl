Homo erectus

Homo erectus (meaning "upright man") is an extinct species of archaic human from the Pleistocene, spanning nearly 2 million years. It is the first human species to evolve a humanlike body plan and gait, to leave Africa, and to have used fire. Fossils have been found across Africa, western Asia (Georgia), and Southeast and East Asia (Indonesia and China). The earliest evidence, from the Republic of Georgia, dates to around 1.8 million years ago.

Discovery

The species was first described by Eugène Dubois from fossils found in Java in 1891 (the "Java Man"), initially named Pithecanthropus erectus. Rich finds followed at Zhoukoudian near Beijing in the 1920s and 1930s ("Peking Man"), at Sangiran in Java, and later at sites such as Dmanisi in Georgia, which preserves some of the earliest hominins outside Africa.

Anatomy and behaviour

H. erectus had a larger brain (roughly 650–1,100 cm³) than earlier hominins, long legs adapted to long-distance walking and running, and reduced sexual dimorphism. It made Acheulean stone tools, hunted or scavenged large animals, and controlled fire — evidence at Zhoukoudian and elsewhere suggests regular use of fire by about 400,000 years ago. Some late populations survived in Indonesia until around 110,000 years ago.

Evolutionary position

Homo erectus is widely regarded as an ancestor of later humans including Homo heidelbergensis, Neanderthals, Denisovans and modern humans, whether directly or through intermediate populations.
