Imaginary number

An imaginary number is the product of a real number and the imaginary unit i, where i is defined by its property i² = −1. The square of an imaginary number bi is −b². For example, 5i is an imaginary number, and its square is −25. By definition, zero is considered to be both real and imaginary.

History

The concept arose in the 16th century as Italian mathematicians, notably Gerolamo Cardano and Rafael Bombelli, manipulated square roots of negative numbers while solving cubic equations. René Descartes coined the term "imaginary" in 1637, intending it somewhat dismissively. The numbers gained full acceptance through the work of Euler, and the geometric interpretation of Caspar Wessel, Jean-Robert Argand, and Carl Friedrich Gauss in the late 18th and early 19th centuries.

Definition and properties

An imaginary number bi can be added to a real number a to form a complex number of the form a + bi, where a is the real part and b the imaginary part. Powers of i cycle with period four: i¹ = i, i² = −1, i³ = −i, i⁴ = 1. Multiplication of two purely imaginary numbers yields a real number.

Applications

Imaginary and complex numbers are used throughout mathematics and are indispensable in electrical engineering (alternating-current analysis), signal processing, control theory, fluid dynamics, and quantum mechanics, where the wave function is inherently complex-valued.
