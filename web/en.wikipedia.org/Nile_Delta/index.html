The Nile Delta is the delta formed in Lower Egypt where the Nile spreads out and drains into the Mediterranean Sea. It is one of the world's largest river deltas, extending from about 30 km north of Cairo some 175 km to the coast, and gave its name — through its triangular shape, like the Greek letter Δ (delta) — to river deltas everywhere, a usage credited to Herodotus.

Geography

West of the delta lie the Rosetta branch and the coastal lagoons of Idku and Burullus; the Damietta branch forms the eastern boundary near Lake Manzala. Classical authors described seven distributaries; five silted up or were canalized over the centuries, leaving the two modern branches. Sediment deposited over millennia built one of the most fertile agricultural plains on earth, historically renewed by the annual Nile flood until the completion of the Aswan High Dam in 1970 ended the inundation cycle.

History and economy

The delta was the heartland of ancient Lower Egypt, with cities such as Sais, Tanis, Mendes and Alexandria; the Rosetta Stone was found near its western coast in 1799. Today the delta produces much of Egypt's cotton, rice, wheat, maize, sugarcane and fruit, and is traversed by dense canal networks centred on barrages such as the Delta Barrage north of Cairo. It also hosts major natural gas fields offshore.

Environmental threats

Deprived of fresh sediment by upstream dams, the delta coast is eroding, while subsidence and Mediterranean sea-level rise threaten low-lying farmland and cities with salinization and inundation; studies have long ranked it among the regions most vulnerable to climate change.
