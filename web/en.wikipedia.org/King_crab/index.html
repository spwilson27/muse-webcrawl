King crab

King crabs are decapod crustaceans of the family Lithodidae, found chiefly in cold polar and subpolar seas. Despite their name and crab-like appearance, they are more closely related to hermit crabs, from which they evolved — an example of convergent evolution known as carcinisation.

Description

King crabs have a heavily calcified, spiny carapace and five pairs of legs, the first bearing claws and the fifth reduced and tucked beneath the shell for cleaning the gills. Unlike true crabs they show slight asymmetry in the abdomen, a legacy of their hermit-crab ancestry. The red king crab (Paralithodes camtschaticus) can reach a leg span of 1.8 metres and weigh over 12 kilograms.

Distribution and fisheries

Major commercial species include the red king crab of the Bering Sea and North Pacific, the blue king crab, the golden king crab, and the southern king crab of South America. Red king crabs were deliberately introduced by the Soviet Union into the Barents Sea in the 1960s, where they spread into Norwegian waters as a valuable but controversial invasive species. King crab fisheries are among the most lucrative — and, in the Bering Sea, historically among the most dangerous — shellfish fisheries in the world.

Biology

King crabs live on soft seabed at depths down to several hundred metres and migrate seasonally to shallow water to mate and release eggs, which females carry beneath the abdomen for nearly a year. Stocks have fluctuated dramatically with overfishing and warming seas; the Bristol Bay red king crab fishery was closed in 2021–2022 after population collapse.
