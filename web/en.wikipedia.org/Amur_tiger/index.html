The Amur tiger is a tiger population of the subspecies Panthera tigris tigris native to the Russian Far East and Northeast China. It is also known as the Siberian tiger, although modern genetic studies group it with the population formerly regarded as the separate subspecies Panthera tigris altaica. It once ranged throughout the Korean Peninsula, northern China and the Russian Far East, but its range contracted drastically during the 20th century.

Characteristics

The Amur tiger is among the largest of all tiger populations, adapted to cold temperate forest: its coat is paler and thicker than those of southern tigers, with a layer of fat on the belly and flanks and large, fur-covered paws that act like snowshoes. Body measurements vary; modern wild males typically weigh 180–210 kg. Main prey species are wild boar, red deer, sika deer and roe deer.

Conservation

By the 1940s, hunting had reduced the population to an estimated 20–30 individuals in the Russian Far East. The Soviet Union banned tiger hunting in 1947, and numbers slowly recovered. A full-range survey in 2015 estimated about 562 Amur tigers in Russia, with a small population across the border in China, where a national park for tigers and leopards was established on the border region. Threats include poaching of tigers and their prey, logging of Korean pine and oak forests, and fragmentation of habitat. Long-term conservation projects, notably those of the Wildlife Conservation Society's Siberia program, have monitored the population with camera traps and radio telemetry since the 1990s.
