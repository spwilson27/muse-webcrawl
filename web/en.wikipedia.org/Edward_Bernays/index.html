Edward Bernays

Edward Louis Bernays (22 November 1891 – 9 March 1995) was an American pioneer in the field of public relations and propaganda. Often called "the father of public relations", he combined the ideas of crowd psychology and the psychoanalytic theories of his uncle, Sigmund Freud, to design some of the first campaigns of mass persuasion for corporate and political clients.

Bernays professionalised the craft of public relations in the 1920s.

Career

Bernays worked for the US Committee on Public Information during the First World War, an experience that convinced him public opinion could be systematically managed. In 1919 he opened his own "public relations counsel" office in New York with Doris Fleischman, his future wife.

Famous campaigns

For the American Tobacco Company in 1929, Bernays staged the "Torches of Freedom" Easter Parade stunt, in which debutantes smoked Lucky Strikes as a symbol of women's emancipation, breaking the taboo on women smoking in public. Other campaigns promoted bacon and eggs as the hearty American breakfast (for the Beech-Nut company), soap sculpting competitions for Ivory soap, and the image of President Coolidge through celebrity endorsements.

Books and ideas

In Crystallizing Public Opinion (1923) and Propaganda (1928), Bernays argued that an "invisible government" of opinion-makers was a necessary feature of democratic society. His work influenced both marketing and political communication; later campaigns included work for the United Fruit Company in the run-up to the 1954 Guatemalan coup.
