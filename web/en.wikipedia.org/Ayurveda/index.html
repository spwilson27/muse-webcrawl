Ayurveda

[Image placeholder: Ayurvedic medicinal plants and oils]

Ayurveda (from Sanskrit āyus, "life", and veda, "knowledge") is a system of traditional medicine that originated in the Indian subcontinent more than two thousand years ago. It remains widely practised in India and Nepal, where it is officially recognised, and it has spread globally as a form of complementary and alternative medicine.

Foundations

The classical texts are the Charaka Samhita, the Sushruta Samhita and the Ashtanga Hridaya. Ayurvedic theory holds that health depends on a balance among three humours or doshas — vata (air/space), pitta (fire/water) and kapha (earth/water) — as well as proper digestion (agni) and elimination of waste. Each person has a constitution (prakriti) combining the doshas in unique proportions.

Practice

Diagnosis traditionally involves examining the pulse, tongue, eyes and general appearance, and taking a detailed history of diet and lifestyle. Treatments include herbal preparations, dietary regulation, massage with medicated oils, yoga, meditation, and the fivefold purification therapy known as panchakarma. Surgery also has ancient roots in the tradition: the Sushruta Samhita describes instruments and procedures including rhinoplasty.

Modern status and criticism

In India, Ayurveda is regulated by the Ministry of AYUSH and taught in dedicated degree programmes (BAMS). Scientific evaluations of Ayurvedic treatments are mixed: some plant-derived compounds have shown pharmacological activity, but many claims lack rigorous clinical evidence, and studies have repeatedly found dangerous levels of lead, mercury and arsenic in some commercially sold Ayurvedic products. Medical organisations generally classify Ayurveda as pseudoscientific where its pre-modern doctrines are concerned, while acknowledging the historical significance of the tradition.
