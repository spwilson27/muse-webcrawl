Aromatic compound

An aromatic compound is a chemical compound containing one or more planar rings of atoms joined by covalent bonds of alternating single and double character, whose conjugated π-electron system gives it unusual stability. The archetype is benzene (C6H6), whose six π electrons are delocalized around the ring. The term originally referred to the fragrant resins and oils from which many such compounds were first isolated, but in modern chemistry it denotes electronic structure, not smell.

Aromaticity

A ring system is aromatic if it is cyclic, planar and fully conjugated, and contains 4n + 2 π electrons, where n is a non-negative integer — Hückel's rule. Aromatic compounds are characterized by enhanced stability (resonance energy), bond lengths intermediate between single and double bonds, an induced ring current visible in NMR spectra, and a tendency to undergo electrophilic substitution rather than addition, preserving the ring system.

Examples

Simple monocyclic aromatic hydrocarbons include benzene, toluene and the xylenes — the industrial "BTX" feedstocks. Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) such as naphthalene, anthracene and phenanthrene contain fused benzene rings. Heterocyclic aromatics, in which one or more ring atoms are not carbon, include pyridine, pyrrole, furan, thiophene and imidazole; the bases of DNA and RNA are aromatic heterocycles. Aromatic rings are ubiquitous in pharmaceuticals, dyes, polymers and biomolecules such as the amino acids phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan.

Reactions

The characteristic reaction of aromatic rings is electrophilic aromatic substitution, including nitration, sulfonation, halogenation and Friedel–Crafts alkylation and acylation. Under forcing conditions aromatics can also be hydrogenated, oxidized or undergo nucleophilic aromatic substitution when suitably activated.
