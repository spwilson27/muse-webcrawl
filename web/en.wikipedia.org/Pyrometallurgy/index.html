Pyrometallurgy

Pyrometallurgy is a branch of extractive metallurgy in which ores and concentrates are treated at high temperatures to bring about physical and chemical transformations that separate and purify metals. It is one of the oldest forms of metallurgy — copper and iron smelting date back thousands of years — and remains the dominant route for producing copper, nickel, lead, and much of the world's iron and steel.

Pyrometallurgy
Field: Extractive metallurgy
Typical temperatures: 800–1700 °C
Processes: Roasting, smelting, converting, refining
Contrasts with: Hydrometallurgy, electrometallurgy

Processes

Unit operations include drying and calcination to remove water and volatiles; roasting, in which sulfide ores are oxidised in air to oxides and sulfur dioxide; smelting, in which the charge is melted so that metal (or a metal-rich matte) separates from a lighter slag of gangue oxides; converting, in which air or oxygen is blown through molten matte to remove iron and sulfur, as in copper converting; and fire refining to remove residual impurities before casting or electrolytic refining.

Advantages and drawbacks

Pyrometallurgical processes are fast, handle large tonnages, and can treat complex feeds, but they are energy-intensive, produce large volumes of off-gas (historically a major source of sulfur dioxide emissions), and are generally unsuitable for very low-grade ores, where hydrometallurgy is preferred. Temperatures in these furnaces are measured with devices such as the pyrometer.
