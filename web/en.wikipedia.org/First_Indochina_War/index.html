First Indochina War
Date19 December 1946 – 1 August 1954
LocationFrench Indochina (Vietnam, Laos, Cambodia)
ResultViet Minh victory; Geneva Accords; French withdrawal

The First Indochina War (1946–1954) was fought between France, seeking to restore its colonial federation of Indochina, and the Viet Minh under Ho Chi Minh and Vo Nguyen Giap, who had proclaimed the Democratic Republic of Vietnam in Hanoi in 1945. It ended with the French defeat at Dien Bien Phu and the Geneva settlement.

Background

Japan's occupation shattered French prestige during the Second World War. Ho's August Revolution and independence declaration of 2 September 1945 were followed by failed negotiations (Fontainebleau, 1946) and a French bombardment of Haiphong. Full war erupted in Hanoi in December 1946.

Course

France held the cities and deltas and installed Bao Dai as head of the anti-communist State of Vietnam, while the Viet Minh waged guerrilla war from rural bases. After the communist victory in China in 1949, Chinese aid transformed the Viet Minh into a regular army, winning border battles in 1950. The United States, framing the war as Cold War containment, funded most of the French war effort by 1954. General Navarre's plan to lure the Viet Minh into a set-piece battle at Dien Bien Phu backfired: Giap's forces, supplied by porters and artillery hauled through jungle — including by Hmong and other highland peoples on both sides — besieged the garrison, which fell on 7 May 1954.

Consequences

Geneva divided Vietnam at the 17th parallel and recognised Lao and Cambodian independence, drawing in the Laotian civil war involving figures such as Vang Pao and Souvanna Phouma. France's defeat marked a turning point in decolonisation and set the stage for American intervention and the Vietnam War.
