Atoll


An atoll with its central lagoon

An atoll is a ring-shaped coral island (or group of islets) that encircles, partly or completely, a lagoon. Atolls occur in warm tropical and subtropical oceans, mainly in the Pacific and Indian Oceans, and are built on the submerged remains of extinct volcanoes. The word comes from atholhu, the term in Dhivehi, the language of the Maldives — the nation made up almost entirely of atolls.

Formation

Charles Darwin proposed the classic explanation in 1842: a volcanic island first fringed by a coral reef slowly subsides; as it sinks, the reef grows upward, becoming a barrier reef separated from the island by a lagoon, and finally, once the volcano disappears beneath the sea, only the reef ring remains as an atoll. Deep drilling at Enewetak and Bikini in the 20th century confirmed volcanic rock far below thick accumulations of shallow-water limestone, vindicating Darwin's subsidence theory. Sand accumulates on the reef rim to form low islets, rarely more than a few metres above sea level.

Distribution and ecology

Famous atolls include Bikini and Enewetak (Marshall Islands), Kiritimati (Christmas Island), the largest atoll by land area, and the many atolls of French Polynesia, Tuvalu and the Maldives. Their lagoons are rich marine ecosystems, while the islets support coconut palms and limited freshwater lenses that float on the seawater beneath. Because of their low elevation, atoll nations such as Tuvalu and the Maldives are among the places most threatened by sea-level rise from climate change.
