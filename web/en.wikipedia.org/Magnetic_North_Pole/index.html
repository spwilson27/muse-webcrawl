Movement of the north magnetic pole across the Canadian Arctic and toward Siberia.

The magnetic north pole (also called the north magnetic pole) is the point on the surface of Earth's Northern Hemisphere at which the planet's magnetic field points vertically downward — that is, where a compass needle's north-seeking end is drawn toward, and where a freely suspended dip needle points straight down (a dip angle of 90°). It is distinct from the geographic North Pole and from the geomagnetic north pole, the northern end of the best-fitting dipole axis of Earth's field.

Movement

Because Earth's magnetic field is generated by fluid motion in the liquid outer core, the magnetic poles wander. The north magnetic pole was first located on 1 June 1831 by James Clark Ross on the Boothia Peninsula in what is now Nunavut, Canada. For most of the twentieth century it drifted slowly northwest through the Canadian Arctic Archipelago at about 10–15 km per year. In the 1990s its motion accelerated dramatically, reaching 50–60 km per year; it left Canadian territory, crossed the geographic pole region, and is now moving toward Siberia. The unusually fast drift prompted an out-of-cycle update to the World Magnetic Model in February 2019, since the pole's motion affects navigation systems that rely on the model.

Expeditions and surveys

After Ross, the pole was revisited by Roald Amundsen in 1903 and by Canadian government surveys beginning in the 1940s under the Dominion Observatory and later the Geological Survey of Canada, which re-located it at intervals on expeditions across the Arctic islands and sea ice. Modern positions are derived less by direct surface observation than by global magnetic models, such as the World Magnetic Model and the International Geomagnetic Reference Field, computed from satellite and observatory data.

Geomagnetic poles and reversals

The geomagnetic poles — the ends of the dipole that best approximates Earth's field — currently lie near Ellesmere Island (north) and near Vostok Station in Antarctica (south); they are antipodal, whereas the actual dip poles are not. Over geological time the field has reversed polarity many times, most recently the Brunhes–Matuyama reversal about 780,000 years ago; during such reversals the "north" and "south" magnetic poles exchange places.
