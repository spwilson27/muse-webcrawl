Strongly correlated material

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A cuprate high-temperature superconductor crystal.

Strongly correlated materials are a wide class of compounds — typically transition-metal oxides and heavy-fermion systems — in which the interactions between electrons are so strong that their behaviour cannot be described by treating the electrons as effectively independent. They host many of condensed matter physics' most striking phenomena.

Examples and phenomena

The class includes the cuprate and iron-based high-temperature superconductors, Mott insulators such as V₂O₃ that switch between insulating and metallic states, heavy-fermion compounds in which electrons behave as if hundreds of times heavier than normal, colossal magnetoresistance manganites, and materials exhibiting quantum criticality and non-Fermi-liquid behaviour.

Why correlations matter

In ordinary metals, band theory works because electron–electron repulsion can be folded into an effective single-particle picture. In strongly correlated systems the on-site Coulomb repulsion (modelled by the Hubbard U) is comparable to or larger than the electron bandwidth, so novel phases emerge that no single-particle theory predicts.

Methods

Because the problems are analytically intractable in general, the field relies on techniques such as dynamical mean-field theory (DMFT), exact diagonalization, quantum Monte Carlo, and on experiments including angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy (ARPES) and neutron scattering.
