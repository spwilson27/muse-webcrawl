John Milton Hay (October 8, 1838 – July 1, 1905) was an American statesman and official whose career in government stretched over almost half a century. Beginning as a private secretary to Abraham Lincoln, he served as Secretary of State under Presidents William McKinley and Theodore Roosevelt, where he promoted the Open Door policy in China.

Lincoln's secretary

Hay studied law in Springfield, Illinois, in an office near Abraham Lincoln's, and became one of Lincoln's two private secretaries in the White House during the Civil War. With John George Nicolay he later wrote the monumental ten-volume Abraham Lincoln: A History (1890).

Diplomatic career

Hay served in diplomatic posts in Paris, Vienna and Madrid, as Assistant Secretary of State, and as ambassador to the United Kingdom (1897–98), where he helped foster Anglo-American rapprochement.

Secretary of State

As Secretary of State from 1898, Hay issued the Open Door Notes (1899–1900) seeking equal trade access in China and the preservation of Chinese territorial integrity, negotiated the Hay–Pauncefote Treaty clearing the way for the Panama Canal, and settled the Alaska boundary dispute. He died in office in 1905.

Writings

Hay also wrote poetry (the Pike County Ballads) and a novel, The Bread-Winners (1883).
