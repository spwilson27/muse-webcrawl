Dark Lady (Shakespeare)

The Dark Lady is a woman described in Shakespeare's sonnets (sonnets 127–154), and so called because the poems make it clear that she has black wiry hair and dark, dusky skin. The description of the Dark Lady contrasts with the conventional Petrarchan "fair" beloved of Elizabethan sonnet sequences, and the relationship depicted is intense, sexual, and frequently unhappy.

The sonnets

The Dark Lady sequence follows the larger group of sonnets addressed to the "Fair Youth." She is introduced in Sonnet 127:

In the old age black was not counted fair,
Or if it were, it bore not beauty's name;
But now is black beauty's successive heir...

Sonnet 130 famously parodies love-poetry conventions: "My mistress' eyes are nothing like the sun." In the narrative implied by the sequence, the Dark Lady becomes the lover of the Fair Youth as well as of the poet, creating a painful triangle; the final sonnets (153 and 154) are versions of a classical epigram about Cupid and a healing spring.

Identity theories

Numerous candidates have been proposed for a historical Dark Lady, including Emilia Lanier (née Bassano), a musician and poet from a family of court musicians, championed by A. L. Rowse; Mary Fitton, a lady-in-waiting; the London brothel-keeper known as "Lucy Negro" or "Black Luce"; and Shakespeare's Stratford contemporary Jane Davenant. None of the identifications commands scholarly consensus, and many scholars doubt the sonnets are straightforwardly autobiographical.

Reception

The Dark Lady has inspired many literary treatments, including George Bernard Shaw's play The Dark Lady of the Sonnets (1910), in which she encounters Shakespeare and Queen Elizabeth I.
