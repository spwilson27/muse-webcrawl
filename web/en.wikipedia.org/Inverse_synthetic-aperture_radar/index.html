Inverse synthetic-aperture radar

Inverse synthetic-aperture radar (ISAR) is a radar technique using radar imaging to generate a two-dimensional high-resolution image of a target. It is analogous to conventional synthetic-aperture radar (SAR), but in ISAR the relative motion needed to synthesize a large aperture comes from the movement of the target itself rather than the radar platform.

Principle

In a typical ISAR scenario, a fixed or slowly moving radar observes a target — for example a ship — that rotates slightly during the observation interval, from its own yaw, pitch, and roll in waves, or from turning. Scatterers on the target at different range map by echo delay, while the target's rotation gives each scatterer a slightly different Doppler shift depending on its cross-range position. Processing the coherent returns over the interval thus forms a two-dimensional range–Doppler image of the target.

Cross-range resolution improves with the total angle through which the target rotates. Because the target's motion is generally unknown and may be non-uniform, ISAR processing requires autofocus algorithms — such as phase gradient autofocus — to compensate for motion errors before a sharp image can be obtained.

Applications

ISAR is used mainly in military surveillance and reconnaissance for the classification and identification of ships and aircraft at long range and in all weather, day or night. Maritime patrol aircraft and shore-based radars use it to image vessels; air defence radars use it to help identify aircraft. Civil and scientific uses include imaging of aircraft for research, observation of rotating asteroids and space debris with ground-based radars, and non-cooperative target recognition studies.

Comparison with SAR

In SAR the radar moves and the scene is assumed stationary, so the geometry is controlled and known. In ISAR the radar is effectively stationary and the target moves, often non-cooperatively, exchanging known platform motion for unknown target motion — the "inverse" arrangement that gives the technique its name.
