Documentary linguistics

Documentary linguistics (also language documentation) is a subfield of linguistics concerned with creating enduring, multipurpose records of languages, especially endangered languages, through audio and video recordings, transcriptions, translations and annotations, together with lexicons and ethnographic notes. Nikolaus Himmelmann, who articulated the field in the 1990s, distinguished documentation (creating a lasting record of communicative practices) from description (analysing the structures of a language in grammars and dictionaries).

Practices

Documentary work involves recording natural discourse across genres — narratives, conversation, ritual speech, song — with rich metadata, time-aligned transcription (using tools such as ELAN), and archiving in digital repositories such as ELAR (SOAS), the Archive of the Indigenous Languages of Latin America (AILLA), PARADISEC and the DoBeS archive. Good practice emphasises informed consent, speaker collaboration, community access and long-term data preservation.

Context

The field expanded in response to accelerating language loss: of the world's roughly 7,000 languages, a large share are endangered. Funders such as the Endangered Languages Documentation Programme (ELDP, founded 2002) and the Volkswagen Foundation's DoBeS programme supported hundreds of documentation projects. Documentation corpora feed into dictionaries, grammars, teaching materials and community revitalisation efforts.

Relationship to other fields

Documentary linguistics overlaps with descriptive linguistics, linguistic anthropology and language revitalization. It has been influential in promoting open data standards and primary-data citation in linguistics.
