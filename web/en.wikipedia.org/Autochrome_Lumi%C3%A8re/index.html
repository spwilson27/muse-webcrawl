Autochrome Lumière

The Autochrome Lumière was an early colour photography process and the first industrially produced method of making colour photographs. Patented by the Lumière brothers in 1903 and marketed from 1907, it remained the principal colour medium until the arrival of subtractive colour films in the 1930s.

Process

An autochrome plate was coated with millions of microscopic grains of potato starch dyed orange-red, green and blue-violet, which acted as colour filters, together with lampblack to fill the gaps and a panchromatic silver halide emulsion. Exposure through the plate and reversal processing produced a positive transparency whose pointillist mosaic of coloured grains formed a soft, painterly image. The dense filter layer absorbed most of the light, so exposures were long by later standards and the finished plates are fairly dark, best viewed by transmitted light or projection.

Use

Autochromes were used by amateur and professional photographers, by National Geographic, and in Albert Kahn's ambitious "Archives of the Planet" project, which sent photographers worldwide to record human cultures in colour. The Lumière company produced plates until the early 1930s, when film-based successors such as Filmcolor replaced them.
