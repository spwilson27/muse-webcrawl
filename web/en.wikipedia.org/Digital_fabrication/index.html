Digital fabrication

Digital fabrication is a type of manufacturing process in which the machine used is controlled by a computer. The most common method of digital fabrication is, by far, additive manufacturing — commonly known as 3D printing. Other computer-controlled processes include CNC milling, laser cutting, plasma cutting, and water jet cutting.

The term is strongly associated with the fab lab movement, which began at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology's Center for Bits and Atoms under Neil Gershenfeld. A fab lab is a small-scale workshop offering personal digital fabrication tools, and the concept has spread to hundreds of laboratories in dozens of countries.

Processes

Additive processes build objects by depositing material layer upon layer, most commonly fused deposition modeling (FDM), stereolithography (SLA), and selective laser sintering (SLS). Subtractive processes remove material from a solid block using computer numerical control (CNC) routers and mills. Formative and cutting processes include laser cutters, which are used for engraving and cutting sheet materials such as acrylic, plywood, and metal.

Workflow

A digital fabrication workflow typically begins with a computer-aided design (CAD) model. The model is converted into machine instructions — for 3D printers, a slicing program generates G-code describing each layer; for CNC machines, CAM software generates toolpaths. Because the design exists as a file, it can be shared, modified, and reproduced anywhere with compatible equipment.

Applications

Digital fabrication is used for rapid prototyping in product design, for customised medical devices and prosthetics, in architecture for scale models and even full-scale building components, and by hobbyists and makers. Its advocates argue it enables distributed, on-demand, and highly personalised manufacturing.
