Fort Stanwix

Fort Stanwix was a colonial fort built by the British in 1758 at the Oneida Carry, in present-day Rome, New York, to protect the strategic portage between the Mohawk River and Wood Creek during the French and Indian War. It was named for its builder, General John Stanwix. The fort is best known for its role in the American Revolutionary War, when its Continental garrison withstood a British siege in August 1777. The reconstructed fort is preserved as Fort Stanwix National Monument.

Construction and treaty

The Oneida Carry was one of the most important portages in North America, linking the Atlantic watershed (via the Mohawk and Hudson rivers) to Lake Ontario (via Wood Creek and Oneida Lake). Stanwix built a large square earth-and-timber fort with four bastions in the summer and autumn of 1758, but it was abandoned after the war and fell into ruin. In 1768 the fort hosted the Treaty of Fort Stanwix, in which the Iroquois Confederacy ceded vast lands south of the Ohio River to the British — a cession that fuelled further colonial expansion and conflict. A second treaty at the site in 1784, imposed after the Revolution, forced further Iroquois land cessions to the United States.

Siege of 1777

American forces under Colonel Peter Gansevoort reoccupied and rebuilt the fort (renamed Fort Schuyler) in 1776–77. In August 1777, as part of the British Saratoga campaign, a force of British regulars, Loyalists, and Native allies under Brigadier Barry St. Leger besieged it. The garrison famously raised a flag made from red, white, and blue cloth — one of the earliest uses of the American flag in battle. The bloody Battle of Oriskany, fought by Tryon County militia marching to relieve the fort, and a successful American sortie and deception by Benedict Arnold's relief column, led St. Leger to abandon the siege on 22 August, contributing to the failure of the British campaign that ended at Saratoga.

Later years

The fort was badly damaged by fire and flood in 1781 and abandoned. The city of Rome grew over the site. In the 1970s the National Park Service reconstructed the fort on its original footprint, guided by archaeological excavations, and it opened for the Bicentennial in 1976.
