Groin vault

A groin vault or groined vault (also known as a double barrel vault or cross vault) is produced by the intersection at right angles of two barrel vaults. The word "groin" refers to the edge between the intersecting vaults, known technically as an arris. Sometimes the arches of groin vaults are pointed instead of round, as in ribbed vaults. Compared with a barrel vault, a groin vault provides good economies of material and labour: the thrust is concentrated along the groins or arrises and is carried down to just four corner supports, so the walls between the corners can be opened up with large windows.

History

Although known in simpler form earlier, the groin vault was exploited on a monumental scale by the Romans, notably in the great imperial thermae (bath complexes) such as the Baths of Diocletian and the Basilica of Maxentius in Rome, where concrete groin vaults spanned vast halls. The technique largely fell out of use in the early medieval West before being revived in Romanesque architecture. In the twelfth century the addition of diagonal ribs to manage the geometry and thrust of the groin vault produced the ribbed vault, a defining element of Gothic architecture that allowed taller, lighter, more luminous interiors.

Geometry and structure

Over a square bay, two barrel vaults crossing at right angles generate four curved triangular webs. The groins — the curves where the surfaces intersect — are elliptical in a simple groin vault, which complicated stone-cutting; medieval masons often plastered over irregularities or stiffened the groins with ribs. Because loads are delivered to the four corners, groin-vaulted bays can be repeated in a grid to cover large rectangular spaces, as in Roman basilicas and later in railway stations and market halls.
