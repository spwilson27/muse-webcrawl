Po Delta

Lagoons and channels of the Po Delta

The Po Delta (Italian: Delta del Po) is the extensive deltaic wetland system formed where the Po, Italy's longest river, empties into the northern Adriatic Sea, spanning parts of the Veneto and Emilia-Romagna regions. Covering roughly 1,500 km² including its lagoons, it is one of the largest wetland complexes in the Mediterranean and is protected by two regional parks and recognised by UNESCO as a Biosphere Reserve; Ferrara and the delta were inscribed as a World Heritage landscape in 1999.

Geography

The modern delta begins near the point where the Po splits into its principal distributaries, including the Po di Goro, Po di Gnocca and the main channel, the Po di Venezia. Centuries of sediment deposition have built the coastline outward — the Roman port of Adria, which gave the Adriatic its name, now lies more than 20 km inland. Since the Porto Viro cut of 1604, engineered by the Republic of Venice to divert sediment away from its lagoon, the delta has advanced south of that channel. Land reclamation (bonifica) in the 19th and 20th centuries drained large areas for agriculture, while subsidence — accelerated by methane and groundwater extraction in the mid-20th century — has left much of the delta plain below sea level, defended by embankments and pumps.

Ecology

The mosaic of lagoons (lagune), reed beds, salt marshes, dune systems, pine woods and brackish fishing valleys (valli da pesca) supports exceptional biodiversity. More than 370 bird species have been recorded, including herons, egrets, spoonbills, pygmy cormorants and one of Italy's few colonies of greater flamingos. The delta is a key node on the Adriatic flyway for migratory waterbirds. Traditional eel and clam fishing, rice cultivation in reclaimed areas, and aquaculture remain important to the local economy.

Threats

The delta faces saltwater intrusion up the river channel during droughts — which in severe summers has reached tens of kilometres inland, damaging agriculture — nutrient pollution from the intensively farmed Po Valley, coastal erosion, and sea-level rise. Management requires balancing the river, the parks, the regions of Veneto and Emilia-Romagna, and the fishing and farming communities within the delta.
