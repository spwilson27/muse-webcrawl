Katana

A katana (刀 or かたな) is a Japanese sword characterized by a curved, single-edged blade with a circular or squared guard (tsuba) and a long grip to accommodate two hands. It was used by the samurai of feudal Japan and has become renowned for its sharpness and cutting ability.

History

The katana emerged during the Kamakura period (1185–1333) as an evolution of the earlier tachi, being worn edge-up thrust through the belt (obi) so that it could be drawn and used to cut in a single motion. Paired with the shorter wakizashi, the set was known as the daishō, the symbol of the samurai class. From the late sixteenth century, laws under Toyotomi Hideyoshi and the Tokugawa shogunate restricted sword-wearing to samurai, and the Haitōrei edict of 1876 ended the era of the sword as an everyday weapon.

Forging

Traditional katana are forged from a specialized steel called tamahagane, produced from iron sand in a tatara furnace. The blade is made by repeatedly folding and hammer-welding steel of differing carbon content, then differentially hardening it by coating it with clay before quenching: the edge hardens into martensite while the spine stays softer and more flexible, producing the visible temper line known as the hamon and the blade's characteristic curve (sori). Famous smiths include Masamune and Muramasa.

Use and legacy

The katana is used in martial arts such as iaidō, kendō and kenjutsu. Antique blades are valued as art objects in Japan, where swordsmithing continues under licence. The weapon remains one of the most recognisable symbols of Japanese culture worldwide.
