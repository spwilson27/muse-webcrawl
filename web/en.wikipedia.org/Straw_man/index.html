Straw man

A straw man (sometimes written strawman) fallacy is the informal logical fallacy of refuting an argument different from the one actually under discussion, while not recognising or acknowledging the distinction. One who engages in this fallacy is said to be "attacking a straw man".

Structure

The typical straw man argument has the following form: Person 1 puts forward position X; Person 2 presents a distorted position Y, which resembles X but is weaker, exaggerated, or subtly different; Person 2 refutes Y and concludes that X is false. The refutation is illusory, because the actual position X has not been addressed. Straw manning can be an intentional rhetorical technique, or an unintentional result of misunderstanding an opponent's view, sometimes encouraged by quoting statements out of context.

Examples and related concepts

Common forms include oversimplifying an opponent's argument, presenting someone's weakest arguments as though they were the whole case, and inventing a fictitious person who supposedly holds an indefensible position. Related discussion includes the principle of charity (interpreting an argument in its strongest reasonable form), the "steel man" (the inverse practice of strengthening an opposing argument before refuting it), and the "weak man" fallacy, in which one selects the weakest real version of an opposing argument to attack. The metaphor derives from the straw-stuffed mannequins once used as practice targets in combat training — far easier to defeat than a living opponent.
