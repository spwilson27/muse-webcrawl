World's Columbian Exposition

The World's Columbian Exposition, also known as the Chicago World's Fair, was a world's fair held in Chicago from May 1 to October 30, 1893, to celebrate the 400th anniversary of Christopher Columbus's arrival in the New World in 1492. The central display was a large water pool representing Columbus's voyage. Chicago bested New York City, St. Louis, and Washington, D.C. for the right to host. The fair drew more than 27 million visitors — nearly half the population of the United States at the time — and is credited with launching the City Beautiful movement and demonstrating Chicago's recovery from the Great Fire of 1871.

The White City

Chief of construction Daniel Burnham and his partner John Wellborn Root planned a harmonious ensemble of monumental Beaux-Arts buildings, mostly faced in white "staff" (a temporary plaster material) and lit at night by thousands of electric lights — hence the "White City". Landscape architect Frederick Law Olmsted designed the grounds around lagoons and canals in Jackson Park. The dazzling classical city profoundly influenced American civic architecture and urban planning for decades.

Attractions and firsts

The Midway Plaisance, a mile-long strip of amusements and ethnographic villages, featured the original Ferris Wheel, built by George Washington Gale Ferris Jr. to rival the Eiffel Tower; it stood 264 feet high with cars carrying up to 60 people each. The fair showcased the triumph of alternating current — Westinghouse, using Tesla's patents, underbid Edison to light the exposition, a decisive moment in the "war of the currents". Debuts and popularisations credited to the fair include the hamburger's spread, Cracker Jack, Aunt Jemima pancake mix, Juicy Fruit gum, and the zipper. Nikola Tesla and others demonstrated fluorescent lighting and a phosphorescent lamp, and the first moving pictures were shown nearby.

Legacy

Almost all the buildings were temporary and were demolished or destroyed by fire after the fair; the Palace of Fine Arts, built with a brick substructure, survives as the Museum of Science and Industry. The fair's dark counterpoint was serial killer H. H. Holmes, whose crimes in Chicago during the fair were chronicled in Erik Larson's The Devil in the White City (2003).
