Demographic dividend

The demographic dividend is the economic growth potential that can result from shifts in a population's age structure, mainly when the share of the working-age population (15 to 64) is larger than the share of non-working-age dependants (14 and younger, and 65 and older). During the demographic transition, mortality and then fertility fall, producing first a youth bulge and then a period in which workers greatly outnumber dependants. With fewer mouths to feed per worker, resources are freed for investment in education, infrastructure, and productive capital, and per-capita income can rise rapidly.

Mechanisms

Economists distinguish a first dividend, arising from the increased share of producers in the population and from rising savings as large working cohorts prepare for retirement, and a second dividend, arising as those savings are invested and deepen the capital stock. The dividend is not automatic: it depends on policies and institutions. Large youth cohorts translate into growth only if they are healthy, educated, and able to find productive employment — the experience of the East Asian "tiger" economies is often cited as a successful capture of the dividend, aided by export-oriented industrialisation and investment in schooling.

Risks and variation

Where jobs and education fail to keep pace, a youth bulge can instead be associated with unemployment and political instability. The dividend is also temporary: as the large working cohorts age, dependency ratios rise again, and societies enter a phase of population ageing with heavier pension and healthcare burdens. Much of sub-Saharan Africa is currently entering the window in which a dividend might be earned in coming decades, while countries such as India are widely discussed as being within it, and East Asian countries such as South Korea and China are passing beyond it.
