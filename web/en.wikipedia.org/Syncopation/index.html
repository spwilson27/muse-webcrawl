Syncopation

In music theory, syncopation is a musical rhythm or accent placed on a normally unaccented beat, or a rhythm in which the strong beats are missed and the weak beats are emphasised. The term derives from the Greek synkopē, meaning "cutting short." Syncopation has been an important element of European musical composition since at least the Middle Ages, and it is a defining feature of many African-American musical genres, including ragtime, jazz, blues, funk, reggae and hip hop.

Definition

Syncopation involves a variety of rhythms which are in some way unexpected, making part or all of a tune or piece of music off-beat. More simply, syncopation is "a disturbance or interruption of the regular flow of rhythm": a "placement of rhythmic stresses or accents where they wouldn't normally occur." It is the correlation of at least two sets of time intervals. Syncopation is used in many musical styles, especially dance music: in the opinion of the British composer and musicologist Wilfrid Mellers, "syncopation is the means by which Western music escapes from the tyranny of the bar line."

Types

Several distinct techniques produce syncopation. A note may be anticipated, played slightly before the beat on which it would normally fall. Alternatively, an accent may be displaced onto an off-beat, a sustained note may be tied across a beat so that the expected attack on the strong beat is absent, or a rest may be inserted where a strong beat is expected. Composer Louis Moreau Gottschalk wrote many syncopated piano works drawing on Caribbean rhythms, and Scott Joplin's rags made syncopation famous in the early twentieth century, to the point that ragtime became popularly synonymous with "syncopated music."

History

Syncopation in European music appears in the works of medieval composers and became a common expressive device in the Baroque and Classical periods. In the Americas, African rhythmic traditions combined with European harmony to produce highly syncopated styles. In jazz, syncopation underlies swing phrasing; in funk, musicians such as James Brown built grooves around sharp off-beat accents; and in reggae, the guitar "skank" and the one-drop drum pattern place accents away from the downbeat.
