An intelligence quotient (IQ) is a total score derived from a set of standardised tests or subtests designed to assess human intelligence. The abbreviation "IQ" was coined by the psychologist William Stern for the German term Intelligenzquotient. Modern IQ tests are normed so that the median score is 100 and the standard deviation is usually 15.

History

The first modern intelligence test was developed by Alfred Binet and Théodore Simon in 1905 to identify French schoolchildren needing extra help. Lewis Terman adapted it as the Stanford–Binet scale in the United States, and David Wechsler later created the widely used Wechsler scales (WAIS for adults, WISC for children), replacing the ratio IQ with deviation scores.

Uses and controversies

IQ scores predict educational attainment and job performance moderately well and are used in diagnosing intellectual disability and assessing giftedness. Their use has also been controversial, including debates over group differences, cultural bias, the heritability of intelligence and the Flynn effect, the sustained rise of scores across generations.
