The Crusader states, also known as the Outremer (from French outre-mer, "overseas"), were a number of mostly 12th- and 13th-century feudal Christian states created by Western European crusaders in the Levant following the First Crusade. The term also covers the Latin Empire and other states founded after the Fourth Crusade in formerly Byzantine territory.

The Levantine states

The four principal states were founded in quick succession: Edessa (1098), Antioch (1098), Jerusalem (1099) and Tripoli (1102/1109). Ruled by Frankish aristocracies over largely Muslim, Eastern Christian and Jewish populations, they depended on Italian maritime republics for shipping, on the military orders — Knights Templar and Hospitaller — for defence, and on periodic crusades from Europe. Their castles, such as Krak des Chevaliers, remain iconic survivals of medieval military architecture.

Decline and fall

Edessa fell to Zengi in 1144, provoking the Second Crusade. Saladin's victory at Hattin in 1187 destroyed the Kingdom of Jerusalem's army and led to the loss of Jerusalem; the Third Crusade recovered the coast but not the city. In the 13th century the states shrank under pressure from the Ayyubids and then the Mamluks of Egypt: Antioch fell in 1268, Tripoli in 1289, and Acre, the last mainland stronghold, in 1291, ending nearly two centuries of Latin rule in the Levant.

Legacy

The Crusader states facilitated trade and cultural exchange — sugar, glassmaking and translations moved between East and West — while leaving a contested legacy of conquest and massacre. Related Latin states in Greece, such as the Principality of Achaea, survived into the 15th century, and the Hospitallers held Rhodes until 1522.
