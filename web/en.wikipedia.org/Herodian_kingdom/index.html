Herodian kingdom

Existed: 37 BC – AD 44 (with interruptions)
Capital: Jerusalem
Rulers: Herod the Great; Agrippa I
Status: Client kingdom of Rome

The Herodian kingdom was a client kingdom of the Roman Republic and later Roman Empire in the Levant, ruled by the Herodian dynasty founded by Herod the Great. Appointed king by the Roman Senate in 40 BC, Herod conquered his kingdom by 37 BC and ruled it until his death in 4 BC.

Under Herod the Great

Herod, an Idumean whose family had converted to Judaism, governed Judea, Galilee, Samaria, Idumea, Perea, and territories east of the Jordan. He was a prodigious builder: he rebuilt the Second Temple in Jerusalem on a grand scale, founded the port city of Caesarea Maritima, and raised fortresses such as Masada, Herodium, and Machaerus. His reign combined economic prosperity and political stability with domestic brutality — he executed his wife Mariamne, her relatives, and three of his own sons.

Partition and aftermath

At Herod's death, Augustus divided the kingdom among his sons: Archelaus received Judea (as ethnarch, deposed in AD 6, after which Judea became a Roman province), Herod Antipas ruled Galilee and Perea as tetrarch, and Philip governed the northeastern territories. In AD 41 the emperor Claudius granted most of the old kingdom to Herod Agrippa I, briefly restoring a united Herodian monarchy; on Agrippa's death in AD 44, the territory reverted to direct Roman provincial rule.

Legacy

The kingdom's infrastructure, cities, and religious tensions form the backdrop of the New Testament narratives and of the events leading to the First Jewish–Roman War (AD 66–73).
