Nikon (Russian: Ни́кон; born Nikita Minin, 1605–1681) was the seventh Patriarch of Moscow and all Rus', serving from 1652 to 1666. His ambitious reform of Russian Orthodox liturgical books and practices to conform with contemporary Greek usage split the Russian Church, producing the schism (Raskol) of the Old Believers, one of the most consequential events in Russian religious history.

Rise

Born a peasant's son, Nikita Minin became a parish priest, then a monk under the name Nikon. His preaching attracted the attention of Tsar Alexis, who made him archimandrite of a Moscow monastery, then Metropolitan of Novgorod, where he won favour by suppressing a revolt in 1650 with a mixture of firmness and clemency. On the death of Patriarch Joseph in 1652, Alexis secured Nikon's election as Patriarch.

Reforms and schism

From 1653 Nikon ordered corrections to Russian service books from Greek originals: the name "Jesus" was to be spelled with two letters instead of one, the sign of the cross made with three fingers rather than two, "hallelujah" sung three times rather than twice, and processions to move counter-clockwise. To traditionalists led by the archpriest Avvakum, these seemingly small changes were heretical corruption. The schism hardened after church councils of 1666–67 anathematised the old rites — and simultaneously deposed Nikon himself, who had quarrelled with the Tsar and abandoned his throne in 1658 asserting the superiority of priesthood over kingship.

Fall and legacy

Nikon spent his remaining years exiled to the Ferapontov Monastery, though Alexis relented near death and permission for Nikon's return was granted in 1681; he died on the journey, at Yaroslavl. His foundation, the New Jerusalem Monastery near Moscow — built as a replica of the Holy Sepulchre — remains a major monument. The Old Believer schism he provoked was never fully healed.
