Abreaction

Abreaction is the expression and consequent release of a previously repressed emotion, achieved by reliving the experience that caused it. The concept was central to the early work of Josef Breuer and Sigmund Freud, particularly the "cathartic method" described in their Studies on Hysteria (1895).

Origins

Breuer found that his patient "Anna O." (Bertha Pappenheim) improved when, under hypnosis, she traced each symptom back to the occasion on which it first appeared and expressed the emotion that had originally been suppressed — a process she called "chimney sweeping" and the "talking cure". Freud generalised this: hysterical symptoms were thought to arise from the "strangulated affect" of traumatic memories, and abreaction — discharging that affect through recollection with feeling — was the cure.

Later developments

Freud later shifted from abreaction toward interpretation, free association, and working through, concluding that abreaction alone gave only temporary relief. The technique was revived in various settings: in the treatment of combat trauma during the World Wars ("narcosynthesis" with barbiturates), and in therapies such as primal therapy that emphasise emotional reliving. Modern trauma-focused therapies involve controlled re-exposure to traumatic memories, though contemporary practice stresses integration and safety rather than raw catharsis, and the standalone therapeutic value of abreaction is debated.
