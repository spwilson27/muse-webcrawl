Expected shortfall

FieldFinancial risk management
Also known asConditional value at risk (CVaR), average value at risk (AVaR), expected tail loss
Compares withValue at risk (VaR)
RegulationBasel III / FRTB market-risk framework

Expected shortfall (ES) is a risk measure used in financial risk management that quantifies the expected loss of a portfolio in the worst cases: formally, the average loss conditional on the loss exceeding the value at risk (VaR) at a specified confidence level. If the 97.5% one-day expected shortfall of a portfolio is $10 million, then on the worst 2.5% of days the average loss is $10 million. Unlike VaR, which reports only a threshold, ES takes account of how bad outcomes beyond that threshold can be.

Definition and properties

Mathematically, ES at level α is the average of the losses in the tail beyond the α-quantile, expressible as the integral of VaR over the tail region. Expected shortfall is a coherent risk measure in the sense of Artzner and co-authors: it is subadditive, meaning diversification never increases measured risk — a property VaR lacks, since VaR can penalise a diversified portfolio relative to its parts. ES can also be computed in closed form for optimisation: Rockafellar and Uryasev showed that CVaR minimisation reduces to a tractable convex (indeed, linear, for discrete scenarios) programme, which made the measure practical for portfolio construction.

Regulatory adoption

Following the 2007–2008 financial crisis, regulators criticised VaR for ignoring tail severity. The Basel Committee's Fundamental Review of the Trading Book (FRTB) moved banks' internal models for market risk from 99% VaR to 97.5% expected shortfall (a level chosen to be roughly comparable under normal distributions), with stressed calibration and liquidity horizons. Difficulties remain: ES is not elicitable on its own, complicating backtesting, though it is jointly elicitable with VaR, and estimates of tail averages are inherently sensitive to data scarcity.
