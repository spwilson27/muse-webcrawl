Direct and indirect realism

In the philosophy of perception, direct realism and indirect (or representative) realism are rival accounts of the relation between perceivers and the external world. Direct realism — also called naïve realism — holds that we perceive the world directly: the objects of perception are the ordinary physical objects themselves, and perceptual experience presents them without intermediary. Indirect or representative realism holds that we perceive the external world only indirectly, by way of mental representations or sense data that stand between the mind and the world.

Indirect realism

Representative realism is associated with John Locke, who held that ideas in the mind represent external objects and that qualities divide into primary qualities (such as shape and motion, which resemble properties of objects) and secondary qualities (such as colour and taste, which do not). René Descartes and other early modern philosophers developed versions of the view in response to the new science of optics. The theory must explain how we can know that our representations correspond to reality — a challenge dramatised by sceptical worries about a "veil of perception" screening the mind from the world.

Arguments from illusion and hallucination

The main motivation for indirect realism is the argument from illusion and hallucination: if a straight stick can look bent in water, and a hallucinating subject can seem to see a pink rat, then — the argument runs — what is immediately present to the mind in every case must be something mental, a sense datum, rather than the physical object itself. Direct realists reply with disjunctivism: veridical perception and hallucination are fundamentally different kinds of mental state, so no common mental intermediary need be posited. They also argue that perceptual experience is "transparent": we attend through it to the world, not to inner items.

Modern positions

Twentieth-century debate was shaped by J. L. Austin's attack on sense-data theory in Sense and Sensibilia (1962), by adverbial theories (perceiving is a way of being appeared to, not a relation to a mental object), and by intentionalism or representationalism, which says experience represents the world but does not interpose objects of awareness. Contemporary direct realists such as John McDowell and Bill Brewer defend relational views of experience, while indirect realism survives in some philosophy of mind and in cognitive-science talk of internal representations, though the latter is usually understood causally rather than as a veil of perception.
