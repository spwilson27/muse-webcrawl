The Ponary massacre (Polish: zbrodnia w Ponarach) was the mass murder of up to 100,000 people, mostly Jews, Poles and Russians, by German SD and SS units together with their Lithuanian collaborators during World War II, at the Ponary (Paneriai) forest near Vilnius (Wilno), then in German-occupied territory and now in Lithuania.

The killings

Between July 1941 and August 1944, victims were brought by train or truck to a former Soviet fuel-storage site in the forest, where large circular pits had been dug. They were shot in groups at the edge of the pits. The great majority of Vilnius's Jewish population — the city was known as the "Jerusalem of Lithuania" — was murdered there, along with Polish intelligentsia, Soviet prisoners of war and others.

Concealment and evidence

As the front approached in late 1943, the Germans forced a detachment of prisoners (the Leichenkommando) to exhume and burn the bodies to destroy evidence; some of these prisoners escaped through a tunnel in April 1944 and testified about the massacre. The Polish journalist Kazimierz Sakowicz, who lived nearby, documented the shootings in a diary that became a key source. A memorial museum now stands at Paneriai.
