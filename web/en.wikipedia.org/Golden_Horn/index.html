The Golden Horn (Turkish: Haliç; Greek: Chrysókeras, "Golden Horn") is a major urban waterway and the primary inlet of the Bosphorus in Istanbul, Turkey. It is a horn-shaped estuary formed where the Alibeyköy and Kağıthane rivers join, separating the old city of Istanbul (the historic peninsula) from the northern districts around Galata and Beyoğlu.

The inlet provided a sheltered natural harbour that protected Greek, Roman, Byzantine, and Ottoman shipping for thousands of years and helped make the city wealthy. Byzantines defended its entrance with a great chain stretched across to Galata; during the Fourth Crusade (1204) and the Ottoman siege of 1453, attackers sought to bypass it — in 1453 Sultan Mehmed II transported ships overland into the Horn on greased logs.

History

Greek colonists founded Byzantium beside the Horn in the 7th century BC, prizing the deep harbour. Under the Byzantine Empire its shores held palaces, churches, and the quarters of Venetian and Genoese merchants. In Ottoman times the imperial shipyards (Tersane) at Kasımpaşa and the suburb of Eyüp, with its sacred mosque and cemetery, developed along its banks. Heavy industrialisation in the 20th century polluted the waterway, but a major clean-up beginning in the 1980s restored much of its marine life.

Bridges

The Horn is crossed by several bridges, including the Galata Bridge (first built 1845, repeatedly rebuilt), the Atatürk (Unkapanı) Bridge, the Haliç Metro Bridge, and the Haliç Bridge carrying the motorway ring.
