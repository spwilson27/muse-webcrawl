Censorship in India

Censorship in India takes various forms, governed by a framework of constitutional guarantees of free speech qualified by statutory restrictions. Article 19(1)(a) of the Constitution of India guarantees freedom of speech and expression, but Article 19(2) permits "reasonable restrictions" in the interests of the sovereignty and integrity of India, the security of the State, friendly relations with foreign States, public order, decency or morality, or in relation to contempt of court, defamation or incitement to an offence. India has a long history of censorship under British colonial rule, and various forms of regulation of the press, cinema, books and the internet have continued since independence in 1947.

History

British colonial authorities regulated the Indian press through measures such as the Vernacular Press Act of 1878 and the Indian Press Act of 1910, and censored publications advocating independence. After independence, the First Amendment (1951) expanded the permissible grounds of restriction following court decisions striking down bans. During the Emergency of 1975–1977 under Prime Minister Indira Gandhi, pre-censorship of newspapers was imposed, electricity to presses was cut, and journalists were jailed; the period is regarded as the most extensive episode of state censorship in independent India.

Film censorship

Films for public exhibition must be certified by the Central Board of Film Certification (CBFC) under the Cinematograph Act, 1952. The Board can refuse certification or require cuts, and issues categories U (universal), U/A (parental guidance), A (adults) and S (specialised audiences). Decisions of the CBFC have frequently been controversial, involving depictions of sexuality, religion, politics and caste, and filmmakers often appeal to the courts, which have sometimes upheld freedom of expression, as in rulings protecting political satire and documentary film.

Books and print

Books may be proscribed under Section 95 of the Code of Criminal Procedure by state governments if they are deemed to promote enmity between groups, outrage religious feelings (Indian Penal Code Section 295A) or threaten public order. India was the first country to ban Salman Rushdie's The Satanic Verses (1988, under customs law). Other works banned at various times include The Hindus: An Alternative History by Wendy Doniger (withdrawn by the publisher in 2014 after litigation) and various books on Gandhi, Shivaji and religious subjects.

Internet censorship

The Information Technology Act, 2000, especially Section 69A, empowers the government to order blocking of online content on grounds mirroring Article 19(2); blocking orders were historically confidential. India has also imposed frequent regional internet shutdowns, among the highest number recorded by digital-rights groups, often during unrest in Kashmir and elsewhere. Social-media and streaming platforms are subject to the IT Rules of 2021, which require grievance officers, content-takedown compliance and, for significant platforms, traceability measures that have been challenged in court by WhatsApp and others. The Supreme Court's judgment in Shreya Singhal v. Union of India (2015) struck down Section 66A of the IT Act as unconstitutional while upholding Section 69A with safeguards.

Press freedom assessments

India's ranking in international press-freedom indices has declined in recent years; Reporters Without Borders placed India in the lower third of its World Press Freedom Index, citing violence against journalists, concentrated media ownership and legal pressure. Government bodies dispute these characterisations.
