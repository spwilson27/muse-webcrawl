Robert FitzRoy (5 July 1805 – 30 April 1865) was an English officer of the Royal Navy and a scientist. He achieved lasting fame as the captain of HMS Beagle during Charles Darwin's famous voyage, and as a pioneering meteorologist who made accurate weather forecasting a reality; he was the founder of the Met Office. FitzRoy also served as a member of Parliament and as the second Governor of New Zealand, from 1843 to 1845.

The Beagle voyages

FitzRoy took command of the Beagle in 1828 after her previous captain died by suicide during a survey expedition to Tierra del Fuego. On the second voyage (1831–1836), fearing isolation on the long survey, he sought a well-educated gentleman companion and selected the young naturalist Charles Darwin. The survey work FitzRoy completed around the coasts of South America was of lasting value to navigation and charting.

New Zealand and weather forecasting

As Governor of New Zealand, FitzRoy struggled with settler–Māori tensions and a bankrupt administration and was recalled in 1845. In 1854 he was appointed head of a new government department for maritime meteorology—the origin of the Met Office—and developed systems for distributing storm warnings to ports. After the wreck of the Royal Charter in 1859, he established a network of reporting stations and began publishing the first daily weather forecasts in The Times in 1861, coining much of modern forecast terminology.

Later life

Deeply religious, FitzRoy was distressed by the controversy over Darwin's On the Origin of Species, to which his voyage had contributed, and by public criticism of his forecasts. Suffering from depression, he died by suicide in 1865. Many places are named for him, including Mount Fitz Roy in Patagonia and the Fitzroy River in Australia.
