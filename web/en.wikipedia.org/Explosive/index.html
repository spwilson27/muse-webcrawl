Explosive

An explosive (or explosive material) is a reactive substance that contains a great amount of potential energy that can produce an explosion if released suddenly, usually accompanied by the production of light, heat, sound, and pressure. An explosive charge is a measured quantity of explosive material, which may either be composed solely of one ingredient or be a mixture containing at least two substances.

Potential energy stored in an explosive material may be chemical energy, such as that stored in nitroglycerin or grain dust; pressurized gas, such as in a gas cylinder or aerosol can; or nuclear energy, as in the fissile isotopes uranium-235 and plutonium-239. Explosive materials are categorized according to the speed at which they expand.

History

The earliest known explosive is gunpowder, developed in China during the Tang dynasty (9th century) and spread across Eurasia by the end of the 13th century. For centuries gunpowder was the only practical explosive. In 1847 Ascanio Sobrero discovered nitroglycerin, which was far more powerful but dangerously unstable. Alfred Nobel's invention of dynamite in 1867 made nitroglycerin-based explosives safe enough for widespread use in mining and construction, and funded the Nobel Prizes. Later discoveries included TNT (trinitrotoluene), plastic explosives such as C-4, and ammonium-nitrate-based agents such as ANFO.

Terminology

Explosion

An explosion is a rapid expansion in volume of a given amount of material, generally accompanied by an extreme outward release of energy in the form of a shock wave. Explosions are categorized as deflagrations if the explosive wave travels subsonically, or detonations if the wave is supersonic.

Detonation

Detonation is characterised by a supersonic exothermic front accelerating through a medium that eventually drives a shock front propagating directly in front of it. Detonations occur in both conventional solid and liquid explosives, as well as in reactive gases.

Properties

Important properties of explosives include detonation velocity, sensitivity to initiation, stability in storage, brisance (shattering capability), and the volume of gas produced. High explosives detonate at velocities of roughly 3 to 9 km/s, whereas low explosives such as gunpowder deflagrate at subsonic speeds and are used mainly as propellants.

Classification

By sensitivity

Primary explosives, such as mercury fulminate and lead azide, are extremely sensitive and are used in small quantities in detonators to initiate less sensitive charges. Secondary explosives, such as TNT and RDX, require a strong shock to detonate and are the main bursting charges. Tertiary explosives, such as ANFO, are so insensitive that they require a booster of secondary explosive.

By velocity

Low explosives burn or deflagrate rather than detonate; high explosives detonate. This distinction is the basis for explosives regulations in many jurisdictions.

Applications

Explosives are used in mining and quarrying, demolition and construction, tunnelling, military ordnance, fireworks and pyrotechnics, automotive airbags, and emergency signalling devices. Their handling and transport are heavily regulated because of the risks of accidental initiation.
