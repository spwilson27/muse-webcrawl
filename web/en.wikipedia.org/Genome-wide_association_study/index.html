Genome-wide association study


A Manhattan plot, the standard visualisation of GWAS results: each dot is a genetic variant, plotted by genomic position and significance.

In genomics, a genome-wide association study (GWA study, or GWAS), also known as a whole genome association study (WGA study, or WGAS), is an observational study of a genome-wide set of genetic variants in different individuals to see if any variant is associated with a trait. GWA studies typically focus on associations between single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and traits like major human diseases, but can equally be applied to any other genetic variants and any other organisms.

When applied to human data, GWAS compare the DNA of participants having varying phenotypes for a particular trait or disease. These participants may be people with a disease (cases) and similar people without (controls), or they may be people with different phenotypes for a particular trait, for example blood pressure. This approach is known as a phenotype-first approach, in which participants are classified first by their clinical manifestations, as opposed to a genotype-first approach. Each person gives a sample of DNA, from which millions of genetic variants are read using SNP arrays. If there is evidence of association — for example, if one type of variant (one allele) is more frequent in people with the disease — the variant is said to be associated with the disease.

Background

Any two human genomes differ in millions of different ways. There are small variations in the individual nucleotides of the genome (SNPs) as well as many larger variations, such as deletions, insertions and copy number variations. Any of these may cause alterations in an individual's traits, or phenotype, which can be anything from disease risk to physical properties such as height. Around the year 2000, prior to the introduction of GWA studies, the primary method of investigation was through inheritance studies of genetic linkage in families. This approach had proven highly successful for single gene disorders. However, for common and complex diseases, the results of genetic linkage studies proved hard to replicate. A suggested alternative to linkage studies was the genetic association study. This study type asks if the allele of a genetic variant is found more often than expected in individuals with the phenotype of interest. Early calculations on statistical power indicated that this approach could be better than linkage studies at detecting weak genetic effects.

Methods

The most common approach of GWA studies is the case–control setup, which compares two large groups of individuals, one healthy control group and one case group affected by a disease. All individuals in each group are typically genotyped at common known SNPs. The exact number of SNPs depends on the genotyping technology, but is typically one million or more. For each of these SNPs it is then investigated if the allele frequency is significantly altered between the case and the control group. In such setups, the fundamental unit for reporting effect sizes is the odds ratio. The odds ratio is the ratio of two odds, which in the context of GWA studies are the odds of disease for individuals having a specific allele and the odds of disease for individuals who do not have that same allele.

When the allele frequency in the case group is much lower or higher than in the control group, the odds ratio is lower or higher than one, respectively, and would be considered differing from no effect. A p-value is calculated for the data to check if these differing odds are just due to random chance. In GWA studies, this p-value threshold is usually set at 5 × 10−8 to control for the large number of tests being made (the genome-wide significance threshold, roughly a Bonferroni correction for one million independent tests).

Results and applications

The first successful GWAS, published in 2005, studied patients with age-related macular degeneration and found two SNPs with significantly altered allele frequency compared to healthy controls. As of 2017, thousands of GWA studies have examined over 200 diseases and traits, and have found several thousand SNP associations. Large repositories such as the NHGRI-EBI GWAS Catalog curate these associations.

Well-powered GWA studies have several applications. They can identify candidate genes contributing to disease risk, which can then be validated functionally; they can inform drug development by pointing to pathways already druggable; and their results can be combined into polygenic scores that estimate an individual's genetic liability to a trait. GWAS have been particularly productive in cardiology, oncology, diabetes research, and psychiatry, identifying, for example, strong associations with type 2 diabetes, Crohn's disease, and schizophrenia.

Limitations

GWA studies have several issues and limitations that are important to consider. One problem is that most SNPs identified have only small effects on risk, and together they explain only a fraction of the heritability estimated from family studies — the "missing heritability" problem. Another is population stratification: systematic differences in ancestry between cases and controls can produce spurious associations, which must be corrected statistically. GWAS also identify regions rather than causal variants, since genotyped SNPs are usually markers in linkage disequilibrium with the true causal variant, and most associated variants lie in non-coding regions whose function is not immediately clear. Finally, the vast majority of GWAS participants have been of European ancestry, limiting the transferability of findings to other populations.
