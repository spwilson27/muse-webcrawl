The Fortress of Louisbourg was a French fortified town on the Atlantic coast of Cape Breton Island (then Île Royale) in present-day Nova Scotia, Canada. Founded in 1713 after France lost Newfoundland and mainland Acadia to Britain under the Treaty of Utrecht, Louisbourg became the capital of the colony of Île Royale, a major fishing port, trading centre, and naval base guarding the approaches to the St. Lawrence River and Quebec.

History

Named for King Louis XIV, the town was ringed with massive stone fortifications designed in the style of Vauban, built largely under chief engineer Jean-François du Verger de Verville. At its height in the 1750s, Louisbourg had a population of several thousand soldiers, merchants, fishermen, and families, and exported great quantities of cod to France and the West Indies.

The fortress was besieged and captured twice. In 1745, during King George's War, New England militia under William Pepperrell, supported by a Royal Navy squadron, took Louisbourg after a 47-day siege. Britain returned it to France in 1748 by the Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle, to the outrage of the New Englanders. In 1758, during the Seven Years' War, a large British force under Jeffery Amherst and James Wolfe captured it again after a siege of about seven weeks, enabling the British campaign against Quebec the following year. The fortifications were systematically demolished in 1760.

Reconstruction

In 1961 the Canadian government began one of the largest historical reconstructions in North America, rebuilding roughly a quarter of the original town and fortifications using thousands of surviving French archival documents. The Fortress of Louisbourg National Historic Site now operates as a living-history museum with costumed interpreters, period bakeries, taverns, and military drills.
