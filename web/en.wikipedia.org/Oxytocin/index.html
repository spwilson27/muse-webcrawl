Oxytocin

TypePeptide hormone and neuropeptide
StructureNonapeptide (9 amino acids) with a disulfide bridge

Drug formsPitocin (synthetic oxytocin) for induction of labour

Oxytocin is a peptide hormone and neuropeptide normally produced in the hypothalamus and released by the posterior pituitary. It plays a central role in childbirth and lactation, and is also involved in social bonding, sexual reproduction, and other behaviours — which has earned it popular (and somewhat exaggerated) nicknames such as the "love hormone."

Physiology

Oxytocin is a nonapeptide differing by only two amino acids from vasopressin. Neurons in the paraventricular and supraoptic nuclei synthesize it as part of a precursor protein and release it from the posterior pituitary into the blood. During labour, cervical dilation triggers oxytocin release, which strengthens uterine contractions in a positive-feedback loop (the Ferguson reflex); synthetic oxytocin is widely used to induce or augment labour and to prevent postpartum haemorrhage — it is on the WHO List of Essential Medicines. After birth, suckling stimulates oxytocin release, causing the milk let-down reflex. Oxytocin also acts within the brain as a neuromodulator.

Behaviour and research

Studies in animals (famously prairie voles) link oxytocin to pair bonding and maternal behaviour. In humans, intranasal oxytocin experiments have reported effects on trust, empathy, and social cognition, though many such findings have proved difficult to replicate and remain debated. Oxytocin was first isolated and sequenced by Vincent du Vigneaud, who in 1953 achieved the first synthesis of a polypeptide hormone, work that contributed to his 1955 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.
