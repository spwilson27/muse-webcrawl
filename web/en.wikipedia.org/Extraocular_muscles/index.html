Extraocular muscles

The extraocular muscles, or extrinsic ocular muscles, are the seven extrinsic muscles of the human eye. Six of the extraocular muscles, the four recti muscles and the two oblique muscles, control the movement of the eye. The actions of the six muscles responsible for eye movement depend on the position of the eye at the time of muscle contraction. The other muscle, the levator palpebrae superioris, controls raising the upper eyelid.

The four rectus muscles originate from the common tendinous ring (annulus of Zinn) at the apex of the orbit and insert on the front of the eyeball. The superior oblique passes through a fibrous pulley, the trochlea, before inserting; the inferior oblique originates from the front of the orbital floor. The extraocular muscles are among the fastest and most fatigue-resistant muscles in the body, enabling saccades — rapid eye movements that can exceed 400 degrees per second.

Levator palpebrae superioris

The seventh extraocular muscle, the levator palpebrae superioris, originates at the apex of the orbit and inserts into the upper eyelid, which it elevates. It is supplied by the oculomotor nerve. Damage causes ptosis (drooping of the eyelid), a classic sign of third nerve palsy.

Clinical significance

Paralysis of individual cranial nerves produces characteristic patterns of strabismus (misalignment) and diplopia (double vision). Sixth nerve palsy prevents abduction of the eye; fourth nerve palsy impairs depression in adduction, causing difficulty reading and walking downstairs; third nerve palsy produces a "down and out" eye with ptosis and a dilated pupil. Extraocular muscle enlargement is a feature of thyroid eye disease (Graves' ophthalmopathy). Strabismus surgery operates on these muscles to realign the eyes.
