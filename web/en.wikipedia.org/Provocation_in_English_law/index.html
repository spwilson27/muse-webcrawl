Provocation in English law

Provocation was a partial defence to murder in England and Wales until 2009.

Provocation in English law was a partial defence to a charge of murder which, where successfully pleaded, reduced the offence to voluntary manslaughter. It recognised that a person who killed while having lost self-control in response to provocation was less culpable than a cold-blooded killer, though the loss of control did not excuse the killing entirely. The defence was codified in section 3 of the Homicide Act 1957 and was abolished and replaced by a new partial defence of "loss of control" under sections 54–56 of the Coroners and Justice Act 2009, effective from 4 October 2010.

The test under the Homicide Act 1957

Section 3 provided that where there was evidence that the accused was provoked — whether by things done, things said, or both together — to lose his self-control, the question whether the provocation was enough to make a reasonable man do as he did was to be left to the jury. The test therefore had a subjective limb (did this defendant actually lose self-control?) and an objective limb (would a reasonable person have reacted similarly?). In R v Duffy [1949], Devlin J defined provocation as an act or series of acts which would render a reasonable man liable to lose self-control and which did in fact deprive the accused of self-control, stressing that the loss of control had to be sudden and temporary.

Development of the objective test

In DPP v Camplin [1978] the House of Lords held that the reasonable person was to be endowed with such of the accused's characteristics as the jury thought relevant to the gravity of the provocation, including age and sex. Later decisions, including R v Smith (Morgan) [2001] and the Privy Council case Attorney General for Jersey v Holley [2005], debated whether characteristics affecting the defendant's capacity for self-control (such as depression or addiction) could also be attributed to the reasonable person; Holley restored a uniform standard of self-control, admitting only characteristics bearing on the gravity of the provocation. A further controversial line of authority allowed the defendant's own conduct, and even cumulative provocation over a long period ("slow burn", as in R v Ahluwalia [1992]), to ground the defence.

Abolition and replacement

The Law Commission's 2004 report Partial Defences to Murder criticised provocation as confusing, inconsistently applied, and potentially available to defendants who killed in anger over trivial slights or alleged infidelity. The Coroners and Justice Act 2009 replaced it with a defence of loss of control requiring a "qualifying trigger": fear of serious violence, or things said or done of an extremely grave character causing the defendant to have a justifiable sense of being seriously wronged. Sexual infidelity was expressly excluded as a trigger. The defendant must still have lost self-control, but the loss of control need not be sudden.
