Hubble’s law

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hubble’s law, also known as the Hubble–Lemaître law, is the observation in physical cosmology that galaxies are moving away from Earth at speeds proportional to their distance. In other words, the farther away a galaxy is, the faster it is receding. The law implies that the universe is expanding and was a key piece of evidence for the Big Bang theory.

v = H0 D

Here v is the recession velocity of a galaxy, D its proper distance, and H0 the Hubble constant, whose value is roughly 70 km/s per megaparsec, though precise measurement of it remains an active controversy known as the Hubble tension.

History

Georges Lemaître derived the relation theoretically in 1927 from general relativity and estimated its constant from available data. Edwin Hubble published the empirical relation in 1929, using redshift measurements assembled by Vesto Slipher and distances calculated with the help of Milton Humason. In 2018 the International Astronomical Union voted to recommend the name “Hubble–Lemaître law” in recognition of both contributions.

Interpretation

The recession is not motion through space but the expansion of space itself: in the standard Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker model, the scale factor of the universe grows with time, stretching the wavelength of light from distant galaxies (cosmological redshift). Hubble’s law in its simple linear form holds only for distances much smaller than the size of the observable universe.
