Transforming growth factor beta

Transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β) is a multifunctional cytokine belonging to the transforming growth factor superfamily, which also includes activins, inhibins, bone morphogenetic proteins and growth differentiation factors. Three mammalian isoforms — TGF-β1, TGF-β2 and TGF-β3 — regulate cell proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, immune function, wound healing and extracellular matrix production in nearly all tissues.

Structure and activation

TGF-β is secreted in a latent complex with its pro-peptide (latency-associated peptide) and often latent TGF-β binding proteins that anchor it in the extracellular matrix. Activation — releasing mature TGF-β — is triggered by integrins (notably αvβ6 and αvβ8), proteases such as plasmin, thrombospondin-1, acidic conditions and reactive oxygen species. The active ligand is a disulphide-linked homodimer of about 25 kDa.

Signalling pathway

TGF-β binds the constitutively active type II receptor, which recruits and phosphorylates the type I receptor (ALK5). This activates receptor-regulated SMADs (SMAD2 and SMAD3), which complex with SMAD4, enter the nucleus and regulate transcription of target genes. Inhibitory SMADs (SMAD6/7) and non-canonical pathways (MAPK, PI3K/AKT) modulate the response.

Biological roles and disease

TGF-β is a potent suppressor of immune responses — TGF-β1 knockout mice die of widespread inflammation — and drives regulatory T-cell differentiation together with IL-2. It stimulates fibroblasts to deposit matrix, making it central to wound healing but also to fibrosis of lung, liver and kidney when overactive. In cancer it is famously two-faced: it suppresses early tumour growth by inhibiting the cell cycle, yet in advanced cancers promotes invasion, immune evasion and metastasis through epithelial–mesenchymal transition. Mutations in its pathway cause hereditary syndromes including Loeys–Dietz syndrome and hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia (via related receptors). TGF-β blockade is an active target of drug development in oncology and fibrosis.
