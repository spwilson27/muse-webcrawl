Text (literary theory)

In literary theory, a text is any object that can be "read", whether a work of literature, a street sign, an arrangement of buildings, or a style of clothing. The concept, developed in semiotics and post-structuralism, liberated the word from its association with written documents alone: any coherent set of signs can be treated as a text.

Development

Roland Barthes distinguished the "work" — a physical object held in the hand — from the "text", a methodological field produced in the act of reading. His 1967 essay "The Death of the Author" argued that meaning is not fixed by an author's intention but created by readers, giving the concept wide influence in literary studies.

Intertextuality

Julia Kristeva coined the term intertextuality to describe how every text is a mosaic of quotations from other texts. Texts are therefore never self-contained; interpretation involves tracing their relations to genres, conventions, and earlier works.

Criticism

Critics have objected that the broad definition makes "text" so inclusive as to lose precision, and that reader-centred theories underplay historical and authorial context. Nonetheless the term remains foundational vocabulary in literary and cultural studies.
