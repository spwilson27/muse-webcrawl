Isoperimetric inequality

In mathematics, the isoperimetric inequality states that, of all closed curves in the plane of a given perimeter, the circle encloses the largest area; equivalently, of all regions of a given area, the circle has the smallest perimeter. For a plane region with area A and boundary length L the inequality reads:

L2 ≥ 4πA,

with equality if and only if the region is a disc. In three dimensions the analogous statement is that the sphere minimises surface area for a given volume: S3 ≥ 36πV2.

History

The problem is ancient — legend attributes knowledge of it to Dido, founder of Carthage — and Zenodorus proved partial results in antiquity. Jakob Steiner gave several elegant proofs in the 1830s using symmetrisation, though they assumed the existence of a maximiser; the gap was filled by later analysts. Hurwitz gave a proof using Fourier series in 1901.

Generalizations

The inequality extends to surfaces of constant curvature, to Riemannian manifolds (where the Cartan–Hadamard and Bishop–Gromov settings give precise analogues), and to higher-dimensional and anisotropic settings relevant to geometry, partial differential equations and materials science. Related concepts include Bonnesen's inequality, which quantifies the deficit, and the notion of isoperimetric dimension of a graph or manifold.
