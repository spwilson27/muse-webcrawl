Single-nucleotide polymorphism

A single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP; pronounced "snip") is a germline substitution of a single nucleotide at a specific position in the genome that is present in a sufficiently large fraction of the population — conventionally at least 1%. SNPs are the most common type of genetic variation among people: the human genome contains well over 100 million catalogued SNPs, occurring on average about once every 300 nucleotides.

Types and effects

Most SNPs lie in non-coding regions and have no effect on health or development, but those in or near genes can influence gene function or expression. A SNP within a coding sequence may be synonymous (not changing the amino-acid sequence) or nonsynonymous; a missense change alters one amino acid, while a nonsense change creates a premature stop codon. SNPs in regulatory regions can alter transcription-factor binding or splicing. Well-known examples include the SNP responsible for sickle cell trait in the HBB gene and variants in APOE associated with Alzheimer's disease risk.

Uses in research and medicine

Because SNPs are numerous, stable and easy to genotype in bulk with DNA microarrays, they serve as markers throughout genetics. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) scan hundreds of thousands of SNPs to find variants statistically associated with diseases and traits; haplotype maps such as the International HapMap Project chart how SNPs are inherited together in blocks (linkage disequilibrium). SNPs underlie much of forensic DNA profiling, ancestry testing, pharmacogenomics (predicting drug response) and personalised medicine.

Nomenclature and databases

Reference SNPs are catalogued in databases such as dbSNP, maintained by the NCBI, and identified by "rs" numbers (for example rs6311). Population frequencies vary between ancestries, so allele frequencies are reported for global reference panels such as the 1000 Genomes Project.
