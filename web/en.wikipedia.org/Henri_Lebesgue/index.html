Henri Léon Lebesgue (28 June 1875 – 26 July 1941) was a French mathematician known for his theory of integration, which was published as Intégrale, longueur, aire in 1902 — his doctoral thesis at the University of Nancy. The Lebesgue integral generalised the Riemann integral by organising integration around a theory of measure, and it transformed real analysis, probability theory, and much of modern mathematics.

Life

Lebesgue was born in Beauvais, the son of a typesetter who died of tuberculosis when Henri was young; his mother, a schoolteacher, supported his education. He studied at the École Normale Supérieure in Paris, where he came under the influence of Émile Borel's new work on measure theory. After his doctorate he taught at Rennes and Poitiers, then at the Sorbonne and the Collège de France, to which he was elected in 1921. He was elected to the French Academy of Sciences in 1922.

Mathematical work

Lebesgue's fundamental insight was to partition the range of a function rather than its domain, and to measure the sets on which the function takes given values. His theory of measure extended length and area to a broad class of sets (measurable sets), and his integral could handle highly discontinuous functions, such as the Dirichlet function, that defeat the Riemann integral. He proved the dominated convergence theorem, the Lebesgue differentiation theorem — that a monotone function is differentiable almost everywhere — and (with Fatou) Fatou's lemma. He also contributed to topology, potential theory, Fourier series, and, late in life, wrote critically on the foundations of mathematics education.

The Lebesgue integral and its descendants now underpin probability theory, functional analysis, partial differential equations, and mathematical physics, and the spaces Lp of Lebesgue-integrable functions are central objects of modern analysis.
