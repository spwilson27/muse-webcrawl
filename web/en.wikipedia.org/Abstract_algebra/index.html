Abstract algebra

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Abstract algebra (occasionally called modern algebra) is the study of algebraic structures: sets equipped with operations satisfying specified axioms. Its principal objects include groups, rings, fields, modules, vector spaces, lattices and algebras.

History

Abstract algebra emerged in the 19th century from work on polynomial equations, number theory and geometry. Évariste Galois introduced groups to study solvability of equations; figures such as Richard Dedekind, Leopold Kronecker and later Emmy Noether systematized rings, ideals and modules. Noether's structural approach in the 1920s, codified in van der Waerden's Moderne Algebra (1930), gave the subject its present form.

Major structures

Group theory studies sets with a single associative operation having identity and inverses. Ring theory studies structures with addition and multiplication modelled on the integers; field theory studies rings in which every non-zero element has a multiplicative inverse, such as the rationals and reals. Category theory and universal algebra provide language for comparing structures across the field.

Applications

Abstract algebra underlies coding theory, cryptography, crystallography, quantum mechanics and theoretical computer science, including the theory of abstract data types.
