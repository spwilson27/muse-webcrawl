Critical thinking

Critical thinking is the analysis of available facts, evidence, observations, and arguments in order to form a judgement by the application of rational, skeptical, and unbiased analyses and evaluation. In modern times, the use of the phrase may be traced to John Dewey, who used the phrase "reflective thinking," which he defined as "active, persistent and careful consideration of any belief or supposed form of knowledge in the light of the grounds that support it, and the further conclusions to which it tends."

The core processes of critical thinking.

Definitions

Traditionally, critical thinking has been variously defined as follows: "the process of actively and skillfully conceptualizing, applying, analyzing, synthesizing, and/or evaluating information gathered from, or generated by, observation, experience, reflection, reasoning, or communication, as a guide to belief and action" (Scriven & Paul, 1987). The National Council for Excellence in Critical Thinking defines it as the "intellectually disciplined process of actively and skillfully conceptualizing, applying, analyzing, synthesizing, and/or evaluating information gathered from, or generated by, observation, experience, reflection, reasoning, or communication, as a guide to belief and action."

History

The roots of critical thinking are as ancient as its name suggests: they can be traced in the teachings and practices of Socrates 2,500 years ago, who discovered by a method of probing questioning that people could not rationally justify their confident claims to knowledge. In the Middle Ages, thinkers such as Thomas Aquinas systematized critical thinking as part of scholastic method. In the modern era, Francis Bacon, René Descartes, and John Locke each advanced forms of disciplined inquiry. In the 20th century, John Dewey's work on reflective thinking and Edward Glaser's 1941 experiments on the ability to think critically laid foundations for critical thinking as an educational goal.

Skills

The list of core critical thinking skills includes observation, interpretation, analysis, inference, evaluation, explanation, and metacognition. According to a 1990 consensus statement by the American Philosophical Association's Delphi Project, the ideal critical thinker is "habitually inquisitive, well-informed, trustful of reason, open-minded, flexible, fair-minded in evaluation, honest in facing personal biases, prudent in making judgments, willing to reconsider, clear about issues, orderly in complex matters, diligent in seeking relevant information, reasonable in the selection of criteria, focused in inquiry, and persistent in seeking results which are as precise as the subject and the circumstances of inquiry permit."

Education

Critical thinking is widely promoted in education policy. In the United States and elsewhere, it is listed among the goals of schooling, and researchers distinguish between teaching critical thinking as a set of general skills and embedding it within subject-matter instruction. Studies suggest that explicit instruction in reasoning strategies, combined with practice, improves students' ability to evaluate arguments and evidence.
