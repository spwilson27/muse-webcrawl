Storm surge

A storm surge is an abnormal rise of seawater level generated by a storm — usually a tropical cyclone or intense extratropical low — over and above the predicted astronomical tide. Caused mainly by wind stress pushing water onshore and, to a lesser extent, by the low central pressure of the storm, surges are typically the most destructive and deadly component of a landfalling cyclone, producing flooding that can extend many kilometres inland over low-lying coasts.

Mechanism

Strong onshore winds pile surface water against the coast (wind setup), while the drop in atmospheric pressure raises the sea surface by roughly one centimetre per millibar (the inverse barometer effect). The surge is amplified by shallow offshore bathymetry, funnel-shaped bays and estuaries, and coincidence with high tide — the combination being termed the "storm tide". Breaking waves add a further, localised "wave setup" and run-up.

Historic events

The 1970 Bhola cyclone's surge of up to about 10 metres killed an estimated 300,000–500,000 people in the Ganges Delta, the deadliest tropical cyclone on record. The North Sea flood of 1953 drowned more than 2,500 people in the Netherlands and England and prompted the Dutch Delta Works and the Thames Barrier. Hurricane Katrina (2005) drove a surge of up to 8.5 metres into the Mississippi coast, and Typhoon Haiyan (2013) devastated Tacloban in the Philippines with a surge of 5–7 metres.

Forecasting and defence

Numerical models such as SLOSH forecast surge heights for evacuation planning. Defences include sea walls, storm surge barriers, raised land, mangrove and wetland buffers, and early-warning and evacuation systems, which have greatly reduced surge mortality in Bangladesh since 1970. Sea-level rise from climate change is expected to increase the frequency of extreme surge flooding.
