Inflation targeting

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Inflation targeting is a monetary policy framework in which a central bank publicly announces a numerical target for the inflation rate over a stated horizon, and steers policy — principally through interest rates — to achieve it. The target is usually expressed as a point (commonly 2 per cent) or a range for consumer price inflation, and the bank accounts publicly for misses.

History

New Zealand pioneered the framework in 1990, when the Reserve Bank of New Zealand was given a statutory target under an agreement with the finance minister. Canada (1991), the United Kingdom (1992), Sweden and Australia (1993) followed, and the approach spread to many emerging economies including Brazil, Chile, South Africa, and India. The United States Federal Reserve formally adopted a 2 per cent longer-run goal in 2012, and the European Central Bank pursues a symmetric 2 per cent target over the medium term.

How it works

Because monetary policy affects inflation with long lags, inflation targeters are forecast targeters: they set interest rates so that projected inflation converges to the target over roughly one to three years. Transparency is central — published forecasts, minutes, and inflation reports anchor the expectations of firms and households, which itself helps keep wage and price setting consistent with the target. "Flexible" inflation targeting also weighs output and employment fluctuations rather than pursuing the target at any cost.

Criticism

Critics argue that inflation targeting can neglect asset-price booms and financial instability — a charge sharpened by the 2008 financial crisis — that strict targets may require excessive output sacrifice, and that supply shocks (such as energy prices) may make targets temporarily unattainable, as seen in the post-2021 global inflation surge. Alternatives and complements proposed include price-level targeting, nominal GDP targeting, and average inflation targeting, adopted by the Federal Reserve in 2020.
