The United States Armed Forces are the military forces of the United States. They consist of six service branches: the Army, Marine Corps, Navy, Air Force, Space Force and Coast Guard. The President is commander-in-chief; the Department of Defense, headed by the Secretary of Defense, is the principal organ of military policy, while the Coast Guard operates under the Department of Homeland Security in peacetime.

History

The armed forces trace their origins to the Continental Army, Navy and Marines created in 1775 during the American Revolutionary War. After periods of small standing forces supplemented by militia, the United States built mass armies for the Civil War and the two World Wars. The National Security Act of 1947 created the modern structure — an independent Air Force and a unified defense establishment — and the Goldwater–Nichols Act of 1986 strengthened joint operations under combatant commands. Conscription ended in 1973; since then the force has been all-volunteer.

Organization

Operational forces are assigned to eleven unified combatant commands (such as CENTCOM, EUCOM, INDOPACOM, NORTHCOM, SOUTHCOM, AFRICOM, plus functional commands for special operations, strategic deterrence, transportation, cyber and space), whose commanders report to the President through the Secretary of Defense. The Chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff is the principal military adviser. Reserve components include the Army and Air National Guard, which also serve state governors.

Scale and posture

With about 1.3 million active-duty personnel and the world's largest defense budget — greater than the next several countries combined — the United States maintains a global posture of overseas bases and alliances including NATO, and bilateral treaties with Japan, South Korea and Australia. It operates 11 nuclear-powered aircraft carriers and the largest air forces, and together with Russia holds the majority of the world's nuclear warheads, organized in a triad of land-based missiles, submarines and bombers.
