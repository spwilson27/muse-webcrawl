The saxhorn is a family of valved brass instruments with conical bores and upward-pointing bells, developed by the Belgian instrument maker Adolphe Sax in Paris in the 1840s. Sax patented the family in 1845. Saxhorns form the horn section of the British-style brass band and were widely adopted by military bands in France, Britain, and the United States during the nineteenth century.

Family members

The saxhorns were designed as a homogeneous family spanning the full range of a band, much like Sax's saxophone family. From highest to lowest, members include the soprano (in E♭, corresponding to the modern soprano cornet family), alto/tenor horn (in E♭), baritone (in B♭), euphonium (in B♭), and bass and contrabass tubas. Most have three piston valves, and their conical bore gives a mellow tone distinct from the brighter, more cylindrical cornet and trumpet.

History

Sax promoted the saxhorns as standardized instruments for French military bands, and in the famous 1845 "battle of the bands" on the Champ de Mars in Paris, his instrumentation prevailed over the traditional band of his rival. The instruments spread rapidly: in the United States, saxhorns—including over-the-shoulder models whose bells pointed backward toward troops marching behind—were the dominant brass instruments of Civil War bands. In Britain, the E♭ tenor horn and B♭ baritone remain standard members of brass bands, while the euphonium—originally the tenor saxhorn's descendant—became a prominent solo instrument.
