Canning

PurposeFood preservation
InventorNicolas Appert (process, 1809); Peter Durand (tin can patent, 1810)
ProcessSealing food in airtight containers and heat-sterilising
Related methodsHome canning in glass jars; retort pouches

Canning is a method of food preservation in which food is processed and sealed in an airtight container (jars like Mason jars, and steel and tin cans). Canning provides a shelf life that typically ranges from one to five years, although under specific circumstances, it can be much longer.


Sealed cans and jars after heat processing.

The process was developed in response to a prize offered by the French government in 1795 for a way to preserve food for its armies and navy. Nicolas Appert, a Parisian confectioner, won the prize in 1809 after demonstrating that food heated and sealed in glass bottles kept for long periods, though the underlying microbiology was not understood until the work of Louis Pasteur decades later.

History

In 1810 the British merchant Peter Durand patented the use of tin-coated iron cans instead of bottles. Bryan Donkin and others commercialised the method in Britain, supplying canned meat and vegetables to the Royal Navy. Early cans were thick and had to be opened with hammer and chisel; the familiar thin-steel can and the can opener (patented in the 1850s–1870s) came later. In the United States, Gail Borden's canned condensed milk (1856) and the demands of the Civil War spurred the industry, and companies such as Campbell's and Heinz built mass markets for canned soups, vegetables, fish, and fruit.

Process

Commercial canning involves cleaning and preparing the food, filling containers, exhausting air, sealing hermetically, and heat processing in retorts (large pressure vessels). The time and temperature are calculated to destroy spoilage organisms and pathogens — most critically the spores of Clostridium botulinum, which require temperatures above boiling and hence pressure processing for low-acid foods. Modern variants include aseptic processing, in which sterilised food is filled into sterile containers, and flexible retort pouches.

Home canning

Home canning uses glass jars with two-piece lids. High-acid foods (fruits, pickles, jams) can be processed in a boiling-water bath, while low-acid foods (vegetables, meats) require a pressure canner to reach safe temperatures. Guidance from authorities such as the USDA emphasises tested recipes and processing times, because improperly home-canned food is a recognised cause of botulism.

Safety and nutrition

Commercial canning is among the safest forms of food processing; failures are rare but have included notable botulism incidents. Canned foods retain most nutrients, though heat-sensitive vitamins are partly reduced, and some products are packed with added salt or syrup. Concerns have been raised about bisphenol A (BPA) in can linings, prompting a shift to alternative coatings.
