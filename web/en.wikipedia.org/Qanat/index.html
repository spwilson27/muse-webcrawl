A qanat or kārīz (Persian: قنات) is a water supply system developed in ancient Persia for transporting water from an aquifer under a hill, by gravity, over long distances to a surface canal. It consists of a gently sloping underground tunnel dug from the foothills — where a "mother well" taps the water table — to an outlet at a settlement or fields, with vertical shafts at intervals for excavation, ventilation and maintenance. Because the water travels underground, evaporation losses are minimal, making qanats ideally suited to hot, arid climates.

History and spread

Qanats were developed in the Iranian plateau around the early 1st millennium BC, possibly by miners applying drainage techniques. From Persia the technology spread along trade and conquest routes: to Oman (where they are called aflaj), Arabia, Egypt, the Levant, North Africa (as foggara and khettara), Spain, and eastward along the Silk Road to Central Asia and western China, where Turpan's karez system still waters vineyards and orchards. Some qanats extend for tens of kilometres; the Qanat of Zarch in Iran runs over 70 km and is among the world's oldest continuously operating water works.

Construction and operation

Skilled diggers (muqannīs) excavated the tunnel by hand, using the spoil rings around the shafts — visible from the air as chains of craters — as their guide. Flow is purely gravitational and self-limiting: a qanat cannot draw down an aquifer below its tunnel level, giving qanats a degree of sustainability, though modern pumped wells have lowered water tables and dried many up. Communal rules historically governed water shares, maintenance duties and protection zones. In 2016 UNESCO inscribed "The Persian Qanat" on the World Heritage List.
