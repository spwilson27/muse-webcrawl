The Cape of Good Hope (Afrikaans: Kaap die Goeie Hoop) is a rocky headland on the Atlantic coast of the Cape Peninsula in South Africa. Contrary to popular belief, it is not the southernmost point of Africa (that is Cape Agulhas, about 150 kilometres to the east-southeast), nor the meeting point of the Atlantic and Indian oceans, but it marks the southwesternmost point of the African continent and the traditional boundary used by mariners.

History

The Portuguese explorer Bartolomeu Dias rounded the cape in 1488, naming it the "Cape of Storms" (Cabo das Tormentas) for the violent weather he encountered; King John II of Portugal is said to have renamed it the Cape of Good Hope because its discovery promised a sea route to India. Vasco da Gama rounded it in 1497 on the first European voyage to India. The cape route became a vital artery of trade for the Dutch, British, and other East India companies until the opening of the Suez Canal in 1869.

Geography and nature

The cape lies within the Cape Point section of Table Mountain National Park, an area of exceptional botanical diversity in the fynbos biome. Wildlife includes chacma baboons, ostriches, bontebok, eland, and rich marine bird life. Treacherous reefs offshore have wrecked many ships, and the cape is associated with the legend of the ghost ship the Flying Dutchman.
