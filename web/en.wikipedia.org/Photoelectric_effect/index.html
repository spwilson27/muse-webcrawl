Photoelectric effect

The photoelectric effect is the emission of electrons from a material when electromagnetic radiation, such as light, strikes it. Electrons emitted in this way are called photoelectrons.

Observations

First observed by Heinrich Hertz in 1887, the effect presented puzzles for classical wave theory: the kinetic energy of the emitted electrons depends on the frequency of the light, not its intensity; below a threshold frequency characteristic of the metal no electrons are emitted, however intense the light; and emission is essentially instantaneous. Increasing intensity instead increases the number of electrons.

Einstein's explanation

In 1905, Albert Einstein proposed that light consists of quanta (later called photons) of energy E = hν, building on Planck's work. An electron absorbs a whole photon; if the photon energy exceeds the material's work function, the electron escapes with the remainder as kinetic energy. Robert Millikan's precision experiments of 1914–1916 confirmed Einstein's equation despite Millikan's intention to disprove it. Einstein received the 1921 Nobel Prize in Physics primarily for this work, and the effect became key evidence for the quantum nature of light.

Applications

The effect underlies photovoltaic solar cells, photomultiplier tubes, image sensors and photoelectron spectroscopy, a major tool for studying electronic structure of materials.
