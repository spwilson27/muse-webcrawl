Radiation effects

Ionising radiation can damage living tissue; effects depend on dose, dose rate and the tissue exposed.

Radiation effects on health arise primarily from exposure to ionising radiation — radiation energetic enough to remove electrons from atoms and molecules. Ionising radiation damages cells principally by breaking chemical bonds in DNA, either directly or through the creation of reactive free radicals. The resulting biological effects are broadly divided into deterministic effects, which occur only above a threshold dose and increase in severity with dose, and stochastic effects, such as cancer, whose probability is assumed to rise with dose but whose severity does not.

Acute effects

A large dose received over a short period can cause acute radiation syndrome — nausea, vomiting, hair loss, haemorrhage and, at doses above roughly 4 gray to the whole body without treatment, death within weeks. Localised high doses produce skin burns resembling thermal burns. Historical examples include the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki, the Chernobyl disaster of 1986, and criticality and industrial accidents such as the Goiânia accident of 1987.

Long-term effects

Follow-up of atomic bomb survivors by the Radiation Effects Research Foundation has provided the principal data on radiation-induced cancer, showing dose-dependent increases in leukaemia and solid cancers with latencies of years to decades. At low doses the shape of the dose–response relationship is debated; radiation protection standards generally adopt the linear no-threshold model as a prudent assumption.

Protection

Radiation protection rests on the principles of justification, optimisation (keeping doses "as low as reasonably achievable", ALARA) and dose limitation, implemented through time, distance and shielding, and regulated internationally using recommendations of the International Commission on Radiological Protection (ICRP).
