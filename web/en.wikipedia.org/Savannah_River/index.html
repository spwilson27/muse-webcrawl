Savannah River

The Savannah River is a major river in the southeastern United States, forming most of the border between the states of South Carolina and Georgia. Two tributaries of the Savannah, the Tugaloo River and the Chattooga River, form the northernmost part of the border. The Savannah River drainage basin extends into the southeastern side of the Appalachian Mountains just inside North Carolina, bounded by the Eastern Continental Divide. The river is around 301 miles (484 km) long.

Course

The river is formed by the confluence of the Tugaloo and Seneca rivers, now submerged beneath Lake Hartwell. Below Hartwell Dam it flows southeast past Augusta—the head of navigation and the fall line—and then through broad coastal-plain swamps and tidal marshes to its mouth at Tybee Island on the Atlantic, just downstream of the port city of Savannah. Major federal reservoirs on the river include Lake Hartwell, Lake Russell, and J. Strom Thurmond Lake (Clarks Hill Lake), which together regulate flow for hydropower, flood control, water supply, and recreation.

History

The Savannah basin was home to Indigenous peoples for more than 11,000 years, including the mound-building cultures of the Mississippian era and later the Cherokee and Creek nations. The river's name derives from the Savano (Shawnee) people who settled near its lower course in the late 17th century. In February 1733 James Oglethorpe founded the colony of Georgia at Savannah on a bluff above the river. Augusta, founded in 1736, became a major inland trading post and, in the 19th century, an industrial centre powered by the Augusta Canal. The river later carried cotton to Atlantic markets and figured in the Civil War, when Savannah fell to General William Tecumseh Sherman at the end of his March to the Sea in December 1864.

Industry and environment

The Port of Savannah, among the busiest container ports in the United States, depends on a deepened navigation channel in the river's lower 20 miles. On the South Carolina bank lies the Savannah River Site, a Department of Energy reservation built in the 1950s to produce materials for nuclear weapons, now largely devoted to environmental cleanup and research. The river supports fisheries for striped bass and American shad and important habitat for the endangered shortnose sturgeon, whose spawning runs are affected by flow regulation and channel dredging.
