Grace in Christianity

Grace is understood as God's unmerited favour toward humanity

In Western Christian theology, grace is the help given to humans by God because God desires them to have it, not necessarily because of anything they have done to earn it. It is understood by Christians to be a spontaneous gift from God to people — "generous, free and totally unexpected and undeserved" — that takes the form of divine favour, love, clemency, and a share in the divine life of God.

The English word grace translates the Greek charis (χάρις) of the New Testament, meaning favour, blessing, or kindness. Grace is a central concept in the doctrines of salvation and sanctification and was the defining issue of the Protestant Reformation, where Martin Luther and others insisted that salvation comes by grace alone (sola gratia) through faith alone.

Definitions and distinctions

Theologians commonly distinguish actual grace (transient divine helps), sanctifying or habitual grace (a permanent disposition infused into the soul), prevenient grace (which precedes and enables the human response to God), and efficacious grace (which unfailingly produces its effect). In Roman Catholic teaching, grace is a participation in the divine nature, communicated especially through the sacraments, and can be lost through mortal sin. Most Protestant traditions emphasise grace as God's unmerited favour imputed or imparted to the believer through faith in Jesus Christ.

Historical development

The doctrine was shaped by the Apostle Paul's letters, Augustine of Hippo's controversy with Pelagius over free will, medieval scholastic distinctions, and the Reformation debates. The Council of Trent (1545–1563) affirmed that grace is necessary for justification while maintaining a role for human cooperation; Calvinist theology stressed irresistible grace within the "five points" summarised by the acronym TULIP.

Grace and the sacraments

Catholic, Orthodox, and many Anglican and Lutheran traditions teach that grace is conferred through the sacraments, above all baptism and the Eucharist. Other traditions regard the sacraments or ordinances as signs and means of grace rather than automatic channels, and emphasise preaching and faith as the ordinary means by which grace is received.
