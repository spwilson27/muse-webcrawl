Party system

A party system is a concept in comparative political science concerning the system of government by political parties in a democratic country. The idea is that political parties control government, enjoy a stable existence, and have a predictable pattern of interaction with one another over time. Party systems are classified by the number of relevant parties and by the nature of competition between them.

Types

Giovanni Sartori's influential classification distinguishes one-party systems, in which only a single party can effectively hold office; hegemonic and predominant party systems, in which one party repeatedly wins despite legal competition; two-party systems, in which two major parties alternate in government, as in the United States; moderate pluralism, with three to five relevant parties and centripetal competition; and polarized pluralism, with many parties and strong ideological distance. Maurice Duverger's law holds that plurality electoral systems tend to produce two-party systems, while proportional representation favours multi-party systems.

Stability and change

Party systems may remain frozen for decades around the social cleavages that produced them, as Seymour Martin Lipset and Stein Rokkan argued for Western Europe, but they can also be reshaped by realignments, new parties, and changes in electoral rules. Political scientists measure fragmentation with indices such as the effective number of parties, and study party system institutionalization — the stability of party organizations, labels, and patterns of competition — as a condition for democratic consolidation.
