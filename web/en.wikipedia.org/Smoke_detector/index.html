Smoke detector

A typical residential smoke alarm

A smoke detector is a device that senses smoke, typically as an indicator of fire. Domestic smoke detectors — usually called smoke alarms — sound an audible warning when smoke is detected, while detectors in commercial and industrial buildings are generally connected to a central fire alarm system. Most jurisdictions require working smoke alarms in homes, and they are credited with substantially reducing deaths from residential fires.

Sensing technologies

Two main sensing methods are used. Ionization detectors contain a tiny amount of radioactive americium-241, which ionises air in a chamber; smoke particles disrupt the resulting current and trigger the alarm. They respond fastest to fast-flaming fires. Photoelectric detectors aim a light beam into a sensing chamber; smoke entering the chamber scatters light onto a photocell. They respond better to slow, smouldering fires and are less prone to nuisance alarms from cooking steam. Combination units and dual-sensor models use both principles, and many modern alarms also include carbon monoxide detection.

History and regulation

The first automatic electric fire alarm was patented in 1890 by Francis Robbins Upton, an associate of Thomas Edison. Ionization detectors were developed in Switzerland in the 1940s, but affordable single-station home detectors only became widespread in the 1970s, following Duane Pearsall's battery-powered SmokeGard. Building codes in many countries now mandate alarms on every level of a dwelling and inside or near bedrooms, with newer sealed 10-year battery models reducing the problem of disabled units.
