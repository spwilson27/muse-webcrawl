The singular is a grammatical number denoting a quantity of one — a single person, thing, or concept — in contrast to plural (more than one) and, in some languages, dual (two), trial (three) or paucal (a few). Singular forms are used for nouns, pronouns, determiners and verbs, which in many languages must agree with the number of the subject.

Marking

In English the singular of most nouns is the unmarked base form: book, house, idea. The plural is typically formed by adding -s or -es. Singular verb forms in the present tense, however, often take the ending: she runs, it is. Some English nouns are singular in form but plural in sense (collective nouns), and a few (such as scissors) are plural in form despite denoting single objects.

Across languages

Languages vary greatly in how they treat number. Classical Arabic, Slovenian and Scottish Gaelic distinguish a dual number alongside singular and plural. Many East Asian languages, such as Mandarin Chinese and Japanese, do not inflect nouns for number at all, using numerals and classifiers instead. In languages with rich inflection, such as Latin, Russian and Sanskrit, singular forms occur in all cases and genders and govern corresponding singular verb endings.

Singular "they"

English has for centuries used they, them and their with singular reference for a person of unspecified gender, a usage known as the singular they. It is now widely accepted in dictionaries and style guides, though historically it attracted prescriptive criticism.
