The Atlantic puffin (Fratercula arctica), also known as the common puffin, is a species of seabird in the auk family. It breeds in large colonies on coastal cliffs and islands in the North Atlantic, from northwestern Russia and Scandinavia to Iceland, the British Isles, and northeastern North America. It is easily recognised by its black-and-white plumage and its large, triangular, brightly coloured bill, which shows vivid orange-red colouring during the breeding season.

Description and behaviour

The Atlantic puffin is about 28–34 cm (11–13 in) long with a wingspan of roughly 47–63 cm. Its bill can hold a dozen or more small fish crosswise, thanks to spines on the upper mandible and a rough tongue; a record load exceeded 60 fish. Puffins are excellent swimmers that "fly" underwater with their wings to chase prey such as sand eels, herring and capelin, and they are fast but awkward fliers, beating their wings up to 400 times per minute.

Breeding

Puffins are highly social and nest in burrows dug into grassy slopes or among rocks. Each pair — puffins are largely monogamous and often return to the same burrow and mate for years — raises a single chick, called a puffling, fed by both parents. After the breeding season, adults disperse across the open North Atlantic, moulting their colourful bill plates and spending the winter at sea.

Conservation

The species is listed as Vulnerable by the IUCN. Numbers have declined in parts of its range because of shortages of prey fish linked to warming seas, overfishing, introduced predators such as rats at some colonies, and historical hunting. Iceland hosts the world's largest breeding population, and the puffin is an unofficial emblem of the country.
