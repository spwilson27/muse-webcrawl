Tamarind

Tamarind (Tamarindus indica) is a leguminous tree bearing edible fruit that is indigenous to tropical Africa and widely naturalized in tropical Asia and Latin America. It is the sole species of the monotypic genus Tamarindus, in the family Fabaceae. The fruit pulp is a staple souring agent in the cuisines of South and Southeast Asia, Mexico, and the Caribbean.

Description

The tamarind tree grows to about 24 metres in height, with a dense, spreading crown of feathery, pinnate leaves. Its inconspicuous yellow-red flowers give rise to brown, pod-like fruits 12–15 cm long, each containing one to twelve hard seeds embedded in a fibrous, sticky pulp that turns from sharply acidic when young to sweet-sour when ripe.

Culinary uses

Tamarind pulp is used in Worcestershire sauce, in dishes such as pad thai and many Indian curries and chutneys, in the Mexican drink agua de tamarindo, and in candies throughout the tropics. Young leaves and flowers are eaten in salads in the Philippines and Thailand. The pulp is rich in tartaric acid, giving it its characteristic sourness, and it keeps well when compressed into blocks.

Other uses

The hard, durable wood is used for furniture and carving. Tamarind seed polysaccharide is used as a thickener and emulsifier in the food and pharmaceutical industries. In traditional medicine across Asia and Africa, preparations of the fruit, bark, and leaves are used for fevers, digestive complaints, and wound care.

Cultivation

The tree is drought-tolerant, deep-rooted, and long-lived, thriving in semi-arid tropics with a distinct dry season. India is the world's largest producer, followed by Thailand and Mexico. Trees grown from seed may take a decade to bear heavily; grafted varieties fruit sooner.
