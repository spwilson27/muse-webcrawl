Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) is a microscopy technique in which a beam of electrons is transmitted through a specimen to form an image. The specimen is most often an ultrathin section less than 100 nm thick, or a suspension on a grid. An image is formed from the interaction of the electrons with the sample as the beam is transmitted through it, and is magnified and focused onto a detector or camera.

Because electrons accelerated to hundreds of kilovolts have picometre-scale de Broglie wavelengths, TEMs can resolve structures far below the diffraction limit of light microscopes, routinely imaging atomic columns in crystals.

History

The first TEM was built by Max Knoll and Ernst Ruska in Berlin in 1931–32; by 1933 Ruska had exceeded the resolution of the light microscope, and commercial instruments followed from Siemens in 1938–39. Ruska received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1986 for this work.

Instrumentation

An electron gun (tungsten filament, LaB6, or field-emission source) generates the beam. Electromagnetic condenser lenses focus it onto the specimen; the objective lens forms the image, and intermediate and projector lenses magnify it. The whole column is held under vacuum to prevent scattering by gas molecules. Modern instruments add aberration correctors, energy filters, and direct electron detectors.

Imaging modes and analysis

Beyond conventional bright-field and dark-field imaging, TEM supports selected-area electron diffraction, high-resolution lattice imaging, scanning TEM (STEM) with atomic-number-sensitive HAADF contrast, and spectroscopic methods such as EELS and EDS for elemental analysis. Cryo-electron microscopy—imaging flash-frozen biological specimens—revolutionised structural biology and was recognised by the 2017 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

Specimen preparation

Samples must be electron-transparent: biological material is fixed, stained with heavy metals, and sectioned with an ultramicrotome; materials samples are polished and ion-milled or prepared as nanoparticles dispersed on carbon films. Beam damage and preparation artefacts are constant concerns.
