Soup

Soup is a primarily liquid food, generally served warm or hot (but may be cool or cold), that is made by combining ingredients of meat, vegetables or grains with stock, milk, or water. Hot soups are characterised by boiling solid ingredients in liquids in a pot until the flavours are extracted, forming a broth. Soups are similar to stews, and in some cases there may not be a clear distinction between the two; however, soups generally have more liquid (broth) than stews.

History

Evidence of soup-making dates back to about 20,000 BC, with the invention of waterproof cooking vessels. The word "soup" derives from the French soupe, ultimately from a Germanic root meaning the bread on which broth was poured. In 18th-century Paris, restorative broths sold by street vendors gave rise to the word "restaurant." Condensed canned soup was commercialised in the late 19th century by the Campbell Soup Company, founded by Joseph A. Campbell and later transformed by John T. Dorrance's condensed-soup process of 1897.

Types

French classifications divide soups into clear soups (such as bouillon and consommé) and thick soups (purées, bisques, veloutés and cream soups). Well-known soups around the world include borscht (Eastern Europe), gazpacho (Spain, served cold), miso soup (Japan), phở (Vietnam), tom yum (Thailand), minestrone (Italy), clam chowder (New England), gumbo (Louisiana) and chicken soup, a traditional remedy for colds in many cultures.

Ingredients and preparation

Most soups begin with a flavoured liquid base — stock or broth made from meat, bones, fish or vegetables — often with a mirepoix of onion, carrot and celery. Grains, pasta, legumes and dumplings may be added for substance. Chilled soups such as vichyssoise are served cold.
