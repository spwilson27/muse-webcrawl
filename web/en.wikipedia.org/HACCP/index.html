HACCP

Hazard analysis and critical control points, or HACCP (ˈhæsʌp/), is a systematic preventive approach to food safety from biological, chemical, and physical hazards in production processes that can cause the finished product to be unsafe. It designs measures to reduce these risks to a safe level. In this manner, HACCP attempts to avoid hazards rather than inspecting finished products for the effects of those hazards.

History

HACCP was developed in the 1960s by the Pillsbury Company working with NASA and the United States Army Natick laboratories, to guarantee safe food for manned space flights. The system was presented publicly in 1971 and gradually adopted by regulators. In the United States, HACCP plans are mandatory for meat, poultry, seafood, and juice processing under USDA and FDA rules; the Codex Alimentarius incorporates HACCP into international food hygiene standards.

The seven principles

  1. Conduct a hazard analysis.
  2. Identify critical control points (CCPs).
  3. Establish critical limits for each CCP.
  4. Establish monitoring procedures.
  5. Establish corrective actions.
  6. Establish verification procedures.
  7. Establish record-keeping and documentation procedures.

Application

A HACCP plan is specific to each product and process line. Teams map the flow of production, identify where hazards can be prevented, eliminated, or reduced to acceptable levels—for example, cooking temperatures, refrigeration, and metal detection—and document monitoring. HACCP is the foundation of modern food safety management systems such as ISO 22000 and is applied from farms and fisheries through processing, distribution, and catering.
