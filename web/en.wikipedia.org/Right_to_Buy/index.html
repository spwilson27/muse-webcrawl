The Right to Buy is a policy in the United Kingdom that gives secure tenants of councils and some housing associations the legal right to buy their home at a large discount. It was introduced nationally by the Housing Act 1980, a flagship policy of Margaret Thatcher's Conservative government, although some councils had previously sold houses voluntarily. More than two million homes have been sold under the scheme since its introduction.

History

The Labour Party's 1959 manifesto had floated a similar idea, and local authorities had discretionary power to sell council houses from the 1930s onwards. The 1980 Act made the right statutory: tenants with a qualifying period of residence could purchase their homes at discounts that rose with the length of tenancy — initially between 33% and 50% — and councils were forbidden to refuse. Sales peaked in the late 1980s at over 100,000 per year.

Eligibility and discounts

Tenants qualify after a minimum period as a public-sector tenant (three years in England since 2015). The discount percentage depends on how long the tenant has lived in the property and whether it is a house or a flat, and is capped in cash terms; the cap is higher in London than elsewhere. Buyers who sell within a few years must repay some or all of the discount.

Impact and criticism

Supporters credit the scheme with greatly expanding home ownership and giving working-class families a stake in their communities. Critics argue that councils were prevented from spending receipts on replacement housing, so the stock of social housing shrank sharply; many sold homes later entered the private rented sector at much higher rents. The scheme was abolished in Scotland in 2016 and in Wales in 2019; it continues in England, where reform of discounts and reinvestment rules remains politically contested.
