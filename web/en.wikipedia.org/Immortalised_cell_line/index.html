Immortalised cell line

An immortalised cell line is a population of cells from a multicellular organism that, because of mutation, transformation or artificial manipulation, can proliferate indefinitely in culture rather than undergoing senescence and death after a finite number of divisions. Immortalised lines are essential tools in cell biology, cancer research, drug screening and biotechnology.

Mechanisms

Normal cells are limited by the Hayflick limit, linked to shortening telomeres. Immortalisation can occur spontaneously in cancer cells, through infection with viruses such as Epstein-Barr virus or human papillomavirus, through expression of telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT), or through inactivation of tumour suppressors such as p53 and Rb.

Examples

The first human immortalised line was HeLa, derived in 1951 from cervical cancer cells of Henrietta Lacks. Other widely used lines include HEK 293, CHO cells from Chinese hamster ovary, HeLa, Jurkat and immortalised B cells.

Applications and concerns

Lines provide reproducible material for experiments, vaccine production and antibody production. Concerns include genetic drift, contamination and misidentification, and differences from cells in vivo.
