Fresco (Italian for "fresh") is a technique of mural painting in which water-based pigments are applied to freshly laid, still-wet lime plaster. As the plaster cures it absorbs the pigment and carbonates, binding the colour permanently into the wall surface. Because the painter must finish each section before the plaster dries, work proceeds in daily patches called giornate, whose seams can often be detected by conservators.

Technique

In true fresco (buon fresco), the wall is prepared with rough coats of plaster (the arriccio), onto which the composition is drawn — first as a red-earth sketch (sinopia), later often transferred from a full-scale cartoon by pouncing or incision. A final smooth coat (intonaco) is applied only to the area that can be painted that day. Only pigments resistant to alkaline lime can be used; details unsuited to wet plaster were added a secco (on dry plaster), which is less durable.

History

Fresco-like techniques appear in Minoan Crete, ancient Egypt, and Roman wall painting (as at Pompeii). The medium reached its height in Renaissance Italy: Giotto's Arena Chapel frescoes (c. 1305) revolutionized European painting, and the tradition culminated in Raphael's Vatican Stanze and Michelangelo's Sistine Chapel ceiling (1508–1512) and Last Judgment. Fresco was revived in the 20th century by the Mexican muralists Diego Rivera, José Clemente Orozco, and David Alfaro Siqueiros.

Conservation

Frescoes are vulnerable to moisture, salts, and air pollution; because the image is part of the wall, damaged works may be detached and transferred to supports by techniques such as strappo and stacco.
