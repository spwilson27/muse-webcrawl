Popular sovereignty

ConceptLegitimacy of the state rests on the consent of the people
Key theoristsJean-Jacques Rousseau, John Locke, Thomas Hobbes
Landmark expressionsU.S. Declaration of Independence (1776), French Declaration of the Rights of Man (1789)
Contrasts withDivine right of kings, absolute monarchy

Popular sovereignty is the principle that the legitimacy of a state is created and sustained by the will or consent of its people, who are the source of all political power. It is closely associated with the social contract philosophers, among whom Thomas Hobbes, John Locke, and Jean-Jacques Rousseau held differing views about the nature of the "people" and how their sovereignty is expressed.

Development

Ideas of consent-based government appear in medieval conciliar and resistance theories, but the doctrine took modern form in the 17th and 18th centuries. Locke argued that government rests on a trust from the people, who retain the right to resist tyranny. Rousseau, in The Social Contract (1762), made sovereignty inalienable and indivisible, residing in the people and expressed through the general will. These ideas were revolutionary weapons against the divine right of kings.

In practice

The United States Declaration of Independence (1776) asserted that governments derive "their just powers from the consent of the governed," and the U.S. Constitution opens with "We the People." The French Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen (1789) proclaimed that the principle of all sovereignty resides essentially in the nation. In the antebellum United States the term "popular sovereignty" also acquired a specific and divisive meaning: Senator Stephen A. Douglas's policy, embodied in the Kansas–Nebraska Act of 1854, that settlers of a territory should decide the status of slavery there — a formula that helped precipitate "Bleeding Kansas" and the road to civil war.

Modern usage

Popular sovereignty remains foundational to democratic constitutionalism, typically exercised through elections, referendums, and constituent assemblies, though theorists debate its tensions with constitutional rights, judicial review, and minority protections.
