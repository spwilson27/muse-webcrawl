Chili pepper

Chili peppers (also spelled chile or chilli) are varieties of the berry-fruit of plants of the genus Capsicum, members of the nightshade family Solanaceae, cultivated for their pungency ("heat"). The pungency derives from capsaicin and related capsaicinoids, and is measured in Scoville heat units (SHU). Chili peppers originated in the Americas, were domesticated independently in several regions, and spread around the world after the Columbian exchange, transforming the cuisines of Europe, Africa, and Asia.

Species and varieties

Five domesticated species account for nearly all cultivated peppers: Capsicum annuum (bell peppers, jalapeños, cayenne, poblanos), C. frutescens (tabasco, piri piri), C. chinense (habanero, Scotch bonnet, and the "superhot" group), C. baccatum (ají peppers of South America), and C. pubescens (rocoto). Heat ranges from 0 SHU for bell peppers to over 2 million SHU for varieties such as Pepper X and the Carolina Reaper.

History

Wild chiltepin peppers were gathered in the Americas for millennia, and domestication occurred by about 6000 years ago. Christopher Columbus encountered the fruit in the Caribbean and misnamed it "pepper" after the unrelated black pepper of Asia. Portuguese and Spanish traders carried chilies to Africa, India, Southeast Asia, and China within decades, where they were enthusiastically adopted — becoming essential to Sichuan, Thai, Korean, and Indian cooking, among many others.

Culinary use

Chilies are eaten fresh, dried (as whole pods, flakes, or powders such as paprika and cayenne), pickled, fermented into hot sauces, and smoked (chipotles are smoke-dried jalapeños). They feature in dishes as diverse as Mexican mole, Hungarian goulash (via paprika), Ethiopian berbere, and Korean kimchi and gochujang. Capsaicin is also used in topical analgesics and in oleoresin capsicum ("pepper spray").

Why peppers are hot

Capsaicin deters mammalian herbivores — whose chewing destroys seeds — while birds, which disperse seeds intact, are insensitive to it. In mammals capsaicin activates the TRPV1 receptor, normally triggered by heat, producing the burning sensation.
