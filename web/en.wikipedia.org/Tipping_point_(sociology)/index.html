Tipping point (sociology)


A small change can trigger rapid, self-reinforcing change

In sociology, a tipping point is a point in time when a group — or a large number of group members — rapidly and dramatically changes its behaviour by widely adopting a previously rare practice. The phrase derives from the physical concept of a tipping point, the moment at which an object shifts from one state of stable equilibrium to another.

Usage and history

The term was applied to social phenomena by Morton Grodzins, who in the 1960s studied white flight from American neighbourhoods: once the proportion of Black residents passed a certain "tip point", remaining white residents departed rapidly. Thomas Schelling's models of segregation formalised how individually mild preferences could produce such tipping dynamics.

Popularisation

The concept was popularised for a general audience by Malcolm Gladwell's 2000 book The Tipping Point: How Little Things Can Make a Big Difference. Gladwell described social epidemics as governed by three rules: the Law of the Few (unusual influence of connectors, mavens and salesmen), the Stickiness Factor, and the Power of Context. The phrase has since been applied to climate change, technology adoption, crime trends and epidemiology, though critics argue the idea is often invoked more loosely than the underlying research supports.
