Hōkūleʻa

Hōkūleʻa (Hawaiian for "Star of Gladness", the zenith star Arcturus over Hawaiʻi) is a performance-accurate replica of a Polynesian double-hulled voyaging canoe (waʻa kaulua). Launched on 8 March 1975 by the Polynesian Voyaging Society, her 1976 voyage from Hawaiʻi to Tahiti — navigated without instruments by the Satawalese master navigator Mau Piailug — demonstrated that ancient Polynesians had intentionally settled the Pacific using traditional wayfinding. Hōkūleʻa became a central symbol of the Hawaiian Renaissance and, from 2013 to 2017, completed a worldwide voyage (the Mālama Honua Worldwide Voyage) promoting sustainability.

Design and construction

Hōkūleʻa was designed from historical accounts, petroglyphs, and museum models of Hawaiian canoes, with two hulls joined by crossbeams (ʻaka) and a central platform. Because suitable native timber was scarce, her hulls were built of plywood and fibreglass rather than carved koa — a compromise that drew some criticism but made construction feasible. She carries two crab-claw sails and is steered with a long sweep oar. The canoe has no auxiliary engine on traditional voyages and relies entirely on wind, though escort vessels accompany her for safety and documentation.

The 1976 voyage to Tahiti

The Polynesian Voyaging Society, founded in 1973 by artist Herb Kawainui Kāne, anthropologist Ben Finney, and sailor Charles Tommy Holmes, sought to test whether Polynesian settlement of the Pacific could have been deliberate voyaging rather than accidental drift. Hōkūleʻa sailed from Honolua Bay, Maui, on 1 May 1976. With no surviving Hawaiian master navigators, Mau Piailug of Satawal in Micronesia — one of the last practitioners of traditional wayfinding — guided her by stars, swell patterns, winds, and seabirds, reaching Papeete, Tahiti, on 4 June 1976. Some 17,000 people, nearly half of Tahiti's population, greeted her arrival. The return voyage used conventional instruments, but the outbound passage proved the feasibility of non-instrument navigation across 2,500 nautical miles of open ocean.

1978 capsize and Eddie Aikau

On a 1978 attempt to repeat the Tahiti voyage, Hōkūleʻa capsized in heavy seas south of Molokaʻi, in the treacherous Molokaʻi Channel. The crew clung to the overturned hull through the night; lifeguard and surfer Eddie Aikau paddled away on a surfboard to seek help and was never seen again — an event commemorated in the Hawaiian saying "Eddie would go". The remaining crew were rescued by the US Coast Guard. Safety procedures were overhauled, and the canoe was rebuilt for later voyages.

Later voyages

Under navigator Nainoa Thompson — who reconstructed wayfinding from Mau Piailug's teaching and planetarium study — Hōkūleʻa completed a Hawaiʻi–Tahiti voyage in 1980, then the 1985–87 Voyage of Rediscovery to New Zealand and back, reaching Rapa Nui (Easter Island) in 1999 and Japan in 2007. The Mālama Honua ("care for the Earth") Worldwide Voyage (2013–2017), sailed with the sister canoe Hikianalia, covered some 60,000 nautical miles and 150 ports in more than 20 countries, carrying messages of environmental stewardship and Indigenous knowledge. Hōkūleʻa remains based in Honolulu and continues educational voyages throughout the Pacific.
