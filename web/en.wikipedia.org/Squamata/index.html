Squamata

Squamata is the largest order of reptiles, comprising lizards and snakes. Squamates are distinguished by their skins, which bear horny scales or shields. With more than 11,000 described species, Squamata is also one of the largest orders of vertebrates, second only to perciform fish among living groups.

Characteristics

Squamates are characterised by a movable quadrate bone in the skull, which gives them great flexibility of the jaws, and by hemipenes — paired reproductive organs — in males. Most species shed their skin periodically in patches or in a single piece. Body size ranges from tiny dwarf geckos of about 16 mm to the extinct mosasaurs and the largest snakes.

Evolution and classification

Squamates evolved in the Jurassic period and diversified extensively in the Cretaceous, when the giant marine mosasaurs flourished. The group traditionally includes lizards (Lacertilia), snakes (Serpentes) and the worm-like amphisbaenians. Modern phylogenetic studies show that snakes evolved from within lizard ancestors, making "lizards" a paraphyletic group.

Distribution

Squamates inhabit every continent except Antarctica and most oceanic islands. They occupy deserts, rainforests, grasslands and, in the case of sea snakes and the extinct mosasaurs, marine environments.
