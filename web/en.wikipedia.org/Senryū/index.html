Senryū

Senryū (川柳, "river willow") is a Japanese form of short poetry similar in structure to haiku — three lines with a total of 17 on (sound units) in a 5–7–5 pattern — but treating different subject matter and tone. Whereas haiku traditionally focus on nature and require a seasonal reference (kigo) and a cutting word (kireji), senryū deal with human nature, everyday life, and social foibles, and are often cynical, humorous, or vulgar.

History

The form takes its name from the Edo-period haikai poet Karai Senryū (1718–1790), whose pen name means "river willow". As the judge (tenja) of popular poetry contests called maekuzuke, he selected witty verses responding to a given two-line prompt; his selections were collected in the anthology Haifū Yanagidaru, first published in 1765. These prize verses — the comic tail end of linked-verse traditions — crystallised into the independent form known as senryū.

Characteristics

Senryū need no seasonal word and are judged on wit, irony, and observation of human behaviour: love, work, money, ageing, politics, and embarrassment are typical subjects. In English-language practice, senryū keep the short three-line shape but abandon strict syllable counting, as with English haiku. Modern senryū appear widely in Japan in newspaper columns and competitions, and in the West in journals devoted to short-form poetry.

Distinction from haiku

A common shorthand is that haiku are about nature and senryū about human nature, and that senryū are funny while haiku are serious. In practice the boundary is porous: humorous haiku exist, and senryū can be poignant. Editors often classify a poem by emphasis — if the human foible is the point, it is likely to be published as senryū.
