Anticoagulants, commonly referred to as blood thinners, are chemical substances that prevent or reduce coagulation of blood, prolonging the clotting time. Some of them are naturally occurring in blood-eating animals, such as leeches and mosquitoes, where they help keep the bite area unclotted long enough for the animal to obtain some blood. As a class of medications, anticoagulants are used in therapy for thrombotic disorders. Oral anticoagulants are taken by many people in pill or tablet form, and various intravenous anticoagulant dosage forms are used in hospitals.

Some anticoagulants are used in medical equipment, such as test tubes, blood transfusion bags, and dialysis equipment, to prevent the blood inside from clotting.

Medical uses

Anticoagulants are closely related to antiplatelet drugs and thrombolytic drugs by manipulating the various pathways of blood coagulation. Many anticoagulants are used to treat or prevent deep vein thrombosis (DVT), pulmonary embolism, and myocardial infarction (heart attack), as well as strokes in people who have atrial fibrillation or mechanical heart valves. Their use requires balancing the benefit of clot prevention against the increased risk of bleeding.

Types

Vitamin K antagonists

Warfarin, the best-known coumarin anticoagulant, acts by inhibiting the synthesis of vitamin K–dependent clotting factors II, VII, IX, and X in the liver. It has been in clinical use since the 1950s and requires regular monitoring of the international normalized ratio (INR). Acenocoumarol and phenprocoumon are related agents used mainly in Europe.

Heparins

Heparin and its derivatives — including low-molecular-weight heparins such as enoxaparin — work by activating antithrombin III, which in turn inactivates thrombin and factor Xa. Heparin is given by injection and is widely used in hospital settings and during pregnancy because it does not cross the placenta.

Direct oral anticoagulants

Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) include the direct thrombin inhibitor dabigatran and the factor Xa inhibitors apixaban, rivaroxaban, and edoxaban. They have largely replaced warfarin for many indications because they have predictable effects, fewer food and drug interactions, and do not require routine INR monitoring.

Adverse effects

The principal adverse effect of all anticoagulants is hemorrhage, which may be minor or life-threatening. Specific reversal agents exist for some drugs: vitamin K and prothrombin complex concentrate for warfarin, protamine sulfate for heparin, idarucizumab for dabigatran, and andexanet alfa for factor Xa inhibitors. Heparin can also cause heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT), a paradoxical prothrombotic condition.
