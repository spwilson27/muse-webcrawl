Antinomy

An antinomy sets two apparently provable propositions against one another

An antinomy (Greek anti, "against," and nomos, "law") is a real or apparent contradiction between two conclusions, each of which seems rationally justified or even inescapable. The term is most closely associated with Immanuel Kant, who used it in the Critique of Pure Reason (1781) to show that human reason, when it attempts to answer questions about the world as a whole, generates equally compelling but mutually contradictory arguments.

Kant's antinomies

Kant described four antinomies of pure reason, each pairing a thesis with an antithesis: (1) the world has a beginning in time and is limited in space, versus the world is eternal and infinite; (2) everything is composed of simple parts, versus nothing simple exists; (3) there is free causality, versus everything happens according to natural necessity; and (4) there exists an absolutely necessary being, versus no such being exists. Kant argued that the contradictions arise because reason wrongly applies the categories of experience to things in themselves, and that resolving them supports his transcendental idealism.

Other uses

In logic, the term has been applied to paradoxes such as Russell's paradox, where a contradiction arises within a seemingly consistent system. In theology and law, "antinomy" has also been used more loosely for conflicts between rules or authorities.
