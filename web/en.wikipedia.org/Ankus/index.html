Ankus

A traditional Indian ankus (elephant goad) with a brass handle

The ankus (from Sanskrit aṅkuśa), also known as an elephant goad, a bullhook, or an ankusha, is a traditional tool used by mahouts in the handling and training of elephants. It consists of a hook, usually of steel or bronze, mounted on a handle of wood, ivory, or metal, and terminating in a sharp point.

Description

The ankus typically ranges from about 60 to 90 centimetres (24 to 36 inches) in length, though ceremonial examples may be much longer and richly ornamented. It is used to apply pressure to sensitive points on the elephant's skin, particularly behind the ears and around the head, guiding the animal's movement during riding, work, and ceremonial processions.

History and cultural significance

The ankus has been used in South and Southeast Asia for many centuries and appears frequently in Indian art and mythology. It is an attribute of several Hindu deities, most notably Ganesha, who is often depicted holding an ankus in one of his hands, symbolising the guidance of devotees away from ignorance and toward enlightenment. In Jain and Buddhist iconography it likewise appears as a symbol of wise control and spiritual direction.

Ornamental ankuses made of precious metals and encrusted with gems were status symbols among royalty, and fine historical examples are preserved in museum collections, including the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York.

Controversy

The use of the bullhook has been criticised by animal welfare organisations, which argue that the sharp point can cause pain and injury and that training methods based on it rely on fear and punishment. Several jurisdictions and circuses have restricted or banned its use, and many modern zoos have moved toward protected-contact and positive-reinforcement methods of elephant management.
