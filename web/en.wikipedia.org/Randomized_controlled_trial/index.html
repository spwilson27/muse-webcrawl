A randomized controlled trial (RCT) is an experimental study in which participants are randomly allocated to receive one of two or more interventions — typically a treatment under test and a control, such as standard care or a placebo — and then followed to compare outcomes. Randomisation balances known and unknown confounders between groups on average, making RCTs the most reliable method for establishing causal effects and the reference standard in hierarchies of evidence for clinical medicine.

Design

Essential elements include a defined population and endpoints, allocation concealment, and, where feasible, blinding of participants, clinicians and assessors (double-blind designs) so that expectations cannot bias results. Trials are commonly classified by phase (I–IV in drug development) and analysed on an intention-to-treat basis. Reporting standards such as the CONSORT statement prescribe transparent description of randomisation, attrition and outcomes, and modern trials are usually pre-registered in public registries.

History

Controlled comparisons go back to James Lind's 1747 scurvy trial, but random allocation was developed in the 1920s–30s by R. A. Fisher in agricultural statistics and introduced to medicine by Austin Bradford Hill. The 1948 Medical Research Council trial of streptomycin for tuberculosis is widely considered the first modern RCT. Randomised designs later spread to psychology, education, development economics and technology (A/B testing).

Limitations

RCTs can be expensive, slow and ethically constrained — one cannot randomise exposure to harm — and their results may not generalise beyond the studied population or setting. Together with cost, these limits sustain complementary roles for observational studies and real-world evidence.
