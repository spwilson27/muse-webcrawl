The Goidelic or Gaelic languages form one of the two groups of Insular Celtic languages, the other being the Brittonic languages. Goidelic languages historically formed a dialect continuum stretching from Ireland through the Isle of Man to the Highlands and islands of Scotland.

There are three modern Goidelic languages: Irish (Gaeilge), Scottish Gaelic (Gàidhlig) and Manx (Gaelg). Manx died out as a native community language in the 20th century but has since been revived. Irish and Scottish Gaelic are recognised minority languages with varying degrees of official support.

History

The earliest attested Goidelic is Primitive Irish, known from Ogham inscriptions of roughly the 4th to 6th centuries AD. Old Irish, attested from the 7th to 10th centuries, developed into Middle Irish, from which the three modern languages diverged as a shared literary Classical Gaelic was maintained by learned classes until the 17th–18th centuries. Goidelic was carried to Scotland by Irish settlement of the kingdom of Dál Riata and eventually displaced Pictish across much of Scotland.

Classification

Within Insular Celtic, Goidelic is characterised by "Q-Celtic" treatment of the Proto-Celtic labiovelar *kʷ, which became a plain velar /k/ (written c), as against Brittonic "P-Celtic", where it became /p/. The family comprises, in historical sequence: Primitive Irish, Old Irish, Middle Irish, Early Modern Irish, and the modern languages Irish, Scottish Gaelic, and Manx.

Characteristics

Goidelic languages share initial consonant mutations (lenition and eclipsis/nasalisation), verb–subject–object word order, inflected prepositions, and a system of broad and slender consonants. All three modern languages are considered endangered to varying degrees, though revitalisation efforts continue through education and media.
