Tree line

The tree line is the edge of the habitat at which trees are capable of growing. It is found at high elevations and high latitudes. Beyond the tree line, trees cannot tolerate the environmental conditions, which are usually cold temperatures, insufficient moisture, or thin air and soils. The alpine tree line separates the montane forest from the treeless alpine tundra, while the Arctic tree line separates forest from tundra in the far north.

Causes

Tree growth requires a minimum length and warmth of growing season; as a rule of thumb, trees fail where mean temperatures during the growing season fall below about 6–7 °C. Wind exposure, poor soil development, snow cover patterns, and low soil temperatures also contribute. Near the limit, trees often grow in stunted, deformed krummholz forms before giving way to shrubs and alpine vegetation.

Distribution

The tree line descends from roughly 4,500–4,800 metres on tropical mountains such as the Andes and Mount Kenya to sea level in the high Arctic. In the Southern Hemisphere it is formed largely by southern beeches and occurs at much lower elevations at comparable latitudes, owing to the cooler oceanic climate.

Climate change

Many tree lines have advanced to higher elevations and latitudes with 20th- and 21st-century warming, although responses vary with local conditions; tree line movement is monitored as an indicator of climate change impacts on mountain and Arctic ecosystems.
