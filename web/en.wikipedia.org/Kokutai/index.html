Kokutai (国体, "national body" or "national polity") is a Japanese concept denoting the fundamental character of the Japanese state, centred on the unbroken imperial line and the relationship between the Emperor and his subjects. It became the central ideological doctrine of the Empire of Japan.

Origins

The term gained currency in the late Edo period among scholars of National Learning (kokugaku) and the Mito school, who contrasted Japan's imperial polity with Chinese and Western systems. After the Meiji Restoration of 1868, kokutai was embedded in the Imperial Rescript on Education (1890) and the Meiji Constitution, which vested sovereignty in the Emperor.

Prewar orthodoxy

In the 1930s the concept hardened into state orthodoxy. The government suppressed Tatsukichi Minobe's "emperor as organ" theory in 1935, and the Ministry of Education's Kokutai no Hongi ("Fundamentals of Our National Polity", 1937) taught that Japan was a divine family-state uniquely united under the Emperor. Preservation of the kokutai was a key Japanese concern in the surrender negotiations of 1945.

Postwar

The 1947 Constitution relocated sovereignty to the people and redefined the Emperor as a symbol of the state. The term survives mainly in historical discussion and in debates over national identity and the imperial institution.
