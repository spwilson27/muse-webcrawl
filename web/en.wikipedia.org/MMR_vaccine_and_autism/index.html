MMR vaccine and autism

Claims of a link between the MMR vaccine (measles, mumps, and rubella vaccine) and autism have been extensively investigated and found to have no scientific basis. The scientific consensus is that there is no causal relationship between the vaccine and autism, and that the vaccine's benefits greatly outweigh its risks.

Origin of the claim

The controversy began with a 1998 paper by Andrew Wakefield and colleagues in The Lancet suggesting a possible link between the MMR vaccine, bowel disease, and autism. The study, based on 12 children, was later shown to be fraudulent: an investigation by journalist Brian Deer revealed undisclosed financial conflicts of interest and manipulated data. The Lancet fully retracted the paper in 2010, and Wakefield was struck off the UK medical register.

Evidence

Numerous large epidemiological studies in the United Kingdom, Denmark, Japan, and the United States — including a 2019 Danish study of more than 650,000 children — have found no association between MMR vaccination and autism, including in children at higher genetic risk. Despite this, the claim fueled vaccine hesitancy and contributed to declining MMR coverage and measles outbreaks in several countries.
