Oncology is a branch of medicine that deals with the prevention, diagnosis, and treatment of cancer. A medical professional who practices oncology is an oncologist. The name's etymological origin is the Greek word ὄγκος (ónkos), meaning "bulk", "mass" or "tumor". Oncology is concerned with the diagnosis of cancer, therapy (including surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy and targeted and immune therapies), follow-up of patients after successful treatment, palliative care of patients with terminal malignancies, and screening and prevention in populations and families at risk.

Diagnosis and staging

Diagnosis rests on biopsy with histopathological examination, increasingly supplemented by immunohistochemistry and molecular/genomic profiling that guides treatment choice (e.g. HER2 status in breast cancer, EGFR mutations in lung cancer). Imaging (X-ray, CT, MRI, PET, ultrasound, mammography) and laboratory tests (tumour markers such as PSA) support diagnosis, staging and monitoring. Staging uses the TNM system (tumour size/invasion, nodal involvement, metastasis) to summarise disease extent and prognosis. Cancer is the second leading cause of death globally, with about 20 million new cases and 10 million deaths annually in recent WHO/IARC estimates; the commonest cancers are breast, lung, colorectal, prostate and stomach.

Treatment

Treatment is usually multidisciplinary, planned by tumour boards. Surgery remains curative for many localised cancers. Radiotherapy uses ionising radiation — external beam, brachytherapy, protons — to kill cancer cells while sparing normal tissue. Systemic therapies include cytotoxic chemotherapy; endocrine therapy for hormone-sensitive tumours; targeted agents against specific molecular drivers (e.g. imatinib for CML, trastuzumab for HER2-positive breast cancer); and immunotherapy, notably immune checkpoint inhibitors (anti-CTLA-4, anti-PD-1/PD-L1) and CAR T-cell therapy, which transformed outcomes in melanoma, lung cancer and some haematological malignancies. Treatment intent may be curative (neo-/adjuvant or definitive), life-prolonging, or palliative, and supportive care (antiemetics, growth factors, pain control) is integral.

Prevention and screening

Between 30% and 50% of cancers are considered preventable: tobacco control, reduced alcohol use, healthy weight and activity, vaccination against HPV and hepatitis B, UV protection, and occupational safety. Organised screening — mammography, cervical cytology/HPV testing, colorectal testing, low-dose CT for heavy smokers — reduces mortality for several cancers. Research in oncology spans carcinogenesis and the hallmarks of cancer, early detection (including liquid biopsies), survivorship, and global disparities in access to care.
