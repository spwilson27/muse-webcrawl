Noble savage

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The noble savage is a literary and philosophical concept of a person who has not been corrupted by civilization, embodying the inherent goodness of humanity in its natural state. The English phrase was first recorded in John Dryden's heroic play The Conquest of Granada (1672), and the idea became a stock figure of Enlightenment thought and Romantic literature, used to criticize the artificiality, inequality and moral failings of European society.

Origins and development

Antecedents appear in ancient ideas of a Golden Age and in early modern accounts of the peoples of the Americas, notably in Montaigne's essay "Of Cannibals" (1580), which contrasted indigenous Brazilians favourably with European cruelty. The figure is often associated with Jean-Jacques Rousseau, whose Discourse on Inequality (1755) described natural man as free and compassionate before the corrupting effects of property and society — although Rousseau himself did not use the phrase "noble savage". In literature the type appears in Aphra Behn's Oroonoko, in James Fenimore Cooper's Native American characters, and in Herman Melville's Typee.

Criticism

The concept has been criticized as a romanticized stereotype that denies the full humanity, complexity and historical agency of indigenous peoples, projecting European fantasies onto them — either as a rebuke to civilization or, in its inverse "ignoble savage" form, as a justification for conquest and colonial rule. Modern anthropologists and historians generally treat it as a rhetorical device of European intellectual history rather than a description of any real society.
