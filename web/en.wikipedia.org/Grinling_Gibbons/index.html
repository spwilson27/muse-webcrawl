Grinling Gibbons

Grinling Gibbons (4 April 1648 – 3 August 1721) was a Dutch-British sculptor and wood carver known for his work in England, including Windsor Castle and Hampton Court Palace, St Paul's Cathedral, and other English country houses, churches, and palaces. He is widely regarded as the finest wood carver working in England in the late 17th and early 18th centuries.

Life

Gibbons was born in Rotterdam in the Netherlands to English parents. He moved to England around 1667, initially working in York before settling in London. His talent was discovered by the diarist John Evelyn, who introduced him to King Charles II and to the architect Christopher Wren. Gibbons became "Master Carver in Wood to the Crown" and worked under Wren on St Paul's Cathedral, carving the choir stalls and organ case.

Work and style

Gibbons carved chiefly in limewood, a pale, fine-grained wood suited to intricate undercutting. His signature work consists of cascading festoons of flowers, fruit, leaves, birds, cherubs, and musical instruments rendered with extraordinary naturalism and lightness, often appearing to hang in space. He also worked in stone and marble, producing monuments, statues, and the bronze statue of James II now in Trafalgar Square.

Major commissions included work at Petworth House, Burghley House, Chatsworth House, Blenheim Palace, Kensington Palace, and Hampton Court. At St Paul's Cathedral his carvings in the choir and around the organ are celebrated masterpieces of the English Baroque.

Legacy

Gibbons's workshop trained many carvers, and his style defined decorative wood carving in England for a generation. Horace Walpole later wrote that Gibbons gave "to wood the loose and airy lightness of flowers." His work remains a high point of craftsmanship in limewood carving.
