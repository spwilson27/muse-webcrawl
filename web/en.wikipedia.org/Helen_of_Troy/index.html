Helen of Troy

In Greek mythology, Helen of Troy, also known as Helen of Sparta, was believed to have been the most beautiful woman in the world. The daughter of Zeus and Leda, she was married to King Menelaus of Sparta. Her elopement with—or abduction by—Paris, prince of Troy, is said to have prompted the Trojan War. Christopher Marlowe called her "the face that launch'd a thousand ships."

Mythology

Helen was hatched from an egg after Zeus, in the form of a swan, approached her mother Leda. Courted by suitors from all Greece, she married Menelaus, and the other suitors swore the Oath of Tyndareus to defend her marriage. After the Judgment of Paris, in which Aphrodite promised Paris the most beautiful woman in the world, Paris carried Helen to Troy. The Greek kings, bound by the oath, raised an armada under Agamemnon to bring her back, besieging Troy for ten years.

After the war

In the Odyssey, Helen is found restored in Sparta with Menelaus, though her feelings remain ambiguous and she is shown drugging the wine of her guests. Alternative traditions, notably in Euripides' Helen, hold that only a phantom (eidolon) went to Troy while the real Helen waited in Egypt—a version meant to exonerate her.

Cultural depictions

Helen has been portrayed throughout Western art and literature, from Homer and the tragedians through Marlowe's Doctor Faustus, Goethe's Faust, and modern novels and films. She remains a symbol of beauty, desire, and the catastrophic consequences attributed to both.
