Tax incidence

Tax incidence is an economic analysis of how the burden of a tax is divided between buyers and sellers — and more broadly among producers, consumers, workers, and owners of capital — regardless of who is legally required to remit the tax to the government. Economists distinguish the statutory incidence (who writes the cheque) from the economic incidence (whose real income actually falls).

Elasticity and burden

In competitive markets, the division of the burden depends on the relative price elasticities of supply and demand. The side of the market that is less able to adjust — the more inelastic side — bears more of the tax: if demand is perfectly inelastic, consumers bear the entire tax through higher prices; if supply is perfectly inelastic (as with land in the classic analysis), sellers or owners bear it all. A tax levied on sellers and an equal tax levied on buyers produce the same economic outcome, the same wedge between the price paid and the price received, and the same deadweight loss.

Applications

Incidence analysis underlies debates over who really pays payroll taxes (divided nominally between employer and employee, but largely borne by labour according to most studies), corporation tax (apportioned among shareholders, workers, and consumers), tariffs (borne by importers' customers and, for large countries, partly by foreign exporters), and carbon taxes. General-equilibrium analysis, following Arnold Harberger's work on the corporation tax, traces burdens across sectors as prices and factor returns adjust throughout the economy.

Policy significance

Because statutory assignment does not determine burden, politicians' choices about which side of a transaction to tax are partly presentational; effective tax design and honest distributional accounting require estimating true incidence, which remains empirically contested for many taxes.
