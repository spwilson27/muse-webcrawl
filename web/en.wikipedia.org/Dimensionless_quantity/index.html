Dimensionless quantity

A dimensionless quantity (also known as a bare quantity, pure quantity, or scalar quantity) is a physical quantity to which no physical dimension is assigned; it is a pure number. Dimensionless quantities are defined as the ratio of two quantities with the same dimensions, so their units cancel out. They are widely used in mathematics, physics, engineering and economics.

Examples

Familiar examples include angles measured in radians, counts of objects, proportions and percentages, and the constants π and e. In engineering and physics, characteristic dimensionless numbers capture the behaviour of systems independently of scale: the Reynolds number (inertial versus viscous forces), the Mach number (speed relative to sound), the Froude number, the fine-structure constant α ≈ 1/137 in atomic physics, and efficiency and coefficient of friction in mechanics.

Dimensional analysis

The Buckingham π theorem states that any physically meaningful equation involving a given set of variables can be rewritten in terms of a smaller set of independent dimensionless parameters, called π groups. This underlies the use of scaled physical models — wind-tunnel and ship-towing tests — in which matching the relevant dimensionless numbers reproduces full-scale behaviour.

Units and the SI

In the International System of Units, dimensionless quantities have the derived unit "one" (symbol 1), usually left unwritten. The radian and steradian, units of angle and solid angle, are dimensionless derivatives of metre-per-metre ratios.
