Goitre

A goitre (also spelled goiter) is a swelling in the neck resulting from an enlarged thyroid gland. A goitre can be associated with a thyroid that is not functioning properly, although most goitres in developed countries are not associated with symptoms.

Worldwide, over 90% of goitre cases are caused by iodine deficiency. The term is from the Latin gutteria. Most goitres are benign in nature.

Signs and symptoms

A goitre can present as a palpable enlargement of the thyroid gland at the front base of the neck. It may be associated with symptoms of an overactive thyroid (hyperthyroidism), such as weight loss, palpitations and tremor, or an underactive thyroid (hypothyroidism), such as weight gain, fatigue and cold intolerance. Large goitres can compress the trachea or oesophagus, causing difficulty breathing or swallowing.

Causes

The most common cause worldwide is iodine deficiency, which impairs the thyroid's ability to produce thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3). The pituitary gland responds by releasing more thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), which causes the thyroid to grow. Other causes include Graves' disease, Hashimoto's thyroiditis, multinodular goitre, thyroid cancer, and certain medications such as lithium.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is by physical examination, blood tests for thyroid function and TSH, ultrasound imaging, and sometimes fine-needle aspiration biopsy to rule out malignancy.

Treatment

Treatment depends on the cause and severity. Iodine supplementation can shrink deficiency-related goitres. Other options include levothyroxine, radioactive iodine therapy, and surgical removal (thyroidectomy) when the goitre is large, obstructive, or suspected to be cancerous.

Epidemiology

Iodine deficiency affects roughly two billion people worldwide and remains the leading preventable cause of intellectual disability. Salt iodisation programmes have greatly reduced goitre prevalence in many countries.
