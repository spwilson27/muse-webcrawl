Wound

A wound is any disruption of or damage to living tissue, such as skin, mucous membranes, or organs. Wounds can be the result of trauma — including cuts, blunt force, burns, or bites — or of underlying disease processes such as vascular insufficiency or pressure damage. Wound healing proceeds through overlapping phases of haemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodelling.

Specialty
Emergency medicine, dermatology, surgery
Types
Incision, laceration, abrasion, puncture, avulsion, burn
Complications
Infection, haemorrhage, dehiscence, scarring

Classification

Wounds are classified as open or closed. Open wounds include incisions (clean cuts), lacerations (torn tissue), abrasions (grazed skin), punctures, penetration wounds, and gunshot wounds. Closed wounds include contusions (bruises), haematomas, and crush injuries. Wounds may also be described as acute or chronic, and surgical wounds are graded by their degree of contamination.

Healing

After injury, platelets form a clot (haemostasis); inflammatory cells clear debris and microbes; fibroblasts then deposit new extracellular matrix and granulation tissue forms (proliferation); finally the tissue remodels and gains tensile strength over weeks to months. Healing may occur by primary intention (edges approximated, as with sutures) or secondary intention (wound left open to granulate).

Treatment

Management includes controlling bleeding, cleaning and irrigation, debridement of dead tissue, closure where appropriate, dressings to maintain a moist environment, tetanus prophylaxis, and antibiotics where infection is present or likely. Chronic wounds such as diabetic foot ulcers and pressure ulcers require treatment of the underlying cause.
