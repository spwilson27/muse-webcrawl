Altruism (biology)

In biology, altruism refers to behaviour by an individual that increases the fitness of another individual while decreasing the fitness of the individual that performs the behaviour. Altruism in this sense is different from the philosophical concept of altruism, in which an action would only be called "altruistic" if it was done with the conscious intention of helping another. In the behavioural sense, there is no such requirement.

The concept of altruism was central to Charles Darwin's theory of evolution by natural selection. Darwin noted that worker bees, which are sterile, appeared to sacrifice themselves for the colony, and he described this as "one special difficulty, which at first appeared to me insuperable, and actually fatal to my whole theory."

Overview

The problem of altruism was largely resolved by the development of inclusive fitness theory. In the 1960s, W. D. Hamilton formulated the theory of kin selection, showing that a gene promoting altruistic behaviour can spread if the benefit to relatives, weighted by their relatedness to the actor, exceeds the cost to the actor. This relationship is expressed in Hamilton's rule: rB > C, where r is genetic relatedness, B is the reproductive benefit to the recipient, and C is the reproductive cost to the donor.

Examples

Altruistic behaviour is widespread in the animal kingdom. Vampire bats regurgitate blood meals to roost-mates that failed to feed. Sterile worker castes in ants, bees and termites forgo reproduction entirely. Vervet monkeys give alarm calls that warn others of predators but draw attention to themselves. In meerkats, individuals take turns standing guard while others forage. Belding's ground squirrels, naked mole-rats, and many bird species exhibit cooperative breeding, in which helpers assist in raising offspring that are not their own.

Mechanisms

Several mechanisms have been proposed to explain the evolution of altruism: kin selection, reciprocal altruism (proposed by Robert Trivers in 1971), group selection and multilevel selection, and indirect reciprocity based on reputation. The prisoner's dilemma and other game-theoretic models are used to study the conditions under which cooperative strategies can be evolutionarily stable.
