An iceberg is a piece of freshwater ice more than 15 m long that has broken off a glacier or an ice shelf and is floating freely in open water — a process known as calving. Smaller pieces of floating glacial ice are called "growlers" or "bergy bits". Because the density of ice is about 917 kg/m³ compared with about 1,025 kg/m³ for seawater, typically only around a tenth of an iceberg's volume is visible above the surface — the origin of the phrase "tip of the iceberg".

Formation and classification

Icebergs form where glacier fronts or ice shelves reach the sea. They are described by size (from growlers under 1 m high to "very large" bergs over 75 m high and 213 m long) and by shape: tabular bergs, with steep sides and flat tops, calve from ice shelves, especially around Antarctica, while non-tabular bergs from Greenland and Arctic glaciers are irregular or dome-shaped. Exceptionally large Antarctic bergs are tracked individually; iceberg A-68, which calved from the Larsen C Ice Shelf in 2017, initially covered about 5,700 km².

Hazards and monitoring

Icebergs are a serious hazard to shipping in the North Atlantic and around Antarctica. The sinking of RMS Titanic in 1912 after striking an iceberg, with the loss of more than 1,500 lives, led to the creation of the International Ice Patrol in 1914, which still monitors iceberg danger in the North Atlantic. Modern monitoring uses satellites, aircraft, and drift modelling. Drifting bergs gradually melt and disintegrate; occasionally they are considered as freshwater sources or, conversely, as local hazards to offshore installations.
