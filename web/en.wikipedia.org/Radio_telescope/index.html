Radio telescope

Radio telescope
Directional radio antenna
Used in radio astronomy and spacecraft tracking

A radio telescope is a specialized antenna and radio receiver used to detect radio waves from astronomical radio sources in the sky. Radio telescopes are typically large parabolic ("dish") antennas similar to those employed in tracking and communicating with satellites and space probes. They may be used singly or linked together electronically in an array.

Design

Resolution depends on the ratio of dish diameter to wavelength, so radio dishes are far larger than optical telescopes of comparable resolving power. Signals are collected by the reflector, focused onto a feed antenna, amplified by cryogenically cooled low-noise amplifiers, and digitized for correlation or spectral analysis.

Notable telescopes

Famous instruments include the 500-metre FAST telescope in China (the largest filled-aperture dish), the 100-metre Green Bank Telescope and Effelsberg dish, the 76-metre Lovell Telescope at Jodrell Bank, and the now-collapsed 305-metre Arecibo Observatory, which operated from 1963 to 2020. Arrays such as the Very Large Array, ALMA and MeerKAT combine many dishes; the Square Kilometre Array is under construction in Australia and South Africa.

Interferometry

By combining signals from separated antennas, interferometers synthesize an aperture as large as the greatest separation between them — a technique central to modern radio astronomy and recognized with the 1974 Nobel Prize awarded to Martin Ryle and Antony Hewish.
