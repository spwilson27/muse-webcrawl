Newton's laws of motion

Newton's laws of motion

AuthorIsaac Newton
FieldClassical mechanics
Key quantitiesMass, force, momentum, acceleration

Newton's laws of motion are three physical laws that together laid the foundation of classical mechanics. Published by Isaac Newton in his Principia Mathematica (1687), they describe the relationship between a body and the forces acting upon it, and the body's motion in response to those forces. For objects of everyday size and speed they remain extraordinarily accurate, and they are among the most famous laws in all of science.

First law (law of inertia)

A body remains at rest, or in motion at a constant velocity in a straight line, unless acted upon by a net external force. This principle of inertia — anticipated by Galileo and Descartes — defines the inertial frames of reference in which the other laws take their simplest form and overthrows the Aristotelian idea that motion requires a continuing cause.

Second law

The acceleration of a body is directly proportional to the net force acting on it and inversely proportional to its mass. In its most general form, the net force equals the rate of change of momentum:

F = dp/dt, and for constant mass, F = ma.

The second law is the workhorse of classical mechanics: given the forces, it predicts the trajectory of a particle or system.

Third law

For every action there is an equal and opposite reaction: when one body exerts a force on a second body, the second body exerts a force of equal magnitude and opposite direction on the first. The two forces act on different bodies and never cancel each other on a single object. The third law implies the conservation of total momentum in isolated systems.

Limitations

Newtonian mechanics fails at speeds approaching the speed of light (where Einstein's special relativity applies), at atomic scales (where quantum mechanics applies), and in extremely strong gravitational fields (where general relativity is required). Within its domain — from projectiles to planetary orbits — it remains indispensable to physics and engineering.
