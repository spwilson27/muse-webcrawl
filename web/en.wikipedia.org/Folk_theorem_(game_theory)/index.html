Folk theorem (game theory)

In game theory, folk theorems are a class of theorems describing the multiplicity of Nash equilibria that can arise in repeated games. The name reflects the fact that the results were widely known among game theorists before being formally published. Roughly, the theorems state that if players are sufficiently patient, then any feasible payoff profile that gives each player at least their maximin value can be sustained as a Nash equilibrium of the infinitely repeated game.

Intuition

In a one-shot prisoner's dilemma, defection is the unique equilibrium. When the game is repeated indefinitely, however, players can threaten to punish deviations in future rounds, for example by reverting to defection forever (a "grim trigger" strategy). If future payoffs matter enough — that is, if the discount factor is close enough to one — the threat is credible and cooperation can be sustained even though it is not an equilibrium of the stage game.

Statement

For an infinitely repeated game with discounting, the set of average discounted payoff profiles sustainable in Nash equilibrium converges, as players become arbitrarily patient, to the set of feasible, individually rational payoffs. Versions of the theorem have been proved for subgame perfect equilibrium as well, notably by Fudenberg and Maskin, under conditions on the geometry of the payoff set.

Significance

The folk theorem explains why cooperation can persist among self-interested agents, with applications to collusion in oligopolies, international agreements and social norms. Its very generality is also a limitation: because it permits so many equilibria, repeated-game models often need additional refinements to make sharp predictions.
