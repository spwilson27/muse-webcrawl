Shale gas in the United States

Major US shale plays include the Marcellus, Haynesville, Permian, and Barnett formations.

Shale gas in the United States is an important source of natural gas. The production of natural gas from shale formations, made economical by the combination of horizontal drilling and hydraulic fracturing ("fracking"), transformed the United States from an expected major importer of natural gas into the world's largest producer and, later, a major exporter of liquefied natural gas (LNG).

Innovations in the Barnett Shale of Texas in the 1990s and early 2000s—particularly by Mitchell Energy under George P. Mitchell—demonstrated that gas could be produced commercially from low-permeability shale rock. Production subsequently expanded to the Fayetteville (Arkansas), Haynesville (Louisiana/Texas), Marcellus (Appalachian Basin, the largest US gas field), Woodford, Eagle Ford, and associated gas from the Permian Basin.

Growth of production

Shale gas grew from a small share of US natural gas supply in 2000 to the majority of dry gas production by the 2010s. The resulting abundance lowered natural gas prices, displaced coal in electricity generation (contributing to declines in US carbon dioxide emissions from power generation), and revived petrochemical manufacturing investment along the Gulf Coast.

Environmental and regulatory debate

Shale gas development has been controversial. Concerns include methane leakage, groundwater contamination risks, large water usage, induced seismicity related to wastewater injection, and local air and traffic impacts. The US Environmental Protection Agency and state regulators oversee aspects of drilling and fracturing, while the federal Safe Drinking Water Act's Underground Injection Control programme largely exempts fracturing fluids (the so-called "Halliburton loophole" of the Energy Policy Act of 2005), leaving much regulation to the states.
