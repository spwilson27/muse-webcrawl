Q factor

In physics and engineering, the quality factor or Q factor is a dimensionless parameter that describes how underdamped an oscillator or resonator is. It is approximately the ratio of the initial energy stored in the resonator to the energy lost in one radian of the cycle of oscillation. Alternatively, Q is the ratio of the resonant frequency to the bandwidth of the resonance: a high Q indicates a narrow bandwidth and low energy loss.

Definition

For a resonator, Q = 2π × (energy stored) / (energy dissipated per cycle). For an RLC series circuit, Q = (1/R)·√(L/C); for a parallel circuit the reciprocal formula applies. The Q of a resonant system is related to its damping ratio and to the sharpness of its frequency response: the bandwidth Δf = f₀/Q.

Applications

Q factors are central to radio-frequency engineering, where tuned circuits in receivers require high selectivity; to optics, where the Q of a laser cavity determines its linewidth; to mechanical systems such as tuning forks and quartz crystal oscillators (whose Q can exceed 100,000); and to atomic clocks and particle accelerators. In electrical systems, losses in inductors and capacitors are often specified by their component Q.

History

The concept was developed in the early 20th century in the context of telephony and radio; the letter Q was chosen, reportedly, simply because other letters were taken, though it has been said to stand for "quality".
