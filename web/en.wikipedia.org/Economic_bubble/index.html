Economic bubble

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The classic trajectory of a bubble: stealth, awareness, mania and blow-off phases.

An economic bubble is a period when current asset prices greatly exceed their intrinsic valuation, being the valuation that the underlying long-term fundamentals justify. Bubbles can be caused by overly optimistic market extrapolation, speculation, or excess liquidity. Examples of bubbles include the Dutch tulip mania of the 1630s, the South Sea Bubble of 1720, the Japanese asset price bubble of the late 1980s, the dot-com bubble of the late 1990s, and the United States housing bubble that preceded the financial crisis of 2007–2008.

Causes

Economists disagree on the causes of bubbles and even on whether they can be identified before they burst. Contributing factors discussed in the literature include expansionary monetary policy and low interest rates, financial deregulation, herding behaviour and irrational exuberance among investors, and the "greater fool" dynamic in which buyers purchase assets not for their fundamental value but in the expectation of selling them at a higher price.

Stages

Economist Hyman Minsky described stages of a bubble's development — displacement, boom, euphoria, profit-taking and panic. Jean-Paul Rodrigue outlined a similar pattern of stealth, awareness, mania and blow-off phases, ending with prices falling back below their long-run mean.

Effects

When a bubble bursts, falling asset prices can destroy wealth, trigger bank failures and debt deflation, and lead to recession. Debate continues over whether central banks should attempt to identify and deflate bubbles or confine themselves to cleaning up after they burst.
