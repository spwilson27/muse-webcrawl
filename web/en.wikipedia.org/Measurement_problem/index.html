Measurement problem

In quantum mechanics, the measurement problem is the problem of how — or whether — wave function collapse occurs. Quantum theory describes a system evolving smoothly and deterministically by the Schrödinger equation into a superposition of states, yet a measurement yields a single definite outcome. The theory does not say where the boundary between the quantum system and the classical measuring apparatus lies, nor what physically distinguishes a "measurement."

Formulation

If the apparatus is itself treated quantum mechanically, it becomes entangled with the system in a superposition of pointer states — the problem dramatised by Schrödinger's cat (1935), in which a cat coupled to a quantum event seems to end up in a superposition of alive and dead. John von Neumann showed the collapse could be placed anywhere along the measurement chain without changing predictions, leaving its status ambiguous.

Interpretations

Responses divide by interpretation. The Copenhagen interpretation treats collapse as a primitive feature tied to classical apparatus. The many-worlds interpretation denies collapse: all outcomes occur in branching worlds. Objective-collapse theories (such as GRW) modify the Schrödinger equation with spontaneous collapses. De Broglie–Bohm theory adds definite particle positions guided by the wave function, so no collapse is fundamental. Decoherence explains why interference between outcome branches becomes unobservable, but — as most agree — does not by itself select a single outcome.

Significance

The measurement problem remains the central foundational question of quantum theory, driving research into interpretations, collapse models testable in the laboratory, and the quantum-to-classical transition.
