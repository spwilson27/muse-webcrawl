Underwater acoustics

Underwater acoustics, also known as hydroacoustics, is the study of the propagation of sound in water and the interaction of the mechanical waves constituting sound with the water, its contents and its boundaries. Because electromagnetic waves are rapidly absorbed in seawater, sound is the principal means of sensing and communication underwater.

Propagation

The speed of sound in seawater is about 1,500 m/s — nearly five times faster than in air — and varies with temperature, salinity and pressure. These variations refract sound paths, creating shadow zones and the deep sound channel (SOFAR channel), in which low-frequency sounds can travel thousands of kilometres. Losses arise from spreading, absorption (increasing with frequency), scattering and reflection at the surface and seabed.

History and applications

Leonardo da Vinci noted that ships could be heard at great distances through a tube in the water. The field expanded rapidly with the development of sonar during the two world wars for submarine detection. Modern applications include active and passive sonar, echo sounders and multibeam seabed mapping, fisheries acoustics, acoustic oceanography and tomography, underwater communication, and monitoring marine mammal vocalisations. Ambient noise from shipping, wind and marine life is a major research topic, including its effects on whales and dolphins.
