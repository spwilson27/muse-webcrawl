Pilaster

In classical architecture, a pilaster is a rectangular support that only slightly projects from the wall to which it is attached, with a base, a shaft and a capital like a flattened column. Pilasters are typically decorative rather than structural: they give the appearance of a supporting column and serve to articulate the surface of a wall and define its bays, rather than carrying significant load. A pilaster that returns on itself at the corner of a building or of a wall panel is sometimes called an angle pilaster; a pilaster strip without base or capital is known as a lesene.

History

The pilaster derives from the engaged half-columns and antae of Greek and Roman architecture. Roman builders used shallow rectangular projections to articulate walls, and the form was codified in Renaissance treatises by architects such as Leon Battista Alberti and Andrea Palladio. In Renaissance, Baroque and Neoclassical architecture, pilasters became one of the most common means of organising a facade: they mark the divisions between windows, frame doorways and porticoes, and carry an entablature visually across the front of a building.

Use

Because they project only a few centimetres from the wall, pilasters can give a classical order to an interior or exterior without the expense or spatial intrusion of full columns. In furniture and woodwork, pilasters appear as flat vertical strips flanking cabinet doors, bookcases and chimneypieces. In contemporary construction, pilasters of brick or concrete may also be used structurally, as stiffening piers built into long masonry walls to resist lateral loads.
