Filoviridae is a family of enveloped, negative-sense single-stranded RNA viruses in the order Mononegavirales. The family takes its name from the Latin filum ("thread") for the long, filamentous, often curved or branched shape of its virions. Members of the family include some of the most dangerous known human pathogens, the Ebola and Marburg viruses, which cause severe hemorrhagic fevers with high case fatality rates.

Virology

Filovirus particles are pleomorphic filaments about 80 nm in diameter and typically 790–970 nm long, though much longer forms occur. The genome is a non-segmented negative-sense RNA of roughly 19 kilobases encoding seven genes (NP, VP35, VP40, GP, VP30, VP24, and L, the polymerase). The surface glycoprotein GP mediates attachment and entry; VP40 is the matrix protein driving budding. Fruit bats are considered the likely natural reservoir of ebolaviruses and marburgviruses.

Genera and diseases

The genus Ebolavirus includes Zaire ebolavirus — responsible for the 2013–2016 West African epidemic, the largest on record, with over 28,000 cases — along with Sudan, Bundibugyo, Taï Forest, Reston, and Bombali species. The genus Marburgvirus contains Marburg virus and Ravn virus, first identified in 1967 in laboratory outbreaks in Marburg and Frankfurt, Germany, and Belgrade. Other genera include Cuevavirus (Lloviu virus, found in bats in Spain) and fish-infecting genera such as Striavirus and Thamnovirus.

Containment and countermeasures

Ebola and Marburg viruses are classified as select agents requiring Biosafety Level 4 containment. An Ebola vaccine (rVSV-ZEBOV, marketed as Ervebo) was licensed in 2019 and is used in ring vaccination during outbreaks; monoclonal antibody treatments have also been approved for Zaire ebolavirus disease.
