In mathematics, a group is a set equipped with an operation that combines any two elements to form a third element, in such a way that the operation is associative, an identity element exists, and every element has an inverse. These axioms — closure being implicit in the definition of the operation — define the most fundamental algebraic structure, and the study of groups is called group theory. Groups arise throughout mathematics and science as the formal expression of symmetry: the set of all symmetry transformations of an object, or of all permutations of a set, forms a group.

Definition

A group is a set G with a binary operation · such that for all a, b, c in G: (1) (a · b) · c = a · (b · c) (associativity); (2) there exists an identity element e with e · a = a · e = a; (3) for each a there is an inverse a−1 with a · a−1 = a−1 · a = e.

If the operation is also commutative (a · b = b · a for all a, b), the group is called abelian, after Niels Henrik Abel. The number of elements of a group is its order; groups may be finite or infinite.

Examples

  • The integers ℤ under addition form an infinite abelian group, with identity 0 and inverse of n equal to −n.
  • The nonzero rational (or real, or complex) numbers under multiplication form abelian groups.
  • The symmetries of a regular n-gon form the dihedral group Dn of order 2n — generally non-abelian.
  • The permutations of a set of n elements form the symmetric group Sn of order n!.
  • Invertible matrices under multiplication form the general linear group GL(n).

History

The concept emerged from Évariste Galois's work (c. 1830) on the solvability of polynomial equations by radicals, in which the permutations of the roots determined solvability; from Augustin-Louis Cauchy's theory of permutations; and from geometry, where Felix Klein's Erlangen program (1872) defined geometries by their symmetry groups. Arthur Cayley gave the first abstract definition in 1854. Group theory subsequently became central to algebra, crystallography, particle physics (where gauge groups underlie the Standard Model) and cryptography (where groups such as elliptic curves are used).

Basic concepts

Key notions include subgroups, cosets and Lagrange's theorem (the order of a subgroup divides the order of a finite group), normal subgroups and quotient groups, homomorphisms and isomorphisms, group actions, and the Sylow theorems. The classification of finite simple groups — completed in the early 1980s and spanning tens of thousands of journal pages — is one of the largest achievements of 20th-century mathematics.
