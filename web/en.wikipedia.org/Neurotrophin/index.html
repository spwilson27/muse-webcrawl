Neurotrophin

Neurotrophins are a family of proteins that induce the survival, development and function of neurons. They belong to a class of growth factors — secreted proteins that signal particular cells to survive, differentiate or grow. Neurotrophins have been shown to be released by target tissues, binding to receptors on the terminals of innervating neurons and being transported back to the cell body, where they promote survival.

Members

In mammals, four structurally related neurotrophins have been identified:

All neurotrophins also bind with lower affinity to the p75 neurotrophin receptor (p75NTR), which can mediate both survival and apoptotic (cell-death) signalling depending on context.

Function

During development, neurotrophins regulate the survival of neurons: neurons that fail to obtain sufficient neurotrophin from their targets undergo programmed cell death, a process that helps match the number of neurons to the size of the target field. In the adult nervous system, neurotrophins continue to support neuronal survival and play roles in synaptic plasticity, learning and memory. BDNF in particular has been implicated in long-term potentiation and in mood disorders such as depression.

History

Nerve growth factor was discovered in the 1950s by Rita Levi-Montalcini and Viktor Hamburger, with biochemical characterisation by Stanley Cohen; Levi-Montalcini and Cohen shared the 1986 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for the discovery. BDNF was purified from pig brain in 1982 by Yves-Alain Barde and colleagues, and NT-3 and NT-4 were identified in the early 1990s through homology cloning.

Clinical significance

Altered neurotrophin signalling has been linked to neurodegenerative diseases, chronic pain and psychiatric disorders. Mutations in the NGF gene cause hereditary sensory and autonomic neuropathy type V, and mutations affecting the TrkA receptor cause congenital insensitivity to pain with anhidrosis (HSAN type IV).
