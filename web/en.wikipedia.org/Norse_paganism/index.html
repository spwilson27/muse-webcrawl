Norse paganism

Norse paganism, also known as Norse religion or Old Norse religion, is a branch of Germanic paganism practised in Scandinavia and in Norse settlements during the Viking Age and earlier. It is a polytheistic tradition centred on deities such as Odin, Thor, Freyja, and Loki, and is known principally from the Icelandic sagas, the Poetic Edda, and the Prose Edda composed by Snorri Sturluson in the 13th century, as well as from archaeology, place names, and runic inscriptions.

Sources

The main textual sources are the mythological and heroic poems of the Poetic Edda, compiled in the Codex Regius manuscript, and Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda (c. 1220), written two centuries after Iceland's conversion to Christianity. The kings' sagas and sagas of Icelanders preserve accounts of rituals and beliefs, while Adam of Bremen's history describes the great temple at Uppsala in Sweden. Material evidence includes picture stones, amulets shaped like Thor's hammer (Mjölnir), and ship burials such as those at Oseberg and Gokstad.

Deities

The gods are divided into two groups, the Æsir and the Vanir, who fought a war in mythic times and later reconciled. Odin, chief of the Æsir, is associated with wisdom, war, poetry, and death; he hung on the world tree Yggdrasil to gain knowledge of the runes. Thor, wielder of the hammer Mjölnir, protects gods and humans against giants. Freyja and her brother Freyr are Vanir deities associated with love, fertility, and prosperity. Other figures include Baldr, Tyr, Heimdall, and the trickster Loki.

Cosmology

The cosmos is structured around Yggdrasil, the great world tree connecting the realms of gods, humans (Midgard), giants, and the dead (Hel). Creation arose from the primordial void Ginnungagap. The world is fated to end at Ragnarök, a great battle in which many gods die, after which the earth rises renewed from the sea.

Ritual practice

Worship included sacrificial feasts known as blót, offerings at temples, howes, and sacred groves, and rites led by chieftains (goðar) in Iceland. Funerary practices ranged from cremation to ship burial. Magic known as seiðr was practised, often by women called völvas.

Christianization and modern revival

Scandinavia converted to Christianity between the 10th and 12th centuries, and the old religion faded, though elements survived in folklore. In the 20th and 21st centuries, modern revivals known collectively as Heathenry (including Ásatrú) have reconstructed Norse pagan practice, with officially recognised communities in Iceland, Denmark, and elsewhere.
