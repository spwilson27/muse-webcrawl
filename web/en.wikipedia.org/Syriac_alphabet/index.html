Syriac alphabet

The Syriac alphabet is a writing system primarily used to write the Syriac language since the 1st century AD. It is one of the Semitic abjads descending from the Aramaic alphabet through the Palmyrene alphabet, and shares similarities with the Hebrew, Arabic, and Mongolian scripts. The alphabet consists of 22 letters, all of which stand for consonants, though some can also serve as vowel indicators (matres lectionis).

Forms

Syriac is written from right to left in a cursive style in which most, but not all, letters connect within a word. Three principal script forms developed: Estrangela, the oldest and most formal, used in early manuscripts; Serto (the "linear" western script), used by West Syriac communities; and the eastern script, often called Madnhaya or Nestorian, used by the Church of the East. Vowels, originally unmarked, came to be indicated by diacritic dots and later by two systems of small vowel signs: one adapted from Greek letters in the west and an eastern system of dots and strokes.

Use

Classical Syriac, the literary dialect of Edessa, became a major vehicle of early Christian literature and scholarship, and the script was historically used for other languages as well, including forms of Arabic (Garshuni), Malayalam (Suriyani Malayalam), Turkish, Persian, and Sogdian. The script is encoded in Unicode in the Syriac block (U+0700–U+074F) and a supplement block.
