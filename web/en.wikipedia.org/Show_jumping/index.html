Show jumping is a part of a group of English riding equestrian events that also includes eventing, hunters, and equitation. Jumping classes are commonly seen at horse shows throughout the world, including the Olympics. Sometimes shows are limited exclusively to jumpers. Jumping classes are typically governed by the rules of the national federation of the country in which it is held, or by the FEI.

Show jumping was first incorporated into the Olympic Games in 1900, at the second modern Games in Paris. It appeared intermittently until it became a permanent fixture from 1912 onwards. Women were not permitted to compete in Olympic show jumping until 1956, although they were allowed in dressage earlier.

Competition format

In a show jumping competition, horse and rider combinations negotiate a course of obstacles, usually between ten and sixteen jumping efforts, in a set order and within an optimum time. Faults are incurred for knocking down a rail (four faults), refusing an obstacle (four faults for the first refusal, elimination for the second), or exceeding the time allowed. Riders who complete the course without faults ("clear rounds") may advance to a jump-off, a shortened course ridden against the clock, in which the fastest clear round wins.

Courses and obstacles

A course designer arranges fences such as verticals, oxers (spread fences), walls, gates, water jumps, and combinations of two or three fences in related distances. Fences are built so that rails fall easily when struck, for the safety of horse and rider; cups holding the rails are shallow, and modern safety cups release under pressure. Course difficulty increases with fence height and spread, technical lines, and tighter time allowances as combinations progress through the levels, from small schooling classes to international Grand Prix classes, where fences may reach 1.60 metres (5 ft 3 in) in height.

Horses and riders

Successful show jumpers are athletic horses with careful technique over fences, scope, and rideability. Warmblood breeds dominate international competition, though Thoroughbreds and other breeds also compete. Riders require a secure seat, an accurate eye for distances, and the ability to adjust a horse's stride between fences. Famous venues include Aachen in Germany, Spruce Meadows in Canada, and Hickstead in England.

Governing and major events

The sport is governed internationally by the Fédération Équestre Internationale (FEI). Major competitions include the Olympic Games, the FEI World Equestrian Games (now the FEI World Championships), the Show Jumping World Cup, the Nations Cup series, and the Global Champions Tour. Grand Prix classes at five-star (CSI5*) level represent the top tier of the sport outside championships.
