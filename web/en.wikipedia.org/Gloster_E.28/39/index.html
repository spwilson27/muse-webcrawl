The Gloster E.28/39, also known as the Gloster Whittle or Gloster Pioneer, was the first British jet-engined aircraft. Designed by George Carter of the Gloster Aircraft Company to test the revolutionary turbojet engine invented by Frank Whittle, it made its first powered flight on 15 May 1941 — a landmark in aviation history.

Development

Air Ministry specification E.28/39 was issued in 1939 for an experimental aircraft to prove Whittle's turbojet concept. The resulting design was a small, low-wing monoplane of duralumin stressed-skin construction with a tricycle undercarriage, the engine mounted behind the cockpit and fed by a nose intake, exhausting beneath the tail.

Flight testing

After taxi trials at Brockworth in April 1941 — during which the aircraft briefly lifted off — the first official flight took place at RAF Cranwell on 15 May 1941, with flight lieutenant Gerry Sayer at the controls, powered by a Power Jets W.1 engine of 850 lbf thrust. The flight lasted 17 minutes and demonstrated that jet propulsion was practical.

Two prototypes were built: W4041 and W4046. Both were progressively fitted with more powerful engines, including the W.2/500. The first prototype, W4041, survives and is displayed at the Science Museum in London; the second was destroyed in a crash in 1943, its pilot escaping safely.

The E.28/39's success led directly to the twin-engined Gloster Meteor, the only Allied jet fighter to see combat in the Second World War.
