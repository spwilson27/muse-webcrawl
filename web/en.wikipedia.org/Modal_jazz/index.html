Modal jazz

Modal jazz is a style of jazz that uses musical modes — rather than the rapidly changing chord progressions of bebop — as the basis of musical structure and improvisation. Developed in the late 1950s, principally by Miles Davis, John Coltrane and Bill Evans, it freed soloists to explore melody over static harmonies sustained for many bars at a time.

Development

George Russell's Lydian Chromatic Concept of Tonal Organization (1953) provided much of the theory. Miles Davis, seeking an escape from bebop's dense chord changes, recorded "Milestones" (1958) and then Kind of Blue (1959) — whose tracks "So What" and "Flamenco Sketches" are built on one or two modes — with John Coltrane, Cannonball Adderley, Bill Evans and Paul Chambers. Kind of Blue became the best-selling jazz album of all time.

Characteristics

Where bebop tunes might change chords every beat or two, modal pieces rest on a single scale for 8, 16 or more bars. Melodic interest shifts to the shape of the solo, quartal harmony (chords built in fourths, as in the famous "So What" voicings) replaces functional harmony, and rhythm sections favour spacious, floating feels. Coltrane took the approach to extremes with long explorations of a single mode in "My Favorite Things" (1961) and the modal intensity of A Love Supreme (1965).

Influence

Modal jazz shaped the work of Herbie Hancock ("Maiden Voyage"), McCoy Tyner and Wayne Shorter, and influenced rock, funk and electronic musicians who adopted its static, groove-based harmonies.
