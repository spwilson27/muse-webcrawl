Tiki

Tiki is the name given in several Polynesian cultures to large wooden or stone carvings of humanoid form, and in Māori tradition to Tiki, the first man created by the gods. Related terms include Māori tekoteko for carved posts and Hawaiian kiʻi for images. Tikis represent deified ancestors and gods — in Māori mythology, Tiki is associated with the god Tāne — and embody spiritual power (mana); carved figures served as boundary markers, guardians of sacred places (marae), and memorials to ancestors.

In Polynesian cultures

Monumental stone tikis are characteristic of the Marquesas Islands, where the largest figures were erected on ceremonial platforms (tohua). In New Zealand, Māori carved wooden posts for meeting houses and palisades, and small tiki figures — the hei-tiki, usually of greenstone (pounamu) — were worn as pendants and treasured as heirlooms connected with fertility and ancestry. Hawaiian kiʻi stood in temples (heiau), including the fierce images of the war god Kū.

Tiki culture

"Tiki" also names a mid-twentieth-century American popular-culture movement, sparked by the success of Don the Beachcomber's and Trader Vic's Polynesian-themed bars in the 1930s and by returning servicemen's fascination with the South Pacific after the Second World War. Tiki culture combined carved tikis, bamboo and thatch décor, exotic rum cocktails such as the Mai Tai and Zombie, and lounge music, reaching its height in the 1950s and 1960s with tiki bars, restaurants and motels across the United States. After a decline, it enjoyed a revival from the 1990s onwards among cocktail and design enthusiasts. The appropriation of sacred Polynesian imagery in popular tiki culture has also drawn criticism from Pacific Islanders.
