Sundial

A sundial is a device that tells the time of day by using the position of the Sun in the sky. In its most common form, a horizontal sundial, a triangular blade called a gnomon casts a shadow onto a flat surface marked with lines indicating the hours of the day.

Principle

As the Sun moves across the sky, the shadow of the gnomon's edge moves across the dial plate at a rate corresponding to the Sun's apparent rotation. The gnomon is aligned parallel to the Earth's axis, pointing toward the celestial pole; its angle therefore depends on the sundial's latitude. The time read from a sundial is local apparent solar time, which differs from clock time by the equation of time and the location's offset within its time zone.

History

Shadow clocks were used in ancient Egypt and Babylon, and sundials were highly developed by the Greeks and Romans — Vitruvius catalogued many types. Elaborate sundials remained important timekeepers into the early modern period, and they continue to be built as garden ornaments and monumental sculptures.

Types

Principal types include horizontal, vertical (such as church-wall dials), equatorial, and portable dials, as well as the large masonry instruments of Indian observatories such as Jantar Mantar.
