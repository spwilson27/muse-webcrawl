Amphotericin B

Amphotericin B is an antifungal medication used for serious, life-threatening fungal infections. It is a polyene macrolide antibiotic originally isolated in 1955 from Streptomyces nodosus, a filamentous bacterium found in soil from the Orinoco River region of Venezuela. For decades it was the mainstay treatment for invasive fungal disease and was nicknamed "ampho-terrible" for its significant side effects.

Mechanism of action

Amphotericin B binds to ergosterol, the principal sterol in fungal cell membranes, forming pores that allow leakage of ions and other molecules, leading to fungal cell death. Its selectivity is imperfect because it also binds cholesterol in human cell membranes, which accounts for much of its toxicity.

Medical uses

The drug is used against invasive infections caused by Candida, Aspergillus, Cryptococcus, and the Mucorales, and it is a first-line treatment (with flucytosine) for cryptococcal meningitis. It is also used to treat the parasitic disease visceral leishmaniasis. Amphotericin B is administered intravenously; lipid formulations (such as liposomal amphotericin B) were developed to reduce kidney toxicity.

Adverse effects

Common adverse effects include fever, chills, and rigors during infusion, as well as dose-dependent nephrotoxicity and electrolyte disturbances such as hypokalemia and hypomagnesemia.
