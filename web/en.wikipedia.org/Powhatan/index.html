Chief Powhatan (Wahunsenacawh), paramount chief of Tsenacommacah.

The Powhatan people are Indigenous peoples of the Northeastern Woodlands, traditionally inhabiting the tidewater region of what is now Virginia. The name also refers to their paramount chief, Wahunsenacawh (c. 1545 – 1618), known to the English as Chief Powhatan, who ruled over a confederacy of some thirty Algonquian-speaking tribes — often called the Powhatan Confederacy or Tsenacommacah — encompassing perhaps 14,000 to 21,000 people when the English founded Jamestown in 1607.

History

Wahunsenacawh inherited rule of several tribes and expanded the confederacy through diplomacy and conquest, governing from the capital at Werowocomoco on the York River. Relations with the Jamestown colonists oscillated between trade and violence: Captain John Smith was captured in 1607 (the occasion of the famous, and debated, story of Powhatan's daughter Pocahontas intervening to save his life), and the First Anglo-Powhatan War ran from 1609 to 1614. Pocahontas's marriage to colonist John Rolfe in 1614 helped secure several years of peace. After Wahunsenacawh's death in 1618, power passed to his brother Opitchapam and then to Opechancanough, who launched major attacks on the colony in 1622 and 1644. Defeat in 1646 broke the confederacy's power and confined the Powhatan peoples to reserved lands.

Descendants today

Powhatan descendant communities persist in Virginia today. The Pamunkey and Mattaponi maintain two of the oldest reservations in the United States, established by treaties in the 17th century. The Pamunkey gained federal recognition in 2016, and six more Virginia tribes — including the Chickahominy, Rappahannock, and Nansemond — were federally recognised by an act of Congress in 2018. The Powhatan language died out in the 18th century, though revival efforts draw on word lists recorded by Smith and William Strachey.
