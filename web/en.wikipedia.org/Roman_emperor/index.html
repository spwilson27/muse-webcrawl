The Roman emperor was the ruler and head of state of the Roman Empire from the establishment of the Principate under Augustus in 27 BC until the fall of Constantinople in 1453. The emperor held supreme political and military power, though the constitutional form of the office changed greatly over fifteen centuries.

The Principate

Augustus, having won the civil wars that ended the Republic, accumulated republican powers — tribunician authority, proconsular imperium, the chief priesthood — while styling himself merely princeps, "first citizen". In practice he commanded the army, controlled appointments and finance, and founded a dynasty. Succession was never governed by a fixed law: adoption, hereditary claim, senatorial recognition and, frequently, military acclamation all played a part, contributing to repeated succession crises.

Crisis and Dominate

During the Crisis of the Third Century (235–284) dozens of emperors and usurpers rose and fell by the sword. Diocletian stabilised the empire and formalised autocratic rule (the Dominate), and with Constantine the empire became Christian and gained a new eastern capital, Constantinople. Theodosius I was the last to rule the whole empire; after 395 it was permanently divided between west and east.

Later history

The last western emperor, Romulus Augustulus, was deposed in 476. The eastern emperors, ruling from Constantinople over what historians call the Byzantine Empire, continued the Roman imperial office until Constantine XI died defending the city against the Ottomans in 1453.
