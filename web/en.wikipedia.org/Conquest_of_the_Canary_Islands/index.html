Conquest of the Canary Islands

The conquest of the Canary Islands by the Crown of Castile took place between 1402 and 1496, when the seven main islands of the archipelago were subdued and their indigenous Guanche peoples subjugated. It was the first European conquest of Atlantic island peoples and a model for later colonisation in the Americas.

Early campaigns

In 1402 the Norman nobles Jean de Béthencourt and Gadifer de la Salle, acting under Castilian sovereignty, conquered Lanzarote, Fuerteventura and El Hierro, enslaving or converting much of the population. The remaining islands — La Gomera, Gran Canaria, La Palma and Tenerife — resisted for decades because of their larger populations and mountainous terrain.

Royal conquest

From 1478 the Catholic Monarchs directed the conquest directly: Gran Canaria fell in 1483 after a five-year campaign by Pedro de Vera; La Palma was taken by Alonso Fernández de Lugo in 1492–93; and Tenerife, after the Guanche victory at the first battle of Acentejo in 1494, was finally conquered in 1496.

Consequences

The Guanche population declined catastrophically through war, enslavement and disease, and the survivors were absorbed into a settler society based on sugar plantations and later wine. The islands became a vital provisioning stop for voyages to the Americas.
