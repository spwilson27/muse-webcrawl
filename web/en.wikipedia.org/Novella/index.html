Novella

FormProse fiction
Typical length~17,500–40,000 words
OriginItalian Renaissance (Boccaccio's Decameron)
ExamplesHeart of Darkness; The Old Man and the Sea; Animal Farm; The Metamorphosis

A novella is a work of written prose fiction that is longer than a short story but shorter than a novel. The English word derives from the Italian novella, meaning a short tale or piece of news. Word-count conventions used by awards bodies such as the Nebula and Hugo Awards place the novella between roughly 17,500 and 40,000 words.

Characteristics

The novella typically focuses on a single unified conflict or chain of events, with few subplots and a small cast, and is often meant to be read at a single sitting. Its compactness allows a novel's depth of character and theme within a tighter structure. Famous examples include Joseph Conrad's Heart of Darkness, Herman Melville's Billy Budd, Franz Kafka's The Metamorphosis, Thomas Mann's Death in Venice, Ernest Hemingway's The Old Man and the Sea, and George Orwell's Animal Farm.

History

The form descends from the framed tale collections of the Italian Renaissance, above all Boccaccio's Decameron (c. 1353), and flourished in Germany as the Novelle in the hands of Goethe, Kleist, and Hoffmann. In the modern market, novellas were long considered awkwardly sized for print publishing, but e-books and digital-first imprints prompted a notable revival in the 2010s.
