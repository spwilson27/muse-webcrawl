The Nintendo 64 (abbreviated N64) is a home video game console developed and marketed by Nintendo. Named for its 64-bit central processing unit, it was released in Japan and North America in 1996 and in Europe and Australia in 1997. It was the last major home console to use cartridges as its primary storage format, a decision that limited third-party support but eliminated loading times. The console's distinctive three-pronged controller introduced a widely imitated analog stick as a standard input.

History

Nintendo developed the system, codenamed "Project Reality," in partnership with Silicon Graphics, whose workstation technology was adapted into the console's Reality Coprocessor GPU. After delays, the N64 launched in Japan on June 23, 1996, with Super Mario 64, Pilotwings 64, and Saikyō Habu Shōgi. The North American launch followed that September.

Games

The console's library of 388 games included landmarks of early 3D gaming: Super Mario 64 defined 3D platforming, while The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time (1998) is frequently cited among the greatest video games ever made. Rare's GoldenEye 007 (1997) popularized the console first-person shooter and four-player split-screen multiplayer. Other notable titles include Mario Kart 64, Star Fox 64, Banjo-Kazooie, and Super Smash Bros.

Hardware

The N64 was powered by a 64-bit NEC VR4300 CPU at 93.75 MHz and 4 MB of RDRAM, expandable to 8 MB with the Expansion Pak. Cartridges ranged from 4 to 64 MB. The 64DD magnetic disk add-on was released only in Japan in 1999 and was a commercial failure.

Reception and legacy

Although the N64 sold 32.93 million units — well behind Sony's PlayStation — it is regarded as one of the most influential consoles, pioneering analog control, controller rumble (via the Rumble Pak), and four built-in controller ports. It was discontinued in 2002 following the launch of the GameCube.
