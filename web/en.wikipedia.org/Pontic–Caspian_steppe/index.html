Pontic–Caspian steppe

The Pontic–Caspian steppe is a steppe ecoregion stretching from the northern shores of the Black Sea (the Pontus Euxinus of antiquity) as far east as the Caspian Sea. It covers parts of present-day Ukraine, southern Russia, and western Kazakhstan, forming the western portion of the great Eurasian Steppe. The region is bounded by the Danube and Dniester valleys in the west and the Ural River in the east.

Geography and ecology

The Pontic–Caspian steppe is a nearly treeless plain of chernozem (black earth) soils that are among the most fertile in the world; much of the steppe has been converted to wheat, sunflower and maize farming. Native vegetation includes feather grasses (Stipa), fescues and sagebrush, supporting animals such as the saiga antelope, steppe marmot (bobak), sousliks, bustards and steppe eagles. The climate is continental, with cold winters, hot summers and modest precipitation.

History

The steppe was the proposed homeland (Urheimat) of Proto-Indo-European speakers under the Kurgan hypothesis, associated with the Yamnaya culture of roughly 3300–2600 BCE, whose horse-riding pastoralists are thought to have spread Indo-European languages into Europe and Asia. In later antiquity and the Middle Ages the steppe was home successively to Cimmerians, Scythians, Sarmatians, Goths, Huns, Avars, Khazars, Pechenegs, Cumans and the Mongol Golden Horde, before the Russian Empire and Cossack hosts settled the region in the 17th–19th centuries.
