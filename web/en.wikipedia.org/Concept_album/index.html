Concept album

A concept album is an album whose tracks hold a larger purpose or meaning collectively than they do individually. This is typically achieved through a single central narrative or theme, which can be instrumental, compositional or lyrical. Sometimes the term is applied more loosely to albums considered unified by a mood or style, but it is most strongly associated with works in which the songs form a continuous story or explore one idea throughout.

History

Early examples are often traced to Woody Guthrie's Dust Bowl Ballads (1940) and Frank Sinatra's themed albums of the 1950s such as In the Wee Small Hours (1955). The format flourished in rock music from the late 1960s: the Beach Boys' Pet Sounds (1966) and the Beatles' Sgt. Pepper's Lonely Hearts Club Band (1967) are landmark cases, followed by narrative works such as the Who's Tommy (1969) and Pink Floyd's The Wall (1979).

Rock operas and progressive rock

Progressive rock made the concept album a signature form, with extended suites and recurring musical motifs in works by Yes, Genesis, Jethro Tull and Rush. A concept album with a fully dramatised plot is often called a rock opera. The format later spread widely: Marvin Gaye's What's Going On (1971) applied it to soul, and artists in hip hop, metal and electronic music have continued to build album-length narratives and thematic cycles.

Influence

The rise of singles-oriented streaming was widely expected to diminish the concept album, yet performers across genres continue to release strongly unified albums, sometimes accompanied by films, stage shows or elaborate packaging that extend the central concept.
