Mule

A mule is the offspring of a male donkey (a jack) and a female horse (a mare). It is the most common and oldest known animal hybrid, bred by humans since antiquity for transport and agricultural work. The reciprocal cross, a male horse with a female donkey, produces a hinny, which is somewhat less common. Mules combine characteristics of both parents: from the donkey they inherit long ears, a thin tail tuft, hardiness and sure-footedness; from the horse, size, strength and speed.

Characteristics

Mules vary in size from small animals under 13 hands to large draft mules of 17 hands bred from draft mares. They are noted for intelligence, patience, endurance, a strong sense of self-preservation, and the ability to work on less feed than a horse of comparable size. Their reputation for stubbornness is often attributed to this self-preservation: a mule will refuse demands it perceives as unsafe or unreasonable.

Sterility

Mules are almost always sterile. A horse has 64 chromosomes and a donkey 62, so the mule has 63 — an odd number that cannot pair evenly during meiosis, preventing the production of viable gametes. Rare cases of fertile female mules have been documented — a few dozen in recorded history — but there is no verified case of a fertile male mule.

History and use

Mules were prized in the ancient world: the Hittites considered them more valuable than horses, and they appear throughout Greek, Roman and biblical history as mounts for royalty and as pack animals. In the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries they were the backbone of agriculture and freight transport in the United States — the famous twenty-mule teams hauled borax wagons out of Death Valley — and armies used them in mountain warfare through both world wars and beyond. Millions of mules still work in developing countries, and they remain valued for trail riding, packing in rugged terrain, and competitive events.
