Shakespearean tragedy

Shakespearean tragedies centre on a noble protagonist whose flaw or error leads to destruction.

Shakespearean tragedy is the designation given to the tragedies written by William Shakespeare. Most famous among them are Hamlet, Othello, King Lear, and Macbeth, often called the "great tragedies", together with Titus Andronicus, Romeo and Juliet, Julius Caesar, Troilus and Cressida, Antony and Cleopatra, Coriolanus, and Timon of Athens.

Shakespeare's tragedies draw on the classical model described in Aristotle's Poetics and on Senecan revenge drama, but they are distinguished by their psychological depth and by the inner life of the protagonist. A Shakespearean tragedy typically presents an exceptional figure—a king, prince, or general—whose character contains a fatal flaw or "hamartia" (such as Hamlet's indecision, Othello's jealousy, Macbeth's ambition, or Lear's pride). The protagonist's error sets in motion a chain of events leading to catastrophe, the death of the hero, and often the deaths of many others, after which a measure of order is restored.

Characteristics

Common features include supernatural elements (ghosts, witches), soliloquies that reveal inner conflict, madness real or feigned, dramatic irony, and the juxtaposition of tragic action with comic subplots. The tragedies were written mostly between about 1598 and 1613, the middle period of Shakespeare's career, when he was writing for the Lord Chamberlain's Men (later the King's Men) at the Globe Theatre.

Critical study

The critic A. C. Bradley's Shakespearean Tragedy (1904) remains the most influential study of the four great tragedies, analysing the plays through the character of the hero. Later critics, including Jan Kott and feminist and materialist readers, have reinterpreted the tragedies in political and contemporary terms.
