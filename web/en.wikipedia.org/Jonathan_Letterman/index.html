Jonathan Letterman

Born: 11 December 1824, Canonsburg, Pennsylvania, US
Died: 15 March 1872 (aged 47), San Francisco, California, US
Allegiance: United States (Union)
Service: U.S. Army Medical Department, 1849–1864
Rank: Major (surgeon)
Known for: The "Letterman system" of battlefield medicine; father of modern battlefield emergency medicine

Jonathan Letterman (December 11, 1824 – March 15, 1872) was an American surgeon credited as the originator of the modern methods for the medical management of mass battlefield casualties. His system of organisation, implemented in the Union Army during the American Civil War as medical director of the Army of the Potomac, enabled thousands of wounded troops to receive rapid effective care, and forms the foundation of modern military and civilian emergency medicine.

Early career

Letterman graduated from Jefferson Medical College in Philadelphia in 1849 and joined the Army Medical Department as an assistant surgeon. He served in campaigns in Florida against the Seminoles, in New Mexico and California, and in frontier postings that taught him the logistics of supplying medical care in the field.

The Letterman system

Appointed medical director of the Army of the Potomac in June 1862, in the chaos that followed the Seven Days Battles, Letterman rapidly reorganized battlefield care. His reforms, formalised in General Orders No. 147 (August 1862), created the first Ambulance Corps: dedicated, trained stretcher-bearers and ambulance drivers under medical rather than quartermaster control. He established a tiered system of care — dressing stations close behind the lines, field hospitals at division level for surgery, and evacuation to general hospitals — with standardized medical supply wagons and record keeping. The system was tested at Antietam and Fredericksburg and proved itself at Gettysburg, where some 14,000 Union wounded were collected and treated in an organized fashion.

Later life and legacy

Letterman resigned his commission in 1864 and entered private practice in San Francisco, serving as coroner and writing his memoir, Medical Recollections of the Army of the Potomac (1866). He died in 1872. The U.S. Army named Letterman Army Hospital at the Presidio of San Francisco and Letterman Army Institute of Research in his honour, and his principles — rapid evacuation, triage at staged facilities, and a dedicated ambulance service — underpin modern trauma care, military and civilian alike.
