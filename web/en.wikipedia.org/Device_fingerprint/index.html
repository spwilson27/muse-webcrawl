Attributes commonly combined to form a device fingerprint.

A device fingerprint is information collected about the software and hardware of a computing device for the purpose of identification. By combining many attributes—such as the operating system, browser version and plug-ins, installed fonts, screen resolution, time zone, language settings, and subtle rendering differences—a website or service can compute a value that is stable for a given device and shared by few others, allowing the device to be recognized on later visits even without cookies.

Techniques

Fingerprinting may be passive, relying on information a device routinely sends, such as HTTP headers and IP characteristics, or active, using JavaScript to probe features directly. Notable active techniques include canvas fingerprinting, in which a hidden image is drawn and its exact pixel output read back, and AudioContext fingerprinting, which exploits small differences in audio processing. Research projects such as the Electronic Frontier Foundation's Panopticlick demonstrated that most browsers carry a fingerprint unique enough to track.

Uses

Fingerprinting is used legitimately for fraud prevention and account security—for example, flagging a login from an unrecognized device—and more controversially for advertising and analytics tracking that persists even when users clear cookies or browse privately.

Countermeasures and regulation

Browsers including Tor Browser, Firefox, and Safari have introduced fingerprinting resistance, standardizing or randomizing reported attributes and partitioning data. Privacy laws such as the EU's ePrivacy rules and GDPR are generally interpreted to require consent for fingerprinting used for tracking.
