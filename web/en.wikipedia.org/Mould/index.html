Mould

[Image: Mould growing on an orange (Penicillium)]
KingdomFungi
StructureMulticellular hyphae (mycelium)
ReproductionSpores (sexual and asexual)
Common generaAspergillus, Penicillium, Cladosporium, Stachybotrys

A mould (American English: mold) is one of the structures that certain fungi can form. Moulds are multicellular filaments called hyphae, collectively forming a mycelium, in contrast to yeasts, which grow as single cells. Thousands of species are known; they grow wherever moisture and organic matter are available, producing characteristic fuzzy patches in colours from black and green to white and orange.

Biology

Moulds reproduce by producing vast numbers of tiny spores, which are dispersed by air, water or animals and can survive harsh conditions. On landing in a suitable environment — damp, warm, and rich in nutrients — spores germinate into hyphae that digest organic material externally by secreting enzymes. Moulds are major decomposers in ecosystems, recycling nutrients from dead plants and animals.

Effects on humans

Moulds spoil food and damage buildings and materials. Some produce mycotoxins — notably aflatoxins from Aspergillus flavus on grains and nuts — and indoor mould growth is associated with allergies and respiratory problems in sensitive people. Other moulds are highly beneficial: Penicillium species gave rise to the antibiotic penicillin and are used to ripen cheeses such as Camembert and Roquefort; moulds are also used in fermenting soy sauce, sake and tempeh, and industrially to produce citric acid and enzymes.

Control

Controlling indoor mould centres on eliminating moisture — fixing leaks, ventilating and dehumidifying. Small areas can be cleaned; extensive contamination of porous materials usually requires their removal.
