Prosciutto

Prosciutto (Italian: [proʃˈʃutto]) is Italian uncooked, unsmoked, dry-cured ham, usually served thinly sliced. The name derives from the Latin perexsuctum, "thoroughly dried". In Italian, the word refers generally to ham — prosciutto crudo is the raw cured form, while prosciutto cotto is cooked ham — but in English usage "prosciutto" almost always means the dry-cured product.

Production

Prosciutto is made from the hind leg of a pig. The leg is cleaned, salted, and left to cure for several weeks, during which it loses moisture; it is then washed, sometimes coated with a mixture of lard, flour and pepper (sugna) to prevent excessive drying, and hung to air-dry in a controlled environment for months. Total ageing ranges from about nine months to over two years for the finest hams. No nitrates or nitrites may be added to the protected Italian varieties besides sea salt.

Protected varieties

Two Italian prosciutti crudi hold PDO status and international fame: Prosciutto di Parma (Parma ham), produced in the hills around Parma from pigs fed partly on the whey of Parmigiano-Reggiano, aged a minimum of 12 months and marked with a ducal crown brand; and Prosciutto di San Daniele, from Friuli, recognisable by its guitar shape and the trotter left attached. Other regional varieties include prosciutto Toscano, di Modena, Veneto Berico-Euganeo and di Carpegna.

Use

Prosciutto crudo is eaten as an antipasto, often with melon or figs, wrapped around grissini or asparagus, or used in cooking (for example in saltimbocca), typically added late so that its delicate texture survives. Related dry-cured hams elsewhere include Spanish jamón, French jambon de Bayonne and Croatian pršut.
