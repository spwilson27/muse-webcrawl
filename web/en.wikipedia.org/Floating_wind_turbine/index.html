Floating wind turbine

A floating wind turbine is an offshore wind turbine mounted on a floating structure, allowing electricity generation in water too deep for fixed-bottom foundations — generally beyond about 50–60 metres. The floating platform is held in position by mooring lines and anchors. Because a large share of the world's best offshore wind resources lie over deep water, floating technology is expected to greatly expand the reach of offshore wind energy.

Platform designs

Floating platforms achieve stability in different ways. Spar buoys use a long, ballasted cylinder extending deep below the surface. Semi-submersibles float on several large columns joined by pontoons and derive stability from their wide footprint. Tension-leg platforms are held down by taut vertical tendons anchored to the seabed, while barge designs rely on a large flat hull. All types must withstand the overturning thrust of the turbine rotor in storms while keeping motions gentle enough for the turbine to operate.

Development

The concept was proposed by William Heronemus in the 1970s. The first full-scale prototype, Equinor's 2.3 MW Hywind spar, was installed off Norway in 2009; the 30 MW Hywind Scotland pilot farm followed in 2017, delivering high capacity factors. Subsequent projects include WindFloat Atlantic off Portugal and Hywind Tampen in Norway, and governments in the UK, France, Japan, South Korea and the US state of California have run leasing rounds and subsidy auctions dedicated to floating wind.

Advantages and challenges

Floating turbines can be assembled in port and towed to site, avoiding heavy-lift vessels, and can access stronger, steadier winds far from shore with less visual impact. Costs remain higher than fixed-bottom wind, and challenges include dynamic export cables, mooring durability, maintenance access and port infrastructure for serial production.
