Patroclus


Patroclus in Greek red-figure painting

Patroclus (Greek: Πάτροκλος, "glory of the father") is a figure of Greek mythology, best known as the companion, charioteer, and closest comrade of the hero Achilles in Homer's Iliad. The son of Menoetius of Opus, Patroclus was exiled as a youth for accidentally killing a playmate and was raised in the household of Peleus alongside Achilles.

Role in the Iliad

When Achilles withdraws from battle during the Trojan War after his quarrel with Agamemnon, the Greeks suffer heavy losses. Seeing the ships threatened, Patroclus begs Achilles to let him enter the fight wearing Achilles' armor. Achilles agrees but warns him only to drive the Trojans from the ships, not to pursue them to Troy.

Patroclus routs the Trojans and kills the hero Sarpedon, but in his triumph he presses on toward the walls, where the god Apollo strikes him, Euphorbus wounds him, and Hector delivers the fatal blow. The news of Patroclus's death drives Achilles back into battle in grief and rage, setting in motion the climax of the poem. Achilles holds elaborate funeral games in Patroclus's honor in Book 23.

Interpretation

Ancient and modern readers have debated the exact nature of the bond between Achilles and Patroclus; in later Greek tradition, including Plato's Symposium, they were often described as lovers. In the Iliad itself their relationship is portrayed as the deepest of friendships, and Patroclus's gentleness contrasts with Achilles' wrath throughout the poem.
