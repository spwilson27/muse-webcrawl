John William Strutt, 3rd Baron Rayleigh

John William Strutt, 3rd Baron Rayleigh (12 November 1842 – 30 June 1919) was an English physicist and mathematician who made wide-ranging contributions to both theoretical and experimental physics. He succeeded his father as Baron Rayleigh in 1873. With William Ramsay, he discovered the element argon, for which he received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1904.

Career

Strutt studied mathematics at Trinity College, Cambridge, graduating as Senior Wrangler in 1865. He succeeded James Clerk Maxwell as Cavendish Professor of Physics at Cambridge in 1879 and held the chair until 1884. Among his best-known results is the explanation of why the sky is blue, now known as Rayleigh scattering, which describes the elastic scattering of light by particles much smaller than the wavelength. His two-volume treatise The Theory of Sound (1877–1878) remains a foundational work in acoustics.

Discovery of argon

While measuring the densities of gases with great precision, Rayleigh found that nitrogen prepared from air was consistently about 0.5% denser than nitrogen prepared chemically. Investigating the discrepancy with Ramsay led in 1894 to the isolation of a previously unknown inert gas, argon — the first of the noble gases to be discovered.

Other work

Rayleigh's name is attached to numerous concepts, including the Rayleigh criterion for resolving optical images, the Rayleigh–Jeans law (an early result that led toward quantum theory), Rayleigh waves in seismology, Rayleigh flow and Rayleigh number in fluid dynamics, and the Rayleigh distribution in statistics. He served as president of the Royal Society from 1905 to 1908.
