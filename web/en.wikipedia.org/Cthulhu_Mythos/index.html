Cthulhu Mythos

The Cthulhu Mythos is a shared fictional universe rooted in the works of American horror writer H. P. Lovecraft (1890–1937) and expanded by many other authors. Named after Cthulhu, the great octopus-headed entity of Lovecraft's 1928 story "The Call of Cthulhu," the Mythos presents a cosmos of ancient, godlike beings indifferent or hostile to humanity, whose discovery typically drives human investigators to madness.

Themes

Central themes include cosmicism — the insignificance of humanity in an indifferent universe — forbidden knowledge, ancient cults, and heredity tainted by alien ancestry. Lovecraft's fictional New England settings (Arkham, Innsmouth, Dunwich) and invented books of occult lore, above all the Necronomicon attributed to the mad Arab Abdul Alhazred, form the shared scaffolding borrowed by later writers.

Principal entities

The pantheon includes the Great Old Ones — Cthulhu sleeping in sunken R'lyeh, Hastur, Tsathoggua — and the Outer Gods such as Azathoth, the blind idiot god at the center of the universe; Yog-Sothoth, the key and the gate; Shub-Niggurath; and Nyarlathotep, the crawling chaos who interacts with humankind. August Derleth, Lovecraft's correspondent and publisher, formalized much of the mythology after Lovecraft's death, controversially recasting it as a struggle between good Elder Gods and evil Great Old Ones.

Expansion and legacy

Writers of Lovecraft's circle, including Clark Ashton Smith, Robert E. Howard, Robert Bloch, and Frank Belknap Long, cross-pollinated the Mythos in their own tales. Later contributors range from Ramsey Campbell and Brian Lumley to Neil Gaiman and Alan Moore. The Mythos has permeated popular culture through role-playing games such as Call of Cthulhu (1981), board games, films, music, and countless references; the tentacled Cthulhu has become a widely recognized icon of cosmic horror.
