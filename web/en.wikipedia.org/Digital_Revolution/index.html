Digital Revolution

The Digital Revolution, also known as the Third Industrial Revolution, is the shift from mechanical and analogue electronic technology to digital electronics, which began in the latter half of the 20th century. Its central developments include the digital computer, the personal computer, and the Internet, which together transformed production, communication, and access to information.

Integrated circuits and personal computers drove the digital transformation.

Origins

Key foundations were laid in the 1940s and 1950s: the transistor (1947), the first programmable digital computers, and the integrated circuit (1958). The invention of the microprocessor in 1971 made inexpensive computing possible, and personal computers spread through homes and offices in the late 1970s and 1980s.

The Internet era

The opening of the Internet to commercial use and the arrival of the World Wide Web in the 1990s connected billions of people. Mobile phones and later smartphones extended digital access worldwide; by the 2020s a majority of the world's population was online.

Impact

The revolution automated manufacturing and office work, created entirely new industries (software, e-commerce, social media), disrupted media and retail, and changed social interaction. It also raised concerns about privacy, misinformation, job displacement, and the digital divide. Historians and economists often frame the data-driven automation that followed as a "Fourth Industrial Revolution."
