Danebury

The earthworks of Danebury hillfort in Hampshire

Danebury is an Iron Age hillfort in Hampshire, England, about 19 km northwest of Winchester. The fort, which encloses roughly 5 hectares, occupies a low chalk hill with views over the Test valley. It is famous among archaeologists for the long-running excavations directed by Barry Cunliffe between 1969 and 1988, which made it one of the most thoroughly investigated hillforts in Britain and transformed understanding of Iron Age society in southern England.

History

Danebury was built around 550 BC and remained occupied for roughly 450 years, until about 100 BC. Its defences — a single bank and ditch with complex timber-revetted ramparts and elaborate entrance passages — were rebuilt several times. Inside, the fort contained hundreds of roundhouses, four- and six-post structures interpreted as granaries, storage pits, shrines and metalled roadways, suggesting a permanent, densely occupied settlement that acted as a centre for the surrounding farming communities.

The later phases show signs of violence: parts of the ramparts were burned, and pits contained disarticulated human remains and deposits of grain, animal bone and artefacts interpreted as ritual offerings. Danebury was largely abandoned around 100 BC, though the site retained ceremonial significance into the Roman period.

Excavation

Cunliffe's excavations opened about half of the interior, an unusually large sample for a hillfort. The enormous finds assemblage — pottery, iron tools, querns, animal bones and human remains — allowed detailed reconstruction of the economy, diet and social organisation of an Iron Age community. The results were published in a multi-volume series, and finds are displayed at the Museum of the Iron Age in Andover.

The site today

Danebury is a Scheduled Ancient Monument and a Local Nature Reserve, managed for its chalk grassland flora and fauna. The earthworks are open to the public.
