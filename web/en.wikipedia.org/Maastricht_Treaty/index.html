The Maastricht Treaty, formally the Treaty on European Union (TEU), is the founding treaty of the European Union. Signed on 7 February 1992 in Maastricht, Netherlands, by the twelve member states of the European Communities, it entered into force on 1 November 1993. The treaty created the European Union, established citizenship of the Union, and set out the framework and timetable for Economic and Monetary Union, culminating in the adoption of the euro.

Signing of the Treaty on European Union at Maastricht, 7 February 1992.

Structure

The treaty organised the Union into three "pillars": the European Communities (the existing Community structures), a Common Foreign and Security Policy, and cooperation in Justice and Home Affairs. This pillar structure remained until the Treaty of Lisbon abolished it in 2009. The treaty also strengthened the European Parliament through the co-decision procedure and enshrined the principle of subsidiarity.

Monetary union and the convergence criteria

The treaty set criteria — the "Maastricht criteria" — that member states had to meet to adopt the euro, covering price stability, sound public finances (deficits below 3% of GDP and debt below 60% of GDP), exchange-rate stability, and convergence of long-term interest rates. The European Central Bank framework and the stages toward monetary union in 1999 followed from these provisions. The United Kingdom and Denmark obtained opt-outs from the single currency.

Ratification

Ratification proved contentious: Denmark initially rejected the treaty in a 1992 referendum before approving it in 1993 after the Edinburgh Agreement granted Danish opt-outs, and France approved it only narrowly in its own referendum, while Germany's Constitutional Court reviewed its compatibility with the Basic Law.
