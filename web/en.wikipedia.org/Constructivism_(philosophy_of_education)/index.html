Constructivism (philosophy of education)

Constructivist classrooms emphasise active, collaborative learning.

Constructivism is a theory in education which holds that individuals construct knowledge and meaning from their experiences, rather than passively receiving information. It is associated with the cognitive theory of Jean Piaget, who described learning as assimilation and accommodation of new experiences into existing mental schemas, and with Lev Vygotsky's social constructivism, which emphasises the role of language, culture and interaction with more knowledgeable others within the zone of proximal development.

Applications

In practice, constructivist pedagogy encourages inquiry-based learning, discovery learning, problem-based learning and collaborative group work. The teacher acts as a facilitator or coach rather than a lecturer, providing scaffolding tailored to the learner. John Dewey's experiential education and Jerome Bruner's work on discovery were important precursors and influences.

Criticism

Critics, particularly from cognitive science, argue that minimally guided instruction is ineffective for novices because it overburdens working memory, and that direct instruction produces better outcomes for beginners. Defenders respond that well-designed constructivist approaches include substantial guidance and that the theory describes how learning happens rather than prescribing the absence of teaching.
