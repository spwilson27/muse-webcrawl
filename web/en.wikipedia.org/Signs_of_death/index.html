Signs of death

Signs of death are the physical changes by which death may be recognized and medically or legally confirmed. Traditionally, the irreversible cessation of breathing and heartbeat—cardiorespiratory death—was the principal criterion. With modern resuscitation and ventilatory support, brain death, the irreversible loss of all functions of the entire brain including the brainstem, has been added as a criterion in many jurisdictions.

Post-mortem changes

After death the body undergoes a characteristic series of changes used by forensic pathologists to estimate the post-mortem interval:

  • Pallor mortis – paleness occurring almost immediately after death as blood ceases to circulate.
  • Algor mortis – cooling of the body toward ambient temperature.
  • Livor mortis (lividity) – gravitational pooling of blood, producing purplish discoloration of dependent parts.
  • Rigor mortis – stiffening of the muscles, beginning within a few hours, complete by roughly 12 hours, and passing off over the following day or two.
  • Decomposition – putrefaction and autolysis, producing discoloration, bloating, and eventual skeletonization.

Determination of death

Historically, fear of premature burial led to reliance on putrefaction as the only certain sign. Modern practice confirms death by the absence of pulse, respiration, reflexes, and responsiveness, supplemented where necessary by electrocardiography, electroencephalography, or cerebral blood-flow studies. Formal criteria for brain death, first published by a Harvard Medical School committee in 1968, generally require coma with known cause, absent brainstem reflexes, and apnoea.
