Blue Zone

[Image placeholder: Blue Zone village]

Blue Zones are regions of the world where people are claimed to live, or to have lived, measurably longer lives than average, with high concentrations of centenarians. The concept arose from demographic work by Gianni Pes and Michel Poulain, who identified a cluster of long-lived villages in the mountains of Sardinia and marked it on a map with a blue pen. Journalist Dan Buettner popularized the idea from 2005 onward through National Geographic reporting and a series of books, identifying five original Blue Zones:

  • Sardinia, Italy (particularly the mountainous Barbagia region)
  • Okinawa, Japan
  • Nicoya Peninsula, Costa Rica
  • Icaria, Greece
  • Loma Linda, California, United States (among its Seventh-day Adventist community)

Common characteristics

Buettner's work attributed the longevity of Blue Zone populations to shared lifestyle features, summarized as regular natural physical activity, a sense of purpose, moderate caloric intake, a largely plant-based diet rich in legumes, moderate alcohol consumption (in some zones), strong family ties, social engagement, and participation in religious or spiritual communities.

Criticism

The concept has been criticized by some demographers and gerontologists. Skeptics argue that records of extreme age in some regions may be unreliable, affected by poor historical birth registration, clerical error, or even pension fraud; research published in the 2010s and 2020s noted statistical associations between claimed supercentenarian concentrations and areas with poor record-keeping, and questioned whether the zones' data would survive rigorous re-validation. Defenders respond that the best-studied zones, such as Sardinia and Okinawa, have undergone careful age validation. The Blue Zones model has nonetheless been commercially influential, spawning community health programs ("Blue Zones Projects") in dozens of cities.
