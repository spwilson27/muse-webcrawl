Golf course

A golf course is the grounds on which the sport of golf is played. It consists of a series of holes, each with a tee box, a fairway, the rough and other hazards, and a green with a cylindrical hole in the ground, known as a "cup". The cup holds a flagstick, known as a "pin". A standard round of golf consists of 18 holes. Most courses contain 18 holes; some have nine holes, and are played twice per round; others have 27 or 36 holes.

Design

Golf course architecture involves the layout of holes to balance challenge, variety, and natural beauty. Designers such as Alister MacKenzie, Donald Ross, and A. W. Tillinghast created celebrated courses in the early twentieth century. Courses are commonly classified as links courses (on sandy coastal terrain, as in Scotland), parkland courses (inland, tree-lined), heathland, and desert courses. A typical 18-hole course covers roughly 60 to 80 hectares and plays between 5,000 and 7,500 yards from the back tees.

Parts of a course

Each hole begins at a teeing ground, from which the ball is first struck. The fairway is the closely mown area leading to the putting green, which is a very finely cut surface surrounding the hole. Hazards include bunkers (sand traps) and water hazards such as ponds and streams. The overall difficulty of a course is expressed by its par, course rating, and slope rating.

Environmental issues

Golf courses have been criticised for their water consumption, use of pesticides and fertilisers, and conversion of natural habitats, particularly in arid regions. Modern course management increasingly emphasises drought-tolerant grasses, reclaimed water irrigation, and habitat preservation, and some courses have earned environmental certifications for sustainable practices.
