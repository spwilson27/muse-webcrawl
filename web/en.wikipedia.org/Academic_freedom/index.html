Academic freedom

Academic freedom is the principle that scholars and students in academic institutions should be free to teach, study, research, publish and speak on their subjects without interference, censorship or retaliation from institutional authorities, governments or outside pressure groups, within the bounds of law and professional standards. It is usually held to encompass freedom of research and publication, freedom in the classroom, and freedom of intramural and extramural expression, and is closely linked to institutional autonomy and to tenure, which is designed to protect it.

History

The modern concept derives from the German university ideals of Lehrfreiheit (freedom to teach) and Lernfreiheit (freedom to learn), articulated by Wilhelm von Humboldt in the founding of the University of Berlin in 1810. In the United States, the American Association of University Professors' 1915 Declaration of Principles and its joint 1940 Statement of Principles on Academic Freedom and Tenure became foundational documents. Academic freedom was gravely violated in Nazi Germany, the Soviet Union (as in the Lysenko affair) and McCarthy-era America, episodes that shaped its modern legal protections, which in the U.S. draw on the First Amendment — the Supreme Court in Keyishian v. Board of Regents (1967) called academic freedom "a special concern of the First Amendment". Internationally it is protected by instruments such as UNESCO's 1997 Recommendation concerning the Status of Higher-Education Teaching Personnel and, in Europe, Article 13 of the EU Charter of Fundamental Rights.

Debates

Contemporary disputes concern the limits of extramural speech, controversial teaching content, donor and government influence, and the balance between academic freedom and institutional policies on harassment and inclusion. Supporters argue academic freedom is essential to the university's truth-seeking mission; critics sometimes argue it must be balanced against other values or professional responsibilities.
