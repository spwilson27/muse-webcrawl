In the Germanic languages, a strong verb is a verb that forms its past tense by altering its root vowel — a process called ablaut — rather than by adding a dental suffix as weak verbs do. English examples include sing–sang–sung, drive–drove–driven and break–broke–broken. The strong verbs descend from the Proto-Indo-European system of vowel gradation and preserve it most faithfully among Indo-European verb systems.

The seven classes

Traditional grammar divides strong verbs into seven classes according to their ablaut pattern. In Germanic, four principal parts are cited: infinitive, past singular, past plural and past participle. Representative Proto-Germanic patterns:

Historical development

Proto-Germanic inherited ablaut from Proto-Indo-European, where vowel grades (e-grade, o-grade, zero grade) had grammatical functions. Class VII verbs originally formed their past by reduplication (as Gothic still partly shows: haitan – haihait, "call"). Over the centuries many strong verbs have become weak by analogy — English help once had past holp — while a few weak verbs have become strong (dig–dug, wear–wore). German retains a fuller system than English; Dutch and the Scandinavian languages have their own distributions, and modern Icelandic keeps ablaut particularly robustly.
