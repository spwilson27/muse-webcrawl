Ramsey problem

The Ramsey problem, in its most famous form known as the party problem, asks for the smallest number of guests that must be invited to a party so that at least m guests will all know each other, or at least n guests will all be mutual strangers. It is the classic motivating question of Ramsey theory, a branch of combinatorics named after the British mathematician Frank P. Ramsey.

Statement

Formally, the problem is to determine the Ramsey number R(m, n): the smallest number of vertices in a complete graph such that, however the edges are coloured red or blue, there is either a red complete subgraph on m vertices or a blue complete subgraph on n vertices. In the party interpretation, vertices are guests, a red edge joins two guests who know each other, and a blue edge joins two who are strangers.

The classic case

The best-known instance is R(3, 3) = 6: at any party of six people there are either three mutual acquaintances or three mutual strangers, while a party of five can avoid both configurations. Ramsey numbers grow explosively and are notoriously hard to compute; even R(5, 5) remains unknown, and Paul Erdős famously remarked that if aliens demanded the value of R(5, 5) humanity should marshal all its resources to compute it — but if they demanded R(6, 6), humanity should instead try to destroy the aliens.

Significance

The Ramsey problem embodies the central insight of Ramsey theory: complete disorder is impossible, and any sufficiently large structure contains highly ordered substructures.
