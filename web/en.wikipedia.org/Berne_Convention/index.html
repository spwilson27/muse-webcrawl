The Berne Convention for the Protection of Literary and Artistic Works, commonly known as the Berne Convention, is an international agreement governing copyright, first adopted in Berne, Switzerland, in 1886. It requires its signatories to recognise the copyright of works of authors from other signatory countries in the same way as it recognises the copyright of its own nationals. As of the 2020s it has 181 parties, and it is administered by the World Intellectual Property Organization (WIPO).

Principles

The Convention rests on three basic principles: national treatment (works originating in one member state receive in others the protection granted to domestic works); automatic protection (protection must not depend on formalities such as registration or a copyright notice); and independence of protection (protection in one country does not depend on protection in the work's country of origin). It sets a minimum copyright term of the author's life plus 50 years for most works (many states, including EU members and the United States, now apply life plus 70), mandates exclusive rights of translation, reproduction, adaptation, and public performance, and recognises authors' moral rights of attribution and integrity (added at Rome in 1928).

Permitted limitations on rights are governed by the "three-step test": exceptions must be confined to certain special cases, must not conflict with normal exploitation of the work, and must not unreasonably prejudice the author's legitimate interests.

History

The Convention grew out of 19th-century frustration with the international piracy of books, championed by the International Literary and Artistic Association founded in 1878 under the presidency of Victor Hugo. Ten states signed the original 1886 text. Successive revision conferences expanded its scope. The United States, long an outlier that protected foreign works only under bilateral arrangements and required registration and notice, finally joined in 1989 (effective 1 March 1989). The Berne standards were incorporated into world trade law by the TRIPS Agreement (1994), which makes them binding on WTO members, and were extended to the digital environment by the WIPO Copyright Treaty (1996).

Criticism

Developing countries and access-to-knowledge advocates have criticised the Convention's high minimum standards and long terms as constraining education, translation, and technology transfer, a tension reflected in the (little-used) Appendix for developing countries added in 1971 and in ongoing WIPO Development Agenda debates.
