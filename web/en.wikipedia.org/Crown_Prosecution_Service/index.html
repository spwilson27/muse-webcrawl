Crown Prosecution Service

The Crown Prosecution Service (CPS) is the principal public agency for conducting criminal prosecutions in England and Wales. It operates independently and decides which cases should be prosecuted, determines appropriate charges in more serious or complex cases, advises the police during investigations, and presents cases in court. The CPS is headed by the Director of Public Prosecutions (DPP), who is appointed by and superintended by the Attorney General.

The Crown Prosecution Service prosecutes cases in the courts of England and Wales.

History

Before 1986, prosecutions in England and Wales were generally initiated and conducted by the police themselves, a system criticised by the Royal Commission on Criminal Procedure (the Philips Commission, 1981) for combining investigation and prosecution. The Prosecution of Offences Act 1985 created the CPS, which began operating in 1986, separating the decision to prosecute from the police investigation for the first time.

The Code for Crown Prosecutors

Prosecutors apply the Full Code Test: first, the evidential stage — is there sufficient evidence to provide a realistic prospect of conviction? — and second, the public interest stage — is a prosecution required in the public interest? If the evidential stage is not met, the case cannot proceed regardless of public interest. Where custody time limits are pressing, a modified Threshold Test may be applied.

Organisation

The CPS employs several thousand prosecutors and caseworkers organised into regional Areas across England and Wales, supplemented by special casework divisions dealing with complex fraud (in coordination with the Serious Fraud Office), terrorism, organised crime and proceeds-of-crime recovery. Crown advocates and self-employed counsel present cases in the Crown Court, while CPS prosecutors appear in magistrates' courts.
