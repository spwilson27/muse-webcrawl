Ageing

Ageing (or aging in American English) is the process of becoming older. The term refers mainly to humans and is defined as the accumulation of a variety of molecular and cellular damage over time, leading to a gradual decrease in physical and mental capacity and an increased risk of disease and death.

Biology

The hallmarks of ageing include genomic instability, telomere attrition, epigenetic alterations, loss of proteostasis, disabled macroautophagy, deregulated nutrient sensing, mitochondrial dysfunction, cellular senescence, stem cell exhaustion, altered intercellular communication, chronic inflammation, and dysbiosis. These processes underlie the progressive decline in tissue function characteristic of old age.

Causes and theories

Evolutionary theories explain ageing as a consequence of declining selection pressure with age, including mutation accumulation, antagonistic pleiotropy, and the disposable soma theory. Damage-based theories emphasise free radicals, DNA damage, and cross-linking of proteins.

Demographics

Populations worldwide are ageing as fertility falls and life expectancy rises. The proportion of people aged 60 years and over is projected to grow substantially during the 21st century, with major implications for health systems, pensions, and labour markets. The study of ageing is called gerontology, and the branch of medicine concerned with diseases of old age is geriatrics.

Interventions

No intervention has yet been proven to stop ageing in humans, but caloric restriction, exercise, and certain drugs such as metformin and rapamycin extend lifespan in model organisms and are under active investigation.
