Aromaticity

In chemistry, aromaticity is a property of cyclic, planar molecular structures with delocalised pi electrons that gives them enhanced stability compared with similar structures with localised bonds. The concept explains the unusual behaviour of benzene and related compounds: rather than undergoing addition reactions like ordinary alkenes, aromatic rings preferentially undergo substitution, preserving the delocalised system.

Hückel's rule

A monocyclic, planar, fully conjugated system is aromatic when it contains 4n + 2 pi electrons, where n is a non-negative integer — the criterion proposed by Erich Hückel in 1931. Benzene (6 pi electrons), the cyclopentadienyl anion (6) and the cycloheptatrienyl cation (6) are aromatic, whereas planar systems with 4n pi electrons are antiaromatic and particularly unstable. Experimental signs of aromaticity include equalised bond lengths, large resonance energies, and characteristic ring currents in NMR spectroscopy.

Scope

Beyond hydrocarbons, heteroaromatic compounds such as pyridine, furan and thiophene — in which a heteroatom contributes to the pi system — are aromatic, as are fused-ring systems like naphthalene (10 pi electrons). Extensions of the concept include Möbius aromaticity in twisted rings and metalloaromaticity in metal-containing clusters. Aromaticity remains a qualitative but indispensable organising concept in organic chemistry.
