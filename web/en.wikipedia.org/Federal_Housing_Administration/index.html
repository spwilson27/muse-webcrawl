The Federal Housing Administration (FHA) is a United States government agency founded by President Franklin D. Roosevelt's National Housing Act of 1934. The FHA insures mortgages made by private lenders, reducing their risk and enabling loans with low down payments and long repayment terms. Since 1965 it has been part of the Department of Housing and Urban Development.

History

Before the FHA, home buyers typically needed down payments of 50 percent and loans were repaid within a few years. The FHA standardised the long-term, fully amortising mortgage with down payments as low as 3.5 percent, greatly expanding home ownership in the United States; the home ownership rate rose from about 44 percent in 1934 to around 62 percent by 1960. The agency's early underwriting standards, however, institutionalised redlining and racial segregation by refusing to insure loans in Black and integrated neighbourhoods, a practice whose effects on the racial wealth gap remain a subject of study. FHA underwriting was formally desegregated by reforms and fair-housing legislation in the 1960s.

Operations

The FHA does not lend money or build housing; it insures loans made by approved lenders, funded by mortgage insurance premiums paid by borrowers. FHA loans are especially important to first-time buyers and borrowers with modest credit histories, and the agency's share of the mortgage market typically expands during housing downturns, as it did after the 2008 financial crisis.
