Endoscopic third ventriculostomy

Endoscopic view of the floor of the third ventricle

Endoscopic third ventriculostomy (ETV) is a surgical procedure for the treatment of obstructive (non-communicating) hydrocephalus, in which an endoscope is used to create a small opening in the floor of the third ventricle of the brain. This allows cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) to bypass the obstruction — most often at the cerebral aqueduct — and flow directly into the subarachnoid space, where it can be absorbed normally.

Procedure

Through a small burr hole in the skull, a rigid or flexible endoscope is advanced through the lateral ventricle and the foramen of Monro into the third ventricle. Under direct vision, the thin floor of the ventricle is perforated, usually between the infundibular recess and the mammillary bodies, and the opening is enlarged with a balloon catheter. Care is taken to avoid the basilar artery, which lies just beneath the floor. The procedure is sometimes combined with coagulation of the choroid plexus (ETV/CPC), particularly in infants.

Indications and outcomes

ETV is most commonly performed for aqueductal stenosis and for hydrocephalus caused by tumours obstructing CSF flow, such as tectal gliomas and posterior fossa tumours. Its main advantage over a ventriculoperitoneal shunt is that it avoids implanted hardware and its associated risks of infection and malfunction. Success rates vary with the cause of hydrocephalus and the patient's age, being highest (often above 70%) in older children and adults with aqueductal stenosis, and lower in young infants; the ETV Success Score is used to predict outcomes.

Complications

Possible complications include bleeding, injury to the basilar artery or hypothalamus, CSF leak, infection and failure of the opening, leading to recurrent hydrocephalus that may require a shunt.
