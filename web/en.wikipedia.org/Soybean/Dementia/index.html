Dementia

Neuroimaging is used in the assessment of dementia

Dementia is a syndrome associated with many neurodegenerative diseases, characterised by a general decline in cognitive abilities that affects a person's ability to perform everyday activities. This typically involves problems with memory, thinking, behaviour and motor control. Aside from memory impairment and a disruption in thought patterns, the most common symptoms include emotional problems, difficulties with language and decreased motivation.

Causes

The most common cause of dementia is Alzheimer's disease, accounting for roughly 60–70% of cases. Other major causes include vascular dementia, dementia with Lewy bodies, and frontotemporal dementia. Some dementias are caused or contributed to by conditions such as stroke, Parkinson's disease, chronic alcohol misuse, and infections, and a small proportion result from potentially reversible causes such as thyroid dysfunction or vitamin B12 deficiency.

Diagnosis and stages

Diagnosis is based on a history of decline in mental abilities, cognitive testing (such as the Mini-Mental State Examination or Montreal Cognitive Assessment), laboratory tests and brain imaging to identify the underlying cause. Dementia is commonly described as progressing through early, middle and late stages, from mild forgetfulness through loss of independence to severe impairment requiring full-time care.

Treatment and epidemiology

There is no cure for most dementias, but cholinesterase inhibitors and memantine can modestly improve symptoms in Alzheimer's disease, and anti-amyloid monoclonal antibodies have been approved in some countries for early disease. Worldwide, around 55 million people were estimated to be living with dementia in the early 2020s, with nearly 10 million new cases each year; the number is expected to rise sharply as populations age. Risk reduction measures include controlling cardiovascular risk factors, physical exercise, and avoiding excessive alcohol.
