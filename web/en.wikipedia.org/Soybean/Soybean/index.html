The soybean (Glycine max) is a species of legume native to East Asia, widely grown for its edible bean, which has numerous uses. One of the world's most important crops, it is a major source of vegetable protein and oil, and the great majority of the harvest is processed into soybean meal for livestock feed and soybean oil.

Description

Soybean plants are annual, hairy herbs growing from about 20 cm to 2 m tall. The trifoliate leaves fall before the seeds ripen. Flowers are small and self-fertile, borne in the leaf axils; the fruit is a hairy pod containing two to four seeds ("beans"), each 5–11 mm in diameter.

History

Soybeans were domesticated in China several thousand years ago — legend credits the mythical emperor Shennong — and spread through East Asia, where they became a dietary staple in foods such as tofu, soy sauce, miso, tempeh and soy milk. Introduced to Europe in the 18th century and North America in the 19th, the crop expanded enormously in the 20th century, especially in the United States, Brazil and Argentina. Like other legumes, soybeans fix atmospheric nitrogen through symbiosis with Bradyrhizobium bacteria, enriching the soil.

Production and uses

Global production exceeds 350 million tonnes per year. Crushed beans yield oil (used in cooking, processed foods and biodiesel) and protein-rich meal used mainly as animal feed. Whole-bean foods include edamame, tofu, soy milk, soy sauce and fermented products. Soy protein is also used in meat analogues and infant formula.

Nutrition

Soybeans are unusually protein-rich for a plant food and contain all essential amino acids, together with isoflavones and other bioactive compounds that have been studied for their health effects.
