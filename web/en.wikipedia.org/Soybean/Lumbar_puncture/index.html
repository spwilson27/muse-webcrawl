Lumbar puncture

Lumbar puncture in the lateral recumbent position

Lumbar puncture (LP), also known as a spinal tap, is a medical procedure in which a needle is inserted into the spinal canal, most commonly to collect cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) for diagnostic testing. A lumbar puncture may also be performed to measure intracranial pressure, to inject medications or contrast agents, or to administer spinal anaesthesia. The procedure was first described in the 1890s by Heinrich Quincke.

Procedure

The patient is positioned on their side with knees drawn up, or seated leaning forward, to flex the spine and widen the gaps between the vertebrae. Under sterile conditions, a spinal needle is inserted in the midline, usually at the L3/L4 or L4/L5 interspace — below the level at which the spinal cord ends — through the skin, ligaments and the dura and arachnoid into the subarachnoid space. Opening pressure may be measured with a manometer, and CSF is collected in sterile tubes for analysis.

Diagnostic use

CSF analysis can help diagnose infections of the central nervous system such as bacterial meningitis and viral encephalitis, subarachnoid haemorrhage not visible on imaging, inflammatory conditions such as multiple sclerosis (oligoclonal bands), and some cancers. Contraindications include raised intracranial pressure with risk of herniation, infection at the puncture site and severe coagulopathy; brain imaging is obtained first when there are warning signs such as reduced consciousness or focal neurological deficits.

Side effects

The most common side effect is post-dural-puncture headache, caused by CSF leakage, which is typically worse when upright and may be treated with an epidural blood patch. Serious complications such as bleeding, infection and nerve injury are rare, especially with atraumatic (pencil-point) needles.
