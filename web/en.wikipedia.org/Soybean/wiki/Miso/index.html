Miso (Japanese: 味噌) is a traditional Japanese seasoning produced by fermenting soybeans with salt and koji (the fungus Aspergillus oryzae), and sometimes rice, barley, seaweed or other ingredients. The resulting thick paste is used to flavour sauces, spreads, pickles and soups — most famously miso soup — and is prized for its deep umami taste.

History

Fermented soybean pastes originated in China and were brought to Japan by Buddhist monks around the 6th–7th centuries. During the Muromachi and Edo periods miso became a staple of the Japanese diet, valued as a source of protein and as a preservative food; regional varieties proliferated.

Types

Miso is classified by its grain and colour. White miso (shiromiso) is fermented briefly and tastes mild and sweet; red miso (akamiso) is aged longer and is saltier and richer; barley miso (mugi miso) and pure soybean miso (mame miso, e.g. Hatcho miso) are other major styles. Flavour, colour and texture vary widely by region and producer.

Use and nutrition

Beyond miso soup, miso is used in marinades, glazes (such as for black cod), dressings and noodle broths. It provides protein, B vitamins and sodium; because the paste is usually not boiled in delicate soups, some fermentation-derived compounds are preserved.
