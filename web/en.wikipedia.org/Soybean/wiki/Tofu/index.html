OriginChina, Han dynasty (traditionally)
Main ingredientsSoy milk, coagulant (nigari or calcium sulfate)
NamesChinese: dòufu; Japanese: tōfu; Korean: dubu

Tofu is a food prepared by coagulating fresh soy milk, pressing the resulting curds into solid white blocks of varying softness — silken, soft, firm and extra-firm. Originating in China over two thousand years ago, it spread across East and Southeast Asia and in recent decades became a staple protein worldwide, especially in vegetarian and vegan cooking.

Production

Dried soybeans are soaked, ground with water and boiled to make soy milk. A coagulant — traditionally nigari (magnesium chloride from seawater) or calcium sulfate — sets the milk into curds, which are pressed: light pressing gives silken and soft tofu; heavier pressing gives firm, sliceable blocks. Calcium-set tofu is a good source of calcium.

Culinary use

Mild and absorbent, tofu takes on the flavours around it. It is stir-fried, deep-fried (agedashi tofu, abura-age pouches for inari sushi), simmered in hot pots, blended into desserts (silken), crumbled as a mince substitute, and featured in dishes such as mapo tofu, miso soup, sundubu-jjigae and stinky tofu. Fermented and dried forms (fuyu, yuba skin, tempeh's cousin products) extend the soy repertoire.

Nutrition

Tofu is high in protein with all essential amino acids, low in saturated fat, and provides iron, calcium and isoflavones. It is naturally gluten-free and cholesterol-free, making it a common meat alternative.
