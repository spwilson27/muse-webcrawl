Umami (Japanese: うま味), or savouriness, is one of the five basic tastes, alongside sweetness, sourness, bitterness and saltiness. It is the taste of glutamates and certain nucleotides, perceived as a pleasant, broth-like or meaty flavour.

Discovery

Umami was identified by Japanese chemist Kikunae Ikeda in 1908, who isolated glutamic acid from kombu seaweed broth (dashi) and patented its salt, monosodium glutamate (MSG). Shintaro Kodama later identified the nucleotide inosinate in bonito flakes, and Akira Kuninaka found guanylate in shiitake mushrooms; these compounds act synergistically, multiplying the umami sensation. The scientific community broadly accepted umami as a basic taste after dedicated taste receptors (T1R1/T1R3) were characterised around 2000.

Properties and sources

Umami has a mild, lasting aftertaste and stimulates salivation. Fermentation and ageing concentrate it: soy sauce, miso, fish sauce, aged cheeses and cured hams are rich in free glutamate. Cooking releases nucleotides from meat, giving the classic synergy of dashi (glutamate from kombu plus inosinate from bonito).
