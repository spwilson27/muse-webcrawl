Tempeh (Indonesian: tempe) is a traditional Indonesian food made by fermenting soybeans with a Rhizopus mould, which binds the beans into a firm, sliceable cake. Unlike most other soy products, tempeh uses the whole bean, giving it a high content of protein, dietary fibre and vitamins, and a firm texture with an earthy, nutty flavour.

History

Tempeh originated in Central Java, probably several centuries ago; it is mentioned in the Serat Centhini, an early 19th-century Javanese manuscript. long a cheap staple protein in Java, it spread through Indonesia and, in the late 20th century, became popular worldwide in vegetarian and vegan cooking.

Production

Soybeans are soaked, dehulled, partly cooked, inoculated with Rhizopus oligosporus or R. oryzae spores, wrapped (traditionally in banana leaves, now often perforated plastic) and incubated at around 30 °C for 24–48 hours, until a dense white mycelium knits the beans together.

Culinary use

In Indonesia tempeh is fried, stewed in coconut-milk curries, crumbled into sambal, or caramelised as tempe kering. In Western cooking it is marinated, grilled or used as a meat substitute in sandwiches and stir-fries. Tempeh-like fermentations can also be made from other beans, grains and seeds.
