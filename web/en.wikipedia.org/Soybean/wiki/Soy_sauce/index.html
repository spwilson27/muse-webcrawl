Soy sauce

Soy sauce
Soy sauce served as a condiment
OriginChina, by the 2nd century AD (Han dynasty)
IngredientsSoybeans, wheat, salt, water, Aspergillus koji
NamesChinese: jiàngyóu; Japanese: shōyu; Korean: ganjang

Soy sauce is a liquid condiment of Chinese origin, traditionally made by fermenting soybeans and roasted grain with the mould culture koji (Aspergillus oryzae or A. sojae) in brine. Dark, salty and rich in umami, it is a cornerstone seasoning of East and Southeast Asian cuisines and is now used worldwide.

Production

In traditional brewing, steamed soybeans and roasted wheat are inoculated with koji and left to form a fragrant mouldy grain; this is mixed with brine to form a mash (moromi) that ferments for months — premium sauces for years — before pressing, pasteurisation and bottling. Cheap "chemical" soy sauces are instead made in days by acid-hydrolysing soy protein, then colouring and flavouring the result.

Varieties

Chinese cooking distinguishes light soy sauce (thin, saltier, for seasoning) from dark soy sauce (aged longer, with molasses, for colour). Japanese shōyu includes koikuchi (standard), usukuchi (lighter), tamari (little or no wheat) and shiro (white) styles. Korean ganjang includes soup soy sauce (guk-ganjang), and Indonesia's kecap manis is a thick, sweetened variant.

Use and nutrition

Soy sauce is used as a table condiment, marinade and cooking ingredient. It is very high in sodium (about 900 mg per tablespoon), though reduced-salt versions exist; it contains free glutamates responsible for its umami depth.
