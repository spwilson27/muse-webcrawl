A ventriculoperitoneal (VP) shunt is a medical device used to treat hydrocephalus by draining excess cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) from the ventricles of the brain into the peritoneal cavity of the abdomen, where it is absorbed. It is the most common type of CSF shunt and a standard treatment for communicating hydrocephalus, including normal pressure hydrocephalus.

Components and procedure

The system consists of a proximal catheter placed through a burr hole into a lateral ventricle of the brain, a valve that regulates flow and prevents backflow, and a distal catheter tunnelled under the skin of the neck, chest and abdomen into the peritoneal cavity. Many modern valves are programmable: their opening pressure can be adjusted non-invasively with a magnetic tool, allowing drainage to be fine-tuned after surgery. Some valves incorporate an anti-siphon device to limit over-drainage when the patient stands.

Indications

VP shunts are placed for obstructive and communicating hydrocephalus of many causes — congenital malformations, haemorrhage, infection, tumours and normal pressure hydrocephalus in older adults. Alternatives include the ventriculoatrial shunt (to the heart), lumboperitoneal shunt, and endoscopic third ventriculostomy, an internal bypass that avoids implanted hardware in selected cases of obstructive hydrocephalus.

Complications

  • Malfunction: blockage by tissue or debris, kinking, disconnection or fracture; a leading cause of repeat surgery, especially in children.
  • Infection: colonisation of the hardware by skin bacteria, requiring antibiotics and usually shunt removal and replacement.
  • Over-drainage: can cause low-pressure headaches, slit-ventricle syndrome or subdural haematoma.
  • Under-drainage and abdominal complications such as pseudocyst or, rarely, bowel perforation.

Because failure rates are substantial — many shunts require revision within the first years — lifelong follow-up is standard, and patients are taught the warning symptoms of shunt failure.
