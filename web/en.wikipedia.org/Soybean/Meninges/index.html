Meninges

The three meningeal layers: dura, arachnoid and pia mater

The meninges (singular: meninx) are the three membranes that envelop the brain and spinal cord. In mammals, the meninges are the dura mater, the arachnoid mater, and the pia mater. Cerebrospinal fluid is located in the subarachnoid space between the arachnoid mater and the pia mater. The primary function of the meninges is to protect the central nervous system.

Layers

The dura mater is the tough outermost layer; around the brain it has two layers, the outer periosteal layer attached to the skull and the inner meningeal layer, which form folds such as the falx cerebri and tentorium cerebelli and contain the dural venous sinuses. The arachnoid mater is the delicate middle layer, named for its spider-web-like appearance; beneath it lies the subarachnoid space filled with cerebrospinal fluid and traversed by trabeculae. The pia mater is the thin innermost layer that closely adheres to the surface of the brain and spinal cord, following their contours. The dura and arachnoid together are sometimes called the leptomeninges' counterpart, the pachymeninx, while the arachnoid and pia are the leptomeninges.

Clinical significance

Inflammation of the meninges is called meningitis, most often caused by viral or bacterial infection and a medical emergency when bacterial. Bleeding can collect in the meningeal spaces: epidural and subdural haematomas lie outside or beneath the dura, while subarachnoid haemorrhage is bleeding into the subarachnoid space, typically from a ruptured aneurysm. Tumours arising from the meninges are called meningiomas and are usually benign. Diagnostic lumbar puncture samples CSF from the subarachnoid space below the end of the spinal cord.
