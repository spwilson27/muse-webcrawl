Choroid plexus

The choroid plexus in the ventricular system

The choroid plexus, or plica choroidea, is a plexus of cells that occurs in each of the four ventricles of the brain. The choroid plexus is a network of capillaries and specialised ependymal cells that form the blood–cerebrospinal fluid barrier and produce the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) that fills the ventricles and bathes the central nervous system.

Structure

Choroid plexus is found in the lateral ventricles, the third ventricle and the fourth ventricle. It consists of a layer of cuboidal epithelial cells surrounding a core of fenestrated capillaries and connective tissue. The cells are joined by tight junctions, creating a selective barrier between the blood and the CSF.

Function

The choroid plexus produces most of the body's cerebrospinal fluid — roughly 400–500 ml per day in adults — through filtration and active secretion. It also removes waste products and foreign substances from the CSF, regulates its chemical composition, and provides a route by which immune cells survey the central nervous system.

Clinical significance

Disorders involving the choroid plexus include choroid plexus papillomas and carcinomas, which can overproduce CSF or obstruct its flow and cause hydrocephalus; choroid plexus cysts, common incidental findings on prenatal ultrasound; and inflammation (plexitis) in some infections. Surgical treatment of hydrocephalus has historically included choroid plexus coagulation, often combined with endoscopic third ventriculostomy.
