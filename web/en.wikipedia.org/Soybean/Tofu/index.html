Tofu

Tofu (bean curd)
OriginChina, Han dynasty (by tradition, attributed to Liu An, 2nd century BC)
Main ingredientsSoy milk, coagulant (nigari or calcium sulfate), water
VarietiesSilken, soft, firm, extra-firm; fermented, dried, fried and flavoured forms
NutritionHigh in protein, low in calories; source of iron and calcium (when set with calcium salts)

Tofu (Chinese: 豆腐, dòufǔ; Japanese: 豆腐, tōfu), also known as bean curd, is a food made by coagulating fresh soy milk and pressing the resulting curds into soft white blocks. Originating in China over a thousand years ago, it became a staple across East and Southeast Asia and, from the 20th century, a major source of protein in vegetarian and vegan cooking worldwide. Tofu is bland on its own and readily absorbs the flavours of marinades, sauces and broths.

History

Chinese tradition credits Liu An, a Han-dynasty prince (d. 122 BC), with tofu's invention. Documented production is clear by the Song dynasty, and tofu spread to Japan (as tōfu), Korea (dubu), Vietnam (đậu phụ) and beyond, often carried by Buddhist monks whose vegetarian cuisine made wide use of it. Benjamin Franklin tasted tofu and described it in a 1770 letter — among the first Western mentions. Commercial production in the West began in the late 19th and 20th centuries with Asian immigration and grew with vegetarian movements.

Production

Soybeans are soaked, ground and boiled to make soy milk; a coagulant — traditionally nigari (magnesium chloride from seawater) or gypsum (calcium sulfate) — curdles the proteins, and the curds are pressed into blocks with varying amounts of water expelled, yielding silken, soft, firm and extra-firm grades. Regional variants include stinky tofu (fermented), tofu skin (yuba, the film skimmed from heated soy milk), dried and pressed doufu gan, and Okinawan and Burmese chickpea-based versions.

Culinary use and nutrition

Tofu appears in dishes from mapo tofu and miso soup to agedashi tofu, hot pot and stir-fries, and in the West as a meat substitute in scrambles, grills and desserts. A typical serving provides all essential amino acids with little fat and no cholesterol.
