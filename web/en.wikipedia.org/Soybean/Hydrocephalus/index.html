Hydrocephalus


Enlarged ventricles in hydrocephalus

Hydrocephalus is a condition, most often congenital, in which an abnormal accumulation of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) within the ventricles of the brain causes increased intracranial pressure and progressive enlargement of the head in infants. The term derives from the Greek words hydro (water) and kephalos (head). It can also be acquired later in life as a result of infection, haemorrhage, tumours, or injury.

Classification

Hydrocephalus is classified as communicating when CSF flows freely between the ventricles but is not adequately absorbed into the venous system, or non-communicating (obstructive) when flow within the ventricular system is blocked, most commonly at the cerebral aqueduct. A special form, normal pressure hydrocephalus, affects older adults and presents with a characteristic triad of gait disturbance, urinary incontinence, and dementia, despite apparently normal CSF pressure.

Signs and symptoms

In infants, whose skull bones have not yet fused, hydrocephalus produces rapid head enlargement, a bulging fontanelle, prominent scalp veins, and the "setting-sun" downward gaze. In older children and adults, symptoms are those of raised intracranial pressure: headache, vomiting, blurred vision, papilloedema, and impaired consciousness. Untreated, the condition can cause brain damage or death.

Treatment

The standard treatment is surgical diversion of CSF, most commonly by inserting a ventriculoperitoneal shunt — a valved tube that drains fluid from a cerebral ventricle to the peritoneal cavity. An alternative procedure, endoscopic third ventriculostomy, creates an opening in the floor of the third ventricle to restore flow. Shunts are effective but prone to infection and blockage, often requiring revision.
