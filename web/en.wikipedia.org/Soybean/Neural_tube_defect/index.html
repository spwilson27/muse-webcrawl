Neural tube defects (NTDs) are a group of birth defects in which an opening in the spinal cord or brain remains from early embryonic development. In the third week of gestation, specialised cells on the dorsal side of the embryo form the neural tube; failure of this tube to close completely produces the defects.

Types

The two most common NTDs are spina bifida, in which the spinal cord or its coverings protrude or develop abnormally, and anencephaly, the absence of a major portion of the brain and skull. Rarer forms include encephalocele (a protrusion of brain tissue through the skull) and iniencephaly.

Causes

NTDs arise from a combination of genetic predisposition and environmental factors. Folate (vitamin B9) deficiency is the most important modifiable risk factor; certain anti-seizure medications (notably valproic acid), maternal diabetes, obesity and hyperthermia also increase risk.

Prevention and screening

Randomised trials showed that periconceptional folic acid supplementation prevents a large proportion of NTDs, leading many countries to mandate folic-acid fortification of staple foods. Prenatal screening uses maternal serum alpha-fetoprotein measurement and ultrasound.

Treatment

Depending on severity, spina bifida may be managed with surgery (sometimes performed before birth), physiotherapy and lifelong care. Anencephaly has no treatment and is invariably fatal in the newborn period.
