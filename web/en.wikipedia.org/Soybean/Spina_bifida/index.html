Spina bifida

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Spina bifida (Latin for "split spine") is a birth defect in which there is incomplete closing of the spine and the membranes around the spinal cord during early development in pregnancy. It is a type of neural tube defect. In the most severe form, myelomeningocele, the spinal cord and its coverings protrude through an opening in the back, causing nerve damage, leg weakness or paralysis, and bladder and bowel problems. Milder forms include meningocele and the often symptomless spina bifida occulta.

Causes and prevention

The neural tube normally closes by the fourth week of pregnancy. Failure to close is associated with low folate levels, certain medications (such as valproate), maternal diabetes and obesity, and genetic factors. Taking folic acid before conception and in early pregnancy reduces the risk of neural tube defects by up to about 70 percent, which has led many countries to fortify flour with folic acid.

Diagnosis and treatment

Spina bifida can be detected before birth by ultrasound and maternal blood tests for alpha-fetoprotein. After birth, myelomeningocele is usually closed surgically within days; a landmark trial (the MOMS trial, 2011) showed that surgery before birth can improve outcomes for some children, though with risks of prematurity. Associated conditions include hydrocephalus — often treated with a shunt — Chiari II malformation, and latex allergy. With modern care, most people with spina bifida live well into adulthood.
