Normal pressure hydrocephalus (NPH) is an abnormal build-up of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) in the ventricles of the brain that can elevate pressure only intermittently, so that measured pressure often remains within the normal range. It chiefly affects older adults and is notable as one of the few potentially treatable causes of dementia.

Signs and symptoms

NPH is classically described by a triad, remembered mnemonically as "wet, wobbly and wacky":

  • Gait disturbance — a slow, broad-based, shuffling "magnetic" gait, often the earliest and most shunt-responsive feature;
  • Urinary incontinence — urgency progressing to incontinence;
  • Cognitive impairment — a subcortical dementia with slowed thinking, apathy and forgetfulness.

Causes and mechanism

Most cases are idiopathic, thought to arise from impaired absorption of CSF at the arachnoid granulations, possibly aggravated by reduced compliance of the brain and vascular disease. Secondary NPH can follow subarachnoid haemorrhage, meningitis, traumatic brain injury or brain surgery, which scar the CSF pathways. Intermittent pressure waves (B-waves) are thought to gradually enlarge the ventricles and injure periventricular white matter, especially fibres serving the legs and bladder.

Diagnosis

Brain imaging shows enlarged ventricles out of proportion to sulcal widening; a high Evans index (ratio of frontal horn width to skull width above ~0.3) supports the diagnosis. Response to CSF removal — a high-volume lumbar puncture ("tap test") or a period of lumbar drainage — helps predict who will benefit from surgery. NPH must be distinguished from Alzheimer's disease and vascular dementia, with which it may coexist.

Treatment

The standard treatment is surgical implantation of a shunt, most commonly a ventriculoperitoneal shunt, which drains excess CSF to the abdominal cavity. Programmable valves allow non-invasive adjustment. Gait improves most reliably; cognitive and bladder symptoms respond less predictably. Risks include infection, obstruction and over-drainage causing subdural haematoma.

History

The syndrome was described in 1965 by the Colombian neurosurgeon Salomón Hakim, together with Raymond Adams, in patients whose dementia improved after CSF shunting — a landmark observation that some dementia could be reversible.
