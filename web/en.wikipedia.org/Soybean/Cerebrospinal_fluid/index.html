Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) is a clear, colourless body fluid found within the tissue that surrounds the brain and spinal cord of all vertebrates. It cushions the central nervous system, circulates nutrients and chemicals filtered from the blood, and removes waste products from the brain.

Production and circulation

CSF is produced mainly by the choroid plexuses in the ventricles of the brain, at roughly 500 mL per day, though the total volume present at any time is only about 150 mL, so the fluid is continuously absorbed and replaced. It flows from the lateral ventricles through the interventricular foramina to the third ventricle, via the cerebral aqueduct to the fourth ventricle, and out into the subarachnoid space surrounding the brain and spinal cord. It is absorbed into the venous blood through arachnoid granulations in the dural sinuses.

Functions

  • Buoyancy: suspended in CSF, the brain's effective weight falls from ~1,400 g to the equivalent of ~50 g, reducing pressure on its base.
  • Protection: the fluid acts as a shock absorber against sudden movements and blows.
  • Chemical stability and waste removal: CSF carries away metabolic waste; during sleep, exchange between CSF and interstitial fluid (the "glymphatic" system) clears solutes such as amyloid beta.
  • Nutrient and hormone transport within the central nervous system.

Clinical significance

CSF can be sampled by lumbar puncture ("spinal tap") to diagnose infections such as meningitis, subarachnoid haemorrhage, multiple sclerosis and other neurological conditions; its pressure, cell counts, protein and glucose are measured. Blockage of CSF flow or absorption causes hydrocephalus, treated with shunting. Leaks of CSF after trauma or spontaneously can cause low-pressure headaches.
