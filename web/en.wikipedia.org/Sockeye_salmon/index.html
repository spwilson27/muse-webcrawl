Sockeye salmon

The sockeye salmon (Oncorhynchus nerka), also called the red salmon, kokanee salmon (in its landlocked form), or blueback salmon, is a species of salmon found in the Northern Pacific Ocean and the rivers discharging into it. It is among the most commercially valuable of the Pacific salmons, prized for its deep red, flavorful flesh rich in omega-3 fatty acids and the pigment astaxanthin.

Description and life cycle

Sockeye are anadromous: they hatch in freshwater, spend one to three years rearing in lakes — a requirement distinguishing them from other Pacific salmon — migrate to the ocean for one to four years, and return to their natal streams to spawn and die. In the ocean they are silvery-blue; on the spawning migration the body turns brilliant red and the head green, and males develop a pronounced humped back and hooked jaw (kype). Adults typically weigh 2–7 kg. The landlocked form, the kokanee, completes its whole life in freshwater lakes and is much smaller. Juveniles feed on zooplankton, especially Daphnia, which supplies the carotenoids coloring the flesh.

Distribution and fisheries

The species ranges from California and Hokkaido north to the Bering Sea and Arctic drainages. The largest runs occur in Bristol Bay, Alaska, where tens of millions of sockeye may return in a season, supporting one of the world's most valuable wild salmon fisheries, celebrated for its sustainability management. Other major systems include the Fraser River in British Columbia, where returns fluctuate dramatically. Indigenous peoples of the Northwest Coast have harvested sockeye for millennia.

Conservation

Some populations — notably in the Columbia and Snake rivers and parts of British Columbia — are listed as endangered or threatened because of dams, habitat loss, warming waters, and overharvest history. Sockeye are also keystone nutrient transporters, carrying marine-derived nitrogen into riparian forests via predators and their own carcasses, a subsidy measured in tree growth along spawning streams.
