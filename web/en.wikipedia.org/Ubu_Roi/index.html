Ubu Roi

Ubu Roi (Ubu the King or King Ubu) is a play by French writer Alfred Jarry, first performed in Paris on 10 December 1896 at the Théâtre de l'Œuvre. A wild, grotesque parody of Shakespearean tragedy — drawing on Macbeth, Hamlet, and Richard III — it follows the cowardly, gluttonous, and murderous Père Ubu, who, egged on by his wife Mère Ubu, assassinates King Wenceslaus of Poland and seizes the throne, then tyrannizes his subjects before fleeing into exile.

Premiere and scandal

The premiere provoked one of the great theatrical scandals of the era: the play's opening word, "Merdre!" (a deliberate deformation of merde), triggered uproar, and the performance could proceed only in fits and starts between the jeering and applause of the audience. Jarry had begun the work as a schoolboy farce mocking a physics teacher at his lycée in Rennes; he presented Ubu with a pear-shaped head, a vast belly decorated with a spiral, and the mechanical delivery of a puppet.

Legacy

Ubu Roi is regarded as a foundational work of modernist theatre, a precursor of Dada, surrealism, and the Theatre of the Absurd. Jarry wrote several sequels and almanacs about Ubu and developed around the character his mock philosophy of 'pataphysics, "the science of imaginary solutions." The figure of Ubu — the brutal, infantile tyrant — has remained a potent satirical symbol and has inspired countless adaptations in theatre, opera, film, and visual art, including works by Joan Miró and Max Ernst.
