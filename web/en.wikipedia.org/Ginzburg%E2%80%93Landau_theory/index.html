Ginzburg–Landau theory is a mathematical theory of superconductivity formulated in 1950 by Soviet physicists Vitaly Ginzburg and Lev Landau. Based on Landau's general theory of second-order phase transitions, it describes a superconductor by means of a complex order parameter, ψ, whose magnitude gives the density of superconducting electrons, and derives equations governing how ψ varies in space, in magnetic fields and near interfaces.

The theory

The theory expands the free energy in powers of the order parameter and its gradient, with coefficients that change sign at the critical temperature. From it follow two characteristic lengths: the coherence length ξ, over which ψ can vary, and the penetration depth λ, over which a magnetic field decays inside the superconductor. Their ratio, the Ginzburg–Landau parameter κ = λ/ξ, separates type-I superconductors (κ < 1/√2) from type-II superconductors (κ > 1/√2), which admit magnetic flux in the form of quantised vortices — the Abrikosov vortex lattice, predicted by Alexei Abrikosov using the theory in 1957.

Legacy

Although phenomenological, the theory was later derived from the microscopic BCS theory by Lev Gor'kov, confirming its validity near the critical temperature. The framework of an order parameter with a free-energy functional became a model for describing phase transitions generally, and its mathematical structure — a gauge theory of a charged scalar field — prefigures the Higgs mechanism in particle physics. Ginzburg and Abrikosov shared the 2003 Nobel Prize in Physics for their work on superconductivity.
