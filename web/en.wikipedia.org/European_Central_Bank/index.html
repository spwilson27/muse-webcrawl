The European Central Bank (ECB) is the central bank for the euro and administers monetary policy for the euro area, which comprises 20 member states of the European Union. It is one of the world's most important central banks and one of seven institutions of the European Union listed in the Treaty on European Union.

Mandate and structure

The ECB's primary objective is price stability, defined as a symmetric 2% inflation target over the medium term. Its decision-making bodies are the Governing Council (the Executive Board plus the governors of the national central banks of the euro area) and the six-member Executive Board, appointed for non-renewable eight-year terms. The bank is independent of political instruction under the treaties. Together with the national central banks it forms the Eurosystem; the wider European System of Central Banks includes all EU central banks. Since 2014 the ECB has also been the central prudential supervisor of significant banks in the euro area under the Single Supervisory Mechanism.

History

The ECB was established on 1 June 1998, succeeding the European Monetary Institute, and the euro was launched on 1 January 1999 (notes and coins followed in 2002). Its first president was Wim Duisenberg, followed by Jean-Claude Trichet (2003–2011), Mario Draghi (2011–2019) — whose 2012 pledge to do "whatever it takes" to preserve the euro marked a turning point in the euro area debt crisis — and Christine Lagarde from 2019. The bank moved into its purpose-built double-tower headquarters on the site of Frankfurt's Großmarkthalle in 2014. During crises it deployed unconventional tools including long-term refinancing operations, the Public Sector Purchase Programme (quantitative easing) and the pandemic emergency purchase programme.
