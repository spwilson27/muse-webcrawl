TypeIgneous intrusion (pluton)
DefinitionExposed area greater than 100 km²

A batholith is a large mass of intrusive igneous rock (a pluton) that forms from cooled magma deep in the Earth's crust. By definition, a batholith has a surface exposure of more than 100 square kilometres (40 sq mi); smaller bodies are called stocks. Batholiths are almost always made mostly of felsic or intermediate rock types such as granite, quartz monzonite, or diorite.

Formation

Batholiths are not usually single intrusions but composites of many plutons emplaced over tens of millions of years, commonly above subduction zones, where mantle-derived melts rise and pond in the crust, melt crustal rock, and assemble large magma chambers. Mechanisms by which magma makes room for itself include stoping (breaking off and assimilating blocks of country rock), assimilation, and inflation of chamber floors.

The granites exposed at the surface today crystallized at depths of perhaps 5–30 km and were revealed only after prolonged uplift and erosion of the overlying rock.

Examples

Notable batholiths include the Sierra Nevada Batholith of California, the Coast Mountains Batholith of British Columbia and Alaska (the largest in North America), the Andean batholiths of Peru and Chile, the Cornubian batholith of southwest England, the Idaho batholith, and the Boulder batholith of Montana. Parts of Yosemite National Park, including Half Dome and El Capitan, are carved from Sierra Nevada batholith granite.

Etymology

The term was coined by Eduard Suess from the Greek bathos ("depth") + lithos ("rock").
