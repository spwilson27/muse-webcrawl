John Snow (15 March 1813 – 16 June 1858) was an English physician, a pioneer of anaesthesia and medical hygiene, and a founder of modern epidemiology, famous for tracing the source of an 1854 cholera outbreak in Soho, London, to a contaminated water pump.

Early life and anaesthesia

Born in York to a working-class family, Snow was apprenticed to a surgeon in Newcastle, studied in London, and qualified as a physician. He became London's leading anaesthetist, meticulously studying ether and chloroform dosages, designing inhalers, and administering chloroform to Queen Victoria during the births of Prince Leopold (1853) and Princess Beatrice (1857), which legitimised obstetric anaesthesia.

Cholera and the Broad Street pump

Against the prevailing "miasma" theory, Snow argued in On the Mode of Communication of Cholera (1849; enlarged 1855) that cholera spread through contaminated water. When cholera killed more than 600 people around Broad Street, Soho, in 1854, Snow mapped the deaths — his celebrated dot map showed cases clustering around the Broad Street pump — and persuaded the parish board to remove the pump handle. He later conducted a "Grand Experiment" comparing cholera mortality among households supplied by the Southwark and Vauxhall company (drawing polluted Thames water) and the Lambeth company (which had moved its intake upstream), demonstrating the waterborne route statistically.

Legacy

Snow died of a stroke in 1858, aged 45, before his ideas were fully accepted; germ theory and the identification of Vibrio cholerae later vindicated him. He is commemorated by the John Snow Society, a memorial pump in Soho, and is regarded as one of the fathers of epidemiology and public health.
