Xi'an Incident

The Xi'an Incident was a political crisis in the Republic of China in December 1936, in which Nationalist generalissimo Chiang Kai-shek was kidnapped in Xi'an by two of his own commanders, Zhang Xueliang and Yang Hucheng, who demanded that the Kuomintang end its civil war against the Chinese Communist Party and instead form a united front of resistance against Imperial Japan.

Background

After Japan seized Manchuria in 1931 and pressed into northern China, Chiang's policy of "first internal pacification, then external resistance" — prioritising the extermination campaigns against the Communists — became deeply unpopular, not least with Zhang Xueliang's Northeastern Army, exiled from its Manchurian homeland. Secret contacts between Zhang and the Communists in 1936 produced a local truce. Chiang flew to Xi'an in December 1936 to compel a final offensive against the Communist base at Yan'an.

The kidnapping and negotiations

In the early hours of 12 December 1936, Zhang and Yang's troops seized Chiang at the Huaqing hot springs outside the city, killing members of his bodyguard; Chiang was caught fleeing in his nightclothes. The mutineers issued demands, including reorganisation of the government and an end to the civil war. Nanjing debated a punitive expedition, but negotiations — involving Zhou Enlai for the Communists, whose policy (guided by Moscow) favoured Chiang's release to lead the anti-Japanese resistance — led to a settlement. Chiang gave verbal assurances of a united front and was released on 25 December, returning to Nanjing with Zhang, who was promptly arrested.

Consequences

The incident led, after the Marco Polo Bridge clash of July 1937, to the formal Second United Front between the Kuomintang and the Communists (1937–1945 in name). Zhang Xueliang spent over half a century under house arrest, first in mainland China and then in Taiwan, before emigrating to Hawaii, where he died in 2001 aged 100. Yang Hucheng was imprisoned and murdered by Nationalist agents in 1949.
