Fractional ownership of aircraft

Fractional programmes let customers buy a share of a business jet rather than the whole aircraft.

Fractional ownership of aircraft is a method of owning and operating private and business aircraft in which several unrelated parties share the costs of purchasing, leasing and operating an aircraft. Each owner holds a fractional share — commonly one sixteenth, corresponding to about 50 hours of flying per year — and a professional management company provides crew, maintenance, hangarage and scheduling across a pooled fleet, so a shareholder can fly on any equivalent aircraft in the fleet, not only "their" airframe.

History

The modern fractional model was pioneered in 1986 by Richard Santulli's Executive Jet Aviation, which became NetJets; its success led Warren Buffett's Berkshire Hathaway to acquire the company in 1998. Competitors followed, including Flexjet, Flight Options and PlaneSense (which applies the model to turboprops), and programmes spread to Europe and the Middle East.

Structure and costs

Owners typically pay (1) an initial acquisition price for their share, (2) a monthly management fee covering fixed costs, and (3) an occupied hourly rate for fuel, catering and other variable costs when they fly. Contracts usually run five years, after which the share can be sold back at fair market value. In the United States, fractional programmes operate under Federal Aviation Regulations Part 91 Subpart K, which sets management and operational standards distinct from both private (Part 91) and charter (Part 135) operations.

Comparison with alternatives

Fractional ownership sits between whole-aircraft ownership and air charter: it offers guaranteed availability and consistent service at lower commitment than sole ownership, though at a higher long-run cost per hour than high-volume charter or jet-card membership for infrequent flyers. It is generally considered economical for users flying roughly 50–400 hours per year.
