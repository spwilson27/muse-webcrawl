Schur's lemma

Schur's lemma is a theorem in representation theory that describes the homomorphisms between simple modules over an algebra. Named after the German mathematician Issai Schur (1875–1941), who established the result in the first decade of the twentieth century, the lemma is one of the foundational results of the representation theory of groups and algebras.

Statement

In its simplest form, the lemma states that if V and W are irreducible representations of a group G and φ is a linear map from V to W that intertwines the two representations — that is, commutes with the action of every element of G — then either φ is an isomorphism or φ is the zero map. In particular, any intertwining map from an irreducible representation to itself (an endomorphism of the representation) over an algebraically closed field must be a scalar multiple of the identity map.

Proof idea

The proof rests on two observations. First, the kernel of an intertwining map is an invariant subspace of the domain; since the domain is irreducible, the kernel is either zero or the whole space, so the map is either injective or zero. Second, the image of an intertwining map is an invariant subspace of the codomain; since the codomain is irreducible, the image is either zero or the whole space, so the map is either surjective or zero. Combining these, a nonzero intertwining map between irreducible representations is an isomorphism. For an endomorphism over an algebraically closed field, an eigenvalue exists, and subtracting that scalar multiple of the identity yields a singular intertwining map, which must therefore be zero.

Consequences

Schur's lemma implies that the group algebra of the abelianisation of a finite group controls its one-dimensional representations, that irreducible representations of abelian groups over algebraically closed fields are one-dimensional, and that the centre of a group acts by scalars in any irreducible representation. It also underlies Schur orthogonality relations for matrix coefficients and plays a key role in the theory of induced representations and in the classification of representations of compact groups.

A generalisation, sometimes called the Dixmier version of Schur's lemma, applies to simple modules over arbitrary algebras and concludes that the endomorphism ring of a simple module is a division ring. In module-theoretic language, the basic statement is that a homomorphism between two simple modules is either zero or an isomorphism, because its kernel and image are submodules.
