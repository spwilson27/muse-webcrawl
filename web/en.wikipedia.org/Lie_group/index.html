In mathematics, a Lie group (pronounced /liː/) is a group that is also a differentiable manifold, with the property that the group operations of multiplication and inversion are smooth maps. Lie groups combine algebraic and geometric structure and provide a natural framework for analysing continuous symmetry, much as permutation groups do for discrete symmetry. They are named after the Norwegian mathematician Sophus Lie, who developed the theory in the 1870s–1890s in connection with the study of differential equations.

Definitions and first examples

A Lie group is a smooth manifold G equipped with group operations such that the maps (x, y) ↦ xy and x ↦ x−1 are smooth. Standard examples include:

  • The real numbers ℝ under addition, and the nonzero reals ℝ* under multiplication.
  • The circle group S1 of complex numbers of absolute value 1 under multiplication.
  • The general linear group GL(n, ℝ) of invertible n×n real matrices.
  • The special orthogonal group SO(n) of rotations, and the special unitary group SU(n), central in particle physics.

Lie algebras

Associated to every Lie group is its Lie algebra, the tangent space at the identity element, equipped with a bilinear operation called the Lie bracket. The exponential map relates the algebra to the group. For matrix groups, the Lie algebra consists of matrices X such that exp(tX) lies in the group, and the bracket is the commutator [X, Y] = XY − YX. The correspondence between Lie groups and Lie algebras — developed by Lie and formalised in Lie's three theorems — reduces many questions about groups to linear algebra.

Classification

Connected simple Lie groups were classified by Wilhelm Killing and Élie Cartan. The simple complex Lie algebras fall into four infinite classical families (An, Bn, Cn, Dn, corresponding to special linear, orthogonal, and symplectic groups) and five exceptional types: G2, F4, E6, E7, and E8. Every connected Lie group can be built from a semisimple part and a solvable radical, via the Levi decomposition.

Applications

Lie groups are fundamental in modern physics: the Standard Model of particle physics is based on the gauge group SU(3) × SU(2) × U(1); special relativity rests on the Lorentz and Poincaré groups; and Noether's theorem links continuous symmetries of a physical system to conservation laws. Representation theory of Lie groups underlies the classification of elementary particles and much of harmonic analysis.
