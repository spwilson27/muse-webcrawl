Shrine of the Three Kings

The Shrine of the Three Kings (German: Dreikönigsschrein) is a reliquary traditionally believed to contain the bones of the Biblical Magi, also known as the Three Kings or Three Wise Men. The shrine is a large gilded and decorated triple sarcophagus placed above and behind the high altar of Cologne Cathedral in Germany. It is considered the high point of Mosan art and the largest reliquary in the Western world.

History

The relics of the Magi were originally held in Constantinople and later transferred to Milan. After Frederick Barbarossa conquered Milan in 1164, the relics were given to Rainald of Dassel, Archbishop of Cologne, who brought them to Cologne. The shrine was created between about 1180/1181 and 1225, largely by the goldsmith Nicholas of Verdun and his workshop.

Description

The shrine is approximately 43 inches (110 cm) wide, 60 inches (153 cm) high, and 87 inches (220 cm) long. It is shaped like a basilica: two sarcophagi stand side by side, with a third placed on top of their roof ridges. The shrine is covered with gilded silver figures, enamel work, and more than 1,000 jewels and beads, including precious stones and antique cameos and intaglios. Its imagery depicts scenes from the lives of Christ and the Magi, prophets, and apostles.

Opening and examination

The shrine was opened in 1864, revealing bones and remnants of ancient cloth. The bones were consistent with the remains of three men, traditionally identified as the Magi.

Significance

The shrine made Cologne one of the most important pilgrimage destinations in medieval Europe, and its presence spurred the construction of the Gothic Cologne Cathedral beginning in 1248. The Three Kings remain central to Cologne's identity, appearing on the city's coat of arms.
