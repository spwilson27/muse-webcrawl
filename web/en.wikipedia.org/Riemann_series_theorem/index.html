Riemann series theorem

The Riemann series theorem, also called the Riemann rearrangement theorem, is a theorem in mathematical analysis due to Bernhard Riemann. It states that if an infinite series of real numbers is conditionally convergent, then its terms can be rearranged into a permutation that converges to any prescribed real number, or that diverges.

By reordering terms, a conditionally convergent series can be made to approach any chosen sum.

Statement

Let (an) be a sequence of real numbers such that the series Σan converges, but the series Σ|an| of absolute values diverges. Then for every extended real number x in [−∞, +∞] there exists a permutation (bn) of the terms such that the partial sums of Σbn converge to x.

Idea of the proof

Separate the terms into the positive terms and the negative terms. Conditional convergence forces both the series of positive terms and the series of (absolute values of) negative terms to diverge, while the terms themselves tend to zero. To hit a target sum x, one greedily adds positive terms until the partial sum first exceeds x, then adds negative terms until it first drops below x, and repeats. Because the terms shrink to zero, the overshoot at each stage shrinks to zero, so the partial sums converge to x.

Example

The alternating harmonic series 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + ⋯ converges to ln 2, but rearranging it by taking two positive terms for each negative term gives a series converging to (3/2) ln 2. More generally, taking p positive terms per q negative terms yields the sum ln 2 + (1/2) ln(p/q).

Generalizations

The theorem contrasts sharply with absolute convergence, where every rearrangement converges to the same sum. Extensions to series in finite-dimensional Euclidean spaces were given by Lévy and Steinitz; the Lévy–Steinitz theorem describes the set of all sums of rearrangements as an affine subspace. In infinite-dimensional spaces the picture is more subtle.
