Robert Leroy Johnson (May 8, 1911 – August 16, 1938) was an American blues musician and songwriter. His landmark recordings in 1936 and 1937 display a combination of singing, guitar skills, and songwriting talent that has influenced generations of musicians. Although his recording career spanned only seven months, he is now widely regarded as a master of the Delta blues style and one of the most influential musicians of the 20th century.

Life

Johnson was born in Hazlehurst, Mississippi, and grew up around Memphis and the Mississippi Delta, where he learned harmonica and guitar. After the deaths of his young wife and child, he devoted himself to music, travelling throughout the Delta and beyond, performing in juke joints and on street corners. Two recording sessions — in San Antonio in November 1936 and Dallas in June 1937 — produced 29 songs. He died near Greenwood, Mississippi, in 1938, aged 27; the cause is unknown, though poisoning by a jealous husband is a longstanding account in blues lore.

Legend

Legend holds that Johnson sold his soul to the Devil at a crossroads in exchange for his guitar mastery — a story attached to his sudden improvement as a player and to songs like "Cross Road Blues" and "Me and the Devil Blues". The myth, developed by promoters and later writers, made him the archetypal bluesman of the 1960s revival.

Legacy

Johnson sold few records in his lifetime, but the 1961 reissue album King of the Delta Blues Singers electrified young British and American musicians — Eric Clapton, Robert Plant, Keith Richards and Bob Dylan among them — and his songs became rock standards. He was inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame in 1986, and the complete recordings won a Grammy; in 2003 Rolling Stone ranked him among the greatest guitarists of all time.
