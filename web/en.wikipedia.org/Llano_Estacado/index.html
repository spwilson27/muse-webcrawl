Llano Estacado

The Llano Estacado (Spanish: [ˈʎano estaˈkaðo], "Staked Plain") is a vast, flat mesa region in the southwestern United States, covering much of the Texas Panhandle and South Plains and extending into eastern New Mexico. It is one of the largest mesas in North America, roughly 400 kilometres north-to-south, bounded on the north by the Canadian River, on the east by the Caprock Escarpment, and on the west by the Mescalero Ridge overlooking the Pecos River valley.

Name

The origin of the name is debated. One tradition holds that Spanish explorers drove stakes into the featureless plain to mark their route across it; another derives "estacado" from the palisade-like appearance of the Caprock escarpment. Coronado crossed the plain in 1541 and described a land so level that his men felt as if they had been "swallowed up by the sea".

Geography and economy

The surface of the Llano is remarkably level, sloping gently southeast, and largely treeless shortgrass prairie. Beneath it lies the southern portion of the Ogallala Aquifer, whose water made large-scale irrigated agriculture possible in the 20th century. The region is a major producer of cotton, sorghum, cattle and — increasingly — wind energy. Principal cities include Lubbock and Amarillo (on its northern edge), Texas, and Clovis and Hobbs, New Mexico. Chronic groundwater depletion of the Ogallala is the region's central environmental concern.

History

For centuries the Llano was the heartland of the Comanche and Kiowa, whose mastery of the plains long deterred European settlement; the Comanchero trade linked them with New Mexico. Anglo-American settlement followed the Red River War of 1874–75, after which ranching outfits such as the XIT Ranch dominated, later giving way to farming.
