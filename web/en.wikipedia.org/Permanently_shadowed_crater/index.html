Permanently shadowed crater

A permanently shadowed crater (or permanently shadowed region, PSR) is a depression on a body in the Solar System, typically the Moon or Mercury, that lies so close to a rotation pole and has such a small axial tilt that sunlight never reaches its floor. Some of these regions have not received direct sunlight for billions of years.

Characteristics

Because the Moon's axis is tilted only about 1.5° to the ecliptic, crater floors near the lunar poles can be in permanent shadow while nearby crater rims enjoy near-permanent illumination (peaks of eternal light). Temperatures in lunar PSRs can fall below 40 K, among the coldest measured anywhere in the Solar System, making them efficient cold traps where volatile compounds such as water ice can accumulate over geological time.

Evidence and exploration

Radar observations from the Clementine and Lunar Prospector missions first suggested ice in polar craters. In 2009, NASA's LCROSS impactor struck the permanently shadowed crater Cabeus and its plume confirmed substantial water. Instruments on India's Chandrayaan-1 and NASA's Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter have mapped PSRs and surface frost in detail. The ice deposits are of great interest both scientifically and as a potential resource (for drinking water, oxygen and rocket propellant) for future lunar exploration, which is why the lunar south pole region is the target of the Artemis programme.
