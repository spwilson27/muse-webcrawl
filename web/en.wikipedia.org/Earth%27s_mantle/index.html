Earth's mantle

Earth's mantle is the mostly solid layer of silicate rock between the crust and the outer core, about 2,900 km thick and comprising roughly 84% of Earth's volume and 67% of its mass. Although solid, mantle rock flows very slowly over geological timescales, and this creeping convection drives plate tectonics, volcanism and the generation of new crust.

Structure

The upper mantle extends from the Mohorovičić discontinuity to about 660 km depth. Its uppermost part, together with the crust, forms the rigid lithosphere; beneath lies the weaker asthenosphere, in which rock is close to its melting point and flows readily. Seismic discontinuities at about 410 km and 660 km depth mark phase transitions in the mineral olivine — to wadsleyite and ringwoodite, and then to bridgmanite plus ferropericlase in the lower mantle. The lower mantle extends to the core–mantle boundary at 2,890 km, where the thin, heterogeneous D″ layer contains large low-shear-velocity provinces and ultra-low velocity zones. Mantle temperatures rise from roughly 500–900 °C near the top to about 4,000 °C at the core boundary, and average composition is modelled by "pyrolite", a peridotite dominated by olivine, pyroxenes and garnet.

Convection and dynamics

Mantle convection transports heat from the interior to the surface through the motion of tectonic plates, subducting slabs (imaged by seismic tomography descending to the lower mantle) and rising plumes such as those thought to feed Hawaii and Iceland. Mantle material can be sampled directly in xenoliths brought up by volcanoes, in ophiolites, and in rare exposures such as orogenic peridotite massifs.
