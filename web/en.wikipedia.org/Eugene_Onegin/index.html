Eugene Onegin (Russian: Евгений Онегин) is a novel in verse written by Alexander Pushkin, one of the classics of Russian literature. It was composed between 1823 and 1831 and published in its complete form in 1833.

Plot

Eugene Onegin, a jaded young St Petersburg aristocrat — the archetypal "superfluous man" — inherits a country estate and becomes neighbours with the idealistic poet Vladimir Lensky and the Larin family. Olga Larin is engaged to Lensky; her sister Tatyana falls in love with Onegin and writes him a frank letter, which he coldly rejects. At Tatyana's name-day party Onegin flirts with Olga to spite Lensky, provoking a duel in which he kills his friend. Years later he meets the now-married Tatyana in St Petersburg, falls in love with her, and is in turn rejected. The novel closes mid-scene.

Form and legacy

The work is written in fourteen-line "Onegin stanzas" of iambic tetrameter and is narrated by a digressive, ironic voice modelled on the author. Tchaikovsky adapted it as an opera in 1879. Vladimir Nabokov's literal English translation and four-volume commentary (1964) remain famous.
