Hare

Hares are mammals belonging to the genus Lepus in the family Leporidae, which also includes rabbits. Hares are typically larger than rabbits, with longer ears and longer, more powerful hind legs, and are adapted for fast running in open country — species such as the European hare can reach speeds around 70 km/h (43 mph) in short bursts. The genus contains more than 30 species distributed across Europe, Asia, Africa and North America (where some are called jackrabbits).

Biology

Unlike rabbits, hares do not burrow; they rest in shallow depressions called forms. Their young (leverets) are born fully furred, with eyes open (precocial), and are left alone in the form, visited by the mother briefly to nurse. Hares are solitary or live in loose pairs, are mostly nocturnal or crepuscular herbivores, and rely on camouflage and speed rather than cover to escape predators. The snowshoe hare famously turns white in winter. Hares change their behaviour in the breeding season — the "mad March hare" of European folklore reflects the boxing and chasing of courting hares, the boxing usually being a female repelling a male.

Relationship to humans

Hares have been hunted for food and sport for millennia and appear in folklore and mythology worldwide, including the Moon rabbit of East Asian tradition, trickster figures in African and Native American tales, and the Easter hare, predecessor of the Easter Bunny. Hare populations have declined in parts of Europe with agricultural intensification, and hares can suffer from diseases such as European brown hare syndrome and rabbit haemorrhagic disease virus 2.
