Factor VIII

Factor VIII (coagulation factor VIII, procoagulant component)
GeneF8
LocationX chromosome (Xq28)
FunctionBlood coagulation cofactor

Factor VIII (FVIII) is an essential blood-clotting protein, also known as anti-hemophilic factor (AHF). In humans, factor VIII is encoded by the F8 gene. Defects in this gene result in hemophilia A, a recessive X-linked coagulation disorder.

Physiology

Factor VIII is produced in the liver's sinusoidal cells and endothelial cells outside the liver, and circulates in the bloodstream in an inactive form, bound to von Willebrand factor. In response to injury, factor VIII is activated by thrombin and separates from von Willebrand factor. The active protein, factor VIIIa, acts as a cofactor for factor IXa, and together they form the "tenase" complex that activates factor X, a key step in the coagulation cascade leading to fibrin clot formation.

Clinical significance

Deficiency of factor VIII causes hemophilia A, characterised by prolonged bleeding, spontaneous joint bleeds and easy bruising. Treatment involves replacement therapy with factor VIII concentrates derived from plasma or produced by recombinant DNA technology. Some patients develop inhibitory antibodies against replacement factor VIII, complicating treatment; the bispecific antibody emicizumab and gene therapies have been developed as alternatives. Elevated factor VIII levels are associated with an increased risk of venous thrombosis.

Structure

Factor VIII is a large glycoprotein of about 330 kDa, synthesised as a single chain that is processed into heavy and light chains held together by a metal ion. Its domain structure (A1-A2-B-A3-C1-C2) is similar to that of factor V.
