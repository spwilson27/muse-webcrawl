The Sand Motor (Dutch: Zandmotor) is an artificial peninsula constructed in 2011 off the coast of South Holland in the Netherlands, between Ter Heijde and Kijkduin. It is a large-scale experiment in dynamic coastal management: instead of repeated small beach nourishments, some 21.5 million cubic metres of dredged sand were deposited in a single hook-shaped peninsula extending about one kilometre into the North Sea, and wind, waves and currents were left to redistribute the sand along roughly 20 kilometres of the Delfland coast.

Background

The Dutch coast is maintained against erosion by regular sand nourishment — about 12 million cubic metres a year nationally. The Sand Motor, built by contractors Van Oord and Boskalis for Rijkswaterstaat and the Province of South Holland, tested whether a concentrated "mega-nourishment" could feed adjacent beaches for up to twenty years while reducing disturbance to marine ecosystems and creating space for recreation and nature.

Evolution and monitoring

Intensive monitoring has shown the peninsula gradually eroding and spreading as designed: a shallow lagoon and dune lake formed on its landward side, new dunes developed, and birds and seals colonised parts of the site. The Sand Motor became a popular destination for walkers, kite-surfers and fossil hunters, as dredged sands yielded Pleistocene mammal bones. The experiment, assessed over a ten-year evaluation programme, has informed similar "Building with Nature" schemes abroad, including the Bacton sandscaping project in Norfolk, England.
