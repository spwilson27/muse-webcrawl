Feeding tube

PurposeEnteral nutrition for patients unable to eat sufficiently by mouth
TypesNasogastric, nasojejunal, gastrostomy (PEG), jejunostomy
Used inStroke, critical illness, prematurity, head and neck cancer, advanced dementia (controversial)
A gastrostomy tube delivers nutrition directly into the stomach.

A feeding tube is a medical device used to provide nutrition to people who cannot obtain nutrition by mouth, are unable to swallow safely, or need additional nutritional supplementation. Feeding by tube is the most common form of enteral nutrition, delivering liquid formula directly into the gastrointestinal tract.

Types

Short-term tubes pass through the nose: nasogastric tubes end in the stomach and nasojejunal tubes in the small intestine. For longer-term feeding, tubes are placed through the abdominal wall — a gastrostomy, commonly created by percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG), ends in the stomach, while a jejunostomy ends in the jejunum and is used when the stomach must be bypassed. Tube position must be verified before use, since misplacement into the airway can be dangerous.

Uses and complications

Feeding tubes are used in premature infants, patients recovering from stroke or major surgery, people with head and neck cancers, and patients in intensive care. Complications include aspiration pneumonia, tube displacement or blockage, infection at the insertion site, and refeeding syndrome when nutrition is restarted in malnourished patients. The use of feeding tubes in advanced dementia is controversial, as evidence does not show improved survival or comfort, and major medical societies recommend careful hand feeding instead. Decisions about long-term tube feeding also raise ethical questions at the end of life. See also enteral administration, parenteral nutrition, and percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy.
