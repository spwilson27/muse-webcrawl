Tertiary color

A tertiary color or intermediate color is a color made by mixing a full saturation of one primary color with half saturation of another primary color, in a given color space such as red–green–blue (RGB) or traditional red–yellow–blue (RYB) pigment mixing. On a twelve-part color wheel, the six tertiary colors sit between the three primaries and the three secondaries.

In pigment (RYB)

In the traditional painter's model, mixing a primary with an adjacent secondary gives six tertiaries, commonly named with compound terms: red-orange (vermilion), yellow-orange (amber), yellow-green (chartreuse), blue-green (teal), blue-violet (indigo/purple), and red-violet (magenta/wine). These names are not standardised and vary between authors. In printing's CMYK model the equivalent hues are similar in appearance.

In light (RGB)

In the additive RGB system, the tertiary colors are azure, violet, rose, orange, chartreuse, and spring green — the hues at 30° intervals between the primaries and secondaries of the HSV/HSL color wheel. Web colors exist for several of them. Because RGB and RYB primaries differ (blue versus the painter's blue, and green in place of yellow), their tertiary sets are shifted relative to one another, a frequent source of confusion in color teaching.

Terminology caveats

Some color theorists use "tertiary" differently, for mixtures of two secondary colors (yielding muted browns, slates, and olives — sometimes called earth colors), restricting "intermediate" to the primary-plus-secondary hues. Art-education usage generally follows the color-wheel definition above. Either way, tertiary colors are less saturated in character than primaries and secondaries and are much used in interior and costume design for harmonious palettes.
