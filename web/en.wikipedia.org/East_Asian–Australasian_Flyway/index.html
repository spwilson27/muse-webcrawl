East Asian–Australasian Flyway

The East Asian–Australasian Flyway is one of the world's nine major flyways used by migratory birds. It stretches from breeding grounds in Siberia and Alaska, through East and Southeast Asia, to non-breeding grounds in Australia and New Zealand, spanning some 22 countries. More than 50 million migratory waterbirds, including over 5 million shorebirds, use the flyway each year.

Species and sites

Species travelling the flyway include the bar-tailed godwit — famous for the longest known non-stop flights, tracked by satellite from Alaska to New Zealand — as well as the great knot, red knot, far eastern curlew, spoon-billed sandpiper and Siberian crane. Critical stopover sites include the tidal flats of the Yellow Sea (such as Yalu Jiang and Yancheng), the coasts of Bohai Bay, and Australian wetlands such as Roebuck Bay and the Eighty Mile Beach.

Threats

Reclamation of intertidal habitat, especially in the Yellow Sea region, has destroyed a large share of the stopover mudflats on which shorebirds depend, driving steep population declines in species such as the great knot and far eastern curlew. Other threats include hunting, disturbance, pollution and climate-change-driven sea level rise.

Conservation

The East Asian–Australasian Flyway Partnership (EAAFP), launched in 2006, coordinates governments, NGOs and conventions including the Ramsar Convention and the Bonn Convention, and maintains a network of internationally important Flyway Network Sites. Yellow Sea tidal flats in China and South Korea have gained World Heritage and Ramsar recognition.
