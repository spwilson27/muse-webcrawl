Visual Artists Rights Act

Murals and sculptures are covered works under VARA

Enacted: December 1, 1990

Codified: 17 U.S.C. § 106A

Subject: Moral rights of visual artists

The Visual Artists Rights Act of 1990 (VARA) is a United States federal law that grants certain moral rights to artists of works of visual art. Codified at 17 U.S.C. § 106A, it was the first federal legislation in the United States to recognize moral rights — rights of attribution and integrity — independent of an artist's copyright and ownership of the physical work.

Rights granted

VARA gives the artist of a qualifying work the right to claim authorship, to prevent the use of their name on works they did not create or that have been mutilated, and to prevent any intentional distortion, mutilation, or modification of the work that would be prejudicial to the artist's honor or reputation. For works of "recognized stature", the artist may also prevent destruction. The rights apply only to a narrow category of works: paintings, drawings, prints, sculptures, and exhibition photographs existing in single copies or limited editions of 200 or fewer signed copies.

Duration and waiver

For works created on or after June 1, 1991, VARA rights last for the life of the artist. Rights may be waived in a signed written agreement but cannot be transferred. Building owners who wish to remove art incorporated into a building must follow statutory notice procedures.

Notable litigation

In Cohen v. G&M Realty (the 5Pointz case), a federal court awarded $6.75 million in statutory damages after a developer whitewashed aerosol murals of recognized stature — the first major VARA damages award, affirmed by the Second Circuit in 2020.
