Pierre Laporte

Pierre Laporte (25 February 1921 – 17 October 1970) was a Canadian lawyer, journalist, and politician in Quebec. He served as deputy premier and minister of labour and immigration in the Liberal government of Robert Bourassa. Laporte is remembered chiefly as the victim of the October Crisis of 1970, in which he was kidnapped and murdered by members of the Front de libération du Québec (FLQ).

Career

Born in Montreal, Laporte worked as a journalist for Le Devoir, where he was a prominent critic of the Union Nationale government of Maurice Duplessis during the 1950s. He entered politics in 1961, winning a by-election, and held several portfolios during the Quiet Revolution under Jean Lesage. Returning to office with Bourassa's victory in April 1970, he was seen as a senior, stabilizing figure in the government.

October Crisis

On 10 October 1970, five days after the kidnapping of British trade commissioner James Cross, Laporte was abducted from his home in Saint-Lambert by the FLQ's Chénier cell. His kidnapping prompted the federal government of Pierre Trudeau to invoke the War Measures Act. On 17 October, Laporte's body was found in the trunk of a car near Saint-Hubert airport; he had been strangled. His killers were later convicted. The Pierre Laporte Bridge in Quebec City, the longest main-span suspension bridge in Canada, is named in his memory.
