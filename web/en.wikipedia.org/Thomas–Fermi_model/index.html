Thomas–Fermi model

The Thomas–Fermi model is a quantum-mechanical statistical model for the electronic structure of atoms, developed independently by Llewellyn Thomas and Enrico Fermi in 1927, shortly after the formulation of the Schrödinger equation. It treats the electrons of an atom as a degenerate Fermi gas whose density at each point is related to the local electrostatic potential, yielding a nonlinear differential equation for the potential — the Thomas–Fermi equation.

Theory

The model assumes electrons fill phase space uniformly (two per volume h3) and move in the self-consistent field produced by the nucleus and the electron cloud itself. Solving the resulting equation gives the electron density and atomic properties without detailed wavefunctions. It is most accurate for large atomic numbers, where the electron number is great enough for statistics to apply.

Limitations and legacy

Thomas–Fermi theory neglects electron exchange, correlation and shell structure, so it cannot reproduce chemical binding quantitatively (as shown by Teller's theorem, it predicts that molecules do not bind). Nevertheless it is a cornerstone of modern density-functional theory: the Thomas–Fermi kinetic-energy functional is the starting point from which orbital-free density functionals are built, and the model remains useful for warm dense matter, plasmas and stellar interiors.
