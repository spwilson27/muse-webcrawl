Motor control

Motor control

Field: Neuroscience, kinesiology, psychology
Key structures: Motor cortex, cerebellum, basal ganglia, spinal cord
Related: Motor learning, motor coordination, reflexes

Motor control is the regulation of movement in organisms that possess a nervous system: the process by which the central nervous system plans, initiates, and guides voluntary and involuntary movements by coordinating muscles and joints. It is studied across neuroscience, psychology, kinesiology, and robotics.

Mechanisms

Movement is produced by motor neurons activating muscle fibres in motor units. The primary motor cortex, premotor and supplementary motor areas plan and command movements, while the cerebellum fine-tunes them using sensory feedback and the basal ganglia help select and initiate actions. Spinal circuits generate rhythmic patterns (central pattern generators) and mediate reflexes, such as the stretch reflex, which operate without conscious direction. Sensory information from proprioceptors, vision, and the vestibular system allows the brain to correct movements in real time and to adapt them through learning.

Theories

Researchers debate how the nervous system solves the "degrees of freedom problem" identified by Nikolai Bernstein—how it chooses one pattern among the countless ways to perform a task. Theories include motor programs, equilibrium-point control, internal forward and inverse models, and optimal feedback control, which holds that the brain corrects only deviations that interfere with task goals. Phenomena such as Fitts's law (the speed–accuracy trade-off) and motor equivalence constrain these theories.

Disorders

Damage or disease affecting motor systems can cause weakness, spasticity, ataxia, tremor, Parkinson's disease, Huntington's disease, and developmental coordination disorder. Rehabilitation and assistive technologies such as brain–computer interfaces draw on motor control research.
