Ashford v Thornton

Ashford v Thornton (1818) 106 ER 149 is a famous English criminal case in which the defendant, Abraham Thornton, having been acquitted of the murder of Mary Ashford, was challenged by her brother William Ashford by appeal of murder. Thornton responded by demanding trial by battle, and the court held that he was entitled to it. Ashford declined to fight, Thornton was released, and Parliament abolished both appeals of murder and trial by battle the following year.

Background

Mary Ashford was found dead in a flooded pit near Erdington, Warwickshire, in May 1817 after attending a dance with Abraham Thornton. Thornton was tried for murder at Warwick Assizes and acquitted after establishing an alibi, in proceedings that lasted only a few hours.

The appeal and wager of battle

Under an archaic procedure, the victim's next of kin could bring a private appeal of murder even after an acquittal. William Ashford did so, and Thornton, appearing in King's Bench before Lord Ellenborough, threw down a gauntlet and pleaded the right to defend himself by trial by combat. The court ruled in April 1818 that the right had never been formally abolished and that Thornton could wage battle.

Aftermath

Ashford refused the combat, and Thornton was discharged. The publicity prompted Parliament to pass the Appeal of Murder, etc. Act 1819, abolishing both appeals of felony and the wager of battle.
