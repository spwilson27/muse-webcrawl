Neuroimaging is the use of quantitative (computational) techniques to study the structure and function of the central nervous system, developed as an objective way of scientifically studying the healthy human brain in a non-invasive manner. Increasingly it is also used for quantitative research studies of brain disease and psychiatric illness. Neuroimaging is a highly multidisciplinary field of research that is not a medical speciality.

Structural imaging

Computed tomography (CT) uses X-rays to produce cross-sectional images and remains standard for acute head injury and stroke assessment. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) provides superior soft-tissue contrast without ionising radiation, and specialised sequences visualise white-matter tracts (diffusion tensor imaging), blood vessels (MR angiography) and cortical anatomy in fine detail.

Functional imaging

Functional MRI (fMRI) measures the blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) signal as a proxy for neural activity and has dominated cognitive neuroscience since the 1990s. Positron emission tomography (PET) and single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) use radioactive tracers to image metabolism, blood flow and specific molecular targets such as amyloid plaques. EEG and MEG measure electrical and magnetic activity directly with millisecond resolution but poorer spatial localisation.

Clinical and research use

Clinically, neuroimaging diagnoses tumours, stroke, epilepsy and dementia; in research it underpins large-scale projects such as the Human Connectome Project and UK Biobank imaging. Criticisms include the replication and statistical-power problems of early fMRI research and debates over reverse inference from activation maps.
