Yuezhi

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Yuezhi were an ancient Indo-European people first described in Chinese sources as pastoral nomads living in the grasslands of the northwestern Gansu corridor, between the Qilian Mountains and Dunhuang. Driven westward by the Xiongnu in the 2nd century BCE, they migrated across Central Asia to Bactria, where one of their clans, the Kushans, later founded the Kushan Empire, a major power linking China, India, Persia, and Rome along the Silk Road.

Origins

Chinese annals such as the Shiji and Hanshu portray the Yuezhi as a powerful nomadic confederation that once dominated the eastern steppe and supplied jade and horses to China. Many scholars identify them with the speakers of the Tocharian languages of the Tarim Basin, though the equation remains debated. Around 176–160 BCE the Xiongnu under Modu Chanyu and his successor defeated the Yuezhi, famously making a drinking cup from the Yuezhi king's skull, and forced their great westward migration.

Migration and settlement in Bactria

The Yuezhi moved first to the Ili Valley, displacing the Sai (Scythians), and then southward into Bactria north of the Oxus (Amu Darya), which they conquered around 130 BCE — an event recorded by the Chinese envoy Zhang Qian, who reached them in about 128 BCE seeking an alliance against the Xiongnu. In Bactria they settled into five tribal principalities (yabghu), one of which, the Guishuang (Kushan), unified the others in the 1st century CE.

Kushan Empire

Under kings such as Kujula Kadphises and Kanishka, the Kushan Empire grew to encompass much of modern Afghanistan, Pakistan, and northern India. The empire facilitated Silk Road trade and the transmission of Buddhism into Central Asia and China, and produced the distinctive Greco-Buddhist art of Gandhara. The Yuezhi are thus a pivotal link between the steppe world, Iran, and India in antiquity.
