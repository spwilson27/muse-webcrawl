Irving Fisher (February 27, 1867 – April 29, 1947) was an American economist, statistician, inventor, eugenicist, and progressive social campaigner. He was one of the earliest American neoclassical economists and one of the first to apply mathematical methods systematically to economic theory. Joseph Schumpeter described him as "the greatest economist the United States has ever produced".

Life

Fisher was born in Saugerties, New York, and educated at Yale University, where he took his bachelor's degree in 1888 and a PhD in 1891 with a dissertation on mathematical economics, Mathematical Investigations in the Theory of Value and Prices (1892). He taught at Yale for most of his career. After contracting tuberculosis in 1898, he became an energetic campaigner for public health, hygiene, and eugenics, and wrote the best-selling How to Live (1915).

Fisher made a fortune from an index-card filing system business and invested heavily in the stock market. In October 1929, days before the crash, he famously declared that stock prices had reached "what looks like a permanently high plateau". He lost most of his wealth in the ensuing collapse, an experience that informed his later work on debt and deflation.

Contributions to economics

Quantity theory of money

In The Purchasing Power of Money (1911) Fisher formalized the equation of exchange, MV = PT, relating the money supply (M) and its velocity (V) to the price level (P) and transactions (T), providing the classical statement of the quantity theory of money.

The Fisher equation

The Fisher equation states that the nominal interest rate equals the real interest rate plus expected inflation. Fisher's The Theory of Interest (1930) developed a theory of intertemporal choice — the trade-off between present and future consumption — that remains foundational; the "Fisher separation theorem" and the two-period Fisher diagram are named for him.

Index numbers

Fisher's The Making of Index Numbers (1922) advanced the theory of price indices. The "Fisher ideal index", the geometric mean of the Laspeyres and Paasche indices, is still used in national statistics.

Debt-deflation

In his 1933 paper "The Debt-Deflation Theory of Great Depressions", Fisher argued that over-indebtedness followed by deflation raises the real burden of debt, forcing distress selling and further deflation in a self-reinforcing spiral — an account of the Great Depression that anticipated later work on financial instability.
