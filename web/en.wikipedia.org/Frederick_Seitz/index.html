Frederick Seitz

Frederick Seitz (July 4, 1911 – March 2, 2008) was an American physicist, a pioneer of solid state physics, and president of Rockefeller University. Seitz was the recipient of the National Medal of Science, NASA's Distinguished Public Service Award, and other honours, and a former president of the United States National Academy of Sciences (1962–1969).

Born in San Francisco, Seitz earned his bachelor's degree from Stanford University in three years and completed his doctorate at Princeton University in 1934 under Eugene Wigner, with whom he devised the Wigner–Seitz cell, a fundamental construction in crystallography. His 1940 book The Modern Theory of Solids helped consolidate the new field of solid-state physics.

Career

Seitz worked on the Manhattan Project during the Second World War and afterwards built the physics department at the University of Illinois at Urbana–Champaign into a leading centre for solid-state research, where he also founded the Materials Research Laboratory. From 1968 to 1978 he served as president of Rockefeller University.

Later controversies

In his later years Seitz became a prominent figure in debates over science policy and public health. He directed a tobacco-industry research programme at R.J. Reynolds, worked with the George C. Marshall Institute arguing against the mainstream assessment of acid rain and ozone depletion, and was a leading scientific critic of the consensus on global warming; he circulated the Oregon Petition urging rejection of the Kyoto Protocol. These activities drew criticism from historians of science, notably in Naomi Oreskes and Erik Conway's Merchants of Doubt, while supporters defended his right to dissent.
