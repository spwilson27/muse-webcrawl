Russkaya Pravda (Old East Slavic: Правда роусьскаꙗ, "Rus' Justice" or "Rus' Truth") was the legal code of Kievan Rus' and the subsequent Rus' principalities during the feudal period. It was written at the beginning of the 11th century and remade through many centuries. The basis of Russkaya Pravda, the Pravda of Yaroslav (compiled about 1016), was written at the time of Yaroslav the Wise; the expanded versions added by his sons (the Pravda of the Yaroslavichi, c. 1072) and by Vladimir Monomakh survive in the "Expanded Pravda" and "Abridged Pravda" redactions.

Contents

The code largely replaced blood vengeance with monetary compensation: the earliest stratum still permits avengers to kill a murderer, but later texts substitute fixed fines (vira for murder, prodazha for lesser offences) scaled by the victim's social rank — from 80 grivnas for a prince's man to 5 for a peasant (smerd). It regulates property, theft (especially of horses and livestock), debt, slavery (kholopy), inheritance, and legal procedure, reflecting the social hierarchy and the growing power of the prince's courts. Notably, it imposed collective responsibility on the local community, the verv, in whose territory an unclaimed body was found — the "wild vira".

Editions and influence

No original manuscript survives; the oldest copies, such as the one in the Novgorod First Chronicle and the Synodal copy of 1282, date from the 13th century. The text was preserved within chronicles and the kormchie knigi (canon-law collections). Russkaya Pravda remained a foundational source of law in the Rus' lands for centuries and influenced later codes, including the Lithuanian Statutes. The first printed edition was issued by Vasily Tatishchev in the 18th century.
