History of South Australia

The history of South Australia covers tens of thousands of years of Aboriginal occupation, European exploration and mapping of the coast, the founding in 1836 of a planned free colony distinct from Australia's convict settlements, and the state's development through federation to the present day.

Aboriginal history

Aboriginal peoples have lived in South Australia for at least 40,000–65,000 years. Groups such as the Kaurna of the Adelaide plains, the Ngarrindjeri of the lower Murray, the Adnyamathanha of the Flinders Ranges and the Pitjantjatjara of the far north-west developed rich cultures adapted to the continent's most arid state. Sites such as Koonalda Cave show very early occupation.

European exploration

Dutch navigators charted part of the coast in 1627. In 1802 Matthew Flinders circumnavigated Australia and charted the South Australian coast, meeting Nicolas Baudin's French expedition at Encounter Bay. Inland exploration followed: Charles Sturt navigated the Murray River in 1829–30, and Edward John Eyre crossed to Albany in 1840–41 and explored the Flinders Ranges region.

Foundation of the colony

The South Australia Act 1834 authorised a colony based on Edward Gibbon Wakefield's theory of "systematic colonisation": land would be sold at a fixed price to fund the emigration of free labourers, and no convicts would be transported. The first settlers arrived in 1836; Governor John Hindmarsh proclaimed the province at Glenelg on 28 December 1836. Colonel William Light surveyed and laid out Adelaide with its ring of park lands. Early crisis gave way to prosperity with wheat farming, copper discoveries at Kapunda (1842) and Burra (1845), and German Lutheran immigration to the Barossa Valley.

Self-government and federation

South Australia gained responsible government in 1857, with an unusually democratic franchise that briefly, through a drafting quirk, extended to women. In 1894 South Australia became the first Australian colony and one of the first places in the world to grant women both the vote and the right to stand for parliament. The state federated with the other Australian colonies in 1901.

Twentieth century and beyond

The state expanded manufacturing under long-serving premier Thomas Playford (1938–1965), while the "Playmander" electoral system distorted rural–urban representation. The Dunstan decade of the 1970s brought social reform, Aboriginal land rights (culminating in the Pitjantjatjara Land Rights Act 1981) and cultural flourishing. The collapse of the State Bank in 1991 caused a major fiscal crisis. Woomera became a centre for rocket testing, and the state has invested in wine, mining (including the Olympic Dam copper-uranium mine), defence and renewable energy.
