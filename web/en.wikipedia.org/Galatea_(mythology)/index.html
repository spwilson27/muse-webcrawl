Galatea (mythology)

Galatea (Greek: Γαλάτεια, "she who is milk-white") is a name applied in Greek mythology to several figures. The best known is the Nereid Galatea, a sea nymph beloved by the Cyclops Polyphemus; the name is also given in later tradition to the statue carved by Pygmalion that Aphrodite brought to life.

The Nereid

Galatea was one of the fifty Nereids, daughters of the sea god Nereus and the Oceanid Doris. In the Sicilian myth told by Ovid in the Metamorphoses and earlier by the poet Philoxenus, Galatea loved the handsome youth Acis, son of Faunus. The Cyclops Polyphemus, who loved Galatea in vain and sang of his passion, discovered the lovers and crushed Acis beneath a rock. Galatea transformed the blood of Acis into a river bearing his name in Sicily. The story became a favourite subject of pastoral poetry, painting, and music, including Handel's serenata Acis and Galatea.

Pygmalion's statue

In Ovid's telling, the sculptor Pygmalion carves an ivory statue of a woman so beautiful that he falls in love with it; at his prayer, Venus animates the statue, and she bears him a daughter, Paphos. Ovid does not name the statue, but post-classical tradition — popularized by Jean-Jacques Rousseau's Pygmalion and George Bernard Shaw's play Pygmalion — calls her Galatea. The name reinforces the theme of art coming to life.

Other figures and legacy

A Galatea also appears as a daughter of Eurytus and wife of Lamprus in a Cretan tale in Antoninus Liberalis, who prayed for her daughter Leucippus to be changed into a son. The name survives in astronomy (the asteroid 74 Galatea and Neptune's moon Galatea) and in Raphael's celebrated fresco The Triumph of Galatea in the Villa Farnesina, Rome.
