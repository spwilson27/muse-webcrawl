Democracy

Core principleRule by the people
Key featuresFree and fair elections, universal suffrage, rule of law, civil liberties
Origin of termGreek dēmokratía — "rule by the people"

Democracy (from Ancient Greek δημοκρατία, dēmokratía, from dēmos "people" and kratos "rule") is a system of government in which state power is vested in the people and exercised directly by them or by their freely elected representatives under a free electoral system. In a direct democracy, citizens vote on policy initiatives themselves; in a representative democracy, they elect officials to deliberate and legislate on their behalf.

History

The term appeared in the 5th century BC in Greek city-states, notably Athens, where adult male citizens participated directly in the assembly. Democratic ideas re-emerged through medieval parliaments, the Enlightenment, and the American and French revolutions. The 19th and 20th centuries saw the gradual extension of suffrage to all adults, including women, and the spread of democratic constitutions after both world wars and the end of the Cold War.

Characteristics

Scholars commonly identify several institutional requirements: competitive elections with broad suffrage, freedom of expression and association, access to alternative sources of information, and the rule of law protecting civil liberties. Liberal democracy combines majority rule with constitutional limits and protections for minorities.

Variants

Democracies may be parliamentary or presidential, unitary or federal, and may use proportional representation or majoritarian electoral systems. Participatory and deliberative models supplement elections with citizen assemblies, referendums and public consultation.

Criticisms and challenges

Critics from Plato onward have warned of majority tyranny, short-termism and manipulation by demagogues. Contemporary concerns include democratic backsliding, disinformation, money in politics and declining voter turnout.
