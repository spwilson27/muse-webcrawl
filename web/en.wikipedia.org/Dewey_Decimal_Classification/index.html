Dewey Decimal Classification

Dewey Decimal Classification (DDC)
Creator: Melvil Dewey
Published: 1876
Owner: OCLC
Used in: libraries in more than 130 countries

The Dewey Decimal Classification (DDC), colloquially the Dewey Decimal System, is a proprietary library classification system devised by Melvil Dewey and first published in the United States in 1876. It organises knowledge into ten main classes, each divided into ten divisions and each division into ten sections, using Arabic numerals with decimal extensions for increasing specificity.

Structure

The ten main classes are: 000 Computer science, information and general works; 100 Philosophy and psychology; 200 Religion; 300 Social sciences; 400 Language; 500 Science; 600 Technology; 700 Arts and recreation; 800 Literature; and 900 History and geography. Auxiliary tables allow numbers to be built for place, time, language and form, so that, for example, a history of a specific country receives a long, precise call number. The notation is purely numeric, making the system language-independent.

Use and revisions

The DDC is the world's most widely used library classification, employed by libraries in over 130 countries and translated into more than 30 languages; most public and school libraries in the English-speaking world shelve by it, while large research libraries more often use the Library of Congress Classification. The schedules are continuously revised in an online database (WebDewey) published by OCLC, which acquired the rights in 1988 when it bought Forest Press.

Criticism

The system has been criticised for biases reflecting its 19th-century American origin — for example, the disproportionate space given to Christianity within the 200 class and to Western literature — and for slow responsiveness to new topics and marginalised subjects. Successive editions have attempted to rebalance these areas.
