History of Singapore

The history of Singapore traces the island at the tip of the Malay Peninsula from its early settlement as Temasek, through its founding as a British trading post in 1819, to its development into an independent global city-state after 1965.

The Singapore River was the heart of the early trading port.

Early history

The island appears in records as Temasek ("Sea Town") and later Singapura ("Lion City"), a trading settlement linked to Srivijaya and then the Malacca Sultanate. In January 1819 Stamford Raffles of the British East India Company signed a treaty with Sultan Hussein and the Temenggong to establish a trading post, chosen for its position on the route between India and China.

Crown colony

Singapore, with Penang and Malacca, formed the Straits Settlements in 1826. As a free port it drew immigrants from China, India and the Malay world, growing rich on entrepôt trade and, later, rubber and tin. The opening of the Suez Canal in 1869 cemented its position.

War and aftermath

Despite its reputation as a fortress, Singapore fell to Japanese forces in February 1942 in one of Britain's worst military defeats; the occupation that followed was brutal, including the Sook Ching massacres. After the war, the Straits Settlements were dissolved and Singapore became a separate crown colony, with growing agitation for self-rule.

Towards independence

Under Lee Kuan Yew and the People's Action Party, Singapore won internal self-government in 1959 and merged with Malaysia in 1963. Political and communal tensions led to Singapore's separation on 9 August 1965. The new republic industrialised rapidly through export-oriented policies, public housing and education, becoming one of Asia's wealthiest states.
