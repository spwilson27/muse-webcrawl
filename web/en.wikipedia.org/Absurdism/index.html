Absurdism

Absurdism is the philosophical theory that the universe is irrational and meaningless and that the human search for meaning leads inevitably to conflict — the "absurd" — between our desire for order, significance and purpose and the universe's silence. The concept is most closely associated with the French philosopher and writer Albert Camus, and has roots in the work of the nineteenth-century Danish philosopher Søren Kierkegaard.

Camus and The Myth of Sisyphus

In his 1942 essay The Myth of Sisyphus, Camus argued that the absurd arises from the confrontation between human longing for meaning and a world that offers none. He rejected two common escapes: physical suicide, which destroys the confrontation, and "philosophical suicide" — a leap of faith into religion or ideology that denies the absurd. Instead he advocated revolt: lucidly accepting the absurd and living fully in defiance of it. Sisyphus, condemned forever to roll his rock uphill, becomes the absurd hero: "One must imagine Sisyphus happy." Camus developed these ideas in the novel The Stranger (1942) and the essay The Rebel (1951).

Relation to existentialism and the theatre

Absurdism is often grouped with existentialism and nihilism but differs from both: unlike existentialism, it denies that one can create meaning that escapes the absurd; unlike nihilism, it insists on responding to meaninglessness with rebellion rather than despair. The term also names the post-war Theatre of the Absurd — plays by Samuel Beckett, Eugène Ionesco, Jean Genet and Harold Pinter in which meaninglessness is dramatised through illogical plots, repetition and futile dialogue, as theorised by Martin Esslin in 1961.
