Arc flash

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hazards
TemperatureUp to ~19,400 °C (35,000 °F) at the arc terminals
EffectsBurns, blast pressure, shrapnel, intense light and sound
Key standardsNFPA 70E; IEEE 1584; OSHA 1910 Subpart S

An arc flash is the light and heat produced as part of an arc fault, a type of electrical explosion or discharge that results from a connection through air to ground or another voltage phase in an electrical system. Arc flash is distinctly different from the arc blast, which is the pressure wave created by the rapid expansion of gases and vaporised material during the same event. Arc-flash energy can reach tens of calories per square centimetre at working distance, enough to cause fatal burns, ignite clothing, and propel molten metal.

Causes

Arc faults are typically initiated by accidental contact (a dropped tool or test probe), deteriorated or damaged insulation, dust or corrosion creating a conductive path, condensation, improperly installed or maintained equipment, or switching operations on faulty gear. Once air is ionised, the resulting plasma sustains a low-impedance path through which the available fault current flows until a protective device clears the fault — the speed of that clearing largely determines the incident energy released.

Hazards and injuries

Arc temperatures can exceed the surface temperature of the sun, vaporising copper conductors, which expand to tens of thousands of times their solid volume. Consequences for nearby workers include severe thermal burns (often indirect, through ignited clothing), blast injuries and hearing damage from sound levels above 160 dB, eye damage from ultraviolet light, and inhalation of toxic vaporised materials. In the United States, arc-flash incidents are estimated to send several workers to burn centres each day.

Protection and regulation

Risk control follows a hierarchy: de-energising equipment before work, engineering controls such as arc-resistant switchgear, current-limiting fuses, remote racking and faster protective relays, and finally personal protective equipment (PPE). NFPA 70E, the standard for electrical safety in the workplace, requires an arc-flash risk assessment, defines the arc-flash boundary, and prescribes PPE categories selected either by incident-energy analysis (commonly performed per IEEE 1584 calculation methods) or by table. Equipment labels must state the nominal voltage, arc-flash boundary, and either the available incident energy or the required PPE category.
