Prize agent

A prize agent was, historically, a person or firm appointed to handle the business of captured enemy ships and cargo — "prizes" — taken in wartime, particularly in Britain during the age of sail. Naval officers and crews who captured a prize were entitled to prize money once an Admiralty court condemned the prize as lawfully taken, and the prize agent managed the legal, financial and commercial steps in between.

Role

When a warship or privateer brought a captured vessel into port, the prize agent arranged for the case to be brought before the High Court of Admiralty (or a vice-admiralty court overseas), which determined whether the capture was lawful. If the prize was condemned, the agent arranged the sale of the ship and cargo, paid the court's fees, customs and other charges, and distributed the proceeds as prize money according to the shares laid down by proclamation or statute — apportioned among the flag officer, captain, officers and crew of the capturing ship.

Fees and abuses

Agents charged a commission, conventionally around five percent, and often advanced money to officers against expected prize proceeds. The system was prone to delay and abuse: some agents went bankrupt holding clients' money, embezzled funds, or charged extravagant fees, and sailors sometimes waited years for their shares. Prize agents in London and the great naval ports — firms near the Navy Office and in the City — became wealthy businesses in their own right, and some agents, such as those who served famous admirals, handled very large sums during the French Revolutionary and Napoleonic Wars.

Decline

The role declined as prize law was reformed in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. In Britain, the Naval Prize Act 1918 and later arrangements centralised prize administration, and the general abolition of prize money after the Second World War ended the traditional business of the prize agent.
