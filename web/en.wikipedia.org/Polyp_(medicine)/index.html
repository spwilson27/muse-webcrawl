Polyp (medicine)

A polyp is an abnormal growth of tissue projecting from a mucous membrane. If it is attached to the surface by a narrow elongated stalk, it is said to be pedunculated; if attached without a stalk, in the form of a broad base, it is said to be sessile. Polyps are commonly found in the colon, stomach, nose, sinuses, urinary bladder, and uterus, though they may occur elsewhere in the body where mucous membranes exist.

Colorectal polyps

Colorectal polyps are among the most clinically significant because some types can develop into colorectal cancer over time. They are broadly classified as neoplastic (such as tubular adenomas, tubulovillous adenomas, and villous adenomas) and non-neoplastic (such as hyperplastic and inflammatory polyps). Adenomatous polyps are considered premalignant; the risk of cancer increases with polyp size, villous architecture, and high-grade dysplasia. Screening by colonoscopy allows polyps to be detected and removed before they become malignant.

Other sites

Nasal polyps are soft, painless, noncancerous growths on the lining of the nasal passages or sinuses, often associated with chronic inflammation, asthma, and aspirin sensitivity. Endometrial polyps occur in the uterus and may cause abnormal bleeding. Gallbladder polyps are usually benign cholesterol deposits found incidentally on ultrasound. Vocal cord polyps can cause hoarseness and are associated with vocal overuse.

Diagnosis and treatment

Polyps are typically discovered during endoscopic examination, imaging studies, or physical examination. Because visual appearance alone cannot reliably distinguish benign from precancerous polyps, removed tissue is usually examined histologically. Treatment generally consists of removal (polypectomy), performed endoscopically with a snare or forceps, or surgically for large or inaccessible lesions. Patients with certain polyp types or hereditary syndromes, such as familial adenomatous polyposis, require ongoing surveillance.
