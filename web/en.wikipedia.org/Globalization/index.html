Globalization

Globalization is the process of increasing interdependence and integration among the economies, markets, societies, and cultures of different countries worldwide. It is driven by international trade and investment, aided by information technology, containerized shipping, and the liberalization of capital markets. The term gained widespread use in the 1990s, though scholars trace earlier waves of global integration to the 19th century and to the early modern age of exploration.

Dimensions

Economic globalization involves the international movement of goods, services, capital, and labour, organized through global supply chains and multinational corporations. Cultural globalization transmits ideas, languages, media, and consumer tastes across borders. Political globalization appears in international institutions such as the United Nations, the World Trade Organization, and the International Monetary Fund. Indices such as the KOF Globalisation Index attempt to measure these dimensions.

Debate

Supporters argue that globalization has raised productivity, lowered consumer prices, and lifted hundreds of millions out of poverty, particularly in East Asia. Critics point to job losses in developed-country manufacturing, rising inequality, financial contagion — as in the 2008 financial crisis — cultural homogenization, and environmental costs. The alter-globalization movement, visible from the 1999 Seattle WTO protests onward, seeks a more regulated and equitable form of integration. Since the 2010s, trade tensions, the COVID-19 pandemic, and geopolitical rivalry have prompted discussion of "slowbalisation" and partial deglobalization.
