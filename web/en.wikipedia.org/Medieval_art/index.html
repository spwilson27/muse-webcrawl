The medieval art of the Western world covers more than a thousand years of art in Europe, and at times in the Middle East and North Africa, from the decline of Rome to the beginning of the Renaissance around 1500. It includes major art movements and periods, national and regional art, genres, revivals, the artists' crafts and the artists themselves.

Overview

Medieval art was overwhelmingly religious in patronage and purpose, produced for churches, monasteries and devout lay patrons. Artists were usually anonymous craftsmen organised in workshops and guilds. Precious materials — gold, lapis lazuli, ivory and silk — signalled the sacred value of objects such as reliquaries, altarpieces and illuminated manuscripts.

Major periods

Early Christian and Byzantine art adapted Roman forms to Christian subjects, developing the icon, the mosaic and the domed church. Migration Period and Insular art produced intricate interlace metalwork and manuscripts such as the Book of Kells. The Carolingian and Ottonian revivals consciously imitated antique models. Romanesque art (c. 1000–1150) brought monumental stone sculpture back to western Europe, while Gothic art (mid-12th century onwards) filled cathedrals with stained glass and increasingly naturalistic sculpture and painting, culminating in the International Gothic style.

Media

Surviving works include cathedral architecture and sculpture, panel paintings and altarpieces, frescoes, mosaics, enamels, tapestries such as the Bayeux Tapestry, and above all illuminated manuscripts — psalters, books of hours and bestiaries. Much medieval art, especially secular and wooden work, has been lost.
