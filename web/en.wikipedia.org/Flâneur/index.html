Flâneur

A flâneur (French: [flanœʁ]) is a figure of French literary and cultural history: an idler or stroller who wanders the city, observing its life with detached curiosity. The word derives from the verb flâner, "to stroll" or "to saunter", with roots possibly in Old Norse. The flâneur became an emblem of modern urban experience in 19th-century Paris.

The Baudelairean flâneur

The poet Charles Baudelaire gave the figure its classic form in essays such as "The Painter of Modern Life" (1863), where the flâneur is an artist-observer who moves through the crowd incognito, at home in the flux of the modern metropolis yet apart from it. For Baudelaire, flânerie was a way of reading the city: its fashions, crowds, arcades and spectacles.

Later interpretations

The philosopher Walter Benjamin made the flâneur central to his unfinished Arcades Project, treating the stroller as a key to understanding Parisian capitalism, commodity culture and modernity. Later writers and scholars have debated whether flânerie was open to women — the invisible "flâneuse" — and have adapted the figure to contemporary cities, photography, and even digital wandering on the internet.
