Algonquian peoples

The Algonquian peoples are a group of Indigenous peoples of North America united by speaking languages of the Algonquian family. Historically they occupied a vast area from the Atlantic coast (Newfoundland and the Maritime provinces, New England, and the mid-Atlantic) through the Great Lakes and the St. Lawrence valley to the northern Great Plains and the subarctic forests of Canada.

Peoples and languages

Well-known Algonquian-speaking nations include the Ojibwe (Anishinaabe), Cree, Mi'kmaq, Abenaki, Lenape (Delaware), Shawnee, Cheyenne, Arapaho, Blackfoot, Menominee, Powhatan, and Montagnais (Innu). Their languages, though related, are not all mutually intelligible. Algonquian languages have been influential in English vocabulary, contributing words such as moose, tomahawk, toboggan, skunk, and moccasin, and many North American place names.

History

Algonquian communities varied from settled farming villages in the south — growing maize, beans, and squash — to mobile hunting bands in the subarctic. From the 17th century, the fur trade, European colonisation, epidemic disease, and conflicts such as the Beaver Wars with the Iroquois Confederacy profoundly reshaped their societies. Many Algonquian nations were displaced westward or onto reservations and reserves, but they remain numerous and culturally vibrant peoples today.
