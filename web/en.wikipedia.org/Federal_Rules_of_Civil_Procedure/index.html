Federal Rules of Civil Procedure


A U.S. district courthouse, where the FRCP govern civil litigation

The Federal Rules of Civil Procedure (FRCP) govern civil procedure in the United States district courts. They were promulgated by the Supreme Court of the United States under the authority of the Rules Enabling Act and took effect on September 16, 1938. The rules unified the previously separate procedures for actions at law and suits in equity into a single system of "one form of action" — the civil action — and are famous for their emphasis on notice pleading, broad pretrial discovery, and deciding cases on their merits.

Structure

The FRCP contain 86 rules organized into eleven titles covering, among other subjects: commencement of actions and pleadings (Rules 3–16), parties and joinder (Rules 17–25), disclosures and discovery (Rules 26–37), trials (Rules 38–53), judgments (Rules 54–63), provisional and final remedies (Rules 64–71), and special proceedings. Well-known provisions include Rule 8 (general rules of pleading), Rule 11 (sanctions for improper filings), Rule 12(b)(6) (failure to state a claim), Rule 26 (scope of discovery and required disclosures), and Rule 56 (summary judgment).

History

Before 1938, federal courts generally conformed their procedure to that of the state in which they sat in actions at law, while following a separate set of federal equity rules. Reformers led by Attorney General Homer Cummings and Yale professor Charles Edward Clark, the rules' principal drafter, sought uniformity and simplicity. The Advisory Committee on Civil Rules, appointed in 1935, produced the draft rules that the Supreme Court adopted in 1937. The rules have been amended many times since, notably in 1966 (class actions under Rule 23), 1993 (mandatory initial disclosures), and 2015 (proportionality in discovery).

Influence

The FRCP profoundly influenced American civil litigation and led most states to adopt similar rules for their own courts. Federal Rule 1 states that the rules "should be construed, administered, and employed by the court and the parties to secure the just, speedy, and inexpensive determination of every action and proceeding."
