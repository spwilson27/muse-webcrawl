Serpent (instrument)

Serpent
ClassificationAerophone; brass (lip-vibrated, wooden body)
Inventedc. 1590, France
Related instrumentsCornett, ophicleide, tuba

A wooden serpent, curved in its characteristic S shape.

The serpent is a bass wind instrument, a distant ancestor of the tuba, with a mouthpiece like a brass instrument but with side holes for the fingers, like a woodwind. It is usually made of wood (sometimes with a leather covering) bent into a serpentine S-shape about 2.4 metres (8 feet) long overall, ending in a small flare or bell. Despite its wooden construction, it is classed with the brass family in the Hornbostel–Sachs system because its sound is produced by the player's lips vibrating in a cup mouthpiece.

History

The serpent is credited to Edmé Guillaume, a clergyman of Auxerre, France, around 1590, who developed it from the bass cornett to support plainchant in church choirs by reinforcing the bass voices. For roughly two and a half centuries it was used in church music, then in military bands, and occasionally in the orchestra: Handel, Haydn, Mozart, Beethoven, Mendelssohn, Berlioz, Wagner and Verdi all wrote for it. In England it was also played in village church bands ("quire bands") before the advent of the harmonium and organ.

Decline and revival

The serpent's intonation was always difficult and its tone uneven, and in the 19th century it was progressively displaced by valved brass basses — first the ophicleide, then the tuba. Interest revived in the 20th century with the historically informed performance movement; makers resumed building serpents, and players such as Christopher Monk championed its reconstruction and repertoire. Bernard Shaw famously mocked its sound, but composers and film composers have occasionally used it since, and it appears in early-music ensembles today.
