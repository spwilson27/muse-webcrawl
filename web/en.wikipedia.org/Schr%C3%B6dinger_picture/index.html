Schrödinger picture

In quantum mechanics, the Schrödinger picture (also called the Schrödinger representation) is a formulation in which the state vectors evolve in time, while the operators (observables and others) are mostly constant with respect to time. It is named after Erwin Schrödinger, who introduced the wave-mechanical formalism in 1926.

Definition

In the Schrödinger picture, the state of a system at time t is given by a ket |ψ(t)⟩ whose evolution is governed by the Schrödinger equation, iħ d/dt |ψ(t)⟩ = Ĥ |ψ(t)⟩, where Ĥ is the Hamiltonian. Formally the state evolves under the unitary time-evolution operator U(t, t₀) = exp(−iĤ(t − t₀)/ħ). Observables are represented by operators that are time-independent unless they depend explicitly on time.

Comparison with other pictures

The Schrödinger picture contrasts with the Heisenberg picture, in which the states are constant and the operators evolve, and with the interaction (Dirac) picture, in which both states and operators evolve, splitting the Hamiltonian into free and interacting parts. All three pictures are mathematically equivalent and yield identical predictions for observable quantities; the choice among them is a matter of convenience for a given calculation.
