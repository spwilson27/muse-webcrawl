Lysozyme

Structure of hen egg-white lysozyme

EC number: 3.2.1.17

Also known as: Muramidase

Discovered: 1922, by Alexander Fleming

Lysozyme is an antimicrobial enzyme produced by animals that forms part of the innate immune system. It catalyses the hydrolysis of 1,4-beta-linkages between N-acetylmuramic acid and N-acetyl-D-glucosamine in peptidoglycan, the main component of the cell walls of Gram-positive bacteria, thereby damaging bacterial membranes and causing cell lysis. Also known as muramidase, lysozyme is abundant in secretions including tears, saliva, milk, and mucus, and in egg white.

Discovery and structure

Lysozyme was discovered in 1922 by Alexander Fleming, who noticed that his own nasal mucus dissolved a culture of bacteria — six years before his discovery of penicillin. Hen egg-white lysozyme, a compact protein of 129 amino acids, became in 1965 the first enzyme whose three-dimensional structure was solved by X-ray crystallography (by David Phillips's team), and it provided the first detailed structural model of how an enzyme binds its substrate in an active-site cleft, with glutamate-35 and aspartate-52 as the catalytic residues.

Uses

Because of its antibacterial action, lysozyme is used as a natural food preservative (E number E1105, for example in cheese to prevent late blowing by Clostridium tyrobutyricum), in some pharmaceuticals, and as a standard laboratory reagent for lysing bacterial cells during plasmid and protein purification.
