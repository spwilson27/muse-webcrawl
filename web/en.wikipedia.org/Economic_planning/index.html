Economic planning

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Economic planning is a resource allocation mechanism based on a computational procedure for solving a constrained maximization problem with an iterative process for obtaining its solution. Planning is a mechanism for the allocation of resources between and within organizations contrasted with the market mechanism. As an allocation mechanism for socialism, economic planning replaces factor markets with a procedure for direct allocations of resources within an interconnected group of socially owned organizations which together constitute the productive apparatus of the economy.

Gosplan, the Soviet planning committee

There are various forms of economic planning that vary based on their specific procedures and approach. The level of centralization or decentralization in decision-making depends on the specific type of planning mechanism employed. In addition, one can distinguish between centralized planning and decentralized planning. An economy primarily based on planning is referred to as a planned economy. In a centrally planned economy, the allocation of resources is determined by a comprehensive plan of production which specifies output requirements.

Central planning in the Soviet Union

The Soviet Union was the first national economy to attempt comprehensive central planning, administered by the State Planning Committee (Gosplan) through a series of Five-Year Plans beginning in 1928. Enterprises received output targets and input allocations; planners used "material balances" to reconcile the planned supplies and uses of thousands of products. The system achieved rapid industrialisation and mobilisation for war, but was criticised for chronic shortages, poor quality, weak innovation, and the information problems identified in the economic calculation debate.

Planning in market economies

Elements of planning also exist within market economies. Individual firms engage in extensive internal planning; governments use indicative planning (as in postwar France, Japan, and South Korea) to coordinate investment expectations and steer credit toward priority sectors. Development planning, public investment programming, and industrial policy are partial forms of economic planning that coexist with markets.

Criticism and debate

Critics, including Ludwig von Mises and Friedrich Hayek, argued that without market prices for the means of production, planners cannot rationally calculate costs (see economic calculation problem). Defenders have responded with proposals ranging from Lange's market socialism to modern computational planning. The debate continues in the context of large firms (such as Amazon and Walmart) that internally plan logistics on a huge scale using information technology.
