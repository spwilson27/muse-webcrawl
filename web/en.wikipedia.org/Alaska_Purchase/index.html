Alaska Purchase

The Alaska Purchase (Russian: Продажа Аляски) was the United States' acquisition of Alaska from the Russian Empire. Alaska was formally transferred on October 18, 1867, through a treaty ratified by the United States Senate. Russia had established a modest presence in the territory a few decades before, but few Russians ever settled there.

Russia was in a difficult financial position and feared losing the territory without compensation in a future war, particularly to the United Kingdom. Unwilling to engage in another conflict so soon after the Crimean War, and judging the colony unprofitable and hard to defend, Russia decided to sell its North American possessions. The United States Secretary of State, William H. Seward, negotiated the purchase for $7.2 million — about two cents per acre.

Background

Russian America was administered by the Russian-American Company, whose fur trade, centred on sea otter pelts, had declined sharply by the 1860s. After the Crimean War (1853–1856), Tsar Alexander II concluded that the colony would be indefensible against Britain, Russia's likely adversary in any future war, and that it was better sold to the friendly United States.

Negotiations and treaty

Negotiations with the Russian minister Edouard de Stoeckl resumed in March 1867. The treaty was signed in the early hours of March 30, 1867, and the Senate ratified it on April 9. President Andrew Johnson signed the treaty on May 28, and the formal transfer ceremony took place at Sitka on October 18, 1867.

Reception

American opinion was mixed; critics mocked the purchase as "Seward's Folly" or "Seward's Icebox". Opinion turned favourable after gold was discovered in the Klondike in 1896 and, later, after the territory proved strategically vital during the Second World War and the Cold War. Alaska became the 49th state on January 3, 1959.
