Soil classification

Soil classification deals with the systematic categorization of soils based on distinguishing characteristics as well as criteria that dictate choices in use. It is a morphometric classification used by soil scientists to organize soils into groups that share observable and measurable properties, enabling soil maps, land-use planning, and communication about soil behavior.

Overview

Unlike biological taxonomy, soil classification reflects the fact that soil formation is continuous and influenced by climate, organisms, relief, parent material, and time — the five soil-forming factors identified by Hans Jenny. Because soils vary gradually across landscapes, classification systems impose boundaries on a continuum.

Classification is generally based on the morphology of the soil profile, including the sequence and properties of horizons, as well as laboratory measurements such as texture, pH, organic matter content, and clay mineralogy.

Major systems

USDA soil taxonomy

The United States Department of Agriculture's soil taxonomy is a hierarchical system with six categories: order, suborder, great group, subgroup, family, and series. It recognizes twelve soil orders, including Alfisols, Andisols, Aridisols, Entisols, Gelisols, Histosols, Inceptisols, Mollisols, Oxisols, Spodosols, Ultisols, and Vertisols. Names are constructed from formative elements derived mainly from Latin and Greek roots.

World Reference Base

The World Reference Base for Soil Resources (WRB), maintained under the auspices of the FAO and the International Union of Soil Sciences, is the international standard. It defines 32 Reference Soil Groups, such as Chernozems, Luvisols, Podzols, and Ferralsols, qualified by principal and supplementary qualifiers.

Other systems

Many countries maintain national systems, including the French Référentiel pédologique, the Australian Soil Classification, and the Canadian system. Engineering classifications, such as the Unified Soil Classification System, group soils by grain size and plasticity for construction purposes.

Diagnostic horizons and properties

Modern systems rely on diagnostic horizons — surface horizons such as mollic and ochric epipedons, and subsurface horizons such as argillic, spodic, and oxic horizons — defined by quantitative criteria. Diagnostic properties include soil moisture and temperature regimes, which are especially important in soil taxonomy.

Uses

Soil classification underpins soil survey and mapping, agricultural suitability assessment, engineering design, environmental modeling, and paleopedology. Properties relevant to processes such as soil liquefaction and erosion are interpreted from classified soil data.
