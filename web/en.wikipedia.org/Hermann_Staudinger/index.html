Hermann Staudinger

Hermann Staudinger (23 March 1881 – 8 September 1965) was a German organic chemist who demonstrated the existence of macromolecules, which he characterised as polymers. For this work he received the 1953 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

He is also known for his discovery of ketenes and of the Staudinger reaction. Staudinger, together with Leopold Ružička, also elucidated the molecular structures of natural compounds such as pyrethrin and pyrethrolone in the 1920s.

Early work

Staudinger was born in Worms and studied at the University of Halle, where he obtained his doctorate in 1903 under Daniel Vorländer. In 1905 he discovered the ketenes, a family of highly reactive molecules with the general formula R2C=C=O. He held positions at Strasbourg and Karlsruhe, and in 1912 he took the chair at ETH Zurich vacated by Richard Willstätter. In 1919 he and his student reported the Staudinger reaction, in which organic azides react with phosphines to give amines via an iminophosphorane intermediate — a reaction later exploited in chemical biology as the basis of the Staudinger ligation.

The macromolecular hypothesis

In 1920 Staudinger proposed that rubber, starch, cellulose, and proteins were not aggregates of small molecules held together by secondary forces (the then-dominant "association theory") but long chains of short subunits joined by ordinary covalent bonds — molecules of very high molecular weight, which he termed Makromoleküle (macromolecules). His hypothesis met strong resistance from leading chemists of the day, including some Nobel laureates, but evidence from viscosity measurements (culminating in the Staudinger viscosity law), osmotic pressure, and X-ray crystallography accumulated through the 1920s and 1930s, and his view ultimately prevailed, founding polymer science.

In 1926 he moved to the University of Freiburg, where he remained for the rest of his career, founding the first institute for macromolecular chemistry. He founded the journal Die Makromolekulare Chemie in 1947.

Honours

Staudinger received numerous honours culminating in the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1953 "for his discoveries in the field of macromolecular chemistry." His work underlies the modern plastics and polymer industries, as well as the understanding of biological macromolecules such as DNA and proteins.

Personal life

Staudinger was a pacifist; during the First World War he publicly criticised the use of chemical weapons, including by his colleague Fritz Haber, at considerable professional cost. His second wife, Magda Staudinger, was a Latvian biologist and collaborator who worked to preserve and promote his scientific legacy.
