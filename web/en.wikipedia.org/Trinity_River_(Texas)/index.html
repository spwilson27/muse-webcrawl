The Trinity River is a 710-mile (1,140 km) river in Texas, the longest river whose entire course lies within the state. It rises in northern Texas, flows through Dallas and Fort Worth, and empties into Trinity Bay, an inlet of Galveston Bay on the Gulf of Mexico near Houston. The river was named by Spanish explorer Alonso de León in 1690, who called it La Santísima Trinidad ("the Most Holy Trinity").

Course

The Trinity is formed by four forks. The West Fork rises in Archer County and flows south-east through Fort Worth. The Clear Fork joins it in Fort Worth, and the Elm Fork, rising north of Gainesville, joins the West Fork near Dallas to form the main stem. The East Fork flows south from near McKinney and joins the main river further downstream. Below Dallas the river winds south-east across the coastal plain, passing near Huntsville and Livingston, and reaches its mouth at Trinity Bay near Anahuac.

History

The Trinity valley was home to Caddo and other Native American peoples long before European contact. In the nineteenth century the river was an important commercial artery: steamboats navigated it as far as Dallas, and it carried cotton and other goods between the interior of Texas and the Gulf. Ambitious schemes to make the river navigable by barge from the Gulf to Fort Worth — involving a series of locks and dams — were pursued for much of the twentieth century but were never completed, and the project was largely abandoned by the 1980s.

Management and flooding

The river basin is managed in part by the Trinity River Authority, created in 1955. A chain of reservoirs — including Lake Bridgeport, Eagle Mountain Lake, Lake Worth, Benbrook Lake, Grapevine Lake, Lewisville Lake, Lavon Lake, Lake Livingston and others — regulates flow and supplies water to the Dallas–Fort Worth metroplex and downstream users. The river is notorious for flooding; major floods in 1908, 1941, 1942 and 1990 inundated parts of Dallas and Fort Worth, prompting extensive levee construction along the urban reaches.
