Plant collector

A plant collector is a person who travels, often to remote regions, to gather living plants, seeds, bulbs, and herbarium specimens for botanical gardens, nurseries, scientific institutions, or private collections. Plant collecting reached its height in the 18th and 19th centuries, when European and American expeditions scoured the world for new ornamental and economically useful species.

History

The great age of plant hunting was driven by institutions such as the Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew, and by wealthy patrons who funded expeditions in search of exotic plants for their estates. Collectors such as Joseph Banks, David Douglas, Robert Fortune, Ernest Henry Wilson, and Frank Kingdon-Ward introduced thousands of species into Western cultivation, including many now-common garden plants from China, Japan, and western North America.

Methods and risks

Collectors pressed and dried specimens for herbaria and transported living material over long voyages. The invention of the Wardian case in the 1830s — a sealed glazed box that maintained humidity — greatly increased the survival rate of transported plants. The work was often dangerous: many collectors died of disease, accidents, or violence in the field, and expeditions regularly faced shipwreck and hardship.

Modern collecting

Today plant collecting is regulated by international agreements such as CITES and the Convention on Biological Diversity, which require permits and benefit-sharing with source countries. Modern collectors typically work for botanic gardens and seed banks, including projects aimed at conserving endangered species.
