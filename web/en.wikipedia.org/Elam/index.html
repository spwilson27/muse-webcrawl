Elam (Elamite: 𒁹𒄬𒆷𒆠 Haltamti) was an ancient civilization centred in the far west and southwest of modern-day Iran, stretching from the lowlands of Khuzestan to the highlands of Fars. Its capitals included Susa and later Dur-Untash (Chogha Zanbil). Elamite history spanned from about 3200 BC to 539 BC, making it one of the oldest civilizations of the ancient Near East and a persistent rival of the kingdoms of Mesopotamia.

History

The Proto-Elamite period (c. 3200–2700 BC) produced one of the earliest writing systems, Proto-Elamite script, which remains undeciphered. Old Elamite kings contended with Sumer and Akkad; the Awan and Shimashki dynasties ruled before Elam fell under Akkadian and Ur III influence. In the Middle Elamite period (c. 1500–1100 BC), kings such as Untash-Napirisha built the great ziggurat of Chogha Zanbil, and Shutruk-Nahhunte sacked Babylon, carrying off the Code of Hammurabi stele to Susa, where it was excavated in 1901.

Decline and fall

Neo-Elamite kings allied with Babylon against Assyria, but in 646 BC the Assyrian king Ashurbanipal devastated Susa. Elam subsequently declined and was absorbed into the Persian Achaemenid Empire of Cyrus the Great.

Language and legacy

The Elamite language is an isolate unrelated to neighbouring Semitic or Sumerian tongues; a late form, written in cuneiform, was one of the three languages of the Behistun Inscription that unlocked the decipherment of cuneiform. Elamite remained an administrative language of early Achaemenid Persia.
