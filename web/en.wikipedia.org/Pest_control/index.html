Pest control

Pest control is the regulation or management of a species defined as a pest — an animal, plant or fungus detrimental to humans or human concerns, including crops, livestock, stored food and health. Pest control is at least as old as agriculture, since farmers have always needed to protect crops from insects, rodents, birds and weeds.

Methods

  • Chemical control — the use of pesticides, including insecticides, herbicides, fungicides and rodenticides. Widely used since the mid-20th century (e.g. DDT), but raising concerns over environmental harm, resistance and residues, highlighted by Rachel Carson's Silent Spring (1962).
  • Biological control — using natural enemies such as predators, parasitoids and pathogens; a famous early success was the vedalia beetle saving California citrus from cottony cushion scale in 1888–89.
  • Cultural control — crop rotation, intercropping, resistant varieties, sanitation and trap crops.
  • Mechanical and physical control — traps, barriers, fencing, hand-picking and exclusion.
  • Genetic control — sterile insect technique and, more recently, gene-drive approaches, especially against disease-carrying mosquitoes.

Integrated pest management

Integrated pest management (IPM) combines methods, using monitoring and economic thresholds to apply pesticides only when necessary, reducing environmental impact and slowing resistance. In homes and cities, professional pest control targets cockroaches, bed bugs, termites, ants and rodents, balancing chemical use against safety.
