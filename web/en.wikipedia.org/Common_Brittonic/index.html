Common Brittonic

Common Brittonic
Region: Great Britain south of the Firth of Forth
Era: c. 6th century BC – mid 6th century AD
Family: Indo-European → Celtic → Insular Celtic → Brittonic
Descendants: Welsh, Cornish, Breton, Cumbric, possibly Pictish

Common Brittonic (also called British, Common Brythonic, or Proto-Brittonic) is the ancestral Celtic language once spoken across most of Great Britain. It developed from Proto-Celtic and, from about the mid-first millennium AD, differentiated into the neo-Brittonic languages: Welsh, Cornish, Breton and Cumbric.

Evidence

No continuous texts survive. The language is reconstructed from place names and river names recorded by Roman writers, inscriptions, loanwords in English and Latin, and comparison of its daughter languages.

Features

Common Brittonic lost final syllables and developed lenition of intervocalic consonants. Brittonic is a "P-Celtic" language: Proto-Celtic *kʷ became *p, so that *kʷetwares "four" yields Welsh pedwar, contrasting with Goidelic ceathair.

Decline

After the Anglo-Saxon settlements of the 5th and 6th centuries, Brittonic retreated to Wales, Cornwall, Cumbria and Strathclyde, while emigration carried it to Brittany, where it became Breton.
