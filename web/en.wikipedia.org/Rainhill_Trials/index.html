Rainhill trials

The Rainhill trials were an important competition run in October 1829 to test George Stephenson's idea that locomotives should power the Liverpool and Manchester Railway, rather than fixed stationary engines hauling trains by cable. Five locomotives were entered, although only three were still competing by the start of the official trials. The prize of £500 was won by Rocket, built by George and Robert Stephenson with Henry Booth.

Background

The directors of the railway were undecided between locomotives and stationary engines and offered the prize to settle the question. Conditions required a locomotive to weigh no more than six tons, to haul three times its own weight at at least 10 mph (16 km/h), to consume its own smoke, and to have boiler pressure not exceeding 50 psi.

The competitors

The entrants included Rocket, Timothy Hackworth's Sans Pareil, John Ericsson and John Braithwaite's Novelty, Timothy Burstall's Perseverance, and the horse-powered Cycloped. Novelty was initially the crowd's favourite for its speed, but its bellows-driven blast failed; Sans Pareil suffered a cracked cylinder. Only Rocket, with its multi-tubular boiler and blastpipe, completed all the trials successfully, reaching speeds of about 30 mph.

Legacy

The trials demonstrated decisively that steam locomotives were practicable for a main-line railway. The Liverpool and Manchester Railway opened on 15 September 1830 — marred by the death of MP William Huskisson, struck by Rocket — and became the model for the railway age that followed.
