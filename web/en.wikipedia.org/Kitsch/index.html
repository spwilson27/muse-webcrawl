Kitsch

Kitsch
Origin of termGerman, Munich art markets, 1860s–70s
MeaningArt or objects in poor taste but appreciated ironically or sentimentally
Key criticsClement Greenberg, Hermann Broch, Milan Kundera

Kitsch is a term applied to art and objects considered to be in poor taste because of excessive garishness or sentimentality, but sometimes appreciated in an ironic or knowing way. The word originated in the Munich art markets of the 1860s and 1870s, possibly from the English "sketch" or the German verkitschen, "to cheapen".

Characteristics

Kitsch products are typically mass-produced copies of culturally significant motifs — plastic religious icons, garden gnomes, paintings of big-eyed children, souvenir miniatures — offering ready-made emotion rather than aesthetic challenge. Kitsch is melodramatic, formulaic and instantly legible.

Critical theory

Clement Greenberg's 1939 essay "Avant-Garde and Kitsch" contrasted genuine avant-garde art with kitsch as its debased, industrial opposite. Hermann Broch called kitsch "the element of evil in the value system of art". In The Unbearable Lightness of Being, Milan Kundera treated kitsch as the aesthetic of totalitarian sentimentality — the denial of everything unacceptable in human existence.

Reappraisal

From the 1960s, Pop art and later postmodernism blurred the boundary, deliberately embracing kitsch imagery. Artists such as Jeff Koons produce kitsch as high art, and camp sensibility celebrates kitsch sincerely and ironically at once.
