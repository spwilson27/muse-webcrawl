Gerrymandering

Gerrymandering is the manipulation of the boundaries of electoral districts to favour one political party or class over another. The term was coined in the United States in 1812, when Massachusetts governor Elbridge Gerry signed a bill redrawing state senate districts to benefit his Democratic-Republican Party; one district's convoluted shape was said to resemble a salamander, producing the portmanteau "gerry-mander" in the Boston Gazette.

Techniques

Two principal tactics are used. Packing concentrates opposition voters into a few districts so they win by overwhelming margins, wasting their votes. Cracking disperses opposition voters across many districts so they form an ineffective minority in each. The measurable effects are often assessed through concepts such as the efficiency gap — the difference between the parties' wasted votes — and by comparing a plan's seat share with the parties' statewide vote shares.

Effects

Gerrymandering can entrench incumbents, reduce electoral competition, produce unrepresentative legislatures, and contribute to political polarisation by making primary elections, rather than general elections, the decisive contests. It has also been used both to dilute and, under the Voting Rights Act, to secure the representation of racial and ethnic minorities through majority-minority districts.

Legal status

In the United States, redistricting follows each decennial census. Racial gerrymandering is unconstitutional under the Fourteenth Amendment and the Voting Rights Act, but in Rucho v. Common Cause (2019) the Supreme Court held that partisan gerrymandering claims are nonjusticiable political questions in federal court, leaving regulation to Congress, state courts, and state constitutions. Some states assign redistricting to independent commissions. Many other democracies avoid the practice by using independent boundary commissions or proportional representation.
