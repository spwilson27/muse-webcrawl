Lancet MMR autism fraud

The Lancet MMR autism fraud refers to a fraudulent 1998 research paper published in The Lancet by Andrew Wakefield and eleven co-authors, which falsely claimed a link between the measles, mumps, and rubella (MMR) vaccine and autism. It is regarded as one of the most damaging episodes of scientific fraud in medical history, and the paper was fully retracted in 2010.

The paper

The paper described twelve children referred to the Royal Free Hospital in London with developmental disorders and bowel symptoms, and suggested a possible connection to the MMR vaccine, coining the proposed syndrome "autistic enterocolitis." At a press conference Wakefield called for suspension of the combined MMR vaccine in favour of single vaccines. Extensive subsequent epidemiology — studying hundreds of thousands of children — found no link between MMR and autism.

Exposure of the fraud

Investigative reporting by Brian Deer of The Sunday Times, beginning in 2004, revealed that Wakefield had been funded by lawyers preparing litigation against vaccine manufacturers, held a patent application for a rival single measles vaccine, and had altered case histories: the BMJ later concluded the paper was "an elaborate fraud," with symptoms and timelines misreported for all twelve children. Ten co-authors retracted the interpretation in 2004; the General Medical Council found Wakefield guilty of serious professional misconduct in 2010 and struck him off the medical register. The Lancet fully retracted the paper in February 2010.

Consequences

MMR vaccination rates in the United Kingdom fell from about 92% to below 80% at their lowest, measles became endemic again in Britain in 2008, and outbreaks followed in several countries. The affair remains a case study in research ethics, media responsibility, and the persistence of vaccine hesitancy.
