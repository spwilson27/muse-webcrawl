Pan-Celticism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Pan-Celticism is a political and cultural movement promoting cooperation, solidarity, and in some strands political unity among the Celtic nations — principally Ireland, Scotland, Wales, Brittany, Cornwall, and the Isle of Man, the regions where Celtic languages survive or have been revived. Like other "pan-" movements of the nineteenth century, it grew out of Romantic nationalism and the scholarly rediscovery of a shared Celtic heritage.

History

Pan-Celtic congresses and institutions developed from the late 19th century: the Celtic Congress (founded 1917, from earlier pan-Celtic congresses beginning in 1899) and the Celtic League (founded 1961) linked language campaigners and national movements across the six nations. Cultural expressions include the Festival Interceltique de Lorient in Brittany, founded in 1971, which draws performers from across the Celtic world, and shared ventures in music, broadcasting, and language revitalisation.

Politics

Most pan-Celtic activity is cultural rather than separatist: language promotion, exchanges, and solidarity statements by nationalist parties such as the SNP, Plaid Cymru, Sinn Féin, and Breton autonomists. Fringe strands have advocated a Celtic federation or confederation of independent states. Critics question how far "Celtic" identity corresponds to historical or linguistic reality, noting that the category is largely a modern construction, though one with real cultural and political effects.
