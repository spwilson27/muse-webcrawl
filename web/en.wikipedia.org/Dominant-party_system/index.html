Dominant-party system
Field: Political science
Contrast: One-party state; two-party system

A dominant-party system, or one-party dominant system, is a political system in which a single political party continuously dominates election results over a long period, even though opposition parties are legally allowed to operate and compete. Unlike a one-party state, opposition is not banned, but the dominant party's advantages make alternation in power rare.

Examples

Historical and current examples include the Liberal Democratic Party in Japan, the African National Congress in South Africa since 1994, the Institutional Revolutionary Party (PRI) in Mexico during most of the 20th century, the People's Action Party in Singapore, and the Bharatiya Janata Party in India in recent years. Sweden's Social Democrats dominated for much of the 20th century.

Causes and criticism

Dominance may arise from liberation-movement legitimacy, electoral systems, fragmentation of the opposition, incumbency advantages, or control of state resources. Critics argue prolonged dominance blurs the line between party and state and weakens accountability; supporters point to policy stability and continuity.
