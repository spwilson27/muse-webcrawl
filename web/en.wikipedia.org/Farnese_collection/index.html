Farnese collection

The Farnese collection is one of the most important collections of ancient Greek and Roman sculpture, glyptic art and painting assembled in Renaissance Italy. It was formed by the Farnese family, above all by Alessandro Farnese, who became Pope Paul III (reigned 1534–1549), and his grandsons Cardinal Alessandro Farnese and Cardinal Odoardo Farnese. The classical sculptures were displayed in the Palazzo Farnese in Rome; through the inheritance of Elisabetta Farnese, the collection passed to the Bourbon kings of Naples and is now largely in the National Archaeological Museum, Naples, with paintings divided between Naples (Museo di Capodimonte) and the National Gallery, Parma.

Formation

Paul III and his family collected antiquities unearthed in Rome, including finds from the Baths of Caracalla. Excavations there in the 1540s yielded the collection's most celebrated pieces: the colossal Farnese Hercules, a Roman copy signed by Glykon after Lysippos, and the Farnese Bull, the largest surviving single sculpture from antiquity, showing the myth of Dirce. The family also acquired the Farnese Atlas, gems such as the Tazza Farnese, and Renaissance paintings including works by Titian, Raphael (the portrait of Paul III) and Annibale Carracci, whose frescoes decorated the Palazzo Farnese gallery.

Transfer to Naples

Elisabetta Farnese, the last of the family, married Philip V of Spain; her son Charles of Bourbon became King of Naples in 1734 and inherited the collection. Between the 1780s and 1800s the sculptures were transferred to Naples, where they formed the core of the royal museum. The collection remains a cornerstone of classical archaeology, and the Farnese marbles in Naples are among the most studied ancient sculptures in the world.
