The Dreaming

The Dreaming
TraditionAboriginal Australian spirituality
Other termsDreamtime; Tjukurrpa (Pitjantjatjara); Ngarrnydjang
Core elementsAncestral beings, Law, songlines, sacred sites

The Dreaming, also called the Dreamtime, is the framework of Aboriginal Australian spirituality and law: an account of the world's creation by ancestral beings, and at the same time an enduring, parallel dimension of existence that continues to shape the present. The English word, popularized by ethnographers working with Arrernte people in Central Australia, imperfectly translates concepts such as Tjukurrpa in Pitjantjatjara and related languages.

Beliefs

In Dreaming narratives, ancestral beings — often part human, part animal or plant — travelled across a formless land, creating its features, species, and customs, then transformed into sites, rocks, and waterholes. Their journeys are remembered in songlines: sung routes that encode geography, law, and kinship obligations across vast distances. Individuals are connected through conception, birth, and totemic affiliation to particular Dreamings, which carry responsibilities for country and ceremony.

Continuity and law

Anthropologist W. E. H. Stanner famously called the Dreaming "everywhen" — not a vanished past but a timeless order continually renewed through ritual, art, and storytelling. It underpins Aboriginal law (jukurr/Law), land tenure, and identity, and has been recognized in Australian jurisprudence through native title claims following the 1992 Mabo decision.

Art and culture

Dreaming stories are expressed in rock art, body painting, bark paintings, and the internationally renowned acrylic dot paintings of the Western Desert movement that began at Papunya in the 1970s. Because many stories are restricted by initiation, gender, and custodianship, aspects of the Dreaming are not shared publicly.
