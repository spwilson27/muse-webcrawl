Gong

A gong is a percussion instrument originating in East and Southeast Asia, consisting of a flat, circular metal disc — usually of bronze — that is struck with a mallet to produce a resonant, ringing sound. Gongs have been made for well over a thousand years; bronze drums and early gongs appear in southern China by the early first millennium AD.

Types

Two broad categories are recognised. Suspended gongs such as the tam-tam (or chau gong) are flat-faced, indefinitely pitched, and produce a shimmering wash of sound; they entered the Western orchestra in the late 18th century. Bossed or nipple gongs have a raised central knob and a definite pitch — these include the tuned gongs of Southeast Asian ensembles such as the Javanese and Balinese gamelan (bonang, kenong, kempul, and the great gong ageng), the Burmese kyee, and the Filipino kulintang. Chinese opera gongs, including rising and falling (jing) gongs, signal entrances and actions on stage.

Uses

Gongs serve in temple and ritual music, processions, theatre, signalling (such as the dinner gong and the boxing gong), meditation and sound therapy, and the symphony orchestra, where composers from Gossec to Mahler and beyond have used the tam-tam for dramatic climaxes.
