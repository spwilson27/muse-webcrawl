An electrical grid, or electric grid, is an interconnected network for electricity delivery from producers to consumers. It consists of power stations that generate electrical power, high-voltage transmission lines that carry power over long distances, and distribution lines that deliver power to individual customers. Grid operators must balance supply and demand continuously, since electricity is consumed essentially the instant it is generated.

Structure

Generation takes place at power plants using fossil fuels, nuclear fission, hydroelectricity, wind, solar and other sources. Transmission moves bulk power at high voltages — which reduces losses — across a networked system of lines and substations, where transformers step voltage up or down. Distribution carries power from local substations to end users at lower voltages. Large synchronous grids span whole regions or continents; North America's major interconnections and the synchronous grid of Continental Europe are among the largest machines in the world.

Operation and stability

Grid frequency (50 or 60 Hz depending on region) is a real-time indicator of the supply–demand balance, and operators dispatch generators, storage and demand response to hold it within tight limits. Disturbances can cascade: major blackouts, such as the 2003 Northeast blackout in North America and the 2003 Italian blackout, prompted stricter reliability standards. Protective relays, islanding schemes and spinning reserves limit the spread of failures.

Modern developments

The growing share of variable renewable generation, rooftop solar, batteries and electric vehicles is driving investment in "smart grid" technologies — advanced metering, sensors, automation and digital control — as well as long-distance high-voltage direct current (HVDC) links and grid-scale energy storage. Microgrids that can operate partly independently are also increasingly deployed for resilience.
