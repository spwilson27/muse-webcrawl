The Scottish potato famine (also called the Highland potato famine) was a period of famine in the Scottish Highlands during the mid to late 1840s and 1850s caused by the same potato blight fungus, Phytophthora infestans, that triggered the Great Famine in Ireland. Although the Highland famine lasted somewhat longer than the Irish famine and was brought on by similar causes, the ensuing hardship, while terrible and bitterly remembered, did not reach the scale of death and emigration of the Irish crisis: the different demographic, social and economic structures of the Highlands, and organised relief, meant that comparatively few died of starvation.

The problem was one of basic subsistence. In the crofting communities of the north and west, the potato had become the staple food of a growing population squeezed into smallholdings after the Clearances had displaced communities to the coasts. By the 1840s the potato provided perhaps three quarters of the diet in the worst-affected districts. When blight arrived in 1846 it destroyed successive crops for nearly a decade.

Relief effort

Unlike Ireland, relief in the Highlands was organised largely through private charity. The Central Board of Management of the Fund for the Relief of the Destitute Inhabitants of the Highlands, formed in early 1847 by the merger of Edinburgh and Glasgow committees — the Free Church Destitution Committee had set an early example — raised over £200,000 and ran a remarkably efficient operation: meal depots, ration rolls, and public works including "destitution roads". Landowners also organised relief on their own estates, though with great variation. The government provided some support through the Board of Supervision and assisted emigration. By the early 1850s the board wound down its operations, transferring its remaining funds and the emigration effort to the Highland and Island Emigration Society, founded in 1851.

Emigration

Between 1846 and 1856 around 11,000 people from the crofting counties emigrated with charitable assistance, mainly to Canada and Australia; many thousands more left unassisted. For impoverished landlords, assisted emigration offered a permanent "solution" to destitute tenantry, and some evictions took place to clear estates. The famine thus accelerated the population decline of the Highlands, and it remains, with the Clearances, a central part of the region's historical memory.
