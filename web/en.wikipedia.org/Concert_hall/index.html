Concert hall

Concert hall
Purpose: Performance of (mainly classical) music
Common layouts: Shoebox, vineyard, arena
Famous examples: Musikverein (Vienna), Concertgebouw (Amsterdam), Boston Symphony Hall

A concert hall is a cultural building with a hall designed for musical performances, particularly classical music concerts. Concert halls may be purpose-built or part of a larger performing arts complex, and their design is dominated by the requirements of architectural acoustics.

Design and acoustics

The classic "shoebox" hall — a tall, narrow rectangle, as in Vienna's Musikverein (1870) and Amsterdam's Concertgebouw (1888) — is prized for warm, resonant sound with reverberation times of around two seconds. The "vineyard" design, pioneered by the Berlin Philharmonie (1963), places terraced audience blocks around a central stage. Modern halls use adjustable reflectors, diffusing surfaces, and computer modelling to tune the acoustic.

History

Public concert halls emerged in the 18th century as concerts moved out of courts and churches; London's Hanover Square Rooms (1775) was among the first purpose-built venues. The 19th century saw grand halls across Europe and America, followed by scientifically designed halls in the 20th century after Wallace Clement Sabine founded modern architectural acoustics with Boston Symphony Hall (1900).

Notable halls

Frequently ranked among the world's finest are the Musikverein, the Concertgebouw, Boston Symphony Hall, the Berlin Philharmonie, Suntory Hall in Tokyo, and newer venues such as Hamburg's Elbphilharmonie (2017).
