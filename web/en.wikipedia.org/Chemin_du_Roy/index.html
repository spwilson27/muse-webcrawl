Chemin du Roy

The Chemin du Roy (French for "King's Highway" or "King's Road") is a historic road along the north shore of the Saint Lawrence River in Quebec, Canada. Built beginning in 1731 under the administration of Intendant Gilles Hocquart, it linked Montreal and Quebec City and was, at the time of its completion in 1737, the longest road in North America north of Mexico, at about 280 kilometres (170 mi).

History

Before the road, travel between the two main towns of New France usually meant poling, paddling, or sailing along the Saint Lawrence. The colonial government ordered a road wide enough for horse-drawn vehicles, maintained by the settlers whose land it crossed. Relay stations with horses were established along the way, and the trip between Montreal and Quebec City took about four to six days by stagecoach.

Today

The modern Route 138 largely follows the Chemin du Roy. The road is promoted as a tourist route passing through some of Quebec's oldest villages — including Berthierville, Louiseville, Trois-Rivières, and Cap-Santé — with their churches, manor houses, and mills. It is often compared, in spirit, to a Quebec counterpart of a heritage trail.
