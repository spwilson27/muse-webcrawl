Representative realism

On the representative view, the mind directly perceives ideas that represent external objects.

Representative realism (also called indirect realism or epistemological dualism) is the philosophical position that we do not perceive the external world directly, but only through mental representations — ideas, sense data, or perceptual experiences — which are caused by and (in some respects) resemble the objects they represent. It is contrasted with direct realism, which holds that perception gives us the world itself, unmediated.

History

The view is closely associated with John Locke, who distinguished the primary qualities of objects, such as shape and motion, which resemble qualities in the objects themselves, from secondary qualities, such as colour and taste, which are powers to produce sensations in us. Versions of representative realism recur throughout modern philosophy and in the representational theory of mind in cognitive science, where perception and thought are modelled as operations on internal symbols.

Objections

The classic objection is that a "veil of perception" threatens scepticism: if we only ever encounter our own representations, we cannot compare them with the world to verify that they match it. Defenders reply that the hypothesis of an external world best explains the order, coherence, and involuntary character of experience, and that inference to the best explanation is a legitimate route to knowledge.
