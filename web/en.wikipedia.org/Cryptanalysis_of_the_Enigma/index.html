Cryptanalysis of the Enigma

Cryptanalysis of the Enigma refers to the Allied codebreaking effort against the German Enigma machine cipher during the Second World War. The work, begun by Polish mathematicians in the 1930s and continued on a vast scale at Britain's Bletchley Park, produced intelligence codenamed Ultra, which historians credit with shortening the war by as much as two years.

Polish beginnings

In 1932, Marian Rejewski of the Polish Cipher Bureau, using permutation theory and intelligence material purchased from French intelligence, reconstructed the wiring of the military Enigma. Together with Jerzy Różycki and Henryk Zygalski, he developed methods and devices — including the bomba kryptologiczna and Zygalski sheets — to recover daily keys. In July 1939, weeks before the German invasion, Poland shared its knowledge and replica Enigmas with France and Britain.

Bletchley Park

At Bletchley Park, Alan Turing and Gordon Welchman designed the bombe, an electromechanical device that exploited known or guessed plaintext ("cribs") and the Enigma's inability to encrypt a letter as itself to test rotor settings rapidly. Dilly Knox attacked Abwehr Enigma variants by hand methods. German operating errors, captured key documents, and lax procedures repeatedly assisted the codebreakers.

Naval Enigma and setbacks

The German Navy's four-rotor M4 machine caused the "Shark" blackout of 1942, during which U-boat traffic could not be read. Capture of codebooks from U-559 in October 1942 helped restore reading. Intelligence from Enigma decrypts influenced campaigns including the Battle of the Atlantic, operations in North Africa, and the planning of the Normandy landings, where elaborate deception protected the secret that the ciphers were broken.
