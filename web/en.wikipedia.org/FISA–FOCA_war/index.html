FISA–FOCA war

The dispute split Formula One in the early 1980s.

The FISA–FOCA war was a political dispute in Formula One racing in the early 1980s between the Fédération Internationale du Sport Automobile (FISA), the governing body led by Jean-Marie Balestre, and the Formula One Constructors' Association (FOCA), representing mainly the British-based independent teams and led by Bernie Ecclestone and Max Mosley.

Background

Conflict centred on control of the sport's commercial revenues and technical regulations. FISA sought to assert authority over television income and rules—including a ban on the sliding "skirts" used for ground-effect aerodynamics—while FOCA, whose members depended on sponsorship and start money, resisted measures they saw as favouring the manufacturer teams (Renault, Ferrari, Alfa Romeo) aligned with the governing body.

Escalation

The quarrel produced boycotts and breakaway threats: FOCA teams withdrew from the 1980 Spanish Grand Prix, which was stripped of championship status, and FOCA even announced a rival "World Federation of Motor Sport" series. The 1982 season opened with a drivers' strike at Kyalami over superlicence conditions, and FOCA teams boycotted the 1982 San Marino Grand Prix—a race remembered for the falling-out between Ferrari drivers Gilles Villeneuve and Didier Pironi.

Resolution

The dispute was settled by the first Concorde Agreement (1981), a contract—kept secret and repeatedly renewed since—that divided Formula One's commercial rights and governance between the federation and the teams. It established the framework under which the sport is still run: the FIA regulates, while commercial rights are exploited by the teams' and Ecclestone's organizations, a structure that transformed Formula One's finances.
