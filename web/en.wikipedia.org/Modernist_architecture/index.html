Modernist architecture

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Modernist architecture, or modern architecture, was an architectural movement that emerged in the early twentieth century and became dominant after the Second World War. Based on new construction technologies — steel, reinforced concrete and plate glass — it rejected applied ornament and historical styles in favour of functional planning, geometric form and the honest expression of materials, summed up in Louis Sullivan's dictum "form follows function" and Mies van der Rohe's "less is more".

Origins

Precursors included the Chicago school of skyscrapers, the German Werkbund, and the Bauhaus school founded by Walter Gropius in 1919. In the 1920s architects such as Le Corbusier, Ludwig Mies van der Rohe and Alvar Aalto developed the vocabulary of white walls, pilotis, ribbon windows, flat roofs and open plans. The 1932 Museum of Modern Art exhibition in New York codified one strand as the International Style.

Post-war dominance

After 1945 modernism became the default language for corporate towers, universities, government buildings and mass housing worldwide: glass-and-steel office slabs (Mies's Seagram Building, 1958), Le Corbusier's Unité d'Habitation and the planned capital of Brasília, and large public housing estates. Related currents included brutalism, which exposed raw concrete, and regional or organic variants.

Criticism and legacy

From the 1960s critics such as Jane Jacobs and Robert Venturi attacked modernist planning for destroying street life, monotony and social failure — symbolised by the demolition of the Pruitt–Igoe housing blocks in St. Louis (1972), often cited as the death of modern architecture. Postmodernism and later movements reacted against it, yet modernist principles of light, open planning and industrialised construction remain embedded in contemporary practice, and many modernist buildings are now protected heritage.
