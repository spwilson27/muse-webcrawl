Herons are long-legged, long-necked wading birds of the family Ardeidae, which comprises some 70 or more species found on all continents except Antarctica. The family also includes the egrets and bitterns. Herons are associated with wetlands, rivers, lakes and coasts, where they feed on fish, frogs and other aquatic creatures.

Characteristics

Herons have long, dagger-like bills, S-curved necks that they retract in flight (unlike storks and cranes, which fly with necks extended), and powder down feathers that help waterproof their plumage. Their size ranges from the diminutive least bittern, about 30 cm long, to the Goliath heron, which stands up to 152 cm tall with a wingspan of some 230 cm.

Behaviour and feeding

Herons are typically patient ambush predators, standing motionless at the water's edge before striking with a lightning-fast thrust of the bill. Some species, such as the black heron, use "canopy feeding", spreading their wings to form an umbrella of shade that attracts fish. Most herons nest colonially in trees in heronries, laying three to seven eggs; both parents incubate and feed the young.

Taxonomy

The family Ardeidae is usually divided into the typical herons and egrets (Ardeinae), the bitterns (Botaurinae) and the tiger-herons (Tigrisomatinae). Well-known species include the grey heron, great blue heron, great egret, cattle egret and the night herons.

In culture

In ancient Egypt the heron-like bennu bird was associated with the sun and renewal and may have inspired the Greek phoenix. Herons appear in heraldry and in the folklore of many cultures as symbols of patience, purity and solitude.
