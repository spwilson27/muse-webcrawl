Garlic

Garlic (Allium sativum) is a species of bulbous flowering plant in the genus Allium, closely related to the onion, shallot, leek, and chive. Native to Central Asia and northeastern Iran, it has been used by humans for thousands of years as a seasoning, a traditional medicine, and a folkloric protection against evil.

Description

The garlic plant grows from a bulb composed of individual cloves enclosed in a papery skin. The plant may produce a flowering stalk (scape) bearing pinkish flowers. Hardneck varieties form a stiff central stalk, while softneck varieties are more pliable and store longer.

Cultivation and production

Garlic is grown worldwide in temperate climates. China is by far the largest producer, accounting for the great majority of world output, followed by India. Cloves are planted in autumn or early spring, pointed end up, and harvested when the lower leaves yellow.

Culinary use

Garlic is a fundamental ingredient in cuisines around the world — Mediterranean, Asian, Middle Eastern, and Latin American cooking all rely on its pungent flavour, which mellows and sweetens when cooked. It is used raw in dressings and aioli, roasted whole, pickled, and dried into powder. The green scapes of hardneck garlic are also eaten.

Chemistry and health

Crushing a clove activates the enzyme alliinase, converting alliin into allicin, the compound chiefly responsible for garlic's odour and much of its biological activity. Garlic has been investigated for cardiovascular and antimicrobial effects; evidence supports a modest blood-pressure-lowering effect, though many popular claims remain unproven. Raw garlic can cause gastrointestinal irritation, and it interacts with some anticoagulant drugs.

History and folklore

Garlic was known in ancient Egypt, where it was fed to labourers and buried with pharaohs, and it appears in Indian, Chinese, and Greco-Roman medicine. In European folklore it was hung in windows or worn to repel vampires and other malevolent beings.
