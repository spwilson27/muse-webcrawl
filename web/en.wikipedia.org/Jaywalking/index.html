Jaywalking

Jaywalking is the act of pedestrians walking in or crossing a roadway that has traffic, other than at a suitable crossing point, or otherwise in disregard of traffic rules. The term originated in the United States as a derivation of the phrase jay-drivers (the word jay meaning 'a greenhorn, or rube'), people who drove horse-drawn carriages and automobiles on the wrong side of the road, before taking its current meaning.

History

Jaywalking laws were enacted in the United States in the 1920s, largely at the urging of the automobile industry, which sought to shift public blame for pedestrian deaths away from drivers and to restrict streets to motor vehicles. Safety campaigns of the era popularized the term "jaywalker" as a figure of ridicule.

Legal status

The legality and enforcement of jaywalking varies widely around the world. In many European countries, pedestrians are generally free to cross roads where they choose, subject to general duties of care, and formal offences are rare or nonexistent. In the United States, rules differ by state and municipality; some jurisdictions have recently decriminalized or deprioritized enforcement of jaywalking offences, arguing that enforcement had little safety benefit and was applied unevenly.

Safety

Pedestrian fatalities are influenced by street design, vehicle speed, lighting, and visibility. Critics of jaywalking laws argue that enforcement focuses on pedestrian behaviour rather than on the road conditions and vehicle speeds that make collisions lethal.
