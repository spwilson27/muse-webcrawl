Quipu

Quipu
Other namesKhipu; "talking knots"
CultureInca Empire and predecessor Andean societies
MediumKnotted cotton or camelid-fibre cords
FunctionRecord-keeping: census, tribute, labour, possibly narrative

A quipu (Quechua: khipu, "knot"), sometimes called a "talking knot," was a recording device used in the Inca Empire and in earlier Andean societies. Quipus consist of cords of cotton or camelid fibre in which knots of various types, positions, and colours encode information. Trained specialists called quipucamayocs ("knot keepers") created and read them for the imperial bureaucracy.

An Inca quipu, with knotted pendant cords

Structure and the decimal code

A typical quipu has a thick primary cord from which pendant cords hang; pendants may carry subsidiary cords in turn, and cords are dyed in many colours. Research beginning with Leland Locke in the 1920s showed that most knots encode numbers in a base-ten positional system: clusters of single knots mark powers of ten by their position along the cord, a figure-eight knot represents one in the units place, and a long knot's turns represent digits two through nine. Colours, cord placement, and knot direction may have indicated what was being counted — men, llamas, taxes, or days of labour.

Uses

The Inca state, which governed millions of subjects without a known system of writing, administered census counts, tribute, storehouse inventories, and labour taxes (mit'a) through quipus. Knot keepers maintained parallel records, and Spanish colonial administrators initially accepted quipu evidence in court. From the later 16th century, colonial and church authorities suppressed quipus as idolatrous, and most were destroyed; roughly a thousand are known to survive in museums and collections.

Decipherment

Whether quipus also recorded language — names, places, or narratives — remains an open research question. Studies by Gary Urton and others have documented a binary coding system in construction choices (spin, ply, knot direction, colour) that could carry more information than numbers alone. In 2017, a Harvard undergraduate, Manny Medrano, matched a set of quipu knots to a Spanish colonial census document, the first time a quipu's numbers were tied to named individuals; subsequent work suggests that cord features may phonetically encode place names.
