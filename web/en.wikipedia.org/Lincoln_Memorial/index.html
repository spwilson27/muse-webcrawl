The Lincoln Memorial honours the 16th president of the United States.

The Lincoln Memorial is a U.S. national memorial honoring Abraham Lincoln, the 16th president of the United States. It is situated on the western end of the National Mall in Washington, D.C., across from the Washington Monument, and takes the form of a neoclassical temple. The architect was Henry Bacon; the huge seated statue of Lincoln inside was sculpted by Daniel Chester French and carved by the Piccirilli brothers.

Design and inscriptions

The memorial is ringed by 36 Doric columns representing the states in the Union at the time of Lincoln's death. Inside, the 19-foot (5.8 m) marble statue of a seated, contemplative Lincoln gazes toward the Reflecting Pool and the Capitol. The walls are inscribed with the full texts of two of Lincoln's most famous speeches: the Gettysburg Address and his second inaugural address. Murals by Jules Guerin depict emancipation and the reunion of North and South.

History and symbolism

Authorized by Congress in 1911, the memorial was built from 1914 to 1922 and dedicated on 30 May 1922 in a ceremony attended by President Warren G. Harding; Lincoln's surviving son, Robert Todd Lincoln, was present. The dedication ceremony was segregated, an irony noted at the time. The memorial has since become a symbolic centre for civil rights: in 1939, after the Daughters of the American Revolution barred her from Constitution Hall, Marian Anderson sang from its steps before a crowd of 75,000; on 28 August 1963, Martin Luther King Jr. delivered his "I Have a Dream" speech during the March on Washington before some 250,000 people from the memorial's steps.

Depictions

The memorial appears on the reverse of the United States five-dollar bill and (formerly) on the penny. It is administered by the National Park Service and is open to the public 24 hours a day.
