Fibrin

Fibrin
TypeFibrous protein
PrecursorFibrinogen
Formed byAction of thrombin on fibrinogen
FunctionBlood clot mesh; wound healing scaffold

Fibrin is a fibrous, non-globular protein involved in the clotting of blood. It is formed from the soluble plasma protein fibrinogen by the action of the enzyme thrombin, and polymerises into a mesh that, together with platelets, forms a haemostatic plug over a wound site.

Formation

At the end of the coagulation cascade, thrombin cleaves fibrinopeptides A and B from fibrinogen, producing fibrin monomers that spontaneously assemble into half-staggered protofibrils and then fibres. Activated factor XIII (fibrin-stabilising factor) cross-links the fibres, greatly strengthening the clot. Fibrin is thus a specific protein, distinct from fibrils in general (see fibril).

Role in disease and medicine

Excessive fibrin formation contributes to thrombosis; its breakdown (fibrinolysis) by plasmin produces degradation products including D-dimer, a clinical marker for thrombotic disorders. Inherited disorders include afibrinogenemia and dysfibrinogenemia. Purified fibrin is used medically as fibrin glue (sealant) and as a scaffold in tissue engineering.
