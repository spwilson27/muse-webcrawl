Higher education in the United States

Higher education in the United States is provided by a large and diverse system of public and private institutions, including research universities, liberal arts colleges, community colleges and for-profit providers. Around two-thirds of American high-school graduates enrol in some form of postsecondary education, and the United States hosts many of the world's most highly ranked research universities.

History

The oldest institutions, such as Harvard College (founded 1636), began as colonial colleges training clergy and gentlemen. The Morrill Land-Grant Acts of 1862 and 1890 created a system of public universities oriented to agriculture, engineering and broad access, and the GI Bill of 1944 dramatically expanded enrolment after the Second World War, followed by the growth of community colleges and federal student aid.

Structure and issues

Undergraduate degrees usually take four years and combine general education with a major; graduate and professional schools award master's, doctoral and professional degrees. Institutions are accredited by private agencies recognised by the federal government, a status required for participation in federal student-loan programmes. Persistent public debates concern rising tuition and student debt, admissions practices, affirmative action, and the role of intercollegiate athletics.
