Taishō period

The Taishō period (大正時代, Taishō jidai) is a period in the history of Japan dating from 30 July 1912 to 25 December 1926, coinciding with the reign of Emperor Taishō. Coming after the rapid modernisation of the Meiji period, it is noted for a liberal movement known as "Taishō democracy" and for Japan's emergence as a major world power during and after the First World War.

Politics

The period opened with a political crisis in 1912–13 over the balance of power between the military and the cabinet, and saw the growth of party governments, culminating in the appointment of Hara Takashi of the Seiyūkai as the first "commoner" prime minister in 1918. Universal male suffrage was enacted in 1925 — the same year as the repressive Peace Preservation Law. The era was punctuated by the Rice Riots of 1918, intervention in Siberia, the Great Kantō earthquake of 1923, and Japan's participation in the Paris Peace Conference and the League of Nations.

Culture

Taishō Japan saw flourishing urban consumer culture, literature, and new media including radio broadcasting, which began in 1925. The era name means "great righteousness". Because Emperor Taishō was in poor health, effective power shifted toward the Empress and the genrō, and the period ended with his death and the accession of Emperor Shōwa in December 1926.
