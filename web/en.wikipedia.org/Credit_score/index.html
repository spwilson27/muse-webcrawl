Credit score

Credit score
Purpose: Predict loan repayment risk
Common models: FICO Score, VantageScore
Typical range (FICO): 300–850
Data sources: Equifax, Experian, TransUnion

A credit score is a number that represents the creditworthiness of an individual, or the likelihood that a person will repay a loan. Lenders use credit scores to evaluate the potential risk posed by lending money to consumers and to mitigate losses due to bad debt. Credit scoring is not limited to banks; insurance companies, landlords, employers and government departments may also use scores.

FICO Score

The FICO Score, created by the Fair Isaac Corporation, is the most widely used credit score in the United States. It ranges from 300 to 850 and is calculated from five categories of data reported by the major credit bureaus:

  • Payment history (35%) — whether bills were paid on time.
  • Amounts owed (30%) — including credit utilisation ratios.
  • Length of credit history (15%) — age of accounts.
  • New credit (10%) — recent applications and openings.
  • Credit mix (10%) — variety of credit types used.

Credit reports and bureaus

Scores are computed from credit reports compiled by bureaus such as Equifax, Experian and TransUnion in the US, and by analogous agencies in other countries. In the US, the Fair Credit Reporting Act gives consumers the right to view their reports and dispute errors; negative information such as bankruptcies and late payments generally remains on a report for seven to ten years.

Uses and criticism

Beyond lending, scores influence insurance premiums, rental decisions and sometimes employment screening. Critics argue that scoring can perpetuate economic inequality and that opaque models make errors hard to correct, while proponents credit scores with expanding access to credit by replacing subjective judgement with standardised risk estimates.
