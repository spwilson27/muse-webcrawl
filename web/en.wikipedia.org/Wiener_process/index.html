Wiener process

In mathematics, the Wiener process is a real-valued continuous-time stochastic process named in honour of Norbert Wiener for his investigations of the mathematical properties of Brownian motion. It is the standard mathematical model of Brownian motion and one of the best-known Lévy processes.

Definition and properties

A Wiener process W(t) starts at zero, has continuous paths, and has independent increments such that the change over any interval of length t is normally distributed with mean 0 and variance t. Its paths are continuous everywhere but differentiable nowhere, and they are self-similar: rescaled copies look statistically the same. The process is also a martingale and a Markov process.

History

The botanist Robert Brown observed the jittery motion of pollen grains in water in 1827. Albert Einstein's 1905 paper explained it as evidence for atoms and molecules, and Norbert Wiener provided the first rigorous mathematical construction of the process in the 1920s. Louis Bachelier had used a similar model for stock prices as early as 1900.

Applications

The Wiener process is foundational in stochastic calculus: it is the driving noise in stochastic differential equations and in the Itô integral. In mathematical finance it underlies the Black–Scholes model of option pricing, while geometric Brownian motion (the exponential of a Wiener process with drift) models asset prices. It also appears in physics (diffusion), queueing theory and population genetics.
