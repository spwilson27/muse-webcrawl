West Virginia coal wars
Date1912–1921
LocationSouthern West Virginia, United States
PartiesCoal miners and the United Mine Workers vs. coal operators, Baldwin-Felts detectives, state and federal forces

The West Virginia coal wars (1912–1921), also known as the mine wars, were a series of armed labor conflicts in southern West Virginia between coal miners seeking to unionize and coal companies that resisted organization, often with the help of private detective agencies, strikebreakers, and government forces. They culminated in the Battle of Blair Mountain in 1921, the largest armed uprising in the United States since the Civil War.

Background

Southern West Virginia's coalfields were dominated by company towns in which operators owned housing, stores, and local government. Miners were paid in scrip, worked in dangerous conditions, and were guarded by mine guards employed by agencies such as Baldwin-Felts. The United Mine Workers of America (UMWA) had organized the northern coalfields but faced fierce resistance in the south.

Paint Creek–Cabin Creek strike

In 1912 miners on Paint Creek and Cabin Creek struck for union recognition and better conditions. The strike turned violent, with mine guards firing on a miners' tent colony and martial law declared repeatedly. Labor leader Mary Harris "Mother" Jones was arrested and tried by a military court. The strike ended in 1913 with a compromise settlement.

Matewan and the march on Logan

On May 19, 1920, Baldwin-Felts agents evicting miners' families in Matewan, Mingo County, clashed with Police Chief Sid Hatfield and townspeople; ten men were killed, including seven detectives. Hatfield's acquittal and later assassination in 1921 helped provoke some 10,000 armed miners to march toward Logan County. The resulting Battle of Blair Mountain (August 25–September 2, 1921) saw miners fight sheriff's forces and private aircraft drop bombs before federal troops intervened and the miners dispersed.

Legacy

Although the union was defeated in the short term and UMWA membership in the region collapsed, the mine wars became a defining episode in American labor history. Southern West Virginia was largely unionized in the 1930s under the New Deal. The red bandanas worn by miners during the march are the origin of the term "redneck" in this labor context.
