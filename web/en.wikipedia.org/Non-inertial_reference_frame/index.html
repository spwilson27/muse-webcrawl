Non-inertial reference frame

Non-inertial reference frame
FieldClassical mechanics
DefinitionA reference frame that accelerates relative to an inertial frame
ConsequencesFictitious forces: centrifugal, Coriolis, Euler, translational pseudo-force
ExamplesRotating turntable/carousel; accelerating vehicle; Earth's rotating surface frame
ContrastInertial frame of reference

A non-inertial reference frame (also non-inertial frame or accelerated frame) is a frame of reference that undergoes acceleration with respect to an inertial frame. In such a frame, Newton's laws of motion do not hold in their simplest form: an accelerometer at rest in the frame will register acceleration, and objects appear to be acted on by fictitious forces (also called pseudo-forces or inertial forces) that have no physical source in other bodies.

Fictitious forces

To retain the form F = ma in an accelerating frame, additional terms must be added to the real forces:

F′ = F − mA − m Ω̇×r − m Ω×(Ω×r) − 2m Ω×v′

These correspond respectively to the translational pseudo-force, the Euler force, the centrifugal force, and the Coriolis force. On the rotating Earth, these effects explain the deflection of winds and ocean currents, the precession of the Foucault pendulum, and the slight variation of effective gravity with latitude.

Detection and relativity

Unlike uniform velocity, acceleration is locally detectable: an observer sealed in a laboratory can tell, from pendulums, accelerometers or thrown objects, whether their frame accelerates or rotates — a fact Newton illustrated with his rotating-bucket argument. In general relativity, Einstein's equivalence principle reinterprets the inertial pseudo-force of gravity itself: a uniformly accelerating frame is locally indistinguishable from a gravitational field, and freely falling frames are treated as the true local inertial frames.
