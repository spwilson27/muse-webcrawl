Woodblock printing

Woodblock printing
TypeRelief printing technique
OriginChina, by the Tang dynasty
Key worksDiamond Sutra (868 CE)

Woodblock printing is a technique for printing text, images or patterns that originated in China in antiquity. The image or text is carved into the surface of a block of wood in reverse; the raised areas are inked and pressed onto paper or fabric, leaving the carved-away areas blank.

History in East Asia

Printing on cloth with wooden blocks was practised in Han-dynasty China, and printing on paper followed the invention of papermaking. The earliest surviving dated printed book is the Chinese Diamond Sutra of 868 CE, a complete woodblock-printed scroll from the Tang dynasty. The technique spread to Korea and Japan, where it later produced the celebrated ukiyo-e prints of the Edo period using multiple colour blocks.

History in Europe

Woodblock printing reached Europe by the late 14th century, first for textiles and playing cards, then for "block books" combining pictures and text. It was the dominant illustration technique of early printed books before being largely displaced by copper engraving, though it remained important for popular prints and wallpapers.

Technique

The block is typically carved from a fine-grained wood such as cherry or pear, cut along the grain. The printer applies ink or watercolour pigment to the block, lays the sheet over it, and rubs the back with a pad (the Japanese baren) or presses it to transfer the image. Colour printing requires a separate block for each colour, aligned with registration marks.
