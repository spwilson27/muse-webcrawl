Jupiter (god)

Jupiter (Latin: Iuppiter)
Abode: Mount Olympus / the heavens
Symbols: Lightning bolt, eagle, oak
Greek equivalent: Zeus
Parents: Saturn and Ops; Consort: Juno

Jupiter, also known as Jove, is the god of the sky and thunder and king of the gods in ancient Roman religion and mythology. He was the chief deity of the Roman state throughout the Republican and Imperial eras, worshipped together with Juno and Minerva as the Capitoline Triad in the great temple on Rome's Capitoline Hill.

Origins and character

Jupiter derives from the Proto-Indo-European sky father *Dyēus, the same root that produced the Greek Zeus, with whom he was closely identified. His name is a contraction of "Diespiter" — sky father. As Jupiter Optimus Maximus ("the Best and Greatest"), he embodied Roman sovereignty, law, and military victory: Roman generals celebrated triumphs at his temple, and his priests, such as the flamen Dialis, were bound by elaborate taboos.

Cult and festivals

The Temple of Jupiter Optimus Maximus, dedicated in 509 BC according to tradition, was the religious center of the Roman Republic. Jupiter presided over the sky's phenomena — lightning strikes were interpreted as his omens — and over oaths and treaties; the fetial priests invoked him in declarations of war and peace. His principal festivals included the Ludi Romani in September and the Epulum Jovis.

Legacy

The largest planet of the solar system is named for Jupiter, and his figure pervades Western art, literature, and political iconography as the archetype of the sovereign sky god. The English word "jovial" descends from Jove, reflecting the astrological belief that those born under the planet Jupiter were cheerful.
