Wojciech Jaruzelski

Wojciech Witold Jaruzelski (6 July 1923 – 25 May 2014) was a Polish military officer and politician who served as the de facto leader of the Polish People's Republic from 1981 to 1989, and then as President of Poland from 1989 to 1990. As First Secretary of the Polish United Workers' Party, he imposed martial law in December 1981 to suppress the Solidarity movement, yet a decade later he presided over the negotiated transition that ended communist rule in Poland.

Early life and military career

Jaruzelski was born into a patriotic gentry family in Kurów. After the Soviet invasion of eastern Poland in 1939, his family was deported to Siberia, where his father died and where labour in the forests damaged Jaruzelski's eyes — the origin of the dark glasses he later always wore. He joined the Soviet-formed Polish army in 1943, fought on the Eastern Front, and after the war rose rapidly through the ranks of the communist Polish People's Army, becoming its chief of the general staff and, in 1968, Minister of National Defence.

Leadership and martial law

Amid strikes and the rise of the independent Solidarity trade union led by Lech Wałęsa, Jaruzelski became Prime Minister in February 1981 and party First Secretary that October. On 13 December 1981 he declared martial law: Solidarity was suspended and later banned, thousands of activists were interned, and dozens of people were killed in the ensuing crackdown. Jaruzelski defended the decision for the rest of his life as the "lesser evil" that averted a Soviet invasion, a claim historians continue to debate.

Transition and later life

By the late 1980s, facing economic crisis and renewed strikes, Jaruzelski authorised the Round Table talks with the opposition in 1989, leading to semi-free elections that Solidarity won decisively. He served briefly as President of the restored Republic of Poland before resigning in 1990 and being succeeded by Wałęsa. In retirement he faced long-running legal proceedings over martial law and the 1970 coastal shootings; he died in Warsaw in 2014, remaining one of the most controversial figures in modern Polish history.
