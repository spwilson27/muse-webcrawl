Decision theory (or the theory of choice) is the study of an agent's choices. It is concerned with the reasoning underlying decisions and with how decisions can be made well. Decision theory can be broken into two branches: normative decision theory, which analyses the outcomes of decisions or determines the optimal decisions given constraints and assumptions, and descriptive decision theory, which analyses how agents actually make the decisions they do.

Normative theory and expected utility

The dominant normative framework is expected utility theory, axiomatised by John von Neumann and Oskar Morgenstern in Theory of Games and Economic Behavior (1944) and given a subjective-probability foundation by Leonard J. Savage in The Foundations of Statistics (1954). An agent's preferences over lotteries are represented by a utility function, and a rational agent chooses the act with the highest expected utility — the probability-weighted average of the utilities of its possible outcomes. Bayesian decision theory integrates this framework with Bayes' rule for updating beliefs from evidence.

Choice under uncertainty

Frank Knight distinguished risk, where probabilities are known, from uncertainty, where they are not. Under ignorance, theorists have proposed alternative criteria, including maximin (choose the act whose worst outcome is best), minimax regret, and the Hurwicz optimism–pessimism index. Maurice Allais and Daniel Ellsberg demonstrated choice patterns (the Allais paradox and Ellsberg paradox) that violate the independence assumptions of expected utility theory.

Descriptive theory

Empirical research, especially by Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman, shows that human choices systematically depart from the normative ideal through heuristics and biases such as loss aversion, anchoring, and framing effects. Their prospect theory (1979) models choices with a value function that is steeper for losses than for gains and with distorted probability weights. Herbert Simon's concept of bounded rationality likewise holds that agents satisfice rather than optimise, given limited information and cognitive capacity.

Applications

Decision theory underpins economics, management science, operations research, medical decision-making, law, and artificial intelligence, where rational-agent models guide the design of autonomous systems. Game theory extends the analysis to situations where outcomes depend on the choices of multiple agents.
