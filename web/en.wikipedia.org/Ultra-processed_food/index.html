Ultra-processed food

Ultra-processed foods are industrial formulations typically made of five or more ingredients, produced by a series of industrial processes and containing substances rarely used in home cooking — such as hydrogenated oils, high-fructose corn syrup, flavour enhancers, emulsifiers, colourings and other cosmetic additives. They are the fourth group of the NOVA classification, developed by researchers at the University of São Paulo led by Carlos Monteiro from 2009.

Definition and examples

NOVA group 4 products include soft drinks, sweet and savoury packaged snacks, confectionery, mass-produced packaged breads and buns, margarines, breakfast cereals, energy drinks, instant noodles and soups, reconstituted meat products such as nuggets and hot dogs, and ready-to-heat meals. They are typically hyper-palatable, energy-dense, high in added sugar, salt and unhealthy fats, low in fibre and micronutrients, durable, convenient and heavily branded.

Consumption

Ultra-processed products provide a majority of dietary energy in several high-income countries — around 57% in the United States and nearly 57% in the United Kingdom, with substantial shares also in Canada and Australia — and their share is rising rapidly in middle-income countries as global food companies expand into those markets.

Health research

A large body of prospective cohort research associates higher consumption with increased risks of obesity, type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, some cancers, depression and all-cause mortality. A 2024 umbrella review in The BMJ covering nearly 10 million participants found direct associations with 32 health outcomes. A 2019 inpatient randomized controlled trial at the US National Institutes of Health found that an ultra-processed diet caused participants to eat about 500 kcal per day more and gain weight compared with an unprocessed diet matched for presented calories and macronutrients.

Debate and policy

Critics argue that the NOVA category is heterogeneous — grouping products of differing nutritional quality — and that associations partly reflect nutrient composition and socioeconomic confounding rather than processing itself. Supporters respond that formulation and processing exert effects beyond nutrients. Several countries' dietary guidelines, beginning with Brazil's 2014 guidelines, advise avoiding ultra-processed foods, and policies under discussion include warning labels, marketing restrictions and taxes.
