The themes or themata (Greek: θέματα, singular θέµα, théma) were the main military and administrative divisions of the middle Byzantine Empire. They were established in the mid-7th century in the aftermath of the Slavic migrations into the Balkans and the Muslim conquests of parts of Byzantine territory, and replaced the earlier provincial system established by Diocletian and Constantine. The thematic system drew its basic structural features from its thematic armies, which were assigned a territory, usually named after the army stationed there.

Origins

Facing the loss of Syria, Egypt, Africa, and pressure in the Balkans, the emperors of the later seventh century withdrew the field armies of the East into Asia Minor and settled them in great districts: the Anatolic theme from the Army of the East, the Armeniac theme from the Army of Armenia, the Thracesian theme from the Army of Thrace, and the Opsikion from the imperial retinue (obsequium). Each was commanded by a strategos, who combined military and civil authority. The system is traditionally associated with Heraclius, though modern scholarship holds that it crystallized gradually under his successors, especially Constans II.

Development

Over the eighth and ninth centuries the great themes were subdivided into smaller ones, both to improve frontier defence and to curb the power of over-mighty strategoi. Soldier-farmers, supported by military lands, formed the backbone of the thematic armies and of Byzantine defence during the centuries of Arab raids. By the tenth century there were dozens of themes across Asia Minor and the Balkans, listed in works such as Constantine VII's De Thematibus. From the later eleventh century the system declined as thematic militias were replaced by professional tagmata and mercenary forces, and the themes survived mainly as administrative and fiscal districts into the Palaiologan period.
