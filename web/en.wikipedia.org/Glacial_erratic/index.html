Glacial erratic

Glacial erratics are large pieces of rock that differ in type from the bedrock of the area in which they are found, having been transported by a glacier or ice sheet, sometimes over hundreds of kilometres, and deposited when the ice melted. They range in size from pebbles to enormous boulders weighing thousands of tonnes, such as the Okotoks Erratic ("Big Rock") in Alberta, Canada, and the Madison Boulder in New Hampshire.

Formation and identification

Erratics are carried within or on top of moving ice. Because their lithology can often be matched to a specific distant source outcrop, geologists use them — including distinctive "indicator erratics" — to reconstruct the flow directions and extent of former ice sheets. Erratics are frequently found perched in conspicuous positions, resting on unlike bedrock where the surrounding glacial till has eroded away.

Historical significance

The puzzling distribution of huge foreign boulders across northern Europe and North America was a major puzzle of early geology. Explanations invoking biblical floods gave way, in the 1830s and 1840s, to Louis Agassiz's theory of a great ice age, for which erratics provided some of the most persuasive evidence. Famous examples include the Pierre à Bot in Switzerland and the erratics of the English Midlands and Yorkshire coast derived from Scandinavia.
