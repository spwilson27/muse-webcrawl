The Baths of Diocletian (Latin: Thermae Diocletiani) were the largest of the imperial thermae (baths) of ancient Rome. Built between 298 and 306 AD during the reign of Diocletian, they covered about 13 hectares (32 acres) on the Viminal Hill and could accommodate some 3,000 bathers at once — roughly twice the capacity of the Baths of Caracalla.

History

Construction began after Diocletian's abdication was anticipated; the baths were dedicated in the names of Diocletian and his co-emperor Maximian. They were supplied with water by the Aqua Marcia aqueduct. The complex remained in use until the aqueducts were cut during the Gothic siege of Rome in 537.

Later use

Parts of the vast complex were adapted over later centuries. In 1561 Pius IV commissioned Michelangelo to convert the great central halls (tepidarium/frigidarium) into the church of Santa Maria degli Angeli e dei Martiri. Other sections house the Museo Nazionale Romano (Baths of Diocletian museum), and the surviving Octagonal Hall long served as Rome's planetarium. The Piazza della Repubblica, with its fountain, follows the curve of the baths' exedra.
