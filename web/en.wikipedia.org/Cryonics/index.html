Cryonics

Cryonics (from Greek: κρύος kryos meaning "cold") is the low-temperature freezing and storage of a human corpse or severed head, with the speculative hope that resurrection may be possible in the future. Cryonics is regarded with skepticism by the mainstream scientific community; it is generally viewed as a pseudoscience, and its practice has been characterized as quackery.

Cryonics procedures can begin only after a person is legally dead. The process involves cooling the body, perfusing it with cryoprotectant solutions intended to reduce ice formation, and storing it at the temperature of liquid nitrogen (about −196 °C). Proponents argue that future medical technology, such as molecular nanotechnology, might one day repair the damage caused by the original disease, the dying process, and the preservation procedure itself.

History

The concept of cryonics was introduced by Robert Ettinger in his 1962 book The Prospect of Immortality. The first person to be cryopreserved was James Bedford, a psychology professor, in 1967. Organizations such as the Alcor Life Extension Foundation and the Cryonics Institute were founded in the 1970s to provide preservation services.

Procedure

After legal death is declared, a standby team stabilizes the body, supports circulation and respiration to limit ischemic damage, and cools the patient. Blood is washed out and replaced with an organ preservation solution, followed by a cryoprotectant mixture. The body is then cooled gradually to liquid nitrogen temperature and stored in a vacuum-insulated dewar.

Criticism

Critics note that no mammal has ever been successfully revived after full-body cryopreservation, that cryoprotectants are toxic at the concentrations used, and that the brain suffers extensive structural damage during the procedure. Cryonics organizations respond that preservation aims to retain enough information about the brain's structure that revival is not ruled out in principle.
