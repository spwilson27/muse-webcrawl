Monoid ring

In abstract algebra, a monoid ring is a ring constructed from a ring R and a monoid M in much the same way as a group ring is built from a ring and a group. One takes all formal finite sums of elements of M with coefficients in R: expressions of the form r1m1 + … + rnmn, where the ri lie in R and the mi in M. Addition is componentwise, and multiplication is defined by the distributive law together with the rule (rm)(sn) = (rs)(mn), extended linearly — the "convolution" product. The resulting ring is denoted R[M].

Formalisation

Equivalently, R[M] is the set of functions from M to R of finite support, where the support of a function is the set of monoid elements sent to non-zero values. The monoid ring has a universal property: ring homomorphisms out of R[M] correspond to pairs consisting of a ring homomorphism out of R and a monoid homomorphism from M into the multiplicative monoid of the target ring. This makes R[M] the free R-algebra generated by M.

Examples

The most familiar example is the polynomial ring: if M is the additive monoid of natural numbers, R[M] is exactly the ring R[x] of polynomials in one variable, the natural number n corresponding to xn. For the free commutative monoid on several generators one obtains polynomials in several variables; for M = Zn one gets Laurent polynomials, allowing negative exponents. Taking M to be a group recovers the group ring, and taking the multiplicative monoid of natural numbers under a suitable ordering leads to rings of formal power series in related constructions.

Properties

Properties of R[M] reflect those of both ingredients. If M is commutative, so is R[M]. Under mild conditions — for instance, if R is Noetherian and M is a finitely generated commutative monoid — R[M] is Noetherian, a consequence of the Hilbert basis theorem. Domain and unique-factorisation properties are subtler: if R is a domain and M is a cancellative, torsion-free commutative monoid that admits a compatible total order, then R[M] is a domain. Monoid rings and their noncommutative analogues are important in combinatorics, representation theory and the theory of semigroup algebras.
