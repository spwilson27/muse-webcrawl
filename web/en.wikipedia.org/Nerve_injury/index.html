Nerve injury

Nerve injury is an injury to nervous tissue, most commonly to the peripheral nerves. Injuries may result from trauma (laceration, compression, stretch or crush), ischaemia, inflammation, infection, metabolic disorders or toxins. Depending on severity, a nerve injury can cause loss of sensation, weakness or paralysis, pain, and autonomic dysfunction in the territory supplied by the affected nerve.

Classification

Two classical schemes are widely used. Seddon's classification (1943) describes three grades: neurapraxia, a transient conduction block with intact axons, usually from compression, which recovers fully; axonotmesis, in which axons are disrupted but the connective-tissue sheaths survive, allowing regeneration along the original pathway; and neurotmesis, complete transection of the nerve, which requires surgical repair for any recovery. Sunderland later subdivided these into five grades based on which connective tissue layers (endoneurium, perineurium, epineurium) are damaged.

Regeneration

After axonal injury, the distal segment undergoes Wallerian degeneration: axons and myelin break down and are cleared by macrophages and Schwann cells. Schwann cells proliferate within the basal lamina tubes (forming bands of Büngner), secreting growth factors that guide regenerating axonal sprouts from the proximal stump. Regrowth proceeds at roughly 1 mm per day, so recovery from proximal injuries can take many months and is often incomplete.

Treatment

Management ranges from observation and physiotherapy for mild injuries to microsurgical repair, nerve grafting (commonly using the sural nerve), or nerve transfers for complete transections. Timing matters: muscle denervation beyond about 12–18 months leads to irreversible loss of motor endplates. Diagnosis relies on clinical examination, electrodiagnostic studies (nerve conduction studies and electromyography), and imaging such as ultrasound or MR neurography.
