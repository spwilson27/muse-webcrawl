Maeshowe

Location: Mainland, Orkney, Scotland

Coordinates: 59.0° N, 3.19° W

Period: Neolithic, c. 2800 BCE

Status: Scheduled monument; part of the Heart of Neolithic Orkney UNESCO World Heritage Site (1999)

Maeshowe (also written Maes Howe) is a Neolithic chambered cairn and passage grave near Stenness on Mainland, the largest island of Orkney in northern Scotland. Built around 2800 BCE, it is one of the finest chambered tombs in Europe, constructed with exceptional stone-masonry from enormous dressed sandstone slabs. Along with Skara Brae, the Stones of Stenness and the Ring of Brodgar, it forms part of the Heart of Neolithic Orkney World Heritage Site inscribed by UNESCO in 1999.

Architecture

The tomb sits inside a circular ditch and bank about 35 metres across. A low entrance passage some 11 metres long leads into a central chamber roughly 4.6 metres square and over 4.5 metres high, with a corbelled roof above great standing stones at the corners. Three side cells open from the chamber above floor level. The passage is aligned so that, for a few weeks around the winter solstice, the setting sun shines along it onto the back wall of the chamber — a phenomenon linked to the nearby Barnhouse Stone.

Runic inscriptions

In the twelfth century, Vikings broke into the tomb through the roof and left behind one of the largest collections of runic inscriptions found outside Scandinavia: more than thirty carved texts and drawings, including boasts about the break-in, a finely carved lion, a dragon and a knotted serpent. The inscriptions, some associated with the Orkneyinga saga's account of Earl Harald Maddadsson's men sheltering from a storm around 1153, were recorded when the tomb was excavated in 1861 by James Farrer.
