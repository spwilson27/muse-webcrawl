Nonparametric statistics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Nonparametric statistics is the branch of statistics concerned with methods that do not require data to be drawn from a population following a particular probability distribution characterized by a fixed set of parameters. Such methods are sometimes called distribution-free. They contrast with parametric methods, which assume, for example, that data are normally distributed with unknown mean and variance.

Methods

Classical nonparametric hypothesis tests are often based on ranks rather than raw values, making them robust to outliers and usable with ordinal data. Well-known examples include the Mann–Whitney U test and Wilcoxon signed-rank test (analogues of two-sample and paired t-tests), the Kruskal–Wallis test (an analogue of one-way ANOVA), and Spearman's rank correlation coefficient. Other techniques include the sign test, runs tests, permutation and bootstrap resampling, and kernel density estimation for describing distributions without assuming their shape.

Advantages and limitations

Nonparametric methods make fewer and weaker assumptions, are less sensitive to violations such as heavy tails, and apply naturally to ranked or categorical data. When parametric assumptions do hold, however, parametric tests are usually more powerful — they extract more information from the same data — and nonparametric procedures may require larger samples for equivalent precision. In modern usage, "nonparametric" also describes flexible models whose complexity grows with the data, such as spline and Gaussian-process models in machine learning.
