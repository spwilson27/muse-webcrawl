Soil liquefaction

Soil liquefaction occurs when a cohesionless soil, usually saturated sand or silt, temporarily loses strength and stiffness in response to applied stress such as earthquake shaking, causing it to behave like a viscous liquid rather than a solid. The phenomenon most often occurs in recent, saturated, loose deposits near rivers, bays, and other bodies of water.

Mechanism

In a saturated granular soil, the load is shared between the grain skeleton (effective stress) and the pore water pressure. During rapid cyclic loading such as an earthquake, loose grains tend to compact, but because the water cannot drain quickly, pore pressure rises. As pore pressure approaches the total stress, effective stress falls toward zero, the grains lose contact, and the soil loses its shear strength — it liquefies. Afterward, as pore pressures dissipate, ejected water may carry sand to the surface forming "sand boils," and the ground can settle unevenly.

Consequences

Liquefaction can cause buildings to tilt or sink, buried tanks and pipes to float upward, bridges and embankments to fail, and slopes to spread laterally toward free faces such as river channels. Famous examples include the 1964 Niigata earthquake in Japan, where apartment buildings rotated dramatically; the Marina District damage in the 1989 Loma Prieta earthquake; the 1995 Kobe earthquake; and widespread destruction in Christchurch, New Zealand, during the 2010–2011 Canterbury earthquake sequence.

Susceptibility and assessment

Susceptibility depends on soil type, density, saturation, and the intensity and duration of shaking. Geotechnical engineers assess liquefaction potential using field tests such as the standard penetration test (SPT) and cone penetration test (CPT), comparing the earthquake-induced cyclic stress ratio with the soil's cyclic resistance ratio. Soil classification and grain-size criteria help identify vulnerable deposits.

Mitigation

Mitigation methods include densifying the ground (vibro-compaction, dynamic compaction), installing stone columns or drains to relieve pore pressure, grouting, deep foundations extending to firmer strata, and designing structures to tolerate settlement. Land-use planning may restrict construction in high-risk zones.

Related phenomena

Cyclic mobility, lateral spreading, and flow failure are related ground-failure modes. Quicksand is a related condition in which upward water flow reduces effective stress. Liquefaction can also be triggered by construction vibration, blasting, and wave action.
