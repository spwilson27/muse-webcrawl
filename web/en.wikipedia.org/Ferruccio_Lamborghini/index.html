Ferruccio Lamborghini (28 April 1916 – 20 February 1993) was an Italian industrialist. Born to grape farmers in the Emilia-Romagna region, he built a successful tractor manufacturing business, Lamborghini Trattori, after the Second World War, and in 1963 founded Automobili Lamborghini in Sant'Agata Bolognese to build high-performance grand touring cars rivalling those of Ferrari. The cars bearing his name — including the Miura and Countach — became icons of Italian sports car design.

Early life

Lamborghini was born in Renazzo, a frazione of Cento in the province of Ferrara, to viticulturists Antonio and Evelina Lamborghini. Fascinated by machinery from childhood, he studied technical drawing in Bologna and worked as a mechanic. During the Second World War he served in the Italian armed forces on the island of Rhodes, where he gained wide experience maintaining and improvising repairs to military vehicles.

Tractors and business ventures

After the war, Lamborghini began converting surplus military vehicles into tractors for Italian farmers, founding Lamborghini Trattori in 1948. The company grew rapidly during Italy's postwar economic boom and became one of the country's leading agricultural machinery manufacturers. He also built successful businesses in oil burners and air conditioning (Lamborghini Bruciatori and Lamborghini Calor).

Automobili Lamborghini

A wealthy sports car enthusiast who owned Ferraris, Lamborghini — according to a famous account — complained to Enzo Ferrari about a clutch problem in his Ferrari and was dismissively rebuffed, prompting him to build a better car himself. In 1963 he founded Automobili Lamborghini, hiring talented engineers including Giotto Bizzarrini, who designed the company's long-lived V12 engine. The 350 GT of 1964 was followed in 1966 by the mid-engined Miura, widely regarded as the first supercar, and later by the Countach. Lamborghini sold controlling interests in his companies in the early 1970s amid labour unrest and the oil crisis, retiring to his estate, La Fiorita, on Lake Trasimeno, where he produced wine.

Death and legacy

Lamborghini died of a heart attack in Perugia on 20 February 1993, fifteen days after suffering a stroke. The Museo Ferruccio Lamborghini, opened by his son Tonino, preserves his cars, tractors and personal memorabilia. His rivalry with Enzo Ferrari remains one of the best-known stories in automotive history, and the bull emblem he chose — reflecting both his zodiac sign, Taurus, and his admiration for Spanish bullfighting — still marks every Lamborghini car.
