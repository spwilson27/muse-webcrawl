Pharmacoepidemiology

FieldPharmacy, epidemiology, public health
FocusUse and effects of drugs in large populations
Key methodsCohort studies, case–control studies, claims-database analyses, spontaneous-report analysis
Related fieldPharmacovigilance

Pharmacoepidemiology is the study of the use and effects of drugs in large numbers of people. It applies the methods of epidemiology to clinical pharmacology, providing estimates of the probability of beneficial effects and of the probability and magnitude of adverse effects of medicines as they are actually used in real-world populations.

Scope

Clinical trials conducted before a drug is marketed typically enrol a few thousand selected patients followed for limited periods; they often cannot detect rare, delayed, or population-specific harms. Pharmacoepidemiology fills this gap after approval, investigating drug safety signals, comparative effectiveness, prescribing patterns, adherence, drug interactions, and the effects of medicines in groups underrepresented in trials, such as pregnant women, children, and the elderly.

Methods and data sources

Researchers use observational designs — cohort, case–control, case-crossover, and self-controlled case series — together with methods to control confounding, especially confounding by indication and protopathic bias. Data come from health-insurance claims, electronic health records, disease and exposure registries, and prescription databases; major resources include the US Sentinel System, Medicare and Medicaid data, UK primary care databases such as CPRD, and Scandinavian national registries. Spontaneous adverse-event reporting systems (such as FDA's FAERS and WHO's VigiBase) are analysed with disproportionality methods for signal detection.

History

The field crystallized in the 1970s and 1980s, spurred by drug-safety crises including thalidomide and the recognition of harms from practolol and benoxaprofen, and by the growth of computerized pharmacy records. The International Society for Pharmacoepidemiology was founded in 1989.

Applications

Pharmacoepidemiologic evidence has led to label changes and withdrawals (for example, cerivastatin and rofecoxib), informs regulator-mandated post-authorization safety studies, and is used by payers and guideline bodies to compare real-world effectiveness of competing therapies.
