Henry Louis Aaron (5 February 1934 – 22 January 2021), nicknamed "Hammerin' Hank", was an American professional baseball right fielder and designated hitter who played 23 seasons in Major League Baseball, from 1954 through 1976. He is widely regarded as one of the greatest baseball players in history, and is best known for breaking Babe Ruth's career home run record, finishing with 755 home runs — a mark he held for 33 years.

Early life and career

Aaron was born in Mobile, Alabama, and grew up in poverty, teaching himself to hit by swinging at bottle caps. After a brief stint in the Negro American League with the Indianapolis Clowns, he signed with the Boston Braves organisation in 1952 and reached the majors with the Milwaukee Braves in 1954. He won the National League batting title in 1956 and was named the league's Most Valuable Player in 1957, the year the Braves won the World Series over the New York Yankees.

The home run chase

Through two decades of remarkably consistent excellence — he hit at least 24 home runs in 19 consecutive seasons and was an All-Star every year from 1955 to 1975 — Aaron closed on Ruth's record of 714. The pursuit brought racist hate mail and death threats, which he kept and later spoke about publicly. On 8 April 1974, in Atlanta, he hit his 715th home run off the Los Angeles Dodgers' Al Downing. Aaron hit his 755th and final home run in 1976 with the Milwaukee Brewers. His records for runs batted in (2,297) and total bases (6,856) still stand, and excluding his home runs he would still have more than 3,000 hits.

Later life

After retiring, Aaron worked as a senior executive with the Atlanta Braves and Turner Broadcasting, became a successful businessman and civil rights advocate, and supported educational causes through the Hank Aaron Chasing the Dream Foundation. He was inducted into the Baseball Hall of Fame in 1982 and received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 2002. Barry Bonds surpassed his home run total in 2007. Aaron died in Atlanta on 22 January 2021, aged 86, and was widely memorialised across American sports and public life.
