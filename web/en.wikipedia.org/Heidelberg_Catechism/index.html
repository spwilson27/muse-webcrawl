The Heidelberg Catechism (1563) is a Protestant confessional document taking the form of a series of questions and answers (a catechism) used for teaching Reformed Christian doctrine. It is one of the most influential Reformed catechisms and, together with the Belgic Confession and the Canons of Dort, forms the Three Forms of Unity of the Dutch Reformed tradition.

Origin

Frederick III, Elector Palatine — a devout Calvinist ruling a largely Lutheran territory — commissioned the catechism to provide a unified, irenic statement of Reformed teaching for his realm and to settle theological disputes. The principal author was Zacharias Ursinus, a young professor at the University of Heidelberg, working with the court preacher Caspar Olevianus and a team of theologians and ministers. It was published in German in January 1563, with Latin editions following.

Structure and content

The catechism contains 129 questions and answers, arranged for instruction across the 52 Sundays ("Lord's Days") of the year. Its famous first question asks, "What is your only comfort in life and in death?", and answers that the Christian belongs, body and soul, to Jesus Christ. The material is organised around three parts — human misery (sin), deliverance (redemption in Christ), and gratitude (the obedient life, including expositions of the Apostles' Creed, the Ten Commandments, the Lord's Prayer and the sacraments) — a pattern echoing the structure of Paul's Epistle to the Romans. Its tone is notably warm and devotional compared with other confessional documents of the era, though its eightieth question, on the Roman Catholic Mass (added in later editions), is sharply polemical.

Influence

Translated into many languages and carried worldwide by Dutch, German and Swiss Reformed churches and their missions, the Heidelberg Catechism remains in regular use for catechetical preaching — including Sunday afternoon "catechism services" — and instruction in Reformed churches today.
