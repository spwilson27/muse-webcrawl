Nominalism

In metaphysics, nominalism is the view that universals and abstract objects do not really exist other than as names or labels. There are at least two main versions of nominalism. One denies the existence of universals — things that can be instantiated or exemplified by particulars. The other denies the existence of abstract objects — things that do not exist in space and time.

Problem of universals

The problem asks what red things share in common. Realists, following Plato, answer that they instantiate the universal redness. Nominalists such as the medieval philosopher William of Ockham replied that only particulars exist; general terms are signs (nomina) we use to group resemblances. Ockham's razor — do not multiply entities beyond necessity — became a guiding nominalist principle. Moderate positions include conceptualism, which locates universals in the mind.

Modern nominalism

In contemporary philosophy, nominalists attempt to paraphrase away apparent reference to numbers, sets and other abstracta in mathematics and science; notable programs include those of Nelson Goodman and W. V. O. Quine. The indispensability argument — that mathematics indispensable to science quantifies over abstract objects — remains the main realist rejoinder.
