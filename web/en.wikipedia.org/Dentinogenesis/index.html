Dentinogenesis

Dentinogenesis is the formation of dentin, a substance that forms the bulk of the tooth. Dentinogenesis is performed by odontoblasts, which are a special type of biological cell on the outer wall of the dental pulp. Dentin formation begins before enamel formation (amelogenesis) and proceeds in rhythmical increments throughout the development of the tooth.

Process

During the bell stage of tooth development, cells of the dental papilla adjacent to the inner enamel epithelium differentiate into odontoblasts. These cells first secrete an unmineralized matrix called predentin, composed largely of type I collagen and other proteins such as dentin sialophosphoprotein. The predentin then mineralizes as hydroxyapatite crystals are deposited, converting it to dentin. As odontoblasts retreat toward the pulp, they leave behind slender processes within fine channels — the dentinal tubules — that characterize dentin structure.

Types of dentin

Primary dentin forms the bulk of the tooth before root completion. Secondary dentin is deposited slowly throughout life after the tooth is formed, gradually reducing the size of the pulp chamber. Tertiary (reparative or reactive) dentin forms locally in response to stimuli such as caries, attrition, or dental procedures, protecting the pulp.

Disorders

Defects of dentinogenesis occur in hereditary conditions: dentinogenesis imperfecta, in which dentin is discolored, weak, and prone to rapid wear, and dentin dysplasia. Dentinogenesis imperfecta type I is associated with osteogenesis imperfecta; other types are linked to mutations in the DSPP gene.
