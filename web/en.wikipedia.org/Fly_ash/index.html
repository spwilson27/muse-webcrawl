Fly ash

Fly ash, also known as pulverised fuel ash in the United Kingdom, is a coal combustion product composed of the particulates driven out of coal-fired boilers together with the flue gases. It is collected by electrostatic precipitators or fabric filters before the flue gases reach the chimneys. Depending upon the source and composition of the coal, fly ash consists mostly of silicon dioxide, aluminium oxide, and calcium oxide.

Classes

Under ASTM C618, fly ash is divided into Class F, produced from anthracite and bituminous coals and pozzolanic in character, and Class C, from sub-bituminous and lignite coals, which is both pozzolanic and somewhat cementitious because of its higher calcium content.

Uses

Because of its pozzolanic properties, fly ash is widely used as a supplementary cementitious material in concrete, replacing a portion of Portland cement. It improves workability, reduces water demand and heat of hydration, and increases long-term strength and durability. Fly ash is also used in road bases, structural fills, bricks, and as a filler in asphalt and plastics. Reusing it diverts a major industrial by-product from landfill and cuts the carbon footprint of concrete.

Environmental concerns

Fly ash contains trace concentrations of heavy metals and other elements, and its storage in ash ponds has caused major contamination incidents, such as the 2008 Kingston Fossil Plant spill in Tennessee. Regulations in many countries govern its disposal and beneficial use to protect groundwater.
