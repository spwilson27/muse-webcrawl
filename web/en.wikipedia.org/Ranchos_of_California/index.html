Ranchos of California

The ranchos of California were large land grants made by the Spanish and Mexican governments between 1784 and 1846 in Alta California. More than 800 grants were claimed, and about 570 ranchos were ultimately confirmed, covering some 8.8 million acres. After the secularisation of the California missions in the 1830s, rancho grants formed the basis of a pastoral economy dominated by cattle raising for hides and tallow, and their names survive in countless modern California place names.

Spanish period

The first concessions were granted in 1784, including Rancho San Pedro and Rancho San Rafael. Under Spanish rule grants were provisional concessions of grazing rights near the presidios and missions, while legal title remained with the Crown and most land was controlled by the missions.

Mexican period

After Mexican independence (1821) and especially after the Secularization Act of 1833, mission lands were distributed as ranchos to prominent Californio families and settlers. Governors made grants typically of one to eleven square leagues, recorded with hand-drawn maps called diseños. Rancho society centred on the hacienda, vaqueros, and the hide-and-tallow trade with foreign ships.

After the US conquest

The Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo (1848) promised to respect Mexican property rights, but the California Land Act of 1851 required grantees to prove their claims before a Land Commission, a lengthy and costly process conducted in English under unfamiliar law. Although most claims were eventually confirmed, legal fees, taxes and the great drought of the 1860s forced many Californio families to sell. Surveys were fixed in federal patents, and rancho boundaries shaped later property lines, roads and city layouts.

Legacy

Names such as Los Angeles-area ranchos (Los Feliz, San Fernando, Santa Anita) survive in cities, suburbs and streets across California. Many modern communities — from Beverly Hills to San Jose suburbs — trace their footprints to rancho boundaries.
