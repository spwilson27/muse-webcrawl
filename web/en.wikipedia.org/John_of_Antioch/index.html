John of Antioch

John of Antioch was a Byzantine chronicler of the 7th century.

John of Antioch (Greek: Ἰωάννης Ἀντιοχεύς) was a Byzantine chronicler who lived in the 7th century AD, during the reign of the emperor Heraclius. He was a monk, apparently of limited education, and is thought to have been a contemporary of the chronicler John Malalas, from whom he is sometimes difficult to distinguish.

Chronicle

John wrote a universal chronicle, the Historia chronike, extending from Adam to the death of the emperor Phocas in 610. The work survives only in fragments, preserved in later compilations such as the Excerpta Constantiniana commissioned by Constantine VII, and in excerpts made by later writers.

Sources and significance

For the earlier period John drew on Sextus Julius Africanus, Eusebius and other chronographers, while for the 5th and 6th centuries he used sources such as Priscus and John Malalas. His fragments are valuable to historians because they sometimes preserve material from lost works, particularly concerning the history of the late Roman Republic and the early empire, where he appears to depend on good sources for events such as the career of Herod the Great and the reigns of the Julio-Claudian emperors.

Identity

Scholars have debated whether John of Antioch is identical with John the Rhetor or with the Syriac patriarch John of Sedre, but the chronicler is now generally treated as a distinct figure. Modern critical editions of the fragments, notably that of Umberto Roberto (2005), have clarified the transmission of the text.
