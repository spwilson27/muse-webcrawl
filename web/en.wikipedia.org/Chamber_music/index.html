Chamber music is a form of classical music composed for a small group of instruments — traditionally a group that could fit in a palace chamber or a large room. Most broadly, it is any music written for a small ensemble with one player to a part, in contrast to orchestral music, in which sections of string instruments are doubled. Because of its intimate nature, chamber music has been described as "the music of friends," and for more than a century amateur performance in the home was a central part of its culture.

History

Chamber music's roots lie in the consort and trio-sonata music of the Baroque era. Joseph Haydn effectively created the modern string quartet (two violins, viola, cello) in the mid-18th century, establishing a format that Mozart, Beethoven, and Schubert raised to the highest artistic level; Beethoven's late quartets are often regarded as among the greatest works in Western music. The 19th century added masterworks by Mendelssohn, Schumann, Brahms, and Dvořák, while the 20th century saw radical expansions of the medium by Debussy, Bartók (six quartets), and Shostakovich (fifteen quartets), as well as mixed and experimental ensembles.

Common ensembles

  • Duo — e.g., violin and piano sonatas
  • Piano trio — piano, violin, cello
  • String trio — violin, viola, cello
  • String quartet — two violins, viola, cello
  • Piano quintet — piano plus string quartet
  • Wind quintet — flute, oboe, clarinet, horn, bassoon
  • Clarinet quintet, string quintet, octet, septet — and many mixed combinations

Performance practice

Chamber ensembles normally perform without a conductor, coordinating through listening and visual cues, with leadership shared or exercised by the first violinist or another designated player. The repertoire's emphasis on dialogue and equality among parts distinguishes it from concertos and symphonies. Major professional ensembles — historically such as the Amadeus and Guarneri quartets, and today groups like the Kronos and Ébène quartets — anchor a large international concert and recording culture.
