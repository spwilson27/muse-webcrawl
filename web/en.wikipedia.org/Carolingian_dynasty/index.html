Carolingian dynasty

The Carolingian dynasty was a Frankish noble family named after Charlemagne (Latin: Carolus Magnus), which rose to rule the Franks as mayors of the palace, then as kings from 751, and as emperors from 800 until the late ninth and tenth centuries.

Origins

The family descended from the union of the Arnulfing and Pippinid clans of Austrasia. Pepin of Herstal and his son Charles Martel made the office of mayor of the palace effectively hereditary and dominant; Charles Martel's victory at Tours in 732 checked Umayyad expansion into Francia. In 751 Pepin the Short deposed the last Merovingian king and took the crown with papal approval.

Height

Pepin's son Charlemagne (r. 768–814) conquered Lombard Italy, Saxony, and Bavaria, and was crowned emperor by Pope Leo III on Christmas Day 800. His reign saw the Carolingian Renaissance, a revival of learning, manuscript production, and administrative reform. The empire was divided among his grandsons by the Treaty of Verdun (843) into West, Middle, and East Francia.

Decline

Repeated partitions, civil wars, and Viking, Magyar, and Saracen invasions weakened Carolingian rule. The dynasty died out in East Francia in 911 and was finally replaced in West Francia by the Capetians in 987, though Carolingian branches persisted locally, such as the counts of Vermandois.
