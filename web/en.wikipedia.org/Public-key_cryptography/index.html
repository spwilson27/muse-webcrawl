Public-key cryptography

Public-key cryptography, also called asymmetric cryptography, is the field of cryptographic systems that use pairs of related keys — a public key that may be disseminated widely and a private key kept secret by its owner. Data encrypted with a public key can only be decrypted with the corresponding private key, and a digital signature created with a private key can be verified by anyone with the public key. First publicly described by Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman in 1976, it underpins modern secure communication on the Internet.

How it works

Public-key algorithms rely on mathematical problems believed to be easy in one direction but infeasible to reverse: integer factorisation (RSA), discrete logarithms (Diffie–Hellman, ElGamal, ECC), and lattice problems (post-quantum candidates). Because asymmetric operations are slow, practical systems are hybrid: a symmetric session key is exchanged asymmetrically and then used for bulk encryption.

History

In 1970, James Ellis at Britain's GCHQ conceived "non-secret encryption", and Clifford Cocks and Malcolm Williamson developed equivalents of RSA and Diffie–Hellman by 1974, though this remained classified until 1997. In the open literature, Diffie and Hellman's 1976 paper "New Directions in Cryptography" and the 1977 RSA algorithm (Rivest, Shamir, Adleman) launched the field. Lattice-based schemes standardised by NIST in 2024 aim to resist attacks by quantum computers, which could break RSA and ECC via Shor's algorithm.
