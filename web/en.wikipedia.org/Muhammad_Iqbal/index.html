Muhammad Iqbal

Sir Muhammad Iqbal (9 November 1877 – 21 April 1938) was a South Asian Muslim philosopher, poet and politician whose poetry in Urdu and Persian is regarded among the greatest of the modern era. Widely known as Allama Iqbal ("the great scholar"), he is honoured as the national poet of Pakistan and is credited with inspiring the Pakistan Movement.

Life

Born in Sialkot, Punjab, British India, Iqbal studied at Government College Lahore and later at Cambridge and Munich, where he earned a doctorate in philosophy with a dissertation on Persian metaphysics. Called to the bar in London, he returned to Lahore to practise law and teach, while his poetry — including the patriotic Tarana-e-Hind ("Sare Jahan se Accha") — made him famous across India.

Works and philosophy

His Persian collections Asrar-i-Khudi (The Secrets of the Self, 1915) and Rumuz-i-Bekhudi developed his philosophy of khudi, or selfhood: the cultivation of the individual ego through love, action and faith. His Urdu works include Bang-e-Dara, Bal-e-Jibril and Zarb-e-Kalim. His prose lectures, published as The Reconstruction of Religious Thought in Islam (1930), sought a modern philosophical reformulation of Islam. In his 1930 presidential address to the All-India Muslim League at Allahabad, Iqbal proposed a consolidated Muslim state in north-western India — a vision later invoked as the intellectual foundation of Pakistan. He died in Lahore in 1938 and is buried beside the Badshahi Mosque.
