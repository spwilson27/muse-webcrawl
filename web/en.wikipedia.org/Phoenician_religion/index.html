Phoenician Religion

Phoenician religion was the polytheistic faith of the Phoenician city-states of the ancient Levant (including Tyre, Sidon, and Byblos) and their colonies around the Mediterranean, notably Carthage. It developed from earlier Canaanite religion and shared many of its gods and rituals.

Deities

Each city had its own patron deity, typically a divine pair or triad. Prominent gods included El, the aged creator; Baal, the storm god; Astarte, goddess of love and war; Melqart, the city god of Tyre, identified by Greeks with Heracles; Eshmun, the healing god of Sidon; and, in Carthage, Baal Hammon and the goddess Tanit.

Practices

Worship centered on temples and open-air sanctuaries (high places), with offerings of food, animals, and votive objects. Greek and Roman sources, and biblical texts, accuse the Phoenicians and Carthaginians of child sacrifice at precincts called tophets, where urns containing cremated infant remains have been excavated; scholars continue to debate the interpretation of this evidence.

Legacy

Phoenician religion influenced and was shaped by neighboring cultures, and its mythology survives mainly through Ugaritic parallels, inscriptions, and hostile or fragmentary classical accounts such as the fragments of Sanchuniathon preserved by Philo of Byblos.
