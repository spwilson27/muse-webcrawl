Tusk

Elephant tusks are elongated incisor teeth

Tusks are elongated, continuously growing front teeth that protrude well beyond the mouth of certain mammal species. They are most commonly canine teeth, as with narwhals, warthogs, and walruses, or, in the case of elephants, elongated incisors. Tusks share common features such as extra-oral position, growth pattern, composition and structure, and lack of enamel.

Structure and growth

Tusks consist mainly of dentine, the same material as the inner part of ordinary teeth. In elephants, tusks are the upper second incisors; they grow throughout life at roughly 17 cm (7 in) per year and can exceed 3 metres in length. The visible portion of an elephant's tusk is only part of its length — the rest is embedded in the skull. Walrus tusks are upper canines that can reach a metre long and are used for hauling out onto ice and for social display. The narwhal's tusk is a spiralled left canine that projects like a horn.

Function

Tusks serve various functions depending on the species: digging and foraging (pigs, warthogs), defence and combat between rivals, display and dominance signalling, and sensing the environment — the narwhal's tusk is richly innervated and may function as a sensory organ.

Ivory trade

Elephant tusks are the principal source of ivory, long prized for carving. Demand for ivory drove catastrophic poaching of African and Asian elephants, leading to the 1989 CITES ban on the international ivory trade; illegal poaching remains a major conservation threat.
