The Lipovans (Romanian: Lipoveni; Russian: Липоване) are an ethnic and religious group of Russian Old Believers living principally in the Danube Delta region of Romania, in neighbouring parts of Ukraine (Budjak and the Kiliya district), and in smaller communities in Moldova and Bulgaria. They descend from Russians who fled religious persecution in the Russian Empire after the schism (Raskol) of the 17th century.

History

Following the liturgical reforms of Patriarch Nikon (1653–1666) and the ensuing schism of the Russian Orthodox Church, the Old Believers — who rejected the reforms and retained the old rites, including the two-fingered sign of the cross and the spelling of Jesus's name with one letter fewer — suffered persecution. Many fled to the peripheries of the empire and beyond its borders. From the late 17th and especially the 18th century, settlements were established in the marshy Danube Delta, then under Ottoman rule, where the newcomers gained a reputation as fishermen, market gardeners and boatmen. The name "Lipovan" is often explained as deriving from the linden groves (lipa) of their settlements, or from Filippovka and the "Filippovtsy" sect, though the etymology is disputed.

Culture and religion

Most Lipovans belong to the priestly branch of the Old Believers; in 1846 many accepted a hierarchy established at Bila Krynytsia (Fântâna Albă) in Bukovina, forming the basis of the Russian Orthodox Old-Rite Church, whose Romanian metropolitan see is at Brăila. Traditional customs long included beards for men (shaving being sinful), distinctive dress, icon corners in homes, and the use of the lestovka prayer rope. Their Russian dialect preserves archaic features and Church Slavonic-influenced vocabulary, and the community has its own Russian-language schools, newspapers and cultural associations in Romania, where Lipovans are a recognised national minority with a guaranteed seat in the Romanian parliament (held by their community organisation).

Economic life historically centred on fishing — including the famous Danube sturgeon fisheries — reed harvesting and small-scale agriculture around villages such as Sarichioi, Jurilovca, Slava Cercheză and, on the Ukrainian side, Vylkove ("the Venice of Ukraine"), where canals serve as streets. Since 1989, emigration and assimilation have reduced the community, estimated at some tens of thousands in Romania.
