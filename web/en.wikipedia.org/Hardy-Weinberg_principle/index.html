Hardy–Weinberg principle

FieldPopulation genetics
Proposed1908
Proposed byG. H. Hardy; Wilhelm Weinberg
Equationp² + 2pq + q² = 1

The Hardy–Weinberg principle, also known as the Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium, states that allele and genotype frequencies in a population will remain constant from generation to generation in the absence of evolutionary influences. It was formulated independently in 1908 by the English mathematician G. H. Hardy and the German physician Wilhelm Weinberg.

The model

For a gene with two alleles A (frequency p) and a (frequency q), with p + q = 1, the expected genotype frequencies under random mating are AA = p², Aa = 2pq, and aa = q². A population in these proportions is said to be in Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium.

Assumptions

The principle assumes no mutation, no natural selection, no gene flow, infinite population size (no genetic drift), and random mating. Departures from equilibrium indicate that one or more of these forces is acting, making the principle a null model for microevolution.

Applications

The principle is used to estimate carrier frequencies of recessive genetic disorders, in forensic DNA analysis, and as a quality-control check in genome-wide association studies.
