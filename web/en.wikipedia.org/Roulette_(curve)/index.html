Roulette (curve)

An ellipse rolling on a line; a fixed point traces a roulette

In the differential geometry of curves, a roulette is a curve traced by a point attached to one curve (the generator or pole) as it rolls without slipping along a second, fixed curve. The term derives from the French roulette, "little wheel". Roulettes generalise many classical curves and were studied from the 17th century onward in connection with cycloidal motion and gear teeth.

Examples

If the rolling and fixed curves are both straight lines, the roulette is an involute-type curve; the best-known case is the cycloid, generated by a point on the rim of a circle rolling along a straight line. A circle rolling outside another circle generates an epicycloid (with the cardioid and nephroid as special cases), while a circle rolling inside another generates a hypocycloid (including the deltoid and astroid). A point on a line rolling against a circle generates an involute of the circle, used in gear tooth profiles.

Other named roulettes include the trochoids (the point not on the rim of the rolling circle), the catenary—which can be generated as the roulette of a parabola's focus rolling along a line—and glissettes, curves traced by a point on a line segment whose endpoints slide along two fixed curves.

Properties

Roulettes can be described parametrically from the rolling condition: the arc lengths travelled along the two curves are equal. Many of their properties—arc length, area, curvature—follow elegantly from this setup; for example, the area under one arch of a cycloid is three times the area of the generating circle, a result proved by Roberval. Families of roulettes generated by conics rolling on lines or on equal conics (the "Delaunay roulettes") are related to the profiles of surfaces of constant mean curvature.

Applications

Roulette curves underlie the design of cycloidal gears, rotary engines and cam profiles, and appear in spirograph toys. The cycloid's isochronous and brachistochrone properties made roulettes central to early calculus and the calculus of variations.
