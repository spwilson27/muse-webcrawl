BB84

BB84 is a quantum key distribution protocol developed by Charles Bennett and Gilles Brassard in 1984. It was the first quantum cryptography protocol, and is provably secure against eavesdropping when implemented with an authenticated classical channel.

Protocol

In BB84, the sender (Alice) prepares qubits — typically photons — each randomly encoded in one of two conjugate bases (rectilinear or diagonal polarisation), representing bits 0 and 1. The receiver (Bob) measures each incoming qubit in a randomly chosen basis. Afterwards, Alice and Bob publicly compare the bases they used for each position, discarding the results where their bases disagreed; the remaining matching bits form the sifted key.

Security

The security of BB84 rests on the no-cloning theorem and the fact that measuring a qubit in the wrong basis disturbs its state. Any eavesdropper (Eve) who intercepts and resends the qubits inevitably introduces errors, which Alice and Bob can detect by sacrificing a random sample of their sifted key and comparing it. If the error rate is below a threshold, they apply error correction and privacy amplification to obtain a shorter, secret key; otherwise they abort.

Legacy

BB84 inspired a family of related protocols, including the six-state protocol and decoy-state methods, and remains the reference design for practical quantum key distribution systems.
