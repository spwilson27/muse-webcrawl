Cacaxtla

Cacaxtla is an archaeological site in the Mexican state of Tlaxcala, near the border with Puebla. It was the capital of a polity controlled by the Olmeca-Xicalanca people and flourished in the Epiclassic period, roughly AD 650–900, after the decline of Teotihuacan, when it dominated the Puebla-Tlaxcala valley from a fortified hilltop.

The murals

Cacaxtla is famous for its exceptionally well-preserved wall paintings, discovered in 1975 when looters exposed them. Painted in a style strongly resembling Classic Maya art—with vivid Maya blue pigment, naturalistic figures and calendrical glyphs—the murals cover the walls of the ceremonial precinct known as the Great Basement. The most famous, the Battle Mural, depicts a violent clash between warriors dressed as jaguars and others in bird regalia; other murals show figures identified as the Old God of Commerce, maize and cacao plants, and water symbolism. Their hybrid style is key evidence for long-distance contacts across Mesoamerica in the Epiclassic.

A protective roof now shelters the Great Basement. Together with the neighbouring hilltop site of Xochitécatl, Cacaxtla is administered by Mexico's INAH.
