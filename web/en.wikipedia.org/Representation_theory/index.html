Representation theory

Representation theory is a branch of mathematics that studies abstract algebraic structures by representing their elements as linear transformations of vector spaces, and studies modules over these abstract algebraic structures. In essence, a representation makes an abstract algebraic object more concrete by describing its elements in terms of matrices and algebraic operations. The theory applies to groups, associative algebras and Lie algebras, among others.

Definitions

A representation of a group G on a vector space V is a group homomorphism from G to GL(V), the group of invertible linear operators on V. A representation is irreducible if V has no proper nonzero invariant subspaces. For finite groups over the complex numbers, Maschke's theorem guarantees that every representation is completely reducible — a direct sum of irreducibles — and the theory of characters (traces of the representing matrices), developed by Frobenius and Schur, completely classifies such representations.

Branches

The representation theory of finite groups is the oldest branch, with applications in crystallography and quantum mechanics. The theory for Lie groups and Lie algebras — including the classification of irreducible representations of semisimple Lie algebras via highest weights, due to Élie Cartan and Hermann Weyl — underlies particle physics, where particles are classified as irreducible representations of symmetry groups such as SU(3) (the "Eightfold Way"). Modular representation theory studies representations over fields of positive characteristic, connected to the classification of finite simple groups.

Applications

Beyond mathematics, representation theory is fundamental in physics (angular momentum and spin in quantum mechanics, gauge theories, the Standard Model) and in chemistry (molecular symmetry and spectroscopy). In number theory, the Langlands program links representations of Galois groups and Lie groups in a vast conjectural web.

History

The subject began with Frobenius's work on group characters in 1896–97, Burnside's treatise of 1904, and Schur's dissertation of 1901. Emmy Noether's module-theoretic viewpoint unified group representations with the representation theory of algebras in the 1920s, and George Mackey extended the theory to infinite-dimensional unitary representations.
