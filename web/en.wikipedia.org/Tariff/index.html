Tariff

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A tariff is a tax imposed by the government of a country or customs territory on imports or exports of goods. Besides being a source of revenue, import duties can also be a form of regulation of foreign trade and policy that taxes foreign products to encourage or safeguard domestic industry. Tariffs are among the oldest and most widely used instruments of trade policy, along with import quotas.

Types

An ad valorem tariff is charged as a percentage of the value of the imported good, whereas a specific tariff is a fixed charge per unit; compound tariffs combine both. Tariffs may be imposed for revenue, to protect infant industries, or as retaliation in trade disputes. Export tariffs are less common but are used by some countries on raw materials.

Economic effects

Economists generally find that tariffs raise prices for domestic consumers, create deadweight losses, and benefit protected producers and the government at the expense of consumers, though in a "large" economy a tariff can improve the terms of trade. Under the rules of the World Trade Organization and its predecessor the GATT, member states have bound their tariff rates and negotiated successive rounds of reductions since 1947; preferential tariffs apply within free trade areas and customs unions.

History

Tariffs were the principal source of federal revenue in the early United States, and fierce political battles were fought over measures such as the 1828 "Tariff of Abominations" and the Smoot–Hawley Tariff Act of 1930, widely blamed for deepening the Great Depression.
