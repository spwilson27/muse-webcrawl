Sense of balance

The sense of balance, or equilibrioception, is the sense by which organisms perceive acceleration, orientation with respect to gravity, and their own position in space, enabling them to keep their balance and move without falling. In humans it arises primarily from the vestibular system of the inner ear, integrated in the brainstem and cerebellum with visual and proprioceptive information.

Mechanism

The vestibular apparatus contains two kinds of sensors. The three semicircular canals, arranged roughly at right angles, detect angular acceleration: when the head rotates, endolymph fluid lags and bends the cupula over hair cells in the ampulla. The otolith organs — the utricle and saccule — detect linear acceleration and gravity, using small calcium carbonate crystals (otoconia) resting on a gelatinous membrane over hair cells. Signals travel via the vestibular nerve to the vestibular nuclei, which drive reflexes such as the vestibulo-ocular reflex that stabilises gaze during head movement.

Integration and disorders

Balance is multisensory: the brain combines vestibular signals with vision and proprioception from muscles and joints. Conflicts between these inputs produce motion sickness; damage to the vestibular organs produces vertigo, nystagmus, and unsteadiness. Clinical conditions affecting balance include benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV), Ménière's disease, vestibular neuritis, and labyrinthitis. Balance also declines with age, contributing to falls in the elderly; balance training and vestibular rehabilitation can improve it.

In other animals

Most vertebrates have a comparable vestibular system. Invertebrates use different organs — for example, many crustaceans and insects use statocysts, fluid-filled sacs containing a dense statolith — while flies use their halteres as gyroscopes.
