Siegfried (German: Siegfried; Old Norse: Sigurd) is a legendary hero of Germanic heroic legend, who killed a dragon — in the Norse tradition named Fafnir — and was later murdered. It is possible he was inspired by one or more figures from the Frankish Merovingian dynasty, with Sigebert I being the most popular contender. Old Norse texts depict Siegfried/Sigurd coming from a line of kings called the Völsungs.

In the German Nibelungenlied (c. 1200), Siegfried is a prince of Xanten who wins the hand of the Burgundian princess Kriemhild, helps King Gunther win the Icelandic queen Brunhild through deception, and is later treacherously murdered by the vassal Hagen, setting in motion Kriemhild's terrible revenge.

The legend in German tradition

Siegfried arrives at the Burgundian court at Worms and falls in love with Kriemhild. To win her, he assists her brother Gunther in wooing Brunhild, using a cloak of invisibility to perform the feats of strength required of Brunhild's suitor, and later to subdue her on the wedding night. Years later, a quarrel between Kriemhild and Brunhild over precedence leads Hagen to plot Siegfried's death. Because Siegfried bathed in the dragon's blood, his skin became invulnerable — except for one spot between his shoulders where a linden leaf fell. Kriemhild unwittingly reveals the spot, and Hagen spears Siegfried while he drinks at a spring during a hunt. Siegfried's treasure, the hoard of the Nibelungs, is later sunk in the Rhine by Hagen.

The Norse tradition

In the Poetic Edda and Völsunga saga, Sigurd, fostered by the smith Regin, reforges his father Sigmund's sword Gram, slays the dragon Fafnir, and — having tasted the dragon's blood — understands the speech of birds, who warn him of Regin's treachery. He roasts and eats Fafnir's heart, rides through a ring of fire to awaken the valkyrie Brynhildr, and later, having been given a potion of forgetfulness, marries Gudrun and helps her brother Gunnar win Brynhildr by shape-exchange. Brynhildr, discovering the deception, demands Sigurd's death; he is murdered in his sleep, and Brynhildr kills herself to be burned beside him.

Origins and interpretation

Scholars have long sought historical kernels for the legend, proposing connections to the Merovingian king Sigebert I (murdered in 575), to Arminius, or to the Burgundian kingdom's destruction by the Huns in 437. The dragon fight and the vulnerable spot have been compared to other Indo-European heroic motifs. Jacob Grimm and later scholars treated the Siegfried material as a central monument of Germanic mythology.

Legacy

Richard Wagner made Siegfried the hero of the third opera of his Ring cycle, Siegfried (premiered 1876), drawing mainly on the Norse sources; his youthful, fearless hero also shapes the final opera's catastrophe. Siegfried imagery — dragon-slaying, the Rhine gold — permeated German nationalism in the 19th and early 20th centuries: the "stab-in-the-back myth" after the First World War was explicitly compared to Hagen's murder of Siegfried, and the Siegfried Line (Westwall) bore his name. Fritz Lang's film Die Nibelungen (1924) fixed a classic visual image of the hero.
