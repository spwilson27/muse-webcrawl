Continental drift

The continents were once joined as the supercontinent Pangaea.

Continental drift is a geological hypothesis that Earth's continents have moved over geologic time relative to each other, thus appearing to have "drifted" across the ocean floor. The theory was proposed in 1912 by the German meteorologist Alfred Wegener, who marshalled evidence that the continents had once formed a single landmass, Pangaea, which began breaking apart about 200 million years ago.

Evidence

Wegener cited the fit of the coastlines of South America and Africa, matching fossil assemblages (such as the freshwater reptile Mesosaurus and the plant Glossopteris) on now-separated continents, and matching mountain ranges and glacial deposits. Earlier mapmakers, including Abraham Ortelius (1596) and Francis Bacon, had remarked on the Atlantic fit, and Alexander von Humboldt and Eduard Suess developed related ideas.

Rejection and revival

The hypothesis was rejected by most geologists because Wegener could offer no plausible driving mechanism. It was revived in the 1950s–60s by palaeomagnetic studies and the discovery of seafloor spreading at mid-ocean ridges (Harry Hess, Robert Dietz), which culminated in the unifying theory of plate tectonics — of which continental drift is a central element.
