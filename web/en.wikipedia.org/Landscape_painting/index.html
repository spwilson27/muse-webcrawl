Landscape painting, also known as landscape art, is the depiction of natural scenery such as mountains, valleys, trees, rivers, and forests, especially where the main subject is a wide view, with its elements arranged into a coherent composition. The word "landscape" entered English from the Dutch landschap in the early 17th century, when Dutch painters made the genre a specialty.

History

Landscape elements appeared in art from antiquity — in Egyptian tomb paintings, Roman frescoes, and as backgrounds to religious scenes — but landscape as an independent subject developed earliest in China, where the ink tradition of shan shui ("mountain-water") painting flourished from the Tang and Song dynasties with a spiritual, meditative purpose. In Europe, landscape backgrounds grew richer in the Renaissance (notably in the work of Joachim Patinir and Albrecht Altdorfer's Danube school), and the genre achieved autonomy in the 17th-century Dutch Republic with artists such as Jacob van Ruisdael and Meindert Hobbema, while Claude Lorrain and Nicolas Poussin developed the idealized "classical" landscape in Rome.

Romanticism to modern art

In the late 18th and 19th centuries, landscape painting became a vehicle for the Sublime and for personal emotion: John Constable and J. M. W. Turner in England, Caspar David Friedrich in Germany, and the Hudson River School in the United States celebrated the grandeur of untamed nature. The Barbizon school and then the Impressionists took landscape outdoors (plein air), making it the central genre of modern painting; Post-Impressionists such as Van Gogh and Cézanne pushed it toward expressive structure, a path continued into 20th-century abstraction.
