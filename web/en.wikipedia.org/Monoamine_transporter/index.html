Monoamine transporter

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Monoamine transporters (MATs) are proteins that regulate the concentrations of monoamine neurotransmitters — serotonin, dopamine, and norepinephrine — in the synaptic cleft and extracellular space. The plasma-membrane transporters, the serotonin transporter (SERT), dopamine transporter (DAT), and norepinephrine transporter (NET), belong to the SLC6 family of sodium- and chloride-dependent transporters; they pump released transmitter back into the presynaptic neuron ("reuptake"), where it is recycled into vesicles by the vesicular monoamine transporters VMAT1 and VMAT2 or broken down by monoamine oxidase.

Pharmacology

These transporters are targets of many widely used medicines and drugs of abuse. Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) such as fluoxetine block SERT and are first-line antidepressants and anxiolytics; serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) block both SERT and NET. Cocaine blocks all three plasma-membrane transporters, while amphetamines are transporter substrates that reverse transport and flood the synapse with monoamines. VMAT2 is inhibited by reserpine and by tetrabenazine, used for hyperkinetic movement disorders.

Structure and mechanism

SLC6 transporters have twelve transmembrane helices and work by an alternating-access ("rocking bundle") mechanism, coupling transmitter movement to the sodium gradient. Crystal and cryo-EM structures of bacterial homologues and of human SERT and DAT have revealed how antidepressants and cocaine bind within the central substrate cavity, guiding drug design.
