Cauchy sequence

In mathematics, a Cauchy sequence is a sequence whose elements become arbitrarily close to each other as the sequence progresses. More precisely, given any small positive distance ε, all but a finite number of elements of the sequence lie within distance ε of each other. Cauchy sequences are named after Augustin-Louis Cauchy, who used them in his work on the foundations of analysis.

Definition

A sequence (x1, x2, x3, …) in a metric space is called a Cauchy sequence if for every positive real number ε > 0 there exists a positive integer N such that for all natural numbers m, n > N, the distance d(xm, xn) < ε.

Properties

Every convergent sequence is a Cauchy sequence, and every Cauchy sequence is bounded. In the real numbers, the converse also holds: every Cauchy sequence of real numbers converges. A metric space in which every Cauchy sequence converges to a limit within the space is called complete. The rational numbers are not complete — for example, a sequence of rationals converging to √2 is Cauchy but has no rational limit — and the standard construction of the real numbers proceeds by taking equivalence classes of Cauchy sequences of rationals.

Generalizations

The notion extends to uniform spaces, topological groups, and topological vector spaces, where Cauchy nets and Cauchy filters generalize sequences. In normed spaces, Cauchy sequences are central to the theory of Banach spaces.
