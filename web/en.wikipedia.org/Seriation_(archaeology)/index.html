Seriation (archaeology)


"Battleship curves" trace the rise and fall of artefact styles through time.

In archaeology, seriation is a relative dating method in which assemblages or artefacts from numerous sites in the same culture are placed in chronological order. Where absolute dating methods such as radiocarbon dating are unavailable or impractical, seriation can establish a sequence for sites and styles within a region. It is especially associated with the study of pottery styles and grave goods.

History

The principle was pioneered by Flinders Petrie in the 1890s, who sequenced predynastic Egyptian graves by the types of pottery they contained ("sequence dating"). In the early 20th century the method was developed in the Americas, notably by Alfred Kroeber and James A. Ford, who formalised frequency seriation for ceramic types in the Mississippi valley and elsewhere.

Approaches

Contextual seriation orders assemblages by the presence or absence and duration of artefact types, assuming that types have a characteristic lifespan. Frequency seriation plots the changing relative frequencies of types through time: a style is introduced, rises to popularity, and declines, producing lens-shaped curves often called "battleship curves". Comparing these curves across assemblages allows them to be aligned in a single chronological sequence. Later developments used statistical and computer-assisted techniques such as correspondence analysis to order assemblages automatically.

Assumptions and limitations

Seriation rests on assumptions that styles change gradually and continuously, that assemblages compared belong to one cultural tradition in one area, and that the frequency of a style follows a unimodal (single-peaked) distribution. Mixed deposits, regional variation and discontinuous change can all violate these assumptions and mislead the ordering.
