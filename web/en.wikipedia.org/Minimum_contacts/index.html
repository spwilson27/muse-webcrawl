Minimum contacts

Area of lawCivil procedure (U.S.)
Source caseInternational Shoe Co. v. Washington, 326 U.S. 310 (1945)
DoctrinePersonal jurisdiction

Minimum contacts is a doctrine of United States civil procedure governing when a court may exercise personal jurisdiction over an out-of-state defendant consistently with the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. The standard comes from the Supreme Court's 1945 decision in International Shoe Co. v. Washington, which held that a defendant not present in a state may still be sued there if it has "certain minimum contacts with it such that the maintenance of the suit does not offend traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice."

Later cases refined the test. Under Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz (1985), the defendant must have purposefully availed itself of the privilege of acting in the forum, such that it could reasonably anticipate being haled into court there; fairness factors include the burden on the defendant, the forum's interest, and the plaintiff's interest in convenient relief. The doctrine distinguishes general jurisdiction, where a defendant's affiliations are so continuous and systematic as to render it essentially at home, from specific jurisdiction, which depends on the defendant's suit-related contacts with the forum.

Applications

Minimum contacts analysis is central to litigation against national businesses and, increasingly, internet actors: courts have applied purposeful-direction tests (such as the Calder effects test) to websites, stream-of-commerce distribution, and online defamation. Cases including Ford Motor Co. v. Montana Eighth Judicial District Court (2021) continue to shape how broadly the doctrine reaches.
