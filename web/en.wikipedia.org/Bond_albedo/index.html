Bond albedo

The Bond albedo (also called the spherical albedo or planetary albedo) is the fraction of power in the total electromagnetic radiation incident on an astronomical body that is scattered back out to space. It takes into account radiation at all wavelengths and all phase angles, and is therefore the quantity that determines how much incoming energy a planet absorbs — and hence, in large part, its equilibrium temperature.

Definition

The Bond albedo A is defined as the ratio of the total radiation scattered by a body to the total radiation incident upon it. It is related to the geometric albedo p and the phase integral q by the equation A = p q. The concept was introduced by the American astronomer George Phillips Bond (1825–1865), who first attempted to determine the proportion of sunlight reflected by the Moon.

Values

Bond albedos vary enormously across the Solar System. Earth's is about 0.30 — roughly 30 percent of incoming sunlight is reflected, mainly by clouds, ice and deserts. Venus, cloaked in bright cloud, reflects about 0.75, while the Moon reflects only about 0.11. The highest known value in the Solar System belongs to Saturn's moon Enceladus, whose fresh water-ice surface gives a Bond albedo of about 0.81 (and a geometric albedo above 1). Dark asteroids may fall below 0.05.

Significance

Because absorbed sunlight drives climate, the Bond albedo is a key input to models of planetary energy balance and habitability. On Earth, changes in albedo — from melting ice caps, deforestation, or changes in cloud cover — form one of the important feedbacks in the climate system: warming that melts bright ice exposes dark ocean, which absorbs more sunlight and amplifies the warming (the ice–albedo feedback).
