Palisades Sill

The Palisades Sill is a Triassic, 200-million-year-old diabase intrusion forming the cliffs called the Palisades along the lower Hudson River in New Jersey and southeastern New York State, in the United States. It is one of the most studied igneous intrusions in the world and a classic example of a differentiated sill, in which minerals crystallised and settled during cooling to produce distinct internal layers.

Geology

The sill was intruded about 200 million years ago, near the end of the Triassic, as part of the Central Atlantic Magmatic Province associated with the breakup of the supercontinent Pangaea and the opening of the Atlantic Ocean. Magma spread horizontally between sedimentary strata of the Newark Basin and solidified into a sheet of diabase (dolerite) up to about 300 metres (1,000 feet) thick, tilted westward by later faulting.

The sill is famous for its layer of olivine-enriched rock near its base, formed as dense olivine crystals sank through the crystallising magma — one of the classic demonstrations of fractional crystallisation and gravitational differentiation in a single intrusive body.

The cliffs

Erosion has exposed the sill's edge as the Palisades, a line of steep cliffs roughly 30 km (20 miles) long and up to 165 m (540 feet) high, rising almost vertically from the Hudson's west bank. Much of the cliff line is protected within Palisades Interstate Park, created in 1900 to stop quarrying that was destroying the cliffs.
