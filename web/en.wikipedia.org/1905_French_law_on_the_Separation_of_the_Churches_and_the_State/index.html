1905 French law on the Separation of the Churches and the State

The 1905 French law on the Separation of the Churches and the State (French: Loi du 9 décembre 1905 concernant la séparation des Églises et de l'État) is a foundational law of the French Third Republic that established state secularism in France. It ended the system established by Napoleon's Concordat of 1801, under which the state recognised and funded Roman Catholicism and several other faiths, and it remains the legal basis of the French principle of laïcité.

Background

Relations between the Republic and the Catholic Church had been strained since the French Revolution. The Bloc des gauches government of Émile Combes pursued an aggressively anticlerical policy, closing religious schools and congregations; France broke diplomatic relations with the Holy See in 1904. A parliamentary commission chaired with Aristide Briand as rapporteur drafted a more moderate separation bill, which Briand steered through the Chamber of Deputies (passed 3 July 1905) and the Senate (passed 6 December 1905); it was promulgated on 9 December 1905.

Main provisions

  • Article 1 guarantees freedom of conscience and the free exercise of worship, subject only to restrictions in the interest of public order.
  • Article 2 declares that the Republic "does not recognise, pay, or subsidise any form of worship" (La République ne reconnaît, ne salarie ni ne subventionne aucun culte).
  • Church property built before 1905 was transferred to the state or communes, which must maintain it and make it available for worship; religious associations (associations cultuelles) were created to administer worship.
  • Clergy ceased to be paid by the state, although pensions were provided for older priests; chaplaincies in schools, hospitals, and prisons remained publicly funded.

Application and exceptions

The law did not apply in Alsace-Moselle, which was part of the German Empire in 1905 and retains the Concordat system to this day; exceptions also persist in French Guiana and some overseas territories. Attempts to inventory church property in 1906 provoked riots (the querelle des inventaires), and Pope Pius X condemned the law in the encyclical Vehementer Nos. The law was subsequently softened by legislation in 1907 and 1908, and in the 1920s diplomatic relations with the Vatican were restored.

Legacy

The 1905 law is the cornerstone of French secularism. The principle of laïcité was written into the constitutions of 1946 and 1958, and the law continues to frame debates over religious symbols, public funding, and the place of Islam in the Republic.
