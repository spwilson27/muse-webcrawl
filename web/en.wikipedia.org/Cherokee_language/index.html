Cherokee (Cherokee: ᏣᎳᎩ, Tsalagi) is an endangered-to-moribund Iroquoian language and the native language of the Cherokee people. Ethnologue states that there were 1,520 Cherokee speakers out of 376,000 Cherokee in 2018, while a tally by the three federally recognized Cherokee tribes in 2019 recorded about 2,100 first-language speakers. Cherokee is the only Southern Iroquoian language, and differs significantly from the Northern Iroquoian languages. It uses an 85-character syllabary invented by Sequoyah.

History

Cherokee diverged from the other Iroquoian languages around 3,500–3,800 years ago, when the Cherokee migrated south from the Great Lakes region. In 1821, Sequoyah completed the Cherokee syllabary, making the Cherokee the first Native Americans to achieve widespread literacy in their own writing system. In 1828 the bilingual newspaper Cherokee Phoenix began publication. Following forced removal on the Trail of Tears (1838–39), most speakers lived in Indian Territory (now Oklahoma), where the two main modern dialects are spoken: the Overhill dialect of Oklahoma and the Middle (Kituwah) dialect of the Eastern Band in North Carolina; the Lower dialect of South Carolina and Georgia is extinct.

Classification and dialects

Cherokee is polysynthetic, with long, complex verbs that can encode the equivalent of whole English sentences. The language distinguishes clusivity in first-person plural pronouns and has an elaborate system of pronominal prefixes. Dialects include Giduwa (Kituwah) in North Carolina and the Overhill (Otali) dialect in Oklahoma.

Syllabary

Each character of the syllabary represents a syllable, usually a consonant-vowel pair. Sequoyah, who spoke no English and could not read it, developed the system after seeing the "talking leaves" of white settlers. The syllabary's characters were adapted for printing presses, and Cherokee-language publishing flourished in the 19th century.

Status and revitalization

UNESCO classifies Cherokee as definitely endangered. Revitalization efforts include the Cherokee Nation's Durbin Feeling Language Center, immersion schools such as the Cherokee Immersion School in Tahlequah, Oklahoma, and the New Kituwah Academy in Cherokee, North Carolina, plus language apps, a Cherokee-language option in software interfaces, and master-apprentice programs training new fluent speakers.
