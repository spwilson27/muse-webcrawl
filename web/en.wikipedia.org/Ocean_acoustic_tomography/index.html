Ocean acoustic tomography

Ocean acoustic tomography
FieldPhysical oceanography, acoustics

Ocean acoustic tomography is a technique used to measure temperatures and currents over large regions of the ocean by transmitting sound pulses between instruments moored far apart and measuring their travel times. Because the speed of sound in seawater depends mainly on temperature, faster travel times indicate warmer water along the sound path.

Principle

The method, proposed by Walter Munk and Carl Wunsch in 1979, is analogous to medical computed tomography: many intersecting acoustic paths sample an ocean volume, and the travel-time measurements are inverted to reconstruct the three-dimensional temperature and current fields. Sound travels at roughly 1,500 metres per second and can propagate thousands of kilometres in the deep sound channel (SOFAR channel), where it is refracted and trapped. A temperature change of a few thousandths of a degree produces a measurable change in travel time over basin-scale paths, making the technique sensitive to ocean warming.

Experiments

Early experiments in the 1980s demonstrated mesoscale mapping over a few hundred kilometres. The Heard Island Feasibility Test of 1991 transmitted low-frequency sound from the southern Indian Ocean that was detected as far away as the coasts of North America, proving basin- and global-scale feasibility. The subsequent Acoustic Thermometry of Ocean Climate (ATOC) program used sources near Hawaii and California to monitor North Pacific temperatures over years.

Controversies and relation to other methods

Concern that intense low-frequency sound might disturb marine mammals led to protests, monitoring studies and modifications of the ATOC program, and debate over the environmental effects of active acoustics. Acoustic tomography complements the Argo float array, which profiles temperature directly but with sparse sampling, whereas tomography gives continuous averages along long paths and is particularly suited to measuring ocean heat content.
