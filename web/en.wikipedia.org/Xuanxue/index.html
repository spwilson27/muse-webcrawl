Xuanxue (Chinese: 玄學; pinyin: xuánxué; Wade–Giles: hsüan-hsüeh), literally "dark learning" or "mysterious learning", is a school of Chinese philosophy that flourished during the Wei and Jin dynasties (roughly the 3rd to 6th centuries CE). It is sometimes called Neo-Taoism, though scholars dispute how neatly it maps onto that label. Xuanxue scholars combined Taoist texts with Confucian concerns, reinterpreting the classics through metaphysical speculation.

Core ideas

Xuanxue took as its canonical sources the "Three Mysteries" (sanxuan): the Tao Te Ching, the Zhuangzi and the I Ching. Central debates concerned wu (non-being) and you (being): Wang Bi argued that non-being is the root from which all things arise, while Guo Xiang's commentary on the Zhuangzi held that things generate themselves spontaneously (zisheng) and are self-so (ziran), needing no external ground.

Another famous controversy opposed "words exhaust meaning" (yan jin yi) to "words do not exhaust meaning" (yan bu jin yi), reflecting Xuanxue's fascination with language, paradox and what lies beyond expression.

Social world

Xuanxue was bound up with the cultural practice of qingtan ("pure conversation") — elegant, witty philosophical dialogue prized at aristocratic salons — and with the reclusive ideal embodied by the Seven Sages of the Bamboo Grove, including Ruan Ji and Ji Kang, whose unconventional behaviour expressed both philosophical conviction and political self-protection in a dangerous age.

Legacy

Xuanxue shaped later Chinese readings of the Taoist classics, influenced the reception of Buddhism in China (early translators used Xuanxue vocabulary in the geyi method), and remains a key chapter in histories of Chinese metaphysics.
