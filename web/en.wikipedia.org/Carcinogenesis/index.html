Carcinogenesis

Also calledOncogenesis, tumorigenesis
ProcessMultistep accumulation of genetic and epigenetic changes
Key gene classesOncogenes, tumor suppressor genes

Carcinogenesis, also called oncogenesis or tumorigenesis, is the formation of a cancer, whereby normal cells are transformed into cancer cells. The process is characterized by changes at the cellular, genetic, and epigenetic levels and by abnormal cell division. It is generally understood as a multistep process in which successive mutations confer growth advantages that are selected over years or decades.

The multistep progression from normal tissue to invasive cancer.

Mechanisms

Cancer arises from alterations in genes that control cell growth and homeostasis. Gain-of-function mutations activate proto-oncogenes (such as RAS and MYC), which act dominantly to drive proliferation. Loss-of-function mutations disable tumor suppressor genes (such as TP53 and RB1), removing brakes on the cell cycle and apoptosis. Defects in DNA-repair ("caretaker") genes increase mutation rates, producing genomic instability. Epigenetic changes — DNA methylation and chromatin modification — can silence genes without altering the sequence.

Stages

The classical experimental model distinguishes initiation (an irreversible genetic change), promotion (clonal expansion of initiated cells, often reversible), and progression (acquisition of invasive and metastatic properties). Vogelstein's model of colorectal cancer mapped an ordered accumulation of mutations — APC loss, KRAS activation, TP53 loss and others — to the adenoma-to-carcinoma sequence. Hanahan and Weinberg's "hallmarks of cancer" summarize the capabilities acquired: sustained proliferative signalling, evasion of growth suppressors and cell death, replicative immortality, angiogenesis, invasion and metastasis, and reprogrammed metabolism.

Causes

Carcinogens include chemical agents (tobacco smoke, aflatoxin, asbestos), physical agents (ultraviolet and ionizing radiation), and biological agents (human papillomavirus, hepatitis B and C viruses, Helicobacter pylori). Inherited defects in genes such as BRCA1 explain a minority of cases; most cancers are sporadic, reflecting accumulated exposures and chance replication errors. The IARC classifies agents by strength of evidence for carcinogenicity.
