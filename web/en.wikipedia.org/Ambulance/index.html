An ambulance is a medically equipped vehicle which transports patients to treatment facilities, such as hospitals. Typically, out-of-hospital medical care is provided to the patient during transportation. Ambulances are used to respond to medical emergencies by emergency medical services (EMS), and are generally fitted with flashing warning lights and sirens. The term comes from the Latin ambulare, "to walk or move about", originally referring to the mobile field hospitals (hôpitaux ambulants) of Napoleon's armies organised by Dominique Jean Larrey.

History

The transportation of the sick and wounded by wheeled carts dates to antiquity. The modern ambulance developed from Larrey's "flying ambulances" of the 1790s through horse-drawn civilian services in the 19th century, such as Cincinnati's Commercial Hospital service of 1865 (often cited as the first civilian ambulance service in the United States) and the Metropolitan Asylums Board service in London. Motor ambulances spread during and after the First World War. The modern era of advanced pre-hospital care began in the 1960s–70s with paramedic programmes, influenced by mobile coronary care units pioneered in Belfast by Frank Pantridge.

Types

Road ambulances are conventionally classified in the United States as Type I (truck chassis with modular box), Type II (van-based), and Type III (van chassis with integrated body). European standards (EN 1789) define Types A, B, and C, from patient-transport vehicles to mobile intensive care units. Other forms include motorcycle ambulances, bicycle ambulances, air ambulances (helicopter and fixed-wing), boat ambulances, and military ambulances. In the Franco-German model of EMS, physicians may travel to the patient; in the Anglo-American model, patients are brought rapidly to hospital-based care.

Equipment and staffing

Ambulances carry stretchers, defibrillators and monitors, oxygen, airway equipment, intravenous supplies, splints, and medications. Crews typically comprise emergency medical technicians and paramedics trained to provide basic or advanced life support en route.

Markings and signalling

Many ambulances bear the Star of Life, a six-armed blue cross adopted as the symbol of EMS in the United States in the 1970s. In Europe, Battenburg markings in reflective yellow and green or blue are common. Word "ambulance" is frequently written reversed on the front of the vehicle so it reads correctly in rear-view mirrors.
