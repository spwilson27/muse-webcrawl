Wernicke encephalopathy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Wernicke encephalopathy (WE) is an acute neurological condition caused by deficiency of thiamine (vitamin B1). It is characterised classically by a triad of confusion, ataxia and eye-movement abnormalities (ophthalmoplegia and nystagmus), though the full triad is present in only a minority of patients. The condition was described by the German neurologist Carl Wernicke in 1881. It is a medical emergency: untreated, it can progress to coma and death or to the chronic amnesic state of Korsakoff syndrome, together termed Wernicke–Korsakoff syndrome.

Causes and pathophysiology

Thiamine is a cofactor for enzymes of carbohydrate metabolism, and deficiency impairs energy production in the brain, injuring vulnerable periventricular structures — the mammillary bodies, dorsomedial thalamus, periaqueductal grey and floor of the fourth ventricle. The commonest cause in developed countries is alcohol use disorder, which combines poor diet, impaired absorption and reduced storage; other causes include starvation, prolonged vomiting (including hyperemesis gravidarum), dialysis, malignancy and bariatric surgery.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is clinical — operationalised by the Caine criteria — and should not wait for laboratory or imaging confirmation, although MRI may show characteristic symmetric lesions. Suspected patients must receive high-dose intravenous thiamine immediately, before any glucose, since glucose loading in a thiamine-deficient patient can precipitate or worsen the encephalopathy. With prompt treatment, eye signs and confusion may resolve; untreated or late-treated cases often retain permanent memory impairment.
