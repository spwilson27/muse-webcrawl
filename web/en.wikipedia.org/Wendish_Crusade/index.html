The Wendish Crusade (German: Wendenkreuzzug) was a military campaign in 1147, one of the Northern Crusades and a part of the Second Crusade, fought against the Polabian Slavs — called Wends — living east of the river Elbe in what is now north-eastern Germany and Poland.

Background

German expansion eastward had stalled after a great Slav uprising in 983 destroyed many missions and bishoprics. When the Second Crusade was preached in 1146–47, Saxon nobles reluctant to travel to the Holy Land obtained papal approval to crusade instead against the pagan Wends. Bernard of Clairvaux supported the expedition and promised the same spiritual rewards; Pope Eugenius III confirmed the arrangements in the bull Divina dispensatione.

The campaign

In the summer of 1147 two armies marched: one under Henry the Lion and Albert the Bear against the Obotrites, another of bishops and princes against the Pomeranians and Lutici, with Danish contingents arriving by sea. The crusaders besieged the strongholds of Dobin and Demmin and attacked Stettin — whose inhabitants revealed they were already Christians. The results were modest: the Obotrite prince Niklot agreed to nominal baptism of his household and tribute, and the bishoprics of Havelberg and Oldenburg were refounded.

Consequences

Though militarily inconclusive, the crusade legitimised German eastward expansion and missionary warfare on the Baltic frontier, paving the way for the later campaigns of Henry the Lion, Danish conquests, and the crusades of the Teutonic Order in Prussia and Livonia.
