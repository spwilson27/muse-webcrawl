Innate immune system

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The innate immune system, also known as the non-specific immune system, is one of the two main immunity strategies in vertebrate animals (the other being the adaptive immune system). It is an evolutionarily older defence system found in plants, fungi, insects, and primitive multicellular organisms as well as vertebrates. The innate system provides the first line of defence against infection, acting immediately or within hours of a pathogen's appearance, in a generic (antigen-independent) fashion and without immunological memory.

Anatomical and chemical barriers

Physical barriers such as the skin and mucous membranes prevent entry of pathogens; secretions add chemical defences — lysozyme in tears and saliva, acidic gastric juice, defensins, and sebaceous secretions. Normal microbiota compete with pathogens for space and nutrients.

Recognition

Innate immune cells recognise conserved microbial structures called pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), such as bacterial lipopolysaccharide, through germline-encoded pattern recognition receptors (PRRs). Important PRRs include Toll-like receptors (TLRs), NOD-like receptors, RIG-I-like receptors, and C-type lectin receptors. Damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) released from injured host cells can trigger similar responses.

Cells

Cellular components include phagocytes (neutrophils, monocytes and macrophages, and dendritic cells), innate lymphoid cells including natural killer (NK) cells, mast cells, eosinophils, and basophils. Phagocytes engulf and destroy microbes in phagosomes using reactive oxygen species and enzymes; dendritic cells act as a bridge to the adaptive system by presenting antigen. NK cells kill virus-infected and tumour cells that have lost MHC class I expression.

Inflammation and complement

Activation of innate receptors triggers inflammation: cytokines and chemokines recruit leucocytes, increase vascular permeability, and produce the classic signs of redness, heat, swelling, and pain. The complement system — a cascade of plasma proteins activated by the classical, lectin, and alternative pathways — opsonises microbes, recruits phagocytes, and can lyse pathogens directly via the membrane attack complex. Fever, driven by pyrogens acting on the hypothalamus, is another systemic innate response.

Relation to adaptive immunity

Unlike the adaptive immune system, the innate system does not confer long-lasting, antigen-specific memory (though some "trained immunity" effects are recognised). Innate signals, especially via dendritic cells, are essential for initiating and shaping adaptive responses.
