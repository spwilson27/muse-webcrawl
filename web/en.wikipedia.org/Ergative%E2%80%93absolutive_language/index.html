Ergative–absolutive language

An ergative–absolutive language, or simply ergative language, is a language in which the subject of an intransitive verb has the same grammatical case as the object of a transitive verb, while the subject of a transitive verb is treated differently. This contrasts with nominative–accusative languages such as English, where the intransitive subject and transitive subject are treated alike.

Alignment

In ergative languages, the single argument of an intransitive clause (S) and the patient-like argument of a transitive clause (P) share the absolutive case, while the agent-like argument of a transitive clause (A) takes the ergative case. Many languages show split ergativity, where ergative alignment appears only in some tenses, aspects, or clause types.

Distribution

Ergativity is prominent in the languages of the Caucasus (such as Georgian), in Basque, in many Australian Aboriginal languages (such as Dyirbal), in Mayan languages, in Eskimo–Aleut languages, and in the Kurdish dialects of northern Iraq and neighbouring regions. Hindi-Urdu shows aspect-based split ergativity in perfective transitive clauses.

Optional ergativity

In some languages, such as several of New Guinea and Tibet, the ergative marker may be omitted when agency is clear from context, a phenomenon known as optional ergativity. This challenges simple typological classifications and suggests ergative marking also serves discourse functions such as highlighting unexpected agency.
