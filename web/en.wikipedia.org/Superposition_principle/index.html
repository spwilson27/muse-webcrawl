Superposition principle

The superposition principle states that, for all linear systems, the net response caused by two or more stimuli is the sum of the responses that would have been caused by each stimulus individually. If input A produces response X and input B produces response Y, then input (A + B) produces response (X + Y).

Applications

In wave physics, superposition explains interference: overlapping waves displace a medium by the algebraic sum of their individual displacements, producing constructive and destructive interference patterns. In electrical engineering, superposition is a circuit-analysis technique for linear circuits with multiple independent sources. The principle holds exactly only for systems governed by linear equations — such as the wave equation, Maxwell's equations in vacuum, and linear ordinary differential equations — and approximately for many nearly linear systems.

Quantum mechanics

In quantum mechanics, superposition is fundamental: a quantum system may exist in a combination of basis states, described by a wavefunction that is a linear combination of eigenstates, with probabilities given by the squared magnitudes of the amplitudes (the Born rule). Famous illustrations include Schrödinger's cat thought experiment and the double-slit experiment with single particles.
