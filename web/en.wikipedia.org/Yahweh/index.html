Yahweh was the national god of the kingdoms of Israel and Judah in the Iron Age Levant, and is the God of Judaism and (with differing theology) Christianity and related traditions. His name is written in the Hebrew Bible with the four consonants יהוה, known as the Tetragrammaton; the original pronunciation is reconstructed as "Yahweh", though Jewish tradition ceased pronouncing the name aloud in the Second Temple period, substituting Adonai ("my Lord").

Origins

The earliest probable mention, in Egyptian texts of the 14th–13th centuries BCE, names a land of the "Shasu of Yhw" in the southern Transjordan or northwest Arabia, leading many scholars to a southern origin for the cult (the Kenite/Midianite hypothesis). In earliest Israelite religion Yahweh was worshipped within a polytheistic milieu: inscriptions from Kuntillet Ajrud bless by "Yahweh of Samaria and his Asherah" and "Yahweh of Teman and his Asherah".

Yahwism to monotheism

During the monarchic period Yahweh absorbed the attributes of the Canaanite high god El and storm god Baal, becoming god of war, weather and the divine council. Prophetic and Deuteronomistic movements increasingly demanded exclusive worship; the exile to Babylon (6th century BCE) was followed by the strict monotheism of Second Isaiah ("I am Yahweh, and there is no other"). By the Persian and Hellenistic periods Judaism was monotheistic, and the name itself became unspeakable, written with the vowels of Adonai — the source of the later mistaken reading "Jehovah".

Worship

The Jerusalem Temple housed the cult's central shrine, with sacrifices, festivals and a priesthood. Yahweh's worship extended to diaspora communities such as the Jewish garrison at Elephantine in Egypt. In Christianity the name is applied to God, with theological debate over its relation to the Trinity.
