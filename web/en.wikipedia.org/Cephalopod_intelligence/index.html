Cephalopod intelligence

Cephalopod intelligence is a measure of the cognitive ability of the class Cephalopoda — octopuses, squid, cuttlefish, and nautiluses. Cephalopods are widely regarded as the most intelligent invertebrates, with nervous systems and behavioural repertoires far more complex than those of other molluscs; an octopus has roughly two-thirds of its neurons located in its arms.

Evidence

Octopuses demonstrate observational learning, problem-solving, tool use — including carrying coconut shells for shelter — and elaborate escape behaviour from aquaria. They can navigate mazes, open jars, and distinguish individual human keepers. Cuttlefish show episodic-like memory and can delay gratification in experiments modelled on the marshmallow test, and they use dynamic camouflage possibly for communication.

Brain structure

The cephalopod brain evolved independently of the vertebrate brain, making its intelligence an example of convergent evolution. Because cephalopods are short-lived and largely solitary, their cognition challenges theories that link intelligence primarily to long life and social complexity. Their abilities have led to legal protections: cephalopods are included in animal-research regulations in the European Union and several other jurisdictions.
