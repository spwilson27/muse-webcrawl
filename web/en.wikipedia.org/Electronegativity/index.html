Electronegativity


Electronegativity increases across a period and decreases down a group

Electronegativity, symbolised by χ, is the tendency for an atom of a given chemical element to attract shared electrons (or electron density) when forming a chemical bond. An atom's electronegativity is affected by both its atomic number and the distance at which its valence electrons reside from the charged nucleus. The higher the associated electronegativity, the more an atom or a substituent group attracts electrons.

Scales

The concept was introduced by Linus Pauling in 1932; on the Pauling scale, values are dimensionless numbers running from about 0.7 (francium and caesium) to 4.0 for fluorine, the most electronegative element. Other scales include the Mulliken scale, based on the average of ionisation energy and electron affinity, and the Allred–Rochow scale, based on effective nuclear charge and covalent radius.

Periodic trends

Electronegativity generally increases from left to right across a period, as nuclear charge rises while shielding changes little, and decreases down a group, as valence electrons lie farther from the nucleus. The noble gases are usually assigned no value because they rarely form bonds.

Applications

Differences in electronegativity between bonded atoms predict bond polarity and bond type: differences below about 0.5 indicate nonpolar covalent bonds, intermediate differences polar covalent bonds, and differences above about 1.7–2.0 ionic character. Electronegativity also correlates with acidity, oxidation state assignments and chemical reactivity.
