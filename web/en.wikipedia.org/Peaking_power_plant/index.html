Peaking power plant


A gas turbine peaking plant

Peaking power plants, also known as peaker plants, are power plants that generally run only when there is high demand — "peak demand" — for electricity. Because they supply power only occasionally, the electricity they generate is usually supplied at a much higher price than baseload power. Peakers are combined with baseload plants, which supply a dependable steady amount of power at lower cost, and load-following plants, which adjust output daily.

Characteristics

A good peaking plant starts quickly (in minutes), ramps output rapidly, and is cheap to build, even if it is relatively inefficient and expensive to run, since it operates only a few hundred hours per year and thus has a low capacity factor. Low efficiency also means cheap, lower-quality fuel can be used economically in some designs.

Types

Historically most peakers were simple-cycle gas turbines burning natural gas or oil, which can reach full power within about ten minutes. Other peaking resources include pumped-storage and reservoir hydroelectricity, which can ramp almost instantly; diesel and reciprocating gas engines; and increasingly, grid-scale battery storage, which responds in milliseconds and is steadily displacing gas peakers in some markets. Demand response — paying customers to curtail use at peak times — serves a comparable role.

Economics and emissions

Because peakers are the marginal, most expensive resource on the system, they frequently set the wholesale price of electricity during peak hours. Older gas and oil peakers can be significant sources of local air pollution, often in urban areas where they are sited near demand.
