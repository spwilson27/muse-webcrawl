Crossing of the Red Sea

The Crossing of the Red Sea or parting of the Red Sea (Hebrew: קריעת ים סוף, Kriat Yam Suph) is an episode in the biblical narrative of The Exodus. It tells of the escape of the Israelites, led by Moses, from the pursuing Egyptians, as recounted in the Book of Exodus. Moses holds out his staff and God parts the waters of the Yam Suph (Reed Sea or Sea of Reeds). The Israelites walk through on dry land, followed by the Egyptian army. Once the Israelites have safely crossed, Moses drops his staff, closing the waters and drowning the pursuing Egyptians.

The Israelites crossing the sea on dry ground.

Biblical narrative

After the Ten Plagues, Pharaoh allowed the Israelites to leave Egypt, but then changed his mind and pursued them with chariots. At the sea, the Israelites despaired, but Moses told them to "stand firm". During the night a strong east wind divided the water, and the Israelites crossed with the water "a wall unto them on their right hand, and on their left". In the morning the Egyptians followed into the seabed; their chariot wheels became stuck, and when Moses stretched out his hand again the waters returned, destroying the Egyptian force. Israel then sang the Song of the Sea (Exodus 15).

Translation and geography

The Hebrew Yam Suph has traditionally been rendered "Red Sea" in English, following the Septuagint, though many scholars translate it "Sea of Reeds" and propose locations among the lakes and marshes of the eastern Nile Delta and the northern Gulf of Suez region. Proposed crossing sites include the Lake of Tanis, the Bitter Lakes, and various points on the Gulf of Suez and Gulf of Aqaba.

Historicity and interpretation

Most scholars regard the Exodus narrative as belonging substantially to Israel's national founding myth, with a historical kernel debated; no direct archaeological evidence of the crossing event is known. The episode is central to Jewish liturgy (recounted daily and at Passover), is referenced in the Quran (which also describes the parting of the sea for Moses and the drowning of Pharaoh), and has been a frequent subject in art and film.
