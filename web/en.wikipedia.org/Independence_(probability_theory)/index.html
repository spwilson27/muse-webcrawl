Independence (probability theory)

In probability theory, independence is a fundamental concept describing events or random variables that do not influence one another: learning that one occurred gives no information about whether the other occurred. Two events A and B are independent if and only if P(A ∩ B) = P(A) · P(B). Equivalently, when P(B) > 0, P(A | B) = P(A).

Definitions

Two random variables X and Y are independent if their joint cumulative distribution function factors: FX,Y(x, y) = FX(x) · FY(y) for all x, y. For discrete variables this means P(X = x, Y = y) = P(X = x) P(Y = y). Independence of more than two events requires the product identity to hold for every subcollection (mutual independence), which is stronger than pairwise independence: pairwise independent events need not be mutually independent.

Properties

If X and Y are independent, then their covariance and correlation are zero (the converse is false in general), and E[g(X)h(Y)] = E[g(X)] E[h(Y)] for suitable functions. A family of random variables that are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) underlies the law of large numbers and the central limit theorem.

Conditional independence

Events or variables may be independent given knowledge of a third variable; this notion of conditional independence is central to Bayesian networks and graphical models. Independence must be distinguished from mutually exclusive events, which are in fact strongly dependent (if one occurs, the other cannot).
