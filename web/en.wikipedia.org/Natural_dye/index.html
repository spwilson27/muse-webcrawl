Natural dye

Natural dyes are dyes or colorants derived from plants, invertebrates, or minerals. The majority of natural dyes are vegetable dyes from plant sources — roots, berries, bark, leaves, and wood — and other biological sources such as fungi and lichens. Until the mid-19th century, virtually all dyes used for textiles and other materials were natural; their replacement by cheaper, more consistent synthetic dyes after William Henry Perkin's synthesis of mauveine in 1856 was rapid, though natural dyes survive in traditional crafts, artisanal textiles, and a revived market for sustainable products.

Common natural dyes

Important historic dyes include madder (red, from the root of Rubia tinctorum), indigo (blue, from Indigofera and woad), weld (yellow), Tyrian purple (from murex sea snails, famously costly), cochineal (red, from scale insects on cacti), logwood, cutch, and turmeric. Mordants — metal salts such as alum, iron, and chrome — are often required to fix dyes to fibers and can modify the resulting color.

History

Dyeing with natural colorants dates back thousands of years across China, India, Egypt, and the Mediterranean world. Natural dye trades shaped colonial economies, and indigo and cochineal became major export commodities. William Morris and the Arts and Crafts movement revived interest in natural dyeing in the late 19th century in reaction against early aniline dyes.

Properties

Natural dyes generally produce softer, harmonious colors but tend to be less light- and wash-fast than synthetics, more variable between batches, and more land- and labor-intensive to produce — factors debated in modern assessments of their environmental benefits.
