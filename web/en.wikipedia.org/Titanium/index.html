Titanium is a chemical element with the symbol Ti and atomic number 22. It is a lustrous transition metal with a silver color, low density, and high strength. Titanium is resistant to corrosion in seawater, aqua regia, and chlorine. It was discovered in Cornwall, Great Britain, by William Gregor in 1791 and was named by Martin Heinrich Klaproth after the Titans of Greek mythology.

Characteristics

Titanium is as strong as some steels but much less dense, giving it an exceptional strength-to-weight ratio. A thin, adherent oxide layer forms on its surface in air, passivating the metal and accounting for its corrosion resistance. It is paramagnetic, a poor conductor of electricity and heat relative to other metals, and has two allotropic forms: a hexagonal alpha phase at room temperature that transforms to a body-centered cubic beta phase at 882 °C. Titanium is not attacked by dilute sulfuric or hydrochloric acid, most organic acids, or chlorine solutions, and it is biocompatible, bonding with bone in medical implants.

Production

Titanium occurs chiefly in the minerals rutile and ilmenite. Because the metal reacts readily with oxygen and nitrogen at high temperatures, extraction is difficult. Commercial production uses the Kroll process: titanium tetrachloride is produced by chlorination of the ore and then reduced with magnesium in an inert atmosphere to yield porous "sponge" metal, which is melted in vacuum arc furnaces. The process is batch-based and energy-intensive, making titanium considerably more expensive than steel or aluminum.

Applications

The majority of titanium produced is used as titanium dioxide (TiO₂), a brilliant white pigment in paints, plastics, paper, and sunscreens. The metal and its alloys—most importantly Ti-6Al-4V—are used in aerospace airframes and jet engines, military equipment, chemical processing plant, desalination, medical implants and prostheses, sporting goods, and jewelry. Notable uses include the Lockheed SR-71 Blackbird, constructed largely of titanium, and deep-submergence vehicles.

History

Gregor identified a new oxide in the mineral menachanite in 1791; Klaproth independently found the element in rutile in 1795 and named it. Pure metallic titanium was first prepared in impure form by Jöns Jacob Berzelius and in purer form by Matthew Hunter in 1910. William J. Kroll's process, developed in the 1930s–1940s, enabled industrial production, which expanded rapidly during the Cold War arms and space races.
