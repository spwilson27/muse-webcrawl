National Labor Relations Act of 1935

The National Labor Relations Act of 1935, also known as the Wagner Act, is a foundational statute of United States labor law that guarantees the right of private sector employees to organise into trade unions, engage in collective bargaining, and take collective action such as strikes. It also established the National Labor Relations Board (NLRB) to enforce these rights and to investigate and remedy unfair labor practices. Sponsored by Senator Robert F. Wagner of New York, the act was a key component of President Franklin D. Roosevelt's New Deal and was signed into law on 5 July 1935.

Background

The act followed the collapse of earlier New Deal labour protections: Section 7(a) of the National Industrial Recovery Act of 1933 had proclaimed organising rights but lacked enforcement machinery, and the Supreme Court struck down the NIRA in 1935. Mounting strike waves in 1934 convinced the administration that a stronger statute was needed to channel industrial conflict into orderly collective bargaining.

Key provisions

Section 7 guarantees employees "the right to self-organization, to form, join, or assist labor organizations, to bargain collectively through representatives of their own choosing, and to engage in other concerted activities for the purpose of collective bargaining or other mutual aid or protection". Section 8 defines unfair labor practices by employers, including interfering with organising, dominating a company union, discriminating against union members, and refusing to bargain. The act created the five-member NLRB, which conducts representation elections and adjudicates unfair labour practice charges. Agricultural workers, domestic workers, supervisors, independent contractors and public-sector employees are excluded from coverage.

Constitutionality and effects

Employers widely defied the act pending a constitutional test; in NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp. (1937) the Supreme Court upheld it as a valid exercise of the Commerce Clause power. Union membership in the United States roughly tripled over the following decade, and collective bargaining became a central institution of the American economy.

Amendments

The Taft–Hartley Act of 1947, passed over President Truman's veto, substantially amended the act: it defined unfair labour practices by unions, outlawed the closed shop and secondary boycotts, permitted states to enact "right-to-work" laws (Section 14(b)), and excluded supervisors. The Landrum–Griffin Act of 1959 added further regulation of internal union affairs.
