Lightning is a giant electrostatic discharge between electrically charged regions of the atmosphere.

Lightning is a natural phenomenon consisting of electrostatic discharges through the atmosphere between two electrically charged regions, either both in the atmosphere or one in the atmosphere and one on the ground. In most cases the discharge is caused by a cumulonimbus cloud in a thunderstorm, and it is accompanied by thunder, the sound produced by the explosive heating and expansion of air along the lightning channel.

Formation

Inside a thundercloud, collisions between ice crystals and graupel separate electrical charge, leaving the top of the cloud positively charged and the base negative. When the charge difference with the ground or another cloud region becomes large enough to overcome the insulation of air, a faint ionised path called a stepped leader descends; an upward streamer from the ground meets it, and the bright return stroke carries the main current, heating the channel to roughly five times the temperature of the Sun's surface.

Types

The most common type is intra-cloud lightning ("sheet lightning"), followed by cloud-to-cloud and cloud-to-ground flashes. Bolt shapes range from forked and ribbon lightning to rare phenomena such as ball lightning. Powerful positive cloud-to-ground "superbolts" and upward lightning from tall structures also occur, as do transient luminous events above storms, such as sprites and jets. Lightning also occurs in volcanic plumes, dust storms, and on other planets, including Jupiter and Venus.

Effects and safety

Lightning strikes kill or injure thousands of people each year worldwide and ignite wildfires, though it also fixes atmospheric nitrogen into compounds usable by plants. Protection systems use lightning rods, surge protectors, and safety guidance such as the "30-30 rule" and the advice to shelter indoors or in a hard-topped vehicle during storms.
