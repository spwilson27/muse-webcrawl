Corsican (corsu) is a Romance language spoken on the island of Corsica in the Mediterranean, a territorial collectivity of France, and in adjacent parts of northern Sardinia. It is closely related to the Tuscan dialects of mainland Italy, reflecting the island's long periods under Pisan and Genoese rule.

History

Corsican developed from Vulgar Latin, heavily influenced by Tuscan after Pisa and then Genoa governed the island. For centuries Italian was the language of administration and culture in Corsica, while Corsican remained the spoken vernacular. France acquired the island in 1768, and French gradually displaced Italian; French is today the dominant language of public life. Varieties spoken in northern Sardinia — Gallurese and Sassarese — are often grouped with Corsican as part of a Corsican–Sardinian continuum.

Dialects and status

Corsican has northern and southern dialect groups, divided roughly along a line through the island's centre; southern varieties shade toward Sardinian. The number of fluent speakers has declined sharply — estimates range from tens of thousands to around 125,000, with many more having passive knowledge — and UNESCO classifies the language as definitely endangered. Since the 1970s a cultural revival (u riacquistu) has promoted Corsican in education and media; it is taught in schools and used by the Corsican Assembly, though French law gives it no official status.

Features

Corsican shares the seven-vowel system and many grammatical features of Tuscan Italian. It is written in the Latin alphabet with conventions close to Italian orthography; characteristic features include the definite articles u, a, i, e and a vocabulary reflecting Latin, Italian, and later French influence.
