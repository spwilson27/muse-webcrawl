Shikantaza

Shikantaza (Japanese: 只管打坐, "just sitting") is the central meditation practice of the Sōtō school of Zen Buddhism, associated above all with the 13th-century master Eihei Dōgen. It is a form of zazen (seated meditation) characterised by objectless, non-striving awareness: unlike practices that concentrate on a koan, the breath, or a mantra, shikantaza involves simply sitting in full alertness, letting thoughts arise and pass without pursuing, suppressing, or analysing them.

Origins

The practice descends from the "silent illumination" (mòzhào) meditation of the Chinese Caodong master Hongzhi Zhengjue (1091–1157). Dōgen, who studied in China under Tiantong Rujing, brought the teaching to Japan and made it the heart of his school, describing it in works such as the Fukan zazengi ("Universal Recommendation of Zazen") and the Shōbōgenzō.

Teaching

Dōgen taught that zazen is not a means to enlightenment but the practice of enlightenment itself — "practice and realisation are one" (shushō ittō). To sit shikantaza is to enact one's original awakening (Buddha-nature) rather than to manufacture a special state of mind. Instructions emphasise correct posture — full or half lotus, spine erect, chin tucked, eyes open and lowered, hands in the cosmic mudra — and an attitude of radical non-doing: "think of not-thinking; how do you think of not-thinking? Non-thinking." Modern Sōtō teachers such as Kōshō Uchiyama ("opening the hand of thought") and Shunryū Suzuki popularised the practice in the West.

Contrast with koan practice

Shikantaza is often contrasted with the koan introspection emphasised in the Rinzai school, which drives the student toward a breakthrough experience (kenshō). Sōtō teaching regards such goal-seeking as itself an obstacle; critics from the Rinzai side have at times derided shikantaza as quietism, while Sōtō masters reply that "just sitting" is the most demanding of disciplines precisely because it offers the mind nothing to grasp.
