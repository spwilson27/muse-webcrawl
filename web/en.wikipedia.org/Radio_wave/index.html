Radio wave

A radio wave as oscillating electric and magnetic fields.

Radio waves are a type of electromagnetic radiation with the lowest frequencies (longest wavelengths) in the electromagnetic spectrum, typically from 3 hertz to 300 gigahertz, corresponding to wavelengths from about 100,000 kilometres down to 1 millimetre. Like all electromagnetic waves, they travel at the speed of light in vacuum and consist of oscillating electric and magnetic fields perpendicular to each other and to the direction of propagation.

Generation and propagation

Radio waves are produced by accelerating electric charges, most usefully by alternating currents in antennas. They propagate as ground waves following the Earth's surface, as line-of-sight (space) waves, or, at lower frequencies, as sky waves reflected by the ionosphere — a property that enables long-distance shortwave communication. Higher frequencies penetrate the ionosphere and are used for satellite links.

Discovery and use

Their existence was predicted by James Clerk Maxwell in 1865 and verified experimentally by Heinrich Hertz in 1887. Radio waves underpin radio communication, broadcasting, radar, navigation systems such as GPS, microwave ovens (using 2.45 GHz radiation), and radio astronomy observations of natural sources in the cosmos.
