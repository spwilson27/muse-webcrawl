Nongqawuse

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Nongqawuse (c. 1840 – 1898) was a Xhosa prophetess whose visions in 1856–57 precipitated the Xhosa cattle-killing movement, one of the most consequential and catastrophic episodes in South African history. Her prophecy — that if the Xhosa destroyed their cattle and crops, the ancestors would rise, replenish the land, and sweep the British into the sea — led to mass slaughter of livestock and destruction of grain; when the prophesied day passed without the resurrection, tens of thousands starved and Xhosa society was shattered.

The prophecy

In April or May 1856, Nongqawuse, then a teenage orphan living near the Gxarha River in what is now the Eastern Cape, reported that two strangers — ancestors, she explained, acting through her uncle Mhlakaza, himself a diviner — had told her that the dead would arise and drive out the Europeans, provided the Xhosa killed all their cattle (many already dying from lungsickness recently introduced from Europe), destroyed their corn, and ceased cultivation. The paramount chief Sarhili endorsed the prophecy, and the movement spread through Xhosaland in 1856–57. Dates for the fulfilment were set and passed; on the final appointed day, 18 February 1857, the sun was said to have risen blood-red but the dead did not come.

Consequences

Estimates suggest some 400,000 cattle were killed and the Xhosa population of British Kaffraria fell from about 105,000 to about 37,000 through starvation, disease, and emigration. The British governor, Sir George Grey, exploited the crisis: famine relief was tied to labour contracts, vast tracts of Xhosa land were alienated to white and German military settlers, and Xhosa independence was effectively broken. Nongqawuse was arrested and imprisoned on Robben Island for several years; she later lived in obscurity near Alexandria under the name Victoria Regina and died in 1898.

Historiography

Historians debate the respective weight of genuine religious belief, the devastation of cattle disease, and political manipulation. J. B. Peires's The Dead Will Arise (1989) reconstructed the movement from oral and archival evidence, arguing the prophecy was a rational response to ecological and colonial crisis. Colonial accounts that blamed a conspiracy by chiefs (or by Grey himself) are now largely discounted.
