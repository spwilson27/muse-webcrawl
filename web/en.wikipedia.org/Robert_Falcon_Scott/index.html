Captain Robert Falcon Scott (1868–1912) was a British Royal Navy officer and explorer who led two expeditions to the Antarctic in the Heroic Age of Antarctic Exploration. On his second expedition, the Terra Nova Expedition of 1910–1913, his five-man polar party reached the South Pole on 17 January 1912, only to find that Roald Amundsen's Norwegian party had preceded them by 34 days. Scott and his four companions all died on the return journey.

The Discovery Expedition

Scott first went south commanding the National Antarctic Expedition aboard Discovery (1901–1904). With Ernest Shackleton and Edward Wilson he sledged to a then-record southern latitude of 82°17′S, and the expedition carried out extensive scientific work around the Ross Sea and McMurdo Sound before being relieved.

The race to the Pole

The Terra Nova Expedition sailed in 1910 with mixed transport — ponies, motor sledges, dogs and man-hauling. Scott's polar party of five (Scott, Wilson, Henry Bowers, Lawrence Oates and Edgar Evans) reached the Pole on 17 January 1912. On the return, Evans died in February; Oates, suffering severe frostbite, famously walked out of the tent into a blizzard on 17 March saying, "I am just going outside and may be some time." Scott, Wilson and Bowers died in their tent, probably on 29 March 1912, only 11 miles from a food depot. Their bodies and Scott's diaries were recovered the following November.

Legacy

Scott became a national hero, and his journals' account of the disaster shaped his reputation for decades. From the 1970s historians debated his competence — criticising his reliance on man-hauling and his planning — while others, including Ranulph Fiennes, have defended him, blaming exceptionally severe weather confirmed by modern meteorological analysis. The Scott Polar Research Institute in Cambridge and the Amundsen–Scott South Pole Station preserve his name. He is frequently compared with his contemporary Robert Peary, the American claimant of the North Pole — two very different explorers of the same polar era. Not to be confused with the engineer Robert Stephenson.
