The Warren Commission, officially the President's Commission on the Assassination of President Kennedy, was a presidential commission established by President Lyndon B. Johnson on November 29, 1963, one week after the assassination of John F. Kennedy in Dallas, Texas. Chaired by Chief Justice Earl Warren, the commission investigated the assassination and published its 888-page report in September 1964.

Membership and work

Besides Warren, the commission comprised Senators Richard Russell Jr. and John Sherman Cooper, Representatives Hale Boggs and Gerald R. Ford, former CIA director Allen Dulles, and banker and former presidential adviser John J. McCloy. J. Lee Rankin served as general counsel, and a young staff of lawyers — including future senator Arlen Specter — conducted much of the investigation. The commission and its staff took testimony from 552 witnesses and examined reports from the FBI, Secret Service, and other agencies; its work was published in the report plus 26 volumes of hearings and exhibits.

Conclusions

The commission concluded that Lee Harvey Oswald, a former Marine employed at the Texas School Book Depository, fired three shots from the building's sixth floor, killing President Kennedy and wounding Texas Governor John Connally, and that he acted alone. A key element was the so-called "single-bullet theory", holding that one bullet passed through Kennedy's neck and caused all of Connally's wounds. The commission also concluded that Jack Ruby acted alone in killing Oswald two days later, that there was no credible evidence of a conspiracy, domestic or foreign, and it criticized the security arrangements for the motorcade and the performance of the news media.

Criticism and later investigations

The report was immediately controversial. Critics such as Mark Lane (Rush to Judgment, 1966) questioned the single-bullet theory, the commission's reliance on FBI summaries, its haste, and evidence about the shots' origin. Polls over the following decades often found a majority of Americans doubting the lone-gunman conclusion. In 1979, the House Select Committee on Assassinations concluded that Kennedy was "probably assassinated as a result of a conspiracy", based largely on acoustic evidence from a police dictabelt that was later disputed by a National Academy of Sciences panel. The Assassination Records Review Board in the 1990s released millions of pages of records; subsequent scholarship continues to debate the commission's findings.
