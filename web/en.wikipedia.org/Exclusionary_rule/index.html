Exclusionary rule

Exclusionary rule
CountryUnited States
AreaCriminal procedure; constitutional law
Key casesWeeks v. United States (1914); Mapp v. Ohio (1961)

The exclusionary rule is a legal rule in the United States, based on constitutional law, which prevents evidence collected or analyzed in violation of the defendant's constitutional rights from being used in a court of law. It is intended to deter police misconduct, particularly violations of the Fourth Amendment's prohibition on unreasonable searches and seizures.

History

The rule was established for federal cases in Weeks v. United States (1914) and applied to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment in Mapp v. Ohio (1961). Evidence derived from illegally obtained evidence may also be excluded as the "fruit of the poisonous tree".

Exceptions and criticism

Exceptions include the good-faith exception, where officers reasonably relied on a defective warrant; inevitable discovery; and independent source doctrines. Critics argue the rule can allow guilty defendants to go free on a technicality, while supporters argue it is essential to judicial integrity and to deterring unconstitutional policing.
