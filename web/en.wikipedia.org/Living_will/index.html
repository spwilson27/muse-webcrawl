A living will is a legal document in which a person specifies what medical treatments they do or do not want if they become unable to communicate their own decisions, for example because of terminal illness, coma, or severe injury. It is a type of advance directive; unlike an ordinary will, it takes effect during the person's lifetime rather than after death.

History

The concept was proposed in 1969 by the American human rights lawyer Luis Kutner, and the first living will legislation was enacted in California in 1976 (the Natural Death Act). The U.S. Supreme Court's 1990 Cruzan decision recognized a constitutional dimension to refusing unwanted treatment, and the federal Patient Self-Determination Act of 1990 required healthcare institutions to inform patients of their rights to make advance directives. High-profile cases, such as that of Terri Schiavo (resolved in 2005), drew public attention and led many more people to record their wishes. Comparable instruments exist in many countries; in England and Wales, "advance decisions to refuse treatment" are legally binding under the Mental Capacity Act 2005.

Contents and effect

A living will typically addresses life-sustaining treatments such as mechanical ventilation, cardiopulmonary resuscitation, tube feeding, dialysis, and surgery, and states whether the signer wishes them used, withheld, or withdrawn in specified circumstances such as terminal conditions or permanent unconsciousness. It may be combined with a healthcare power of attorney (durable power of attorney for healthcare), which names a surrogate decision-maker. Physicians' orders for life-sustaining treatment (POLST/MOLST forms) convert such preferences into portable medical orders, particularly for seriously ill patients.

Limitations

Living wills can fail in practice if documents are unavailable in an emergency, worded too vaguely to apply to a real clinical situation, or overridden by disagreements among family members and clinicians. For these reasons, health professionals emphasize discussing values and preferences with relatives and doctors, appointing a surrogate, and reviewing documents periodically rather than relying on a form alone.
