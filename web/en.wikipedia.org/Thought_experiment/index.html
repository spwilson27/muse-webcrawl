Thought experiment

A thought experiment is a hypothetical scenario in which a hypothesis, theory or principle is laid out for the purpose of thinking through its consequences. Such experiments are used across philosophy, physics, economics, history and many other fields, often to test ideas that cannot (yet) be tested physically, or to expose inconsistencies in a theory.

Famous examples

In physics, celebrated thought experiments include Galileo's falling bodies, Newton's cannonball and bucket, Maxwell's demon, Einstein's elevator and light-beam chase (which helped lead to relativity), the EPR paradox, and Schrödinger's cat, designed to dramatise the measurement problem in quantum mechanics. In philosophy, examples include Plato's cave, Descartes's evil demon, Locke's inverted spectrum, John Searle's Chinese room, Frank Jackson's Mary's room, Robert Nozick's experience machine, and Philippa Foot's and Judith Jarvis Thomson's trolley problem in ethics.

Function and debate

Philosophers disagree about how thought experiments yield knowledge: some treat them as disguised arguments, others as appeals to intuition or as encounters with possible worlds. Their vivid scenarios have nonetheless repeatedly driven real theoretical progress — several celebrated thought experiments in physics were later realised, at least approximately, in the laboratory.
