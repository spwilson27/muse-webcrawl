War Communism

War Communism (Russian: военный коммунизм, voenny kommunizm) was the economic and political system that existed in Soviet Russia during the Russian Civil War, from 1918 to 1921. Enforced by the Bolshevik government, it combined emergency measures to supply the Red Army and the cities with an ideological attempt to move rapidly toward a moneyless, classless economy.

Policies

The system, administered through the Supreme Council of the National Economy (Vesenkha), included the nationalisation of all large industry, the state monopoly of foreign trade, strict centralised control of production and distribution, and the abolition or severe restriction of private enterprise. Its most notorious feature was prodrazvyorstka, the forcible requisitioning of grain and other foodstuffs from peasants at nominal prices to feed the army and urban workers. Labour became compulsory, and workers were organised along military lines.

Effects

Rather than relief, War Communism deepened economic collapse. Industrial output fell to a small fraction of pre-war levels, the currency collapsed into barter, and peasants responded to requisitioning by reducing sowing and hiding grain. Shortages contributed to the catastrophic famine of 1921–22, which killed an estimated five million people, and to widespread peasant revolts such as the Tambov Rebellion, as well as the Kronstadt rebellion of March 1921 by sailors who had been among the Bolsheviks’ earliest supporters.

End and legacy

At the Tenth Party Congress in March 1921, Lenin replaced War Communism with the New Economic Policy (NEP), which substituted a fixed tax in kind for requisitioning and permitted limited private trade. Historians debate whether War Communism was primarily an improvised response to civil war conditions or an ideological attempt to leap directly into communism; Lenin himself later described it as a temporary measure forced by war and ruin, though contemporary Bolshevik theorists such as Nikolai Bukharin had defended it as a coherent system.
