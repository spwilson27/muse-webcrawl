Pylon (architecture)

A pylon is a monumental gateway to an ancient Egyptian temple, formed by two massive, truncated, pyramidal towers flanking a lower central entrance. The word comes from the Greek pylōn, "gateway". Pylons were the most prominent exterior feature of Egyptian temple architecture of the New Kingdom and later periods, and temples often accumulated a series of pylons along their processional axis over centuries of building.

Description

Each tower of a pylon is a solid-massed structure of mudbrick or stone with battered (inward-sloping) walls, crowned by a cavetto cornice. The front faces were decorated with sunk relief scenes, typically showing the king presenting offerings to the gods or smiting Egypt's enemies before them. Shallow vertical grooves in the façade held tall wooden flagstaffs topped with banners, and obelisks and colossal statues of the king were frequently placed in front. Behind the entrance passage, staircases inside the towers led to small chambers and to the roof.

Symbolism

The paired towers are thought to represent the horizon (akhet) — the two hills between which the sun rose and set — and also to evoke the twin mountains flanking the eastern horizon of the afterlife. Passing through the pylon, a procession moved from the profane world into progressively darker and more sacred temple spaces.

Examples

Surviving pylons include the great series at Karnak, where ten pylons line the two main axes; the well-preserved pylon of the Temple of Horus at Edfu; the pylons of Luxor Temple, fronted by obelisks and statues of Ramesses II; and the pylon of the temple of Isis at Philae. The form also appears, adapted, in Nubian and later Ethiopian sacred architecture.
