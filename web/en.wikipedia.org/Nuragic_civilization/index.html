Nuragic civilization

The Nuragic civilization was a prehistoric culture that flourished on the island of Sardinia from roughly the 18th century BC until the Roman conquest in 238 BC. It is named for its most characteristic monument, the nuraghe, a truncated cone-shaped tower built of massive dry-laid stones, of which more than 7,000 survive.

Origins and development

The civilization developed from earlier Neolithic cultures on the island during the Bronze Age. The first protonuraghes appeared around 1800 BC; single-tower nuraghes followed, and in the later Bronze Age many were expanded into complex fortresses with multiple towers and surrounding villages, the finest example being Su Nuraxi di Barumini, a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Society and culture

Nuragic society was organised in tribal cantons, probably led by chiefs based in the nuraghes. The culture produced distinctive small bronze figurines (bronzetti) depicting warriors, priests, animals, and boats, as well as sacred wells and giants' graves (large collective tombs). Sardinians of this era were accomplished metalworkers and seafarers, trading with Cyprus, Mycenaean Greece, Etruria, and Iberia; some scholars connect them with the Sherden, one of the "Sea Peoples" recorded in Egyptian sources.

Decline

From the 9th century BC the Phoenicians and then Carthage established coastal colonies, confining Nuragic culture increasingly to the interior. Roman annexation ended its political independence, although its language and customs survived in the island's mountainous Barbagia region for centuries.
