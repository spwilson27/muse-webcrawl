SOLAS Convention

The International Convention for the Safety of Life at Sea (SOLAS) is an international maritime treaty administered by the International Maritime Organization (IMO) that sets minimum safety standards in the construction, equipment and operation of merchant ships. The first version was adopted in 1914 in response to the sinking of the Titanic two years earlier; the current convention dates from 1974 and has been amended many times. It is generally regarded as the most important international treaty concerning the safety of merchant ships.

SOLAS sets global standards for shipboard lifesaving appliances.

History

The 1914 convention, prompted by the sinking of RMS Titanic in 1912, introduced requirements for lifeboats for all aboard, iceberg patrols and continuous radio watch. Successive versions followed in 1929, 1948 and 1960. The 1974 convention introduced the "tacit acceptance" amendment procedure, under which amendments enter into force on a set date unless a specified fraction of parties object — allowing the treaty to keep pace with technology. Major amendments have addressed bulk carrier safety, the ISM Code on safe management, and, after 2001, maritime security through the ISPS Code.

Provisions

The convention's chapters cover, among other subjects: surveys and certification; construction, subdivision and stability; fire protection and lifesaving appliances; radiocommunications (linked to the GMDSS distress system); safety of navigation; carriage of cargoes and dangerous goods; nuclear ships; and safety and security management. Flag states are responsible for ensuring their ships comply, and port states may inspect foreign vessels under port state control.
