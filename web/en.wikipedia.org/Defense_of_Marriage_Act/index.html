The Defense of Marriage Act (DOMA) was a United States federal law passed by the 104th United States Congress and signed into law by President Bill Clinton on 21 September 1996. It banned federal recognition of same-sex marriage by limiting the definition of marriage to the union of one man and one woman, and it allowed states to refuse to recognise same-sex marriages granted under the laws of other states.

Background and passage

DOMA was introduced amid litigation in Hawaii — the Baehr v. Lewin case of 1993 — that raised the prospect that one state might legalise same-sex marriage and that the Full Faith and Credit Clause might oblige other states to recognise it. The bill passed the House 342–67 and the Senate 85–14. Clinton signed it while personally describing marriage as between a man and a woman, though he later came to oppose the law and urged its repeal.

Provisions

Section 2 provided that no state would be required to give effect to a public act, record, or judicial proceeding of another state respecting a same-sex relationship treated as a marriage. Section 3 defined "marriage" and "spouse," for all federal purposes — more than a thousand statutes and regulations involving taxes, Social Security, immigration, and federal employee benefits — as applying only to opposite-sex unions.

Legal challenges and repeal

In United States v. Windsor (26 June 2013), the Supreme Court held Section 3 unconstitutional under the Fifth Amendment's guarantee of equal protection, after Edith Windsor was required to pay estate taxes that a surviving spouse in a recognised marriage would not have owed. Two years later, Obergefell v. Hodges (2015) held that the Fourteenth Amendment requires all states to license and recognise same-sex marriages, rendering Section 2 inoperative. Congress formally repealed DOMA with the Respect for Marriage Act, signed on 13 December 2022, which requires federal and interstate recognition of marriages valid where celebrated.
