Scientific misconduct

Scientific misconduct is the violation of the standard codes of scholarly conduct and ethical behavior in the publication of professional scientific research. A central definition, used by many governments and institutions, comprises fabrication (making up data or results), falsification (manipulating research materials, equipment, processes, or data), and plagiarism (appropriating another person's ideas, results, or words without credit) — collectively "FFP". Honest error and differences of opinion are not misconduct.

Forms and related practices

Beyond FFP, questionable research practices include duplicate and salami publication, gift and ghost authorship, selective reporting of results, HARKing (hypothesizing after results are known), p-hacking, image manipulation, and failure to disclose conflicts of interest. Journals may respond with corrections or retractions; watchdog projects such as Retraction Watch track the growing number of retracted papers, which number in the tens of thousands in its database.

Notable cases

Famous cases include the Piltdown Man hoax in paleoanthropology; Hwang Woo-suk's fabricated stem cell research in South Korea; Jan Hendrik Schön's falsified physics results at Bell Labs; Diederik Stapel's fabricated social psychology data; and Andrew Wakefield's fraudulent 1998 paper linking the MMR vaccine to autism, retracted in 2010. Investigations are typically conducted by the researcher's institution, funders, or national bodies such as the U.S. Office of Research Integrity.

Prevalence and causes

Survey studies suggest a small percentage of scientists admit to fabricating or falsifying data, while a much larger share report questionable practices. Incentive structures — pressure to publish in high-impact journals, secure funding, and win promotion — are widely cited as drivers, motivating reforms such as preregistration, open data, and registered reports.
