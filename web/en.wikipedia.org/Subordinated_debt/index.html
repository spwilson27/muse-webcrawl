Subordinated debt

In finance, subordinated debt (also known as a subordinated debenture or junior debt) is a loan or security that ranks below other loans or securities with regard to claims on a company's assets or earnings. Subordinated debt is thus more risky than unsubordinated (senior) debt, and creditors holding it are repaid only after senior creditors have been paid in full in the event of bankruptcy or liquidation.

Characteristics

Because of its lower priority, subordinated debt generally carries a higher interest rate than senior debt to compensate investors for the additional risk. It typically has a longer maturity and may include provisions restricting repayment before senior obligations are met. Mezzanine capital is a common form of subordinated financing, often combined with equity-like features such as warrants.

Bank capital

Subordinated debt plays a role in bank regulation: because it absorbs losses ahead of depositors and senior creditors while the bank remains a going concern or in resolution, qualifying subordinated instruments count as Tier 2 capital under the Basel III framework. Instruments such as contingent convertible bonds ("CoCos") convert to equity or are written down when capital falls below a threshold.

Seniority structure

A typical capital structure, from first claim to last, runs: senior secured debt; senior unsecured debt; subordinated debt; preferred stock; and common stock.
