Leviathan (Hobbes book)

Author: Thomas Hobbes
Published: 1651
Language: English (also Latin edition, 1668)
Subject: Political philosophy

Leviathan or The Matter, Forme and Power of a Commonwealth Ecclesiasticall and Civil, commonly referred to as Leviathan, is a book written by the English philosopher Thomas Hobbes and published in 1651. Its name derives from the biblical Leviathan. The work concerned the structure of society and legitimate government, and is regarded as one of the earliest and most influential examples of social contract theory.

Content

Hobbes argues that in the state of nature, human life would be "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short", because individuals, roughly equal in ability, compete for scarce resources and live in constant fear of violent death. To escape this condition, people agree to a social contract, surrendering most of their natural rights to an absolute sovereign — the Leviathan — in exchange for security and order.

The book is divided into four parts: Of Man; Of Commonwealth; Of a Christian Commonwealth; and Of the Kingdom of Darkness. Hobbes sets out his materialist account of human nature, his doctrine of the commonwealth, his interpretation of Scripture, and his critique of scholasticism and the Roman Catholic Church.

Frontispiece

The famous frontispiece, etched by Abraham Bosse, depicts a giant crowned figure holding a sword and a crozier, its torso and arms composed of more than three hundred individual human figures, symbolising the multitude united in one person: the sovereign.

Influence

Leviathan profoundly influenced Western political philosophy, shaping later thinkers from John Locke to modern theorists of the state. See also state of nature and absolutism.
