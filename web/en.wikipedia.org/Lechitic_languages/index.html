Lechitic languages

The Lechitic languages (or Lekhitic languages) are a subgroup of the West Slavic languages, comprising Polish, Kashubian, and the extinct Polabian language, and sometimes Silesian. They were originally spoken by the Lechitic tribes in the region between the Oder and Vistula rivers, in what is now Poland and eastern Germany. The name derives from the legendary Lech, the mythical founder of the Polish nation.

Classification and members

  • Polish — the official language of Poland, with around 45 million speakers.
  • Kashubian — spoken in Pomerania in north-central Poland, recognised as a regional language in Poland; sometimes considered a dialect of Polish.
  • Silesian — spoken in Upper Silesia; its status as a separate language or a Polish dialect is debated.
  • Polabian — extinct since the 18th century, formerly spoken by Slavs in the lower Elbe region of present-day Germany.
  • Pomeranian — a group of extinct or moribund dialects of the Baltic coast, of which Slovincian (extinct in the early 20th century) was the last-surviving variety besides Kashubian.

Features

Lechitic languages share several innovations from Common Slavic, including the development of the nasal vowels (in Polish and Kashubian, the only Slavic languages to retain them), the lack of the change of g to h found in Czech, Slovak, and Ukrainian, and characteristic treatments of the Proto-Slavic groups *tort, *tolt (Polish trot, tłot). Polabian, documented in wordlists compiled before its extinction, preserved archaic features lost in Polish.

History

The Lechitic dialects differentiated from Common Slavic during the early medieval period as Slavic tribes settled Central Europe. Polish emerged as the dominant language of the Polish state from the 10th century onward, while the western Lechitic varieties gradually receded under German linguistic and political pressure, a process completed with the extinction of Polabian around 1756.
