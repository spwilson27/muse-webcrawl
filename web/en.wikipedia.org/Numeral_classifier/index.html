Numeral classifier

FieldMorphology, linguistic typology
Found inChinese, Japanese, Korean, Thai, Vietnamese, Mayan languages, and others

A numeral classifier — often called a measure word or counter word — is a word or morpheme used in some languages together with a numeral to indicate the count of nouns. Classifiers typically categorise the noun by shape, animacy, function, or arrangement: in Mandarin Chinese one says sān běn shū ("three [bound-volume] book") and sān tiáo yú ("three [long-flexible] fish"), using a different classifier for each kind of object.

Distribution and types

Numeral classifier systems are characteristic of many East and Southeast Asian languages (Chinese languages, Japanese, Korean, Thai, Vietnamese, Malay, Burmese), of numerous Austronesian and Australian Aboriginal languages, and of many indigenous languages of the Americas, including Mayan languages such as Yucatec. Linguists distinguish sortal classifiers, which categorise the referent itself, from mensural classifiers (true measure words), which name units or containers, like "cup" in "three cups of rice". English lacks a general sortal system but retains mensural constructions and vestiges such as "head" of cattle.

Examples

Japanese uses counters such as -hon for long cylindrical objects, -mai for flat objects, and -hiki for small animals. Bengali, Persian, and many languages of South Asia also use classifiers to varying degrees. Large classifier inventories may number in the hundreds, although everyday speech uses a few dozen, and many systems include a default or general classifier (Mandarin gè) usable with most nouns.
