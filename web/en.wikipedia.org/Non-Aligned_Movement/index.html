The Non-Aligned Movement (NAM) is a forum of states not formally aligned with or against any major power bloc. It was founded during the Cold War by countries seeking a third path between the Western alliance led by the United States and the Eastern bloc led by the Soviet Union, and it remains the largest grouping of states after the United Nations itself.

Origins

The movement grew out of the 1955 Bandung Conference in Indonesia, where 29 Asian and African nations asserted the solidarity of the decolonising world. At the Belgrade Summit of September 1961, leaders including Jawaharlal Nehru of India, Josip Broz Tito of Yugoslavia, Gamal Abdel Nasser of Egypt, Kwame Nkrumah of Ghana, and Sukarno of Indonesia formally launched the movement. Its principles — the Panchsheel — emphasised sovereignty, non-aggression, non-interference, equality, and peaceful coexistence.

Cold War and after

NAM championed decolonisation, opposed apartheid South Africa, and campaigned for a New International Economic Order giving developing countries fairer terms in trade and resource ownership. Its unity was often strained: members fought wars with each other (India–Pakistan, Iran–Iraq) and some leaned heavily toward one superpower. After 1991 the movement lost its original rationale but persisted, refocusing on development, UN reform, and South–South cooperation. Summits continue roughly every three years, with chairmanship rotating among members; recent summits were hosted by Venezuela (2016), Azerbaijan (2019, extended), and Uganda (2024).
