National Park Service Organic Act

Enacted: 25 August 1916

Signed by: President Woodrow Wilson

Created: National Park Service

Codified: 54 U.S.C. §§ 100101 et seq. (recodified 2014)

The National Park Service Organic Act is a United States federal law enacted on 25 August 1916 that established the National Park Service (NPS), an agency within the Department of the Interior, to administer the national parks and monuments then under the department's control. Before 1916 the parks — including Yellowstone, Yosemite and others — had been managed individually, at times with U.S. Army protection. Businessman Stephen Mather, who became the Service's first director, and his aide Horace Albright were central to lobbying for the Act.

Mandate

The Act famously states that the Service shall "promote and regulate the use of the Federal areas known as national parks, monuments, and reservations" so as "to conserve the scenery and the natural and historic objects and the wild life therein and to provide for the enjoyment of the same in such manner and by such means as will leave them unimpaired for the enjoyment of future generations." This dual mandate — conservation and public enjoyment, with non-impairment as the guiding standard — has governed the National Park System ever since and has been a recurring source of legal and policy debate when the two purposes conflict.

Legacy

The system administered under the Act has grown to more than 430 units covering over 85 million acres, including national parks, monuments, historical parks, recreation areas, seashores and battlefields. Later statutes, including the 1970 General Authorities Act and the 1978 Redwood amendments, reaffirmed that park purposes are cumulative and that conservation takes precedence where conflicts arise. In 2014 the Organic Act's provisions were recodified as part of Title 54 of the United States Code.
