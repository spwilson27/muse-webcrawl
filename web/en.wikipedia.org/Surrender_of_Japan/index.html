Surrender of Japan

The surrender of Japan in August 1945 brought the Second World War to an end. Emperor Hirohito announced Japan's acceptance of the Potsdam Declaration in a radio broadcast on 15 August 1945, and the formal instrument of surrender was signed aboard the battleship USS Missouri in Tokyo Bay on 2 September 1945, in the presence of representatives of the Allied powers.

Background

By the summer of 1945 Japan's navy and air power were destroyed, its cities devastated by firebombing, and its home islands blockaded, but its leadership remained divided over surrender terms. The Potsdam Declaration of 26 July demanded unconditional surrender of the Japanese armed forces while promising the emperor's fate would be determined by the Japanese people's free will — a point Tokyo's reply left ambiguous.

The decisive events

The United States dropped atomic bombs on Hiroshima (6 August) and Nagasaki (9 August); the Soviet Union declared war and invaded Manchuria on 9 August. In a deadlocked cabinet, Emperor Hirohito broke the tie in favour of accepting the Potsdam terms — his "sacred decision" — and, after surviving an attempted military coup, recorded the Imperial Rescript on Surrender. His broadcast of 15 August, heard by most Japanese subjects for the first time, announced the decision.

Formal ceremony and aftermath

On 2 September 1945, Japanese envoys signed the instrument of surrender aboard USS Missouri; General MacArthur signed for the Allied powers, followed by representatives of the United States, China, the United Kingdom, the Soviet Union and other nations. Japan was then occupied by Allied forces under MacArthur until 1952, demilitarised and democratised, while war-crimes trials were held in Tokyo.
