Frequency mixer

In electronics, a mixer, or frequency mixer, is an electrical circuit that creates new frequencies from two signals applied to it. Typically one signal is the input (RF) and the other comes from a local oscillator (LO); a nonlinear device such as a diode or transistor produces an output containing the sum and difference of the two frequencies. The process is called heterodyning.

Use in receivers

Mixers are central to the superheterodyne receiver architecture invented by Edwin Armstrong: the incoming radio signal is mixed with a tunable local oscillator to produce a fixed intermediate frequency (IF), which can then be amplified and filtered with high selectivity. Mixers are also used for upconversion in transmitters, in phase-locked loops, and in measurement instruments.

Types

Common implementations include single-diode mixers, balanced and double-balanced diode ring mixers (which suppress both input signals at the output), and active transistor mixers such as the Gilbert cell. Key performance parameters include conversion loss or gain, noise figure, isolation between ports, and intermodulation distortion.
