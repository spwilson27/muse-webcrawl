Sexual selection

The peacock's train, a classic product of sexual selection

Sexual selection is a mode of natural selection in which members of one sex choose members of the opposite sex to mate with (intersexual selection, usually female choice), and members of the same sex compete for access to mates (intrasexual selection). Proposed by Charles Darwin in On the Origin of Species (1859) and developed in The Descent of Man, and Selection in Relation to Sex (1871), it explains traits — such as the peacock's train, deer antlers, and elaborate bird song — that appear detrimental to survival yet aid reproduction.

Mechanisms

Intrasexual competition favours weapons, large size, and aggression used in contests between (usually) males. Intersexual choice favours ornaments and displays preferred by the choosing sex. Several models explain why preferences evolve: Fisherian runaway selection, in which a preference and a trait become genetically linked and amplify each other; "good genes" or indicator models, in which costly ornaments honestly signal health and genetic quality (the handicap principle); and sensory bias, in which preferences exploit pre-existing features of the chooser's sensory system.

Parental investment

Following Robert Trivers (1972), the sex that invests more in offspring — typically females, through eggs, gestation, or care — is expected to be the choosier sex, while the lower-investing sex competes for mates. In sex-role-reversed species such as pipefish and jacanas, males invest more and females compete and are more ornamented, supporting the theory.

Scope

Sexual selection is now studied across taxa, in plants as well as animals, and is implicated in speciation, since diverging mate preferences can split populations. Modern work also examines sexual conflict — the evolutionary antagonism arising when the reproductive interests of the sexes differ — and post-copulatory processes such as sperm competition and cryptic female choice.
