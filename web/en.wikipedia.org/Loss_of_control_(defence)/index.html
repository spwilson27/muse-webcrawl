Loss of control (defence)

Loss of control is a partial defence to murder in English criminal law. Introduced by the Coroners and Justice Act 2009, it replaced the former defence of provocation. If successful, the defence reduces a conviction for murder to one of voluntary manslaughter.

Statutory basis

Under sections 54 and 55 of the Coroners and Justice Act 2009, the defence applies where a defendant's acts or omissions in killing resulted from a loss of self-control, the loss of control had a qualifying trigger, and a person of the defendant's sex and age, with a normal degree of tolerance and self-restraint and in the circumstances of the defendant, might have reacted in the same or in a similar way.

Qualifying triggers

A qualifying trigger includes the defendant's fear of serious violence from the victim, things said or done (or both) of an extremely grave character which caused the defendant to have a justifiable sense of being seriously wronged, or a combination of the two. Sexual infidelity is expressly to be disregarded as a trigger, a provision introduced after controversy over defendants who killed partners in cases of infidelity. Delayed reactions are permitted: the loss of control need not be sudden, though evidence of a considered desire for revenge will exclude the defence.

Reform of provocation

The defence was enacted after criticism of the old provocation doctrine, particularly in cases of domestic abuse and so-called "nagging and shagging" defences; reformers sought a doctrine better able to account for defendants – notably victims of prolonged abuse – whose loss of control built up over time rather than erupting suddenly.
