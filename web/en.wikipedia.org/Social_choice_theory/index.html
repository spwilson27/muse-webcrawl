Social choice theory

Social choice studies how individual preferences are combined into collective decisions

Social choice theory is a theoretical framework for analysing how individual opinions, preferences and interests are aggregated to reach a collective decision or social state. It draws on economics, political science, mathematics and philosophy, and underlies the formal study of voting systems, constitutions and welfare economics.

Key results

The field's foundational result is Arrow's impossibility theorem (1951), in which Kenneth Arrow showed that no rank-order voting system can simultaneously satisfy a small set of seemingly reasonable fairness criteria when there are three or more options — unless it is a dictatorship. The Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem similarly shows that every reasonable voting rule with three or more outcomes is vulnerable to strategic manipulation. Earlier insights include the Condorcet paradox, showing that majority preferences can cycle even when each individual's preferences are consistent, and the work of the Marquis de Condorcet and Jean-Charles de Borda in the 18th century.

Applications

Social choice theory informs the design of electoral systems, constitutional rules, fair division procedures and judgement aggregation in committees and courts, and it connects to welfare economics through social welfare functions and Amartya Sen's work on poverty, famine and the liberal paradox.
