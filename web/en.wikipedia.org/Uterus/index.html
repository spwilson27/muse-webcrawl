The uterus (pl. uteri) or womb is the organ in the reproductive system of most female mammals, including humans, that accepts a fertilized ovum and supports the development of the embryo and fetus. It is situated in the pelvis, between the bladder and the rectum, and is roughly the size and shape of an inverted pear in non-pregnant women.

Anatomy

The uterus is a hollow, thick-walled muscular organ divided into the fundus (top), body (corpus), and cervix (neck), which opens into the vagina. Its wall consists of three layers: the inner endometrium, which thickens each menstrual cycle and is shed if no pregnancy occurs; the middle myometrium, a powerful muscle that contracts during labour; and the outer perimetrium. It is held in position by ligaments including the broad, round, and uterosacral ligaments.

Function

The uterus provides the environment for implantation, sustains the placenta during pregnancy, and drives delivery through contractions of the myometrium. Outside of pregnancy, the endometrium undergoes the menstrual cycle under hormonal control.

Clinical aspects

Common uterine conditions include fibroids (leiomyomas), endometriosis, adenomyosis, endometrial cancer, and uterine prolapse. Diagnosis uses ultrasound, hysteroscopy, and biopsy; treatments range from medication to hysterectomy (surgical removal).
