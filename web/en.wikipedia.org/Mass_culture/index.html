Mass culture

Culture produced for and consumed by large audiences

Mass culture is the set of cultural products, practices and ideas that arise from the mass production and mass distribution of culture for large, heterogeneous audiences. It is closely associated with mass media — print, radio, cinema, television and, most recently, the internet — and with cultural industries that produce standardised entertainment and information as commodities. The concept has been central to debates about the effects of commercial media on taste, politics and social cohesion since the early twentieth century.

Critical theory

Thinkers of the Frankfurt School, notably Theodor Adorno and Max Horkheimer in Dialectic of Enlightenment (1944/47), attacked the "culture industry" for producing formulaic works that pacify audiences and integrate them into capitalist consumer society. Walter Benjamin, by contrast, saw emancipatory potential in mechanical reproduction's destruction of the "aura" of unique artworks. Conservative critics such as T. S. Eliot and F. R. Leavis lamented mass culture's levelling of standards from a different direction.

Later views

From the 1960s, cultural studies scholars such as Stuart Hall and the Birmingham Centre for Contemporary Cultural Studies argued that audiences actively interpret and resist mass-cultural texts, and that popular culture is a site of negotiation rather than simple manipulation. The rise of participatory digital media — fan fiction, remixing and social platforms — has further blurred the line between producers and consumers.
