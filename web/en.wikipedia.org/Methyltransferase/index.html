Methyltransferase

Methyltransferases are a large group of enzymes that catalyse the transfer of a methyl group (–CH3) from a donor molecule to an acceptor. In most biological methylation reactions the donor is S-adenosyl-L-methionine (SAM), which becomes S-adenosyl-L-homocysteine after donating the methyl group; vitamin B12-dependent enzymes use methylcobalamin instead. Methylation can modify DNA, RNA, proteins, lipids and small molecules, and methyltransferases are classed in EC 2.1.1.

DNA methyltransferases

DNA methyltransferases add methyl groups to cytosine (forming 5-methylcytosine, usually at CpG sites) or adenine. In mammals, DNMT1 maintains methylation patterns after replication while DNMT3A and DNMT3B establish new ones; these marks are central to epigenetic regulation, genomic imprinting, X-chromosome inactivation and silencing of transposons. Bacterial DNA methyltransferases form part of restriction-modification systems defending against foreign DNA.

Protein and RNA enzymes

Histone methyltransferases methylate lysine and arginine residues on histones, creating marks read by other proteins to activate or repress genes; protein arginine methyltransferases (PRMTs) and lysine methyltransferases are important drug targets in cancer. RNA methyltransferases install modifications such as m6A on messenger RNA, affecting splicing, stability and translation.

Other roles

Catechol O-methyltransferase (COMT) breaks down catecholamine neurotransmitters and drugs such as levodopa; thiopurine methyltransferase (TPMT) activity determines thiopurine drug toxicity and is tested pharmacogenetically.
