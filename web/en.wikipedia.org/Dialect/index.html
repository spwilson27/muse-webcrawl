Dialect

A dialect is a variety of a language distinguished by features of vocabulary, grammar and pronunciation, typically associated with a particular region (a regional dialect) or social group (a social dialect or sociolect). Dialects are mutually intelligible varieties of the same language, in contrast to separate languages, though the boundary between "dialect" and "language" is shaped as much by politics and identity as by linguistics — a point captured in Max Weinreich's aphorism that "a language is a dialect with an army and navy".

Dialect areas of a language form a continuum across a region.

Terminology

Linguists use variety as a neutral term for any characteristic form of a language. A regional dialect continuum is a network of local varieties that shade gradually into one another, so that neighbouring dialects are mutually intelligible while distant ones may not be. The study of dialects is called dialectology.

Standard and non-standard dialects

A standard dialect is the variety codified for education, government and media, and it typically enjoys social prestige. Non-standard dialects are not linguistically deficient: all dialects are equally systematic and rule-governed. Prestige differences reflect social attitudes rather than any inherent linguistic quality.

Dialect and accent

The term accent refers only to pronunciation, whereas a dialect encompasses grammar and vocabulary as well. Every speaker has both a dialect and an accent. Regional variation within a country can be striking: English, for example, includes dialects such as Geordie, Scouse, Appalachian English and African American Vernacular English.
