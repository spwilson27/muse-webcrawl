Multiculturalism

Multiculturalism
ConceptCoexistence and public recognition of multiple cultures within one society
Key thinkersWill Kymlicka, Charles Taylor, Bhikhu Parekh
Official policiesCanada (1971), Australia (1970s)

Multiculturalism describes, first, the presence of several distinct cultural or ethnic groups within a society, and second, a range of policies and philosophies that promote the maintenance of cultural diversity and equal standing for different traditions, rather than requiring assimilation into a single dominant culture.

History

The term came into wide use in the 1960s and 1970s. Canada adopted an official policy of multiculturalism in 1971 under Prime Minister Pierre Trudeau, and in 1982 multiculturalism was recognized in the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms; the Canadian Multiculturalism Act followed in 1988. Australia adopted similar policies in the 1970s as it dismantled the White Australia policy. Several European states also experimented with multicultural policies during this period.

Philosophy

Political theorists such as Charles Taylor, in his essay "The Politics of Recognition" (1992), argued that recognition of identity is a fundamental human need. Will Kymlicka's Liberalism, Community and Culture (1989) and Multicultural Citizenship (1995) defended group-differentiated rights for national minorities and immigrant communities within a liberal framework.

Criticism and debate

Critics argue that multiculturalism can entrench segregation, undermine social cohesion, or tolerate practices at odds with liberal values. In the 2010s several European leaders declared state multiculturalism a failure, while supporters respond that integration outcomes depend on policy design and that diversity itself is not the cause of social problems. Debates continue over education, language policy, religious accommodation, and national identity.
