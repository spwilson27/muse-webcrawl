Thomas Hopkins Gallaudet (December 10, 1787 – September 10, 1851) was an American educator who, along with Laurent Clerc and Mason Cogswell, co-founded the first permanent institution for the education of the deaf in North America, and he became its first principal. The institution opened in Hartford, Connecticut, in 1817 as the Connecticut Asylum for the Education and Instruction of Deaf and Dumb Persons, later known as the American School for the Deaf.

Founding of deaf education in America

Gallaudet, a Yale graduate who had studied theology, became interested in deaf education after meeting Alice Cogswell, the deaf daughter of his Hartford neighbour Dr. Mason Cogswell. In 1815 he travelled to Europe to study methods of teaching the deaf. Finding the British oralist schools unwilling to share their methods, he went to Paris, where the Abbé Sicard demonstrated the French manual method; Gallaudet returned to America with the deaf teacher Laurent Clerc, and the two men, together with Cogswell, established the Hartford school. Their success established sign-language-based education in the United States and influenced deaf schools across the country.

Legacy

Gallaudet University in Washington, D.C., the world's only university designed for deaf and hard-of-hearing students, is named for him; its charter was signed by President Abraham Lincoln in 1864. His son Edward Miner Gallaudet became the university's first president, while another son, Thomas Gallaudet, was an Episcopal priest who worked with deaf congregations. A well-known statue on the university campus depicts Thomas Hopkins Gallaudet with the young Alice Cogswell.
