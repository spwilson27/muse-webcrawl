A salp (plural salps or salpae) is a barrel-shaped, planktonic tunicate — a free-swimming chordate related, surprisingly, to vertebrates. Salps move by contracting bands of muscle around their gelatinous bodies, pumping water through themselves in one of the most efficient jet-propulsion systems in nature. They are among the fastest-growing multicellular animals known.

Life cycle

Salps alternate between two generations. A solitary asexual form (oozoid) buds off long chains of clones — the aggregate generation — in which each individual (blastozooid) is a sequential hermaphrodite, female first, then male. Fertilised eggs develop inside the parent, nourished by a placenta-like structure, unusual for an invertebrate. Released embryos grow into the solitary generation, completing the cycle.

Feeding and ecology

Salps filter-feed on phytoplankton using an internal mucus net, capturing particles as small as bacteria. Their feeding pellets and carcasses sink rapidly, making salps important to the ocean's biological carbon pump: in the Southern Ocean, salp blooms can rival krill in abundance and may sequester significant carbon. They are eaten by fish, turtles and other predators, and their abundance fluctuates hugely with phytoplankton conditions.

Distribution

Salps occur in all the world's oceans but reach their greatest densities in cold, productive waters such as the Southern Ocean. Periodic mass strandings and blooms wash up on temperate beaches.
