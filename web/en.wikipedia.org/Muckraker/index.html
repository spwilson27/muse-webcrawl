Muckraker

The muckrakers were reform-minded journalists in the Progressive Era in the United States (roughly the 1890s to the 1920s) who exposed corruption, corporate wrongdoing and social injustice in mass-circulation magazines such as McClure's, Collier's, and Cosmopolitan. Their work helped spur major reforms in food safety, labour law, antitrust enforcement and municipal government.

The name

President Theodore Roosevelt coined the label in a speech on 14 April 1906, comparing crusading journalists to the "Man with the Muck-rake" in John Bunyan's The Pilgrim's Progress, who is so intent on raking filth that he never looks up. Roosevelt criticised an excess of muckraking even as he benefited from it; many of the journalists adopted the name as a badge of honour.

Notable muckrakers and works

  • Ida Tarbell — The History of the Standard Oil Company (1904), documenting John D. Rockefeller's monopolistic practices; the company was broken up in 1911.
  • Upton Sinclair — the novel The Jungle (1906), exposing Chicago meatpacking conditions; it led directly to the Meat Inspection Act and the Pure Food and Drug Act of 1906.
  • Lincoln Steffens — The Shame of the Cities (1904), on municipal corruption.
  • Jacob Riis — How the Other Half Lives (1890), photojournalism exposing New York tenement poverty (a precursor of the movement).
  • Ray Stannard Baker and Samuel Hopkins Adams — on railroad abuses and patent-medicine fraud respectively.

The term survives in modern usage for investigative journalists who expose wrongdoing by powerful institutions.
