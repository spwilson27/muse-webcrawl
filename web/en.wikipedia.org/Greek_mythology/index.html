Greek mythology

Greek mythology is the body of myths originally told by the ancient Greeks, and a genre of ancient Greek folklore, today absorbed alongside Roman mythology into the broader designation of classical mythology. These stories concern the ancient Greek religion's view of the origin and nature of the world; the lives and activities of deities, heroes, and mythological creatures; and the origins and significance of the ancient Greeks' cult and ritual practices.

The Greek myths were initially propagated in an oral-poetic tradition; today, they are known primarily from Greek literature, represented by the Iliad and Odyssey of Homer and the Theogony and Works and Days of Hesiod, all dating to the 8th century BC.

The gods

At the center of Greek mythology is the pantheon of deities who were said to reside on Mount Olympus, the highest mountain in Greece. The twelve Olympians were Zeus (king of the gods, sky and thunder), Hera, Poseidon (sea), Demeter (harvest), Athena (wisdom and war), Apollo (prophecy, music, healing), Artemis (the hunt), Ares (war), Aphrodite (love), Hephaestus (fire and craft), Hermes (travel and trade), and either Hestia or Dionysus (wine and ecstasy). Below them stood Hades, god of the underworld, and a host of lesser gods, nymphs, and spirits.

Origins of the world

In Hesiod's Theogony, the universe begins with Chaos, from which arose Gaia (Earth), Tartarus, and Eros. Gaia bore Uranus (Sky), whose children the Titans — led by Cronus — ruled until Cronus's son Zeus overthrew them in the ten-year war known as the Titanomachy. Humanity, in one tradition, was fashioned from clay by the Titan Prometheus, who stole fire from the gods and gave it to mortals, for which he was eternally punished.

Heroes

The age of heroes fills the space between the world of the gods and historical humanity. Famous heroes include Heracles, whose twelve labors are among the best-known of all Greek stories; Perseus, slayer of the Gorgon Medusa; Theseus, who killed the Minotaur in the Cretan Labyrinth; Jason, who led the Argonauts in quest of the Golden Fleece; and Achilles and Odysseus, the central figures of the Trojan War cycle.

The Trojan War

The Trojan War — sparked, in myth, by the Judgment of Paris and the abduction of Helen — was the defining event of the heroic age, recounted in Homer's Iliad and echoed through centuries of Greek and Roman literature, including Virgil's Aeneid.

Legacy

Greek mythology profoundly shaped Western art, literature, astronomy (through the names of planets and constellations) and psychology. Its stories were adapted by Rome — Zeus becoming Jupiter, Athena Minerva — and rediscovered with enthusiasm during the Renaissance; they remain a living source for modern fiction, film and games.
