The Carbonari (Italian for "charcoal burners") were members of a network of secret revolutionary societies founded in early 19th-century Italy. Inspired by liberal and nationalist ideals, they sought constitutional government and the liberation of Italy from foreign — principally Austrian — domination, playing a leading role in the uprisings of 1820–21 and 1831.

Organisation

The society borrowed rituals and symbolism from charcoal burners' guilds and from Freemasonry: members met in lodges called baracche, were organised in grades (apprentice and master), and used passwords and signs. Its membership drew heavily on army officers, professionals, and the middle classes.

Revolts

In July 1820 Carbonari officers under Guglielmo Pepe forced King Ferdinand I of the Two Sicilies to grant a constitution; a similar rising occurred in Piedmont in 1821. Both were crushed by Austrian intervention sanctioned by the Congress system of the Holy Alliance. After further failed revolts in 1831, the Carbonari declined, and many members gravitated toward Giuseppe Mazzini's Young Italy movement, which carried the cause of Italian unification — the Risorgimento — forward.
