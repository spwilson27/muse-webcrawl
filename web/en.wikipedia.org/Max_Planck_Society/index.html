The Max Planck Society for the Advancement of Science (German: Max-Planck-Gesellschaft zur Förderung der Wissenschaften e. V., abbreviated MPG) is a non-profit research organization funded by the German federal and state governments. It operates more than 80 Max Planck Institutes and research facilities devoted primarily to basic research, and is regarded as one of the world's leading scientific organizations.

History

The society was founded in Göttingen in 1948 as the successor to the Kaiser Wilhelm Society (established 1911), whose reputation had been compromised by its role under National Socialism. Its first president was the Nobel laureate Otto Hahn; the new society was named for the physicist Max Planck, who had been president of the Kaiser Wilhelm Society. During the Cold War the society grew steadily, absorbed institutes in the former East Germany after reunification in 1990, and expanded abroad with partner institutes outside Germany.

Organization

Max Planck Institutes are built around world-class directors under the "Harnack principle": the society selects outstanding scientists and gives them exceptional freedom and resources to define their own research. Institutes are grouped into three sections — Biology and Medicine; Chemistry, Physics and Technology; and the Human Sciences. The society's researchers have won numerous Nobel Prizes; laureates associated with the MPG and its predecessor include Albert Einstein, Werner Heisenberg, Emmanuelle Charpentier and Stefan Hell.

Education

Together with universities, the society runs International Max Planck Research Schools that train doctoral students from around the world.
