Drag culture

A drag queen performing

Drag culture is the subculture and performance art centred on drag queens (performers, usually men, who present in exaggerated feminine clothing and personas) and drag kings (performers presenting masculine personas). Drag involves clothing, makeup, hair, gesture, lip-syncing, comedy and dance, and has roots in theatre, vaudeville and the underground ball culture of Black and Latino LGBTQ communities in New York City.

History

Female impersonation is ancient in theatre: in Shakespeare's time, women were barred from the stage and boys played female roles. The term "drag" is often traced to the long dresses that trailed or "dragged" on the floor. In the late 19th century, performers such as Julian Eltinge became mainstream stars of female impersonation. In the 1920s "pansy craze" and later Harlem's Hamilton Lodge ball nurtured drag balls, which by the 1980s had developed into the ball culture documented in the film Paris Is Burning (1990), with its "houses", "mothers", voguing and categories of competition.

Mainstream popularity

Drag entered global popular culture through performers such as Divine, RuPaul — whose reality competition RuPaul's Drag Race (premiered 2009) became a worldwide franchise — and events such as DragCon. Drag story hours, brunches and tours are popular; at the same time, drag has been a focus of political debate and legislation in several US states and elsewhere.

Terminology

Ball and drag culture contributed much slang to mainstream English, including "shade", "read", "werk", "fierce", "tea" and "slay". A performer's "drag mother" mentors newcomers; a "house" is a chosen family and performance troupe.
