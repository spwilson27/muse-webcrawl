Road bicycle racing

The peloton in the mountains during a Grand Tour

Road bicycle racing is the cycle sport discipline of road cycling, held primarily on paved roads. It is the most popular form of bicycle racing, especially in Europe, and is governed internationally by the Union Cycliste Internationale (UCI). Road races vary in format from one-day events to multi-week stage races, and riders compete both individually and as members of professional teams.

Race formats

Major formats include one-day races—such as the five prestigious "Monuments" (Milan–San Remo, the Tour of Flanders, Paris–Roubaix, Liège–Bastogne–Liège, and the Giro di Lombardia)—and stage races, in which riders contest daily stages and accumulate an overall time classification. The three Grand Tours are the Tour de France (July), the Giro d'Italia (May), and the Vuelta a España (August–September), each lasting three weeks. Other formats include individual and team time trials, criteriums on short urban circuits, and the road races at the Olympic Games and UCI Road World Championships, whose winner wears the rainbow jersey for a year.

Tactics

Because air resistance dominates the energy cost of cycling at race speeds, riders cooperate and compete through drafting in a main group called the peloton. Teams protect their leader, chase breakaways, set up sprint finishes, and use domestiques to fetch supplies and shelter captains from the wind. Colored jerseys denote classification leaders—most famously the yellow jersey (maillot jaune) of the Tour de France, the pink jersey of the Giro, and the red jersey of the Vuelta. Mountain and sprint competitions award the polka-dot and green jerseys at the Tour.

History

Organized road racing began in France in the late 19th century; the first documented race ran from Paris to Rouen in 1869. The Tour de France was created in 1903 to promote the newspaper L'Auto and became the sport's flagship event. The sport's history has been repeatedly marked by doping controversies, from early stimulant use to the EPO era of the 1990s and 2000s, which culminated in Lance Armstrong being stripped of his seven Tour titles in 2012. Modern anti-doping relies on testing and the athlete biological passport.
