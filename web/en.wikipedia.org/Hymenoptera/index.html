Hymenoptera

Hymenoptera
Order of insects: ants, bees, wasps, sawflies
Described species: Over 150,000
Key traits: Two pairs of membranous wings; narrow “wasp waist” in Apocrita; haplodiploid sex determination in many groups

Hymenoptera is a large order of insects comprising the sawflies, wasps, bees and ants — over 150,000 described species and many more undescribed. The name derives from the membranous wings of the insects (Greek hymen, membrane, and ptera, wings). Hymenopterans range from tiny parasitoid wasps under 0.2 mm long to large hornets and sawflies.

Characteristics

Adults have two pairs of membranous wings hooked together in flight, chewing mouthparts (modified for lapping in bees), and large compound eyes. In the suborder Apocrita the first abdominal segment is fused to the thorax and separated by a narrow petiole — the wasp waist — giving the abdomen great mobility for stinging or egg-laying. Females possess an ovipositor modified in many lineages into a sting.

Reproduction and sociality

Many hymenopterans are haplodiploid: fertilised eggs become females and unfertilised eggs males. The order includes the most advanced eusocial insects — ants, honey bees, bumblebees and many wasps — with colonies of sterile workers headed by queens. Relatedness under haplodiploidy was proposed by W. D. Hamilton to help explain the evolution of altruism, though the question remains debated.

Ecology

Parasitoid wasps, which lay eggs in or on other arthropods, dominate the order's diversity and are important biological control agents. Bees are the world's principal pollinators of flowering plants, making Hymenoptera essential to ecosystems and agriculture. Ants shape soils and food webs worldwide.

Evolution

Hymenoptera appeared in the Triassic, with sawfly-like fossils among the oldest. Ants and bees diversified alongside flowering plants in the Cretaceous.
