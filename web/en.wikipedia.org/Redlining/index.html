Redlining

Redlining is the systematic denial of various services or goods by federal government agencies, local governments, or the private sector either directly or through the selective raising of prices. The term originated in the United States in the 1930s, when the Home Owners' Loan Corporation (HOLC) drew colour-coded maps of American cities grading neighbourhoods by perceived lending risk; areas deemed "hazardous" — most often those with large African American populations — were outlined in red.

History

Created in 1933 during the Great Depression, the HOLC refinanced mortgages to prevent foreclosures. Between 1935 and 1940 its City Survey Program produced "residential security maps" for more than 200 cities, grading neighbourhoods from A ("best") to D ("hazardous"). The Federal Housing Administration (FHA) adopted similar standards, and its underwriting manuals explicitly favoured racially homogeneous, white neighbourhoods.

Effects

Redlining restricted access to mortgages, insurance and credit in marked neighbourhoods for decades, depressing homeownership — the principal vehicle of middle-class wealth accumulation in the United States — among Black families. Scholars link the practice to the modern racial wealth gap, neighbourhood disinvestment, and patterns of segregation that persist long after the maps were withdrawn.

Legislation

The Fair Housing Act of 1968 prohibited discrimination in housing, and the Community Reinvestment Act of 1977 required banks to meet the credit needs of all communities in which they operate. Enforcement actions and lawsuits over discriminatory lending have continued into the twenty-first century, and researchers have documented modern analogues in insurance, retail siting and digital services.
