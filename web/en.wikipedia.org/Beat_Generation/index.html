Beat Generation

The Beat Generation was a literary subculture movement started by a group of authors whose work explored and influenced American culture and politics in the post–World War II era. The bulk of their work was published and popularised by Silent Generationers in the 1950s, although more commonly the "Beats" are identified with the authors who came of age in the late 1940s. Central elements of Beat culture are the rejection of standard narrative values, spiritual quest, exploration of American and Eastern religions, rejection of economic materialism, explicit portrayals of the human condition, experimentation with psychedelic drugs, and sexual liberation and exploration.

Origins

The movement began in the late 1940s around Columbia University in New York City, where Jack Kerouac, Allen Ginsberg, and William S. Burroughs met, along with Lucien Carr, Herbert Huncke, and Neal Cassady. The phrase "Beat Generation" was introduced by Kerouac around 1948; John Clellon Holmes brought it to public attention in his 1952 New York Times Magazine article "This Is the Beat Generation." "Beat" carried multiple meanings for Kerouac: beaten down, beatific, and the beat of jazz.

Key works

Allen Ginsberg's poem Howl (1956) — first performed at the Six Gallery reading in San Francisco in October 1955 — and Jack Kerouac's novel On the Road (1957) are considered the defining works of the movement. William S. Burroughs's Naked Lunch (1959) followed. Both Howl and Naked Lunch were the subjects of obscenity trials that ultimately expanded publishing freedom in the United States; the 1957 Howl trial ended with Judge Clayton W. Horn ruling the poem had "redeeming social importance."

Themes and style

Beat writing favoured spontaneity — Kerouac's "spontaneous prose" method was famously applied to the scroll manuscript of On the Road — jazz rhythms, vernacular speech, and first-person confession. The Beats wrote about road travel, bebop, Buddhism and Zen, drug experience, homosexuality (when it was criminalised), and the search for ecstatic experience, in conscious opposition to the conformity of the Eisenhower era. The San Francisco Renaissance poets, including Gary Snyder, Michael McClure, and Lawrence Ferlinghetti (whose City Lights bookstore published Howl), formed the movement's West Coast wing.

Beatniks and influence

In 1958 San Francisco columnist Herb Caen coined the pejorative "beatnik," yoking the Beats to the Soviet satellite Sputnik, and the mass media turned the movement into a caricature of berets, bongos, and goatees. Despite this, the Beats profoundly influenced the counterculture of the 1960s: Ginsberg became a bridge figure to the hippie era, and the movement's fingerprints are on the work of Bob Dylan, the Doors, and later punk and indie culture. The women of the Beat Generation — including Diane di Prima, Joyce Johnson, Hettie Jones, and Joanne Kyger — were long marginalised in accounts of the movement and have received renewed scholarly attention.
