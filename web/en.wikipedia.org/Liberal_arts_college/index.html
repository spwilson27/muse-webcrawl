A liberal arts college is a college with an emphasis on undergraduate study in the liberal arts and sciences. Such colleges aim to impart broad general knowledge and develop general intellectual capacities, in contrast to a professional, vocational or technical curriculum. Although found elsewhere, the model is most characteristic of the United States, where institutions such as Williams College, Amherst College and Swarthmore College are prominent examples.

Characteristics

Liberal arts colleges are typically small — most enrol fewer than 2,500 students — residential, and focused on teaching rather than research. Classes are small, student-to-faculty ratios are low, and undergraduates are taught almost exclusively by full-time faculty rather than graduate students. Curricula require breadth across the humanities, social sciences, natural sciences and mathematics, usually combined with depth in a chosen major.

History

The concept descends from the medieval liberal arts — the trivium (grammar, rhetoric, logic) and quadrivium (arithmetic, geometry, music, astronomy) — and from the curricula of early American colleges such as Harvard, founded in 1636. In the 19th century many small denominational colleges were founded across the United States, a large number of which survive as liberal arts colleges today.

Criticism and debate

Liberal arts colleges are frequently among the most expensive institutions in American higher education, and their value relative to vocational training is debated. Defenders argue that the model produces adaptable graduates with strong critical-thinking and communication skills; critics note high tuition and question employment outcomes. A small number of colleges follow distinctive models, such as the Great Books curriculum of St. John's College or the work-college model of Berea College.
