Reichsadler

The imperial eagle of the Holy Roman Empire.

The Reichsadler ("Imperial Eagle") is the heraldic eagle used by the Holy Roman Empire and by subsequent German states as a national emblem. Derived from the eagle standard (aquila) of the Roman legions, the black eagle displayed on a gold field became associated with the German kingship from the reign of Charlemagne and was borne by the Hohenstaufen emperors.

History

By the fifteenth century the imperial eagle was shown double-headed, most famously in the Quaternion Eagle, whose wing feathers carried the coats of arms of the Empire's constituent states. The double eagle symbolised the emperor's dual dignity — as Roman emperor and as German king — while the single-headed eagle was associated with the King of the Romans. After the Empire's dissolution in 1806, the Austrian Empire retained the double eagle, and the German Confederation adopted a double eagle in 1848.

At unification in 1871 the German Empire adopted a single-headed Reichsadler, redesigned in imperial style. The Weimar Republic introduced a simplified, modern eagle in 1919 (designed by Karl-Tobias Schwab), and a version of this design — popularly called the Bundadler ("federal eagle") — is the coat of arms of the Federal Republic of Germany today. During the Nazi period the eagle was combined with the swastika in the Parteiadler and a distinct Wehrmacht insignia.

Use today

The federal eagle appears on German coins, passports, flags of state institutions and the wall of the Bundestag chamber in Berlin, where a large aluminium version — nicknamed the "fat hen" (fette Henne) — hangs behind the president's chair.
