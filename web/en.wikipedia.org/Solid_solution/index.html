Solid solution

Substitutional solid solution: solute atoms replace solvent atoms in the lattice

A solid solution is a solid-state solution of one or more solutes in a solvent, in which the mixture remains a single homogeneous phase with one crystal structure. Such a mixture is a solution rather than a compound when the crystal structure of the solvent remains unchanged by the addition of the solutes, and when the mixture remains in a single homogeneous phase. Solid solutions are fundamental to metallurgy and mineralogy: most alloys and many minerals are solid solutions.

Types

In a substitutional solid solution, solute atoms occupy lattice sites in place of solvent atoms, as in brass (copper–zinc). In an interstitial solid solution, small solute atoms fit into the spaces between solvent atoms, as carbon does in iron to form steel. The Hume-Rothery rules describe the conditions favouring extensive substitutional solubility: similar atomic radii, the same crystal structure, similar electronegativity, and equal valence.

Properties and examples

Dissolved solute atoms distort the lattice and impede dislocation movement, so solid solutions are generally stronger and harder than the pure solvent metal — an effect exploited in solid-solution strengthening. Natural examples include olivine, a continuous solid solution between forsterite (Mg2SiO4) and fayalite (Fe2SiO4), and the plagioclase feldspars between albite and anorthite. Some systems show complete miscibility at all proportions; others exhibit a miscibility gap, exsolving into two phases on cooling.
