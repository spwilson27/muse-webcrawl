Venipuncture

Other namesPhlebotomy (blood collection)
PurposeVenous blood sampling; intravenous access
Common siteMedian cubital vein (antecubital fossa)
Performed byPhlebotomists, nurses, physicians

In medicine, venipuncture (also spelled venepuncture, venipuncture or venepuncture) is the process of obtaining intravenous access for the purpose of intravenous therapy or venous blood sampling of venous blood. It is one of the most commonly performed invasive medical procedures.

Procedure

Blood is most commonly obtained from the superficial veins of the upper limb, particularly the median cubital vein in the antecubital fossa (the bend of the elbow), because it is large, close to the skin, and relatively painless to puncture. A tourniquet is applied above the site to engorge the vein; the skin is disinfected; and a needle — often a butterfly needle or part of a vacuum-tube system such as a Vacutainer — is inserted at a shallow angle. A standard order of draw governs the sequence in which additive tubes are filled, to avoid cross-contamination of additives between tubes. After collection, pressure is applied to prevent a haematoma.

Uses

Venous blood samples support an enormous range of laboratory tests, including the full blood count, basic metabolic panel, coagulation studies, blood cultures, and blood typing and crossmatching. Venipuncture is also used therapeutically — therapeutic phlebotomy removes blood in conditions such as haemochromatosis and polycythaemia vera — and is the route by which blood donations are collected. Specialised healthcare workers who perform the procedure are called phlebotomists.

Complications

Complications are usually minor: pain, bruising, and haematoma formation are common; fainting (vasovagal syncope) occurs in a small minority of patients. Rarer complications include nerve injury, arterial puncture, infection, and thrombophlebitis.
