Strategic bombing during World War II

Strategic bombing during World War II was the sustained aerial attack on railways, harbours, cities, workers' housing, and industrial districts in enemy territory, conducted by all major belligerents. It was distinct from the close air support of ground forces and from tactical air power. Strategic bombing was intended to destroy the enemy's ability and will to wage war, and it grew into the largest long-term military campaign of the war, involving millions of personnel and vast quantities of resources.

The principal campaigns included the German Blitz on British cities (1940–41), the Allied Combined Bomber Offensive against Germany, the Soviet and Japanese campaigns in the east, and the American air campaign against Japan, which culminated in the firebombing of Tokyo and the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki in August 1945.

The European theatre

Germany opened the air war with attacks on Warsaw, Rotterdam and, from September 1940, the Blitz on London and other British cities. Britain's RAF Bomber Command under Arthur Harris pursued night-time area bombing of German cities, while the U.S. Eighth Air Force, arriving from 1942, claimed to favour daylight precision bombing of industrial targets. The Combined Bomber Offensive targeted the German aircraft industry (Pointblank), oil production, and transportation. Major episodes included the thousand-bomber raid on Cologne (1942), the firestorm at Hamburg (Operation Gomorrah, 1943), and the bombing of Dresden (February 1945), which remains controversial. By 1944–45 attacks on oil and transport had largely paralysed the German war economy, although German armaments production had in fact peaked only in mid-1944.

The Pacific theatre

In the Pacific, XXI Bomber Command B-29s initially bombed Japan from high altitude with disappointing results; under General Curtis LeMay the Americans switched in March 1945 to low-level night incendiary attacks. The firebombing of Tokyo on 9–10 March 1945 killed around 100,000 people, making it the deadliest air raid of the war. The campaign ended with the atomic bombings of Hiroshima (6 August) and Nagasaki (9 August 1945), which preceded Japan's surrender.

Effectiveness and ethics

The United States Strategic Bombing Survey concluded that air power had been decisive in Western Europe and had brought Japan to the point of surrender. Historians continue to debate the balance between industrial effects, civilian morale, and the diversion of German resources to air defence. Strategic bombing of cities also raised enduring ethical and legal questions about the targeting of civilians, debated since the war under the laws of armed conflict.
