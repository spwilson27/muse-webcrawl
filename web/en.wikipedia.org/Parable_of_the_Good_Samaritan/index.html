Parable of the Good Samaritan

The parable of the Good Samaritan is a parable of Jesus found in the Gospel of Luke (Luke 10:25–37). It tells of a traveller — in many translations implicitly Jewish — who is stripped of clothing, beaten, and left half dead alongside the road. A Jewish priest and then a Levite come by, but both avoid the man. Finally, a Samaritan — a member of a despised ethnic and religious out-group — happens upon him and immediately renders aid.

The parable

The story is prompted by a lawyer who asks Jesus, "Teacher, what shall I do to inherit eternal life?" and then, "wishing to justify himself," asks, "And who is my neighbour?" Jesus replies with the story:

A man was going down from Jerusalem to Jericho, and he fell among robbers, who stripped him and beat him and departed, leaving him half dead. Now by chance a priest was going down that road, and when he saw him he passed by on the other side. So likewise a Levite, when he came to the place and saw him, passed by on the other side. But a Samaritan, as he journeyed, came to where he was, and when he saw him, he had compassion. He went to him and bound up his wounds, pouring on oil and wine. Then he set him on his own animal and brought him to an inn and took care of him. And the next day he took out two denarii and gave them to the innkeeper, saying, "Take care of him, and whatever more you spend, I will repay you when I come back."

Jesus then asks which of the three "proved to be a neighbour to the man who fell among the robbers." The lawyer answers, "The one who showed him mercy," and Jesus concludes: "You go, and do likewise."

Context

The road from Jerusalem to Jericho descends about 1,000 metres over roughly 27 kilometres through rocky, bandit-prone desert — a plausible and notorious setting for first-century hearers. Relations between Jews and Samaritans were hostile: the Samaritans worshipped on Mount Gerizim rather than in Jerusalem, and the two peoples largely avoided contact. Casting a Samaritan as the moral hero would have shocked the original audience.

Interpretation

The parable is commonly read as teaching that a "neighbour" is defined not by kinship, religion, or nationality, but by merciful action toward anyone in need. Early church writers such as Augustine read it allegorically, with the wounded man as Adam, the Samaritan as Christ, and the inn as the Church. Modern scholars debate how much of the allegorical reading reflects the parable's original intent. The phrase "Good Samaritan" has entered general usage for someone who selflessly helps a stranger, and "Good Samaritan laws" in many jurisdictions protect rescuers from liability.

In art and culture

The parable has been depicted by artists including Rembrandt, Van Gogh and Delacroix, and is referenced in literature, film, and the names of hospitals and charities worldwide.
