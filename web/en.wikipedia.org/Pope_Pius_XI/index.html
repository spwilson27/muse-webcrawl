Pope Pius XI

Pope Pius XI (Italian: Pio XI; born Ambrogio Damiano Achille Ratti, 31 May 1857 – 10 February 1939) was head of the Catholic Church from 6 February 1922 to his death in February 1939. He is remembered for negotiating the Lateran Treaty of 1929, which created Vatican City as an independent state, for the encyclicals Quadragesimo anno (1931) on social justice and Mit brennender Sorge (1937) against Nazi ideology, and for his motto, "Pax Christi in Regno Christi" ("The Peace of Christ in the Reign of Christ").

Early life and career

Ratti was a scholar-priest: he earned doctorates in philosophy, canon law and theology, worked at the Ambrosian Library in Milan, and headed the Vatican Library from 1914. He was also a keen mountaineer in the Alps. In 1919 Benedict XV sent him as apostolic visitor and then nuncio to the reborn Poland, where he remained during the Soviet siege of Warsaw in 1920. He became Archbishop of Milan and a cardinal in 1921, and was elected pope on the fourteenth ballot of the 1922 conclave.

Pontificate

Pius XI concluded concordats with numerous states and settled the "Roman Question" with the Lateran Treaty of 11 February 1929, by which Italy recognized the sovereignty of the Holy See over the new Vatican City and the Church accepted financial compensation for the lost Papal States. He promoted Catholic Action, established the Feast of Christ the King (1925), founded Vatican Radio (1931) with Guglielmo Marconi's help, and issued more than thirty encyclicals, including Casti connubii (1930) on marriage and Divini Redemptoris (1937) against atheistic communism. In his later years he grew increasingly alarmed by Nazism and antisemitism: Mit brennender Sorge, smuggled into Germany and read from pulpits in March 1937, condemned Nazi violations of the 1933 Reichskonkordat and the regime's racial ideology, and he publicly declared in 1938 that Christians are "spiritually Semites". He died on 10 February 1939, on the eve of the Second World War.
