Dinoflagellate

The dinoflagellates (phylum Dinoflagellata) are a monophyletic group of single-celled eukaryotes constituting the phylum Dinoflagellata and are usually considered protists. Dinoflagellates are mostly marine plankton, but they also are common in freshwater habitats. Their populations vary with sea surface temperature, salinity and depth. Many dinoflagellates are photosynthetic, but a large fraction are in fact mixotrophic, combining photosynthesis with ingestion of prey. In terms of numbers of species, dinoflagellates are one of the largest groups of marine eukaryotes, although this group is substantially smaller than diatoms. Some species are endosymbionts of marine animals and play an important part in the biology of coral reefs.

Morphology

Most dinoflagellates are unicellular and possess two dissimilar flagella: a transverse flagellum that beats in a groove around the cell (the cingulum), providing forward motion and spin, and a longitudinal flagellum that trails behind and acts as a rudder. Many forms have a theca, an armour of cellulose plates within the cell membrane, giving genera such as Ceratium and Peridinium their characteristic shapes. The nucleus (dinokaryon) is unusual: its chromosomes remain condensed throughout the cell cycle and lack typical histones.

Ecology

Photosynthetic dinoflagellates are major primary producers in the oceans. Symbiotic dinoflagellates of the family Symbiodiniaceae (popularly called zooxanthellae) live within reef-building corals, anemones and giant clams, supplying their hosts with photosynthetic products; their expulsion causes coral bleaching. Other species are predators, parasites, or decomposers.

Under favourable conditions dinoflagellates can multiply rapidly to form harmful algal blooms known as red tides. Some species produce potent toxins — including saxitoxin (from Alexandrium), brevetoxins (Karenia brevis) and ciguatoxins (Gambierdiscus) — that accumulate in shellfish and fish and can poison humans and marine life. Many marine dinoflagellates are bioluminescent, producing flashes of blue light when disturbed.

Life cycle and fossils

Many dinoflagellates form dormant resting cysts that sink to the seabed. Fossil dinoflagellate cysts (dinocysts) have a rich record extending back to the Triassic and are used in biostratigraphy for dating and correlating sedimentary rocks, particularly in petroleum exploration.
