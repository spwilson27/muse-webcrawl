Liquidity crisis

In financial economics, a liquidity crisis is an acute shortage (or "drying up") of liquidity. Liquidity is the ability of an asset to be bought or sold quickly without affecting its price; a liquidity crisis occurs when participants in a financial market find that they cannot sell assets, roll over short-term debts, or obtain funding except at punitive prices (a "fire sale").

Causes

A liquidity crisis can arise when holders of short-term liabilities suddenly seek to withdraw them amid loss of confidence — producing a bank run — or when market-makers withdraw from trading and bid–ask spreads widen sharply. Because modern financial institutions fund long-term illiquid assets with short-term borrowing, a fall in asset prices can trigger margin calls and forced sales, which depress prices further in a self-reinforcing "liquidity spiral". Liquidity crises often accompany, and can deepen, solvency crises, as fundamentally sound assets must be sold at deep discounts.

Examples and responses

The global financial crisis of 2007–2008 involved a systemic liquidity crisis in interbank and repo markets. Central banks typically respond as lender of last resort, lending freely against good collateral at a penalty rate (Bagehot's rule), and through facilities and quantitative easing that restore market funding. The risk that an institution will be unable to meet obligations as they fall due is termed liquidity risk.
