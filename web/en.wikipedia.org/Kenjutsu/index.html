Kenjutsu (剣術, "sword technique") is an umbrella term for the traditional Japanese martial arts of swordsmanship taught in the schools (ryūha) of the samurai class. Unlike the modern sport-oriented kendo, kenjutsu schools preserve combative techniques — cutting, thrusting, parrying, disarming and grappling with the sword — largely through paired forms (kata) practised with wooden swords (bokken) or blunted metal blades.

History

Kenjutsu schools proliferated during the Sengoku period (1467–1600), when battlefield experience refined instruction in the use of the tachi and later the katana. Traditions such as Tenshin Shōden Katori Shintō-ryū (founded in the 15th century by Iizasa Chōisai Ienao) and Kashima Shintō-ryū are counted among the oldest extant martial traditions in Japan. During the peaceful Edo period, schools emphasised duelling, personal development and kata, and hundreds of ryūha existed. The Haitōrei edict of 1876, which banned the public wearing of swords, and the dissolution of the samurai class threatened the arts, but dedicated teachers preserved them.

Training

Instruction is transmitted through kata: pre-arranged sequences performed with a partner, covering distance (maai), timing, targeting and spirit. Many schools are sōgō bujutsu — comprehensive systems also teaching the spear, staff, naginata, grappling and other weapons. Students may also practise test cutting (tameshigiri) and, historically, sparring with protective equipment, which contributed to the development of kendo.

Modern practice

Today kenjutsu is practised in Japan and worldwide under organisations such as the Nihon Kobudo Kyokai, which recognises classical schools designated as cultural assets. Transmission remains lineage-based: senior practitioners receive teaching licences such as menkyo kaiden ("licence of total transmission") rather than modern dan grades.
