Morgenthau Plan

[Image: Henry Morgenthau Jr.]
ProposedSeptember 1944
AuthorHenry Morgenthau Jr., US Treasury Secretary
PurposeDeindustrialise Germany after World War II
StatusAbandoned; superseded by the Marshall Plan

The Morgenthau Plan was a proposal drawn up in 1944 by United States Secretary of the Treasury Henry Morgenthau Jr. for the treatment of Germany after the Second World War. It called for the complete deindustrialisation of Germany — dismantling its heavy industry, flooding or removing mines, and converting the country into a primarily agricultural and pastoral state — so that it could never again wage war.

Contents

The plan proposed partitioning Germany into northern and southern states, internationalising the Ruhr industrial district, transferring territories such as the Saar and Silesia to neighbouring countries, and liquidating German heavy industry. Germany would be reduced to a light agricultural economy, and its standard of living deliberately kept low.

Reception and abandonment

President Franklin D. Roosevelt and Winston Churchill initialled a memorandum endorsing the plan at the Quebec Conference in September 1944, but the proposal was leaked to the press and provoked strong criticism in the United States. The Secretaries of State and War, Cordell Hull and Henry Stimson, objected that it would cause mass starvation and lasting bitterness. Roosevelt retreated from the plan, and after his death the Truman administration formally abandoned it. Elements of "pastoralisation" briefly influenced early occupation policy (directive JCS 1067), but by 1947 policy shifted toward rebuilding the German economy, culminating in the Marshall Plan.

Legacy

Nazi propaganda seized on the plan to argue that Germany faced annihilation, stiffening resistance in the war's final months. The plan remains a subject of debate among historians about Allied intentions and the origins of the Cold War division of Germany.
