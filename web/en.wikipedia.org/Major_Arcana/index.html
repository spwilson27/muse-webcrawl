The Major Arcana are the 22 trump cards of a standard 78-card tarot pack, as distinct from the 56 suited cards of the Minor Arcana. Used originally for trick-taking games such as tarocchini and French tarot, in which they form the permanent trump suit, they acquired divinatory and esoteric interpretations from the late eighteenth century onward, and are the cards most often reproduced as individual images of the tarot.

History

The trumps originated in fifteenth-century northern Italy, where painted trionfi packs for courts such as Milan and Ferrara added a fifth sequence of allegorical picture cards to ordinary playing cards. The sequence—from the lowly Fool and Magician up through Fortune, Death, and the Last Judgement to the World—has been read as a medieval hierarchy of earthly and divine order. Standardized in the Tarot de Marseille pattern, the cards spread across Europe primarily as a game.

Esoteric interpretation

Beginning with the Comte de Mellet and Court de Gébelin in 1781, occult writers claimed Egyptian and Kabbalistic origins for the trumps, associating the 22 cards with the 22 letters of the Hebrew alphabet and the paths on the Tree of Life, an approach developed by Éliphas Lévi, the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, and Arthur Edward Waite, whose Rider–Waite deck (illustrated by Pamela Colman Smith) fixed the modern divinatory image of the Major Arcana. In cartomancy the majors are usually read as signifying major life themes and archetypal forces—the so-called "Fool's Journey" through the sequence.
