The Tlingit language (Tlingit: Lingít, pronounced roughly "klin-kit") is spoken by the Tlingit people of Southeast Alaska and Western Canada. It is a branch of the Na-Dene language family and is severely endangered: fluent native speakers number in the low hundreds, most of them elderly, though active revitalisation programmes are under way in Alaska and Canada.

Phonology

Tlingit is famous for its complex consonant system. It has a large inventory of obstruents, including a full series of ejective consonants and ejective fricatives, and a rare uvular series — but almost no labial consonants; the sound /l/ and its voiceless counterpart /ɬ/ are among the few sonorants. Ejective sounds and the lack of labials make Tlingit phonology unusual among the world's languages. The language is tonal in a limited way, with a two-way (or in some analyses variable) tone contrast marked in practical orthographies with an acute accent.

Grammar

Tlingit is a polysynthetic, strongly head-marking language with an extremely complex verb. Verbs carry prefixes encoding subject, object, aspect, mode and a system of "classifiers", and the language is notable for having no true independent tense. Word order in clauses is typically subject–object–verb. Nouns are relatively simple, though possession is marked and some nouns are obligatorily possessed.

History and revitalisation

Tlingit was first documented in the Russian colonial period, and a Cyrillic-based orthography was developed by Russian Orthodox missionaries; the modern Latin-based alphabet was developed in the 1960s by linguists including Constance Naish and Gillian Story. Twentieth-century boarding-school policies suppressed the language, causing steep decline. Since the late twentieth century, organisations such as the Sealaska Heritage Institute have run immersion preschools, master–apprentice programmes and university courses in Juneau and elsewhere to rebuild the speaker population.
