The Golgi apparatus, also called the Golgi complex or Golgi body, is a membrane-bound organelle found in most eukaryotic cells. It receives proteins and lipids from the endoplasmic reticulum, modifies and sorts them, and dispatches them in vesicles to their destinations — the plasma membrane, lysosomes, or secretion from the cell.

Structure

The organelle consists of stacks of flattened membrane sacs called cisternae. The stack has polarity: the cis face receives vesicles from the endoplasmic reticulum, while the trans face and the associated trans-Golgi network ship finished products. In plant cells the apparatus is dispersed as many small stacks called dictyosomes.

Functions

Within the Golgi, enzymes trim and extend sugar chains on glycoproteins and glycolipids (glycosylation), add sulfate and phosphate groups, and cleave some precursor proteins into their active forms, such as proinsulin into insulin. The organelle also synthesises polysaccharides — pectin and hemicellulose for plant cell walls — and produces lysosomes. Products are labelled with molecular "addresses", such as mannose-6-phosphate for lysosomal enzymes, and sorted into vesicles accordingly.

History

The Italian physician Camillo Golgi described the "internal reticular apparatus" in nerve cells in 1898 using his silver-staining method; its reality was long disputed as a staining artefact until electron microscopy confirmed it in the 1950s.
