Moral absolutism

Moral absolutism is the ethical position that certain actions are intrinsically right or wrong, regardless of context, consequences or intentions — stealing, for example, might be held to be always immoral, even to feed a starving child. It contrasts with moral relativism, which holds that moral judgements are valid only relative to a culture, standpoint or framework.

Absolutists hold that some moral rules admit no exceptions.

Forms

Moral absolutism is often grounded in religion — divine command theory holds actions right or wrong because God commands or forbids them — or in rationalist ethics such as Kant's categorical imperative, which requires maxims that can be willed as universal law. Graded absolutism, a moderated form, maintains absolute principles but resolves conflicts between them by a hierarchy, holding that the greater duty overrides the lesser and the agent is not guilty for the overridden breach. Natural law theory also typically supports absolute prohibitions on certain act-types.

Relation to other positions

Absolutism should be distinguished from moral universalism, which claims moral truths apply to all similar agents but need not treat any act as exceptionlessly forbidden; consequentialists may be universalist without being absolutist. Critics of absolutism cite dilemmas — lying to protect a fugitive from murderers, as in Kant's famous exchange with Benjamin Constant — while defenders argue that apparent dilemmas always admit a permissible option, or that conceding exceptions destroys the notion of a moral rule.
