Mexico–United States border

The Mexico–United States border is an international border running from the Pacific Ocean at San Diego–Tijuana in the west to the Gulf of Mexico at Brownsville–Matamoros in the east. At 3,145 kilometres (1,954 miles), it is the tenth-longest international border in the world and the most frequently crossed, with hundreds of millions of legal crossings annually.

Geography

The border follows the Rio Grande for much of its length, crosses the Chihuahuan and Sonoran deserts, and passes through major twin-city pairs including San Diego–Tijuana, El Paso–Ciudad Juárez, and Laredo–Nuevo Laredo. Four US states (California, Arizona, New Mexico, Texas) and six Mexican states (Baja California, Sonora, Chihuahua, Coahuila, Nuevo León, Tamaulipas) lie along it.

History

The boundary was largely settled by the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo (1848), which ended the Mexican–American War, and the Gadsden Purchase (1853). A joint boundary commission maintains boundary monuments and manages river shifts.

Security and migration

Since the 1990s, the United States has progressively fortified the border with fencing, sensors, and Border Patrol deployments. The border is a focal point of debates over immigration, drug trafficking, and trade — the two countries share one of the world's largest trading relationships.
