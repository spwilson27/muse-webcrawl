The Azores (Portuguese: Açores), officially the Autonomous Region of the Azores, is one of the two autonomous regions of Portugal (along with Madeira). It is an archipelago composed of nine volcanic islands in the Macaronesia region of the North Atlantic Ocean, about 1,400 km (870 mi) west of Lisbon, on the boundary where the North American, Eurasian, and African tectonic plates meet.

Geography

The islands fall into three groups — eastern (São Miguel, Santa Maria), central (Terceira, Graciosa, São Jorge, Pico, Faial), and western (Flores, Corvo) — strung out over more than 600 km. Mount Pico (2,351 m), on the island of the same name, is the highest point in Portugal. The climate is mild and oceanic, humid, and famously changeable; the "Azores High" anticyclone is a dominant feature of North Atlantic weather patterns. Caldera lakes, fumaroles, and black-sand beaches record the islands' volcanic origin; the last major eruptions were at Capelinhos (1957–58) and the submarine Serreta ridge.

History

The archipelago was uninhabited when Portuguese sailors reached it in the early 15th century (Santa Maria by 1427–1432, under Gonçalo Velho Cabral); settlement followed under the patronage of Prince Henry the Navigator. Angra do Heroísmo on Terceira became a vital port of call for treasure fleets and was made a UNESCO World Heritage Site. After Portugal's 1974 revolution, the Azores gained autonomy in 1976. Lajes Field on Terceira hosts a long-standing US Air Force presence.

Economy

Dairy farming and cattle, tea and pineapple production, fishing, and increasingly tourism dominate the economy. The Azores are an outermost region of the European Union and are celebrated for whale watching and lush hydrangea-lined landscapes.
