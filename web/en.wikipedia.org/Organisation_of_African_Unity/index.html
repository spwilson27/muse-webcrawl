The Organisation of African Unity (OAU; French: Organisation de l'unité africaine, OUA) was an intergovernmental organisation established on 25 May 1963 in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, with 32 signatory governments. Some of the key aims of the OAU were to encourage political and economic integration among its member states, eradicate colonialism and neo-colonialism from the African continent, and defend the sovereignty and territorial integrity of member states.

The OAU was widely criticised as a "talking shop" that did little; its charter enshrined non-interference in members' internal affairs, which limited action against abuses by member governments. It was disbanded on 9 July 2002 by its last chairperson, South African president Thabo Mbeki, and replaced by the African Union (AU), launched in Durban the same day.

History

The OAU emerged from rivalry between the Casablanca Group (favouring immediate political union, led by Ghana's Kwame Nkrumah) and the Monrovia Group (favouring gradual cooperation); the Addis Ababa charter of 1963 was a compromise. The organisation supported liberation movements against colonial rule and apartheid through its Liberation Committee based in Dar es Salaam, and it upheld the inviolability of colonial-era borders (the 1964 Cairo resolution), a principle that has largely kept interstate border wars rare in Africa.

Structure and achievements

The OAU's organs included the Assembly of Heads of State and Government, the Council of Ministers, and a Secretariat headed by a Secretary-General (among them Diallo Telli, Nzo Ekangaki, William Eteki, Edem Kodjo, Peter Onu, Salim Ahmed Salim and Amara Essy). It founded the African Development Bank (1964, separately headquartered) and mediated some border disputes. Its successor, the African Union, adopted a broader mandate, including a right of intervention in grave circumstances — a deliberate break with the OAU's non-interference doctrine.
