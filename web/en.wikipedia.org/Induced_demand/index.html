Induced demand

Induced demand
FieldTransport economics, urban planning
Related conceptLatent demand; Downs–Thomson paradox
Key evidence"Fundamental law of road congestion" (Duranton & Turner, 2011)

Induced demand is the phenomenon in which increasing the supply of a good makes it more likely to be consumed. In transportation, the term describes how expanding road capacity tends to generate additional vehicle travel, so that congestion relief is partly or wholly offset over time. Drivers who previously avoided a route because of traffic, travelled at other times, or used other modes shift their behaviour when travel times fall.

Evidence

Empirical studies of United States metropolitan areas by Gilles Duranton and Matthew Turner (2011) found that vehicle-kilometres travelled increase in near one-to-one proportion with lane-kilometres of capacity—the "fundamental law of road congestion". Earlier reviews, including the UK Standing Advisory Committee on Trunk Road Assessment (SACTRA) report of 1994, reached similar conclusions for trunk roads.

Mechanisms

Induced travel arises through several channels: route switching, retiming of trips into peak periods, mode shifts from transit or carpooling to driving alone, new trips that would not otherwise have been made, and longer trips to more distant destinations. Over the long term, cheaper road travel encourages dispersed land-use patterns (urban sprawl), further increasing driving.

Policy implications

Induced demand is a central argument in debates over highway expansion. Transport planners increasingly incorporate induced-travel effects into project appraisal, and policies such as congestion pricing, road space reallocation, and transit investment are proposed as alternatives; conversely, removing road capacity can produce "disappearing traffic", as documented in several urban case studies.
