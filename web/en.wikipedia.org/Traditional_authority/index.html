Traditional authority

Hereditary monarchy is a classic form of traditional authority

Traditional authority is a form of leadership and social organisation in which authority derives from long-established customs, traditions and social structures. The term was developed by the German sociologist Max Weber in his analysis of domination (Herrschaft), in which he distinguished three ideal types of legitimate authority: traditional, charismatic and rational-legal.

Weber's typology

In Weber's scheme, traditional authority rests on "an established belief in the sanctity of immemorial traditions and the legitimacy of those exercising authority under them." Obedience is owed to the person of the ruler — a hereditary monarch, tribal chief, patriarch or feudal lord — not to an office or a body of rules, and commands are legitimate insofar as they conform to precedent and custom. Administration is carried out by personal retainers, kin or favourites rather than impersonal bureaucrats, and there is little separation between the ruler's private and public resources. Weber identified subtypes including gerontocracy, patriarchalism, patrimonialism and feudalism.

Characteristics and change

Traditional authority tends to be resistant to change: because the legitimacy of the order lies in its claim to have always existed, innovation must be justified as the recovery of old ways. Weber argued that traditional systems could be destabilised by the emergence of charismatic leaders or by the rationalising pressures of markets and bureaucracy, and historians and sociologists have used the category to analyse institutions from pre-modern kingdoms to modern organisations governed by inherited custom. Contemporary constitutional monarchies represent a hybrid, in which traditional symbols coexist with rational-legal frameworks.
