Nuwa (simplified Chinese: 女娲; traditional Chinese: 女媧; pinyin: Nǚwā) is a mother goddess and creation deity in Chinese mythology and folk religion. She is credited with creating humanity, repairing the pillars of heaven, and instituting marriage, and is often depicted with a human head and the body of a serpent, intertwined with her brother and husband Fuxi.

Myths

In the best-known creation account, Nuwa fashioned human beings from yellow clay; the figures she moulded carefully became the nobility, while droplets flicked from a rope dipped in mud became common people. When the fire god Zhurong's battle with the water god Gonggong shattered Mount Buzhou, one of the pillars holding up the sky, the heavens tilted and floods and fires ravaged the earth. Nuwa smelted five coloured stones to mend the broken sky, cut off the legs of a giant turtle to serve as new pillars, slew a black dragon, and dammed the floodwaters with reed ashes. She is also said to have invented the sheng reed organ and established the rites of marriage, acting as the first matchmaker.

Worship and legacy

Nuwa appears in early texts including the Chu Ci and the Classic of Mountains and Seas, and in Han-dynasty tomb art paired with Fuxi, the two holding the carpenter's square and compass that symbolise cosmic order. Temples to Nuwa survive in China, notably at She County, Hebei, and she remains a figure of folk devotion and modern popular culture.
