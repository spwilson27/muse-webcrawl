Authors Guild, Inc. v. Google, Inc.

Authors Guild, Inc. v. Google, Inc. was a copyright lawsuit in the United States in which the Authors Guild and individual authors challenged Google's project to scan and digitize millions of books from research libraries, arguing that the copying infringed their copyrights.

Background

In 2004 Google announced partnerships with major libraries, including the New York Public Library and university libraries at Harvard, Stanford, Michigan, and Oxford, to scan their collections. Google made the full text searchable and displayed short excerpts, known as "snippets," around search terms. In 2005 the Authors Guild filed a class action suit in the Southern District of New York alleging massive copyright infringement.

Settlement and its rejection

The parties announced a proposed settlement in 2008 that would have created a Book Rights Registry and allowed Google to sell access to out-of-print books. Judge Denny Chin rejected the amended settlement in 2011, finding that it would have given Google a de facto monopoly over orphan works and raised antitrust and international law concerns.

Fair use ruling

Litigation continued on the narrower question of fair use. In November 2013 Judge Chin granted summary judgment to Google, holding that the book-scanning project was "highly transformative," served a public benefit, and did not harm the market for the original works. The Second Circuit affirmed in October 2015, and the Supreme Court declined to hear an appeal in 2016, leaving the fair use ruling in place.

Significance

The decision is a leading precedent on fair use in the digital environment, particularly for mass digitization and search technologies.
