Operation Catapult

Operation Catapult was the code name for a British naval operation of the Second World War to neutralise the French Navy after the Fall of France in June 1940, so that its powerful modern warships could not fall into German or Italian hands under the terms of the Franco-German armistice. The operation culminated in the attack on Mers-el-Kébir on 3 July 1940.

The French battleship Bretagne exploding at Mers-el-Kébir.

Background

Britain feared that the French fleet — the fourth largest in the world — would be seized by the Axis despite French assurances. French naval commander Admiral François Darlan had ordered his ships to sail to French colonial ports or scuttle rather than surrender, but Winston Churchill's War Cabinet doubted the guarantees would hold if Germany applied pressure.

Actions

Mers-el-Kébir

At Mers-el-Kébir in French Algeria, Force H under Admiral James Somerville presented the French squadron with options: join the British, sail to a British or neutral port with reduced crews, or scuttle within six hours. Negotiations failed, and the British opened fire, sinking the battleship Bretagne with heavy loss of life, and damaging Dunkerque and Provence. Nearly 1,300 French sailors were killed. A follow-up attack days later further damaged Dunkerque.

Other ports

French ships in British ports (Plymouth and Portsmouth) were boarded and seized, mostly peacefully; the submarine Surcouf was taken with some bloodshed. At Alexandria in Egypt, Admiral Andrew Cunningham negotiated a peaceful demilitarisation of the French squadron there. An attack on Dakar (Operation Menace) followed in September 1940.

Consequences

The attack shocked France; Vichy severed diplomatic relations with Britain, and the episode embittered Anglo-French relations for years. However, it demonstrated to the United States Britain's determination to continue the war, and removed much of the risk of the French fleet augmenting the Axis navies.
