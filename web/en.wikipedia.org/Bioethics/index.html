Bioethics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Bioethics is the study of the ethical issues emerging from advances in biology, medicine and technology. It involves the systematic analysis of controversies about the goals, limits and conduct of healthcare and life-science research, and about how society should govern practices such as organ transplantation, genetic engineering, reproduction and end-of-life care.

History

The term was coined in the 1920s by the German theologian Fritz Jahr and popularised in 1970 by the American biochemist Van Rensselaer Potter, who envisaged a "science of survival" linking biology and human values. The field grew rapidly after revelations of unethical human experiments — including the Tuskegee syphilis study — prompted formal research-ethics codes such as the Nuremberg Code (1947), the Declaration of Helsinki (1964) and the Belmont Report (1979). Institutions such as the Hastings Center (founded 1969) and the Kennedy Institute of Ethics (1971) professionalised the discipline.

Principles

A widely used framework, "principlism", organised by Tom Beauchamp and James Childress, evaluates problems through four mid-level principles: respect for autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, and justice. Other approaches include consequentialism, deontology, virtue ethics, casuistry and feminist and narrative ethics, and different religious and cultural traditions articulate their own bioethical positions.

Topics

Bioethics addresses informed consent, medical confidentiality, allocation of scarce resources, euthanasia and physician-assisted dying, abortion, reproductive technologies, human cloning, stem-cell research, genetics and genomics, neuroethics, research on animals, environmental ethics, and the governance of emerging technologies such as gene editing and artificial intelligence in medicine.
