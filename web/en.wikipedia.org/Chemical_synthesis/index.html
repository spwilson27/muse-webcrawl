Chemical synthesis (chemical combination) is the artificial execution of chemical reactions to obtain one or several products. This occurs by physical and chemical manipulations usually involving one or more reactions. In modern laboratory uses, the process is reproducible and reliable.

A chemical synthesis involves one or more compounds (known as reagents or reactants) that will experience a transformation under certain conditions. Various reaction types can be applied to formulate a desired product. Many reactions require some form of heating or cooling, and catalysts are often used to accelerate reactions or direct their selectivity.

Strategies

Chemists begin designing syntheses by selecting starting materials and a route. A key intellectual tool is retrosynthetic analysis, developed by E. J. Corey, in which the target molecule is conceptually broken down into simpler precursors through imaginary bond disconnections. Important strategic considerations include:

  • Convergent vs. linear synthesis — building fragments separately and joining them late usually gives higher overall yields than a long linear sequence.
  • Stereocontrol — ensuring the correct three-dimensional arrangement of atoms, often through asymmetric catalysis or chiral auxiliaries.
  • Protecting groups — temporarily masking reactive functional groups.
  • Atom economy and green chemistry — minimizing waste and hazardous reagents.

Total synthesis

Total synthesis is the complete synthesis of a complex natural product from simple, commercially available starting materials. Landmark achievements include Robert Burns Woodward's syntheses of cholesterol, cortisone, and vitamin B12, and K. C. Nicolaou's synthesis of Taxol. The first total synthesis of an organic compound is often credited to Friedrich Wöhler's 1828 synthesis of urea from inorganic ammonium cyanate, which undermined the doctrine of vitalism.

Other branches

Organic synthesis deals with carbon compounds; inorganic synthesis covers metals, minerals, and organometallic compounds. Combinatorial chemistry generates large libraries of related compounds for drug screening, while semisynthesis starts from a complex natural intermediate, as in the production of the anticancer drug paclitaxel from baccatin precursors in yew needles.

Industrial synthesis

At scale, synthesis is the business of the chemical industry: ammonia (Haber–Bosch), sulfuric acid (contact process), polymers such as polyethylene, and pharmaceuticals are produced in dedicated plants optimized for yield, purity, safety, and cost.
