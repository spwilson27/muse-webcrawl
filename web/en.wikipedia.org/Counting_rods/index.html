Counting rods

Counting rods
RegionAncient China (and later Japan and Korea)
PeriodIn use by the Warring States period (475–221 BC); standard until the abacus era
SystemDecimal place-value, with rod numerals

Counting rods (籌) were small bars, typically of bamboo, ivory, or iron, used for calculation in ancient China. Arranged on a board in patterns called rod numerals, they implemented a true decimal place-value system centuries before such notation became standard elsewhere.

The rod numeral system

Digits were formed by grouping rods vertically or horizontally, with the two orientations alternating by place value to avoid ambiguity, and a blank space representing zero. Negative numbers were shown with black rods or rods of different colour or shape, while positive numbers used red rods — a convention echoed in modern Chinese usage where red denotes positive figures.

Calculations

With counting rods, Chinese mathematicians performed addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, extraction of roots, and the solution of systems of linear equations by methods resembling Gaussian elimination. Fractions and polynomial equations were also handled, enabling sophisticated results such as the calculations in the Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art.

Spread and decline

The system spread to Korea and Japan (where it developed into sangi rods). It declined in China during the Ming dynasty as the abacus (suanpan) became dominant, though rod-based methods left a lasting legacy in East Asian mathematics.
