Galvanic corrosion

Galvanic corrosion (also called bimetallic corrosion or dissimilar-metal corrosion) is an electrochemical process in which one metal corrodes preferentially when it is in electrical contact with a different metal in the presence of an electrolyte, such as seawater or condensed moisture. The pair forms a galvanic cell: the less noble metal becomes the anode and dissolves, while the more noble metal (the cathode) is protected.

Mechanism and the galvanic series

The tendency of metals to act as anode or cathode is ranked in the galvanic series for a given environment. Metals far apart in the series, such as aluminium and copper, corrode faster when coupled than metals close together. The rate also depends on the relative surface areas: a small anode coupled to a large cathode suffers rapid, concentrated attack, whereas a large anode and small cathode spread the corrosion thinly.

Examples and prevention

A famous case is the Statue of Liberty, whose copper skin was in contact with its iron armature, insulated only by shellac-soaked asbestos that eventually failed; the resulting corrosion required major restoration in the 1980s. Prevention methods include choosing metals close in the galvanic series, electrically insulating dissimilar metals from each other, applying coatings, and cathodic protection using sacrificial anodes of zinc or magnesium. The effect is also exploited deliberately: galvanized steel relies on a zinc coating corroding preferentially to protect the steel beneath.
