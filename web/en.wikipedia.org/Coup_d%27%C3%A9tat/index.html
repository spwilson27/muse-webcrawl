Coup d'état

Type: Unconstitutional seizure of power
Actors: Military, political faction, or armed group
Target: Incumbent government

A coup d'état (French for "stroke of state") is the sudden, illegal, and often violent overthrow of an existing government by a small group, typically the military or a faction within the state apparatus. Unlike a revolution, which involves mass popular participation, a coup is usually carried out by a limited number of conspirators who seize key institutions — government buildings, broadcast media, and transport hubs — and announce a new leadership.

History

The term entered English in the 19th century from French political usage. Coups have been a recurring feature of political life from ancient Rome — where the Praetorian Guard repeatedly made and unmade emperors — to the modern era. Notable examples include Napoleon Bonaparte's Coup of 18 Brumaire (1799), the 1973 Chilean coup that brought Augusto Pinochet to power, and numerous military interventions across Africa, Latin America, and Southeast Asia during the Cold War.

Types and outcomes

Scholars distinguish between coups that merely replace one authoritarian ruler with another and those that open paths toward democratization. Research by Naunihal Singh and others suggests the success of a coup often depends less on material strength than on the conspirators' ability to convince other actors that the coup has already succeeded. Failed coups frequently lead to purges, civil conflict, or the consolidation of the incumbent regime.

Legality and international response

Because coups violate constitutional order, regional bodies such as the African Union and the Organization of American States have adopted policies of suspending member states following unconstitutional changes of government. The United States is legally required to suspend most foreign aid to governments installed by military coup, a provision that has occasionally led to diplomatic disputes over whether an overthrow qualifies as a coup at all.
