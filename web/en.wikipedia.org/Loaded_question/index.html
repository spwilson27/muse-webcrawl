A loaded question is a question that contains a controversial or unjustified assumption—a presupposition—such that any direct answer appears to accept that assumption. It is a form of the complex question fallacy (also called the fallacy of many questions), in which two questions are effectively asked as one, hiding a claim the respondent may not accept.

The classic example

The traditional illustration is the courtroom question, "Have you stopped beating your wife?" Answering "yes" concedes that the respondent once beat his wife; answering "no" implies that he still does. Since the question presupposes the very fact in dispute, neither direct answer is safe if the presupposition is false. The correct response is to reject the presupposition rather than choose between the offered alternatives: "I have never beaten my wife."

How it works

Loaded questions work by smuggling an unproven premise into what looks like a neutral request for information. The device appears in cross-examination, political debate, opinion polling ("push polls"), and everyday argument. Related but distinct is the leading question, which merely suggests a preferred answer; a loaded question goes further by embedding an assumption that the answerer is trapped into endorsing whichever way they reply.

Responding

Standard rebuttals include identifying and challenging the hidden assumption, answering the separate underlying questions individually, or refusing the question's framing outright. In formal legal settings, objections may be raised that a question "assumes facts not in evidence."
