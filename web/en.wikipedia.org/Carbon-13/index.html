Carbon-13 (13C) is a natural, stable isotope of carbon with a nucleus containing six protons and seven neutrons. It accounts for about 1.07% of all carbon on Earth, the remainder being almost entirely carbon-12. As one of the environmental isotopes, it plays a major role in isotope geochemistry, and because its nucleus has a spin of 1/2 it is the isotope used in carbon-13 nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy.

Detection and fractionation

Plants discriminate between the two stable carbon isotopes during photosynthesis: the enzyme RuBisCO fixes 12C slightly more readily than 13C, so plant tissue is depleted in 13C relative to the atmosphere. The effect is stronger in C3 plants (a 13C depletion, δ13C, of about −27‰) than in C4 plants (about −13‰), which concentrate CO2 before fixation. Measurements of δ13C in sediments, ice cores, tree rings and animal tissues therefore reveal past vegetation types, diets, ocean circulation and changes in the global carbon cycle; the steady dilution of atmospheric 13C by fossil-fuel CO2 is known as the Suess effect.

NMR spectroscopy

Whereas 12C is NMR-inactive (spin 0), 13C gives useful NMR signals at a resonance frequency about one quarter that of the proton. Because only about one carbon atom in a hundred is 13C, spectra are weak, but the wide chemical-shift range (roughly 200 ppm) makes 13C NMR a routine structural tool in organic chemistry, commonly run with proton decoupling to collapse the signals to single lines.

Applications

Enriched 13C compounds are used as tracers in metabolic studies — for example the 13C-urea breath test for Helicobacter pylori infection — and in mass-spectrometric measurement of protein turnover. In Earth science, 13C labelling tracks the fate of carbon through soils and ecosystems, and the isotope ratio of methane helps distinguish biogenic from thermogenic gas sources.
