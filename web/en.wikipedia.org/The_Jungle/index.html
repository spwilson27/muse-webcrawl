The Jungle is a novel by American journalist and novelist Upton Sinclair, first serialised in 1905 and published in book form in 1906. It portrays the harsh conditions and exploited lives of immigrants — centred on the Lithuanian Jurgis Rudkus and his family — in the meatpacking industry of Chicago's stockyards.

Impact

Sinclair intended to expose the exploitation of workers and promote socialism, but the public was most outraged by the book's descriptions of unsanitary practices in meat processing, including diseased cattle and contamination reaching consumers' tables. Sinclair ruefully remarked that he "aimed at the public's heart, and by accident I hit it in the stomach". The outcry helped spur the passage of the Meat Inspection Act and the Pure Food and Drug Act in 1906, early landmarks of American consumer protection.

Legacy

The novel remains a classic of muckraking literature and is widely taught in American schools as an example of literature's power to drive legislative reform.
