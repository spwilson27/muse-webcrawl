Eadweard Muybridge

Eadweard Muybridge (born Edward James Muggeridge, 9 April 1830 – 8 May 1904) was an English photographer known for his pioneering work in photographic studies of motion, and in motion-picture projection. He adopted the first name Eadweard as the original Anglo-Saxon form of Edward, and the surname Muybridge, believing it to be similarly ancient.

The horse in motion

In 1878, sponsored by railroad magnate Leland Stanford, Muybridge used a row of cameras triggered in sequence to photograph a galloping horse at Palo Alto, California. The resulting images proved that at one point in the stride all four hooves leave the ground, settling a famous debate, and demonstrated the potential of chronophotography. He later developed the zoopraxiscope, a device for projecting painted versions of his photographs in motion, a forerunner of cinema.

Animal locomotion

Between 1884 and 1887, at the University of Pennsylvania, Muybridge produced more than 100,000 images of animals and humans in motion, published as Animal Locomotion (1887). His work influenced artists, anatomists and filmmakers, and he is regarded as a founding figure of motion pictures. Earlier in his career he had been a noted landscape photographer of the American West, and he was acquitted in 1875 of the murder of his wife's lover on grounds of justifiable homicide.
