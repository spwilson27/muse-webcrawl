Bivector

A bivector can be visualized as an oriented area, such as the parallelogram spanned by two vectors.

In mathematics, a bivector or 2-vector is a quantity in exterior algebra or geometric algebra that extends the idea of scalars and vectors. If a scalar is considered a degree-zero quantity, and a vector is a degree-one quantity, then a bivector can be thought of as being of degree two. Bivectors have applications in many areas of mathematics, including geometry, physics (particularly relativity), and the study of rotations.

A bivector results from the wedge product (exterior product) of two vectors. For vectors a and b, the bivector a ∧ b represents the oriented plane segment (parallelogram) spanned by the two vectors, with a magnitude equal to the area of the parallelogram and an orientation given by the rotational sense from a to b. Unlike an ordinary vector such as the cross product, a bivector exists naturally in any dimension.

History

Bivectors were introduced in the 1840s by the German mathematician Hermann Grassmann in his work on exterior algebra, and were developed further by William Kingdon Clifford, who combined Grassmann's exterior product with Hamilton's quaternions to form geometric algebra. Grassmann himself noted the similarity between bivectors and quaternions.

Properties

The wedge product of a vector with itself is zero, so a ∧ a = 0, and swapping the order of the factors reverses the sign: a ∧ b = −b ∧ a. A bivector that can be written as the wedge product of two vectors is called a simple bivector. In three dimensions every bivector is simple, and the space of bivectors is three-dimensional, which is the algebraic reason behind the cross product being peculiar to three dimensions. In four and higher dimensions there exist non-simple bivectors that are sums of simple bivectors.

In three dimensions, bivectors are Hodge dual to ordinary vectors: the bivector formed from components in the yz, zx and xy planes corresponds to the vector given by the cross product. This correspondence explains why angular velocity, torque and angular momentum, which are naturally bivectors (they involve a plane of rotation), are conventionally represented as vectors in three-dimensional physics.

Applications

Bivectors are used in geometric algebra to generate rotations via rotors, in the formulation of electromagnetism where the electromagnetic field tensor is treated as a bivector field in spacetime, and in the description of planes and rotations in computer graphics and robotics. In special relativity, the electromagnetic field can be represented as a single bivector in the spacetime algebra, unifying the electric and magnetic fields.
