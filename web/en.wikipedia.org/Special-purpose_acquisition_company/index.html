Special-purpose acquisition company
Abbreviation: SPAC
Also called: Blank-cheque company
Purpose: Acquire a private company and take it public

A special-purpose acquisition company (SPAC), also known as a blank-cheque company, is a shell corporation listed on a stock exchange with the sole purpose of acquiring an unidentified private company, thereby taking it public without a traditional initial public offering (IPO). SPACs have existed for decades but surged dramatically in popularity in 2020 and 2021, when they raised record sums on US exchanges.

How a SPAC works

A SPAC is formed by sponsors — often experienced investors or executives — who raise capital through an IPO of units, typically priced at $10 and consisting of a share plus a fraction of a warrant. The proceeds are placed in a trust account. The SPAC then has a fixed window, usually 18–24 months, to find and merge with a target company; the merger ("de-SPAC" transaction) requires shareholder approval, and public shareholders who disapprove may redeem their shares for their pro-rata share of the trust. If no deal is completed in time, the SPAC liquidates and returns the money.

Economics and criticism

Sponsors typically receive a "promote" of about 20% of the SPAC's shares for a nominal sum, aligning their incentive to complete a deal but heavily diluting later investors. Critics argue that SPACs have produced poor returns for post-merger shareholders, that projections given to investors were often optimistic, and that the structure allowed private companies to go public with less scrutiny than an IPO. Regulators including the US Securities and Exchange Commission adopted tighter disclosure rules for de-SPAC transactions in 2024.
