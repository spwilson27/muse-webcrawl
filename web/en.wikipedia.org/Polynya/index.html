Polynya

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A polynya: open water within the sea-ice pack

A polynya (British spelling polynia) is an area of open water surrounded by sea ice. The word comes from the Russian полынья (polyn'ya), meaning a natural ice hole, and was adopted by polar explorers in the 19th century. Polynyas range in size from a few hundred square metres to enormous features such as the North Water Polynya between Greenland and Canada, which covers tens of thousands of square kilometres.

Formation

Two main mechanisms create polynyas. Latent-heat polynyas form where persistent offshore winds or ocean currents push newly formed ice away from a coast or ice edge as fast as it freezes, continuously exposing open water — such areas act as "sea-ice factories" producing large volumes of ice and dense, salty brine. Sensible-heat polynyas form where warm water upwells to the surface and melts ice or prevents it from forming, as in the Weddell Polynya observed in the Antarctic in the 1970s and again in 2016–2017.

Ecological significance

Polynyas are biological hotspots in polar seas. Sunlight reaching the open water triggers early phytoplankton blooms, supporting dense concentrations of zooplankton, fish, seabirds and marine mammals. They provide breathing access for seals and whales in winter and feeding grounds for species such as polar bears. Indigenous hunters in the Arctic have long known and used recurring polynyas.

Climate role

Because latent-heat polynyas produce brine as seawater freezes, they contribute to the formation of dense bottom water and hence to global ocean circulation. Changes in polynya behaviour are monitored as indicators of climate change in both the Arctic and Antarctic.
