Indicative

CategoryGrammatical mood
FunctionStates facts or believed facts
Contrasts withSubjunctive, imperative, conditional

The indicative mood is a grammatical mood used in most languages for statements of fact and positive beliefs. It is the default, most common mood — the one used in ordinary declarative sentences such as "She walks to school" — as opposed to moods that express wishes, commands, uncertainty or hypothetical situations.

Usage

In languages with rich verbal inflection, such as Spanish, French, Latin and German, the indicative is formally distinguished from the subjunctive. Spanish, for instance, uses the indicative in main clauses asserting facts (Él habla, "He speaks") and reserves the subjunctive for subordinate clauses expressing doubt, desire or emotion. English marks the indicative largely by the absence of special forms, retaining only remnants of other moods.

Realis and irrealis

Linguists often group the indicative under the broader category of realis moods, which present situations as actual, in contrast to irrealis moods (subjunctive, conditional, optative, imperative) that present them as potential or non-actual. Not all languages have a distinct indicative form; some mark factuality through particles or context.

Examples across languages

Latin verbs have fully inflected indicative forms across six tenses. Modern German uses the indicative for factual statements and reported speech traditionally uses Konjunktiv I. In many languages, questions and negations remain in the indicative unless the speaker signals uncertainty.
