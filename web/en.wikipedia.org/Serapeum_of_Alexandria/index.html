Serapeum of Alexandria

The Serapeum of Alexandria in the Ptolemaic Kingdom and Roman Egypt was a temple built by Ptolemy III Euergetes (reigned 246–222 BC) and dedicated to Serapis, the syncretic Greco-Egyptian god who was made the tutelary deity of Alexandria. The temple stood in the Rhakotis district on a rocky plateau, approached by a staircase of one hundred steps, and was described by ancient authors as one of the most splendid buildings in the city after the Capitol.

History

The cult of Serapis combined Osiris and the Apis bull with Greek gods such as Zeus and Dionysus, and was promoted by the Ptolemies as a unifying deity for Greeks and Egyptians. Ptolemy III's Serapeum replaced an earlier shrine of Ptolemy I's era. The complex included colonnades, subterranean galleries, and a "daughter" collection associated with the Great Library of Alexandria; many scholars believe part of the Library's holdings was stored there, and the Serapeum became a place of pilgrimage, incubation healing, and learning.

Destruction

In AD 391, after the emperor Theodosius I outlawed pagan sacrifice, Patriarch Theophilus of Alexandria led Christians against the temple following street fighting between pagans and Christians; the Serapeum was stormed, its cult statue smashed, and the site converted to Christian use. Its destruction became a symbol, in later historical writing, of the triumph of Christianity over paganism and — sometimes inaccurately, conflating it with the Great Library — of the loss of ancient learning.

Archaeology

The site, now dominated by the great monolithic column known as Pompey's Pillar (erected c. AD 298 for Diocletian), was excavated by Alan Rowe in the 1940s, revealing foundation deposits of Ptolemy III and IV, tunnels, and a Nilometer. Finds include statues of the Apis bull and foundation plaques in gold, silver, bronze, and glass.
