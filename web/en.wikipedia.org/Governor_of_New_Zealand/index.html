The Governor of New Zealand was the representative of the British monarch and the head of the executive government in New Zealand from the establishment of the Crown colony in 1841 until 1917, when the office was restyled Governor-General of New Zealand following New Zealand's recognition as a Dominion (proclaimed in 1907).

Colonial governors

William Hobson arrived in 1840 as Lieutenant-Governor, proclaimed British sovereignty, and negotiated the Treaty of Waitangi with Māori chiefs; he became the first Governor when New Zealand became a separate Crown colony in 1841. Early governors wielded real executive power, clashing with settlers and Māori; Sir George Grey, governor twice (1845–53 and 1861–68), was the most influential. Captain Robert FitzRoy and Thomas Gore Browne also held the post in turbulent years.

After responsible government

With responsible government from 1856, real power shifted to elected ministries, and the governor became a largely ceremonial figure acting on ministerial advice, though retaining reserve powers and a role in relations between the colonial government and London — and a continuing, contested role in Māori affairs during the New Zealand Wars.
