Pluralism (political philosophy)

Pluralism
[ Image: Diverse interest groups in a democracy ]
FieldPolitical philosophy, political science
Key thinkersHarold Laski, Robert Dahl, Isaiah Berlin, John Rawls
Core claimPower is dispersed among many groups; diversity of values is irreducible
Contrasts withMonism, elitism, totalitarianism

Pluralism as a term in political philosophy and political science denotes, first, the recognition and affirmation of diversity within a political body—of interests, beliefs, and ways of life—and the institutional accommodation of that diversity; and, second, an empirical theory of power holding that power in modern democracies is dispersed among a plurality of competing interest groups rather than concentrated in a single elite.

Pluralist theory of democracy

In the mid-20th century, theorists such as David Truman and Robert Dahl (in Who Governs?, 1961) argued that policy emerges from bargaining among organized groups—business, labour, farmers, professions—none of which dominates across all issues. Critics from C. Wright Mills to later "elite pluralists" objected that resources and access are unequally distributed, biasing outcomes toward the organized and wealthy; Dahl and Charles Lindblom responded with accounts of "deformed" but still pluralist systems.

The English pluralists

An earlier tradition—the English pluralists, including Harold Laski, G. D. H. Cole, and John Neville Figgis, drawing on Otto von Gierke—challenged the doctrine of absolute state sovereignty, arguing that churches, guilds, unions, and other associations possess real group personality and authority deserving legal recognition, anticipating themes of guild socialism and associational democracy.

Value pluralism

A related but distinct doctrine, associated with Isaiah Berlin, holds that ultimate human values (liberty, equality, justice) are plural and sometimes incommensurable and conflicting, so political philosophy cannot derive a single harmonious order of goods; this underlies liberal arguments for toleration and also Berlin's distinction between negative and positive liberty.

Criticism

Pluralism is criticized for neglecting unorganized interests, agenda control ("non-decisions"), and structural inequalities, and—from communitarian and republican directions—for fragmenting the common good into interest-group bargaining. Nonetheless it remains a foundational account of liberal democracy and of civil society.
