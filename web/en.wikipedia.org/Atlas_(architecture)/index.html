Atlas (architecture)


Atlantes on a building facade

In European architectural sculpture, an atlas (plural atlantes) is a support sculpted in the form of a man, which may take the place of a column, a pier or a pilaster. The term derives from Atlas, the Titan of Greek mythology condemned to hold up the heavens. The Roman term for such a figure is a telamon. The female equivalent is the caryatid, sculpted in the form of a woman.

Origins

The motif has ancient roots: colossal male figures served as supports in Greek temples in Sicily, most famously the giant telamons of the Temple of Olympian Zeus at Agrigentum (ancient Akragas), among the largest Doric temples ever built. Unlike caryatids, whose weight rests through the head and neck, atlantes often strain visibly under their burden, arms raised or the body bent — a pose that expresses the effort of bearing the structure above.

Later use

Atlantes returned to prominence in Renaissance, Baroque and Beaux-Arts architecture, where they flank doorways, support balconies and cornices, and animate the facades of palaces and civic buildings across Europe and the Americas. Notable examples include the atlantes of the Sanssouci palace at Potsdam and the giant figures of the Winter Palace (Hermitage) portico in St. Petersburg, carved from granite in the mid-19th century.

Related forms

Similar load-bearing figures appear in other traditions, including the dwarapala and yaksha figures of South and Southeast Asian temple architecture, and in Mesoamerican art, where the term "Atlantean figures" is used for the warrior columns of Tula.
