Campbell's Soup Cans

Campbell's Soup Cans (1962), also called 32 Campbell's Soup Cans, is a work of art by American artist Andy Warhol. It consists of thirty-two canvases, each 20 by 16 inches, each depicting one variety of Campbell's Soup then sold. First exhibited in July 1962 at the Ferus Gallery in Los Angeles, it is regarded as a landmark of Pop art. The work is in the collection of the Museum of Modern Art (MoMA) in New York.

Creation

Warhol painted the canvases using a semi-mechanized process, stamping and hand-painting the familiar red-and-white labels; the fleur-de-lis border at the bottom of each can was stamped individually. The 32 flavors — from Tomato to Pepper Pot and Scotch Broth — matched Campbell's full product line at the time. Legend holds that Warhol, advised by dealer Muriel Latow, chose soup because he ate it for lunch every day for twenty years.

Exhibition and reception

At the Ferus Gallery the canvases were displayed on shelves like groceries in a store aisle. Initial reaction was bewildered or hostile, and a neighboring gallery mockingly offered real cans for sale. Irving Blum bought the full set for about $1,000, paid in installments — a purchase that prevented the work from being broken up.

Significance

The work helped launch Pop art's challenge to Abstract Expressionism, treating mass-produced consumer imagery with deadpan repetition. It made Warhol famous and turned the Campbell's can into one of the most recognizable images in modern art. MoMA acquired the set in 1996.
