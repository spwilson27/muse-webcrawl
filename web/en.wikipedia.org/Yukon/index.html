The Yukon is the smallest and westernmost of Canada's three northern territories. It borders Alaska to the west, British Columbia to the south and the Northwest Territories to the east. Most of its roughly 46,000 residents live in the capital, Whitehorse. The territory takes its name from the Yukon River, whose name derives from a Gwich'in phrase meaning "great river". About a quarter of the population is Indigenous, belonging to fourteen First Nations, most of which have settled modern land-claim agreements.

History

Indigenous peoples have inhabited the region for at least 10,000 years; the area includes some of the earliest evidence of human presence in North America. Fur traders of the Hudson's Bay Company arrived in the 1840s. The Klondike Gold Rush of 1896–99 brought some 100,000 prospectors toward Dawson City, though only a fraction arrived, and transformed the territory overnight; the rush prompted Canada to create the Yukon Territory in 1898 and send the North-West Mounted Police to assert control.

The population collapsed after the rush, then grew again with the construction of the Alaska Highway in 1942, which shifted the territory's centre of gravity to Whitehorse (capital from 1953).

Geography

The Yukon's southwest contains Kluane National Park and Reserve and the St. Elias Mountains, including Mount Logan, Canada's highest peak, within the largest non-polar icefields in the world. The territory is drained by the Yukon River system and covered largely by boreal forest and tundra, with a subarctic and arctic climate.

Economy and government

Mining (gold, silver, zinc, copper), tourism and government dominate the economy; placer gold mining continues in the Klondike. Under the 2003 devolution agreement the territorial government exercises province-like control over land and resources, and its legislative assembly is elected on a party basis, unlike in the Northwest Territories.
