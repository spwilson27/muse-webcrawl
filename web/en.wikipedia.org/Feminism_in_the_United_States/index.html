Feminism in the United States

American suffragists campaigning for the vote, won nationally in 1920.

Feminism in the United States refers to the collection of movements and ideologies aimed at establishing the political, economic, personal and social rights of women in the United States. Historians commonly narrate it in "waves", though the wave model is debated.

First wave: suffrage

The first wave is conventionally dated from the Seneca Falls Convention of 1848, organised by Elizabeth Cady Stanton and Lucretia Mott, whose Declaration of Sentiments demanded equality including the vote. After decades of campaigning — intertwined with abolitionism and temperance — the Nineteenth Amendment, ratified in 1920, prohibited denying the vote on account of sex.

Second wave

The second wave (1960s–1980s) broadened the agenda to workplace equality, reproductive rights, sexuality and family law. Betty Friedan's The Feminine Mystique (1963) was a catalyst; the National Organization for Women was founded in 1966. Landmarks included Title IX (1972), the Supreme Court's decision in Roe v. Wade (1973), and the campaign for the Equal Rights Amendment, which Congress passed in 1972 but which fell short of ratification by the states before its deadline.

Third and fourth waves

From the 1990s, a third wave emphasised diversity, intersectionality — a framework developed by legal scholar Kimberlé Crenshaw — and the inclusion of women of colour, lesbians and trans women. A fourth wave, associated with social media from around 2012, focused on sexual harassment and assault, exemplified by the viral spread of the #MeToo movement in 2017.
