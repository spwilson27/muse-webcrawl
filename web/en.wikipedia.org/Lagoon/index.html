Lagoon

An atoll lagoon in the Pacific Ocean

A lagoon is a shallow body of water separated from a larger body of water by a narrow landform, such as a barrier island, barrier peninsula, coral reef or sandbank. Lagoons are commonly divided into coastal lagoons and atoll lagoons, and their waters may range from fresh to brackish to hypersaline depending on climate and the balance of inflow and exchange with the sea.

Coastal lagoons

Coastal lagoons form along gently sloping coasts where sediment transported by waves and currents builds barrier islands or spits offshore, enclosing a shallow basin behind them. They connect to the ocean through one or more inlets, which may migrate or close. Examples include the Venetian Lagoon in Italy, Pamlico Sound in North Carolina, the lagoons of the Baltic coast (such as the Curonian Lagoon, partly a haff), and Laguna Madre in Texas, one of the world's few hypersaline lagoons. Coastal lagoons are highly productive ecosystems, sheltering seagrass meadows, salt marshes, fisheries and migratory waterfowl, but they are vulnerable to eutrophication, dredging and sea-level rise.

Atoll lagoons

An atoll lagoon is enclosed by a ring-shaped coral reef built atop a subsiding volcanic island, as described by Darwin's subsidence theory of atoll formation. As the volcano sinks, the reef grows upward, leaving a shallow lagoon — sometimes tens of kilometres across — where the island once stood, as at Bikini, Enewetak and Kiritimati (whose lagoon system is among the largest in the world).

Terminology

The word derives from Italian laguna, itself from Latin lacuna, "pool" or "gap". In Spanish-speaking countries similar features are often called lagunas costeras; river-mouth pools called lagoons in some regions (such as those of Ghana) are more precisely limans or estuaries.
