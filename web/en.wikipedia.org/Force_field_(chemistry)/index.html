Force field (chemistry)

In the context of molecular modelling, a force field refers to the functional form and parameter sets used to calculate the potential energy of a system of atoms. Force field functions and parameter sets are derived from both experimental work and high-level quantum mechanical calculations. In a typical molecular mechanics force field, the total energy is decomposed into bonded terms (bond stretching, angle bending, torsional rotations, and improper dihedrals) and non-bonded terms (electrostatic interactions modelled by Coulomb's law and van der Waals interactions modelled by a Lennard-Jones potential).

Energy terms in a classical molecular mechanics force field.

Functional form

Bond-stretching and angle-bending terms are usually harmonic (quadratic) approximations to the true potentials. Torsion terms use periodic cosine series, while out-of-plane bending is treated with improper dihedrals. Non-bonded interactions are generally computed between all pairs of atoms not bonded to each other, with scaling rules for 1–4 interactions (atoms separated by two bonds). Partial atomic charges represent the electrostatic term.

Common force fields

Widely used biomolecular force fields include AMBER, CHARMM, GROMOS, and OPLS, each with parameter sets optimised for proteins, nucleic acids, lipids, and small molecules. Water is typically represented by a simple model such as TIP3P or SPC/E. Reactive force fields such as ReaxFF allow bonds to break and form, while coarse-grained models (e.g. MARTINI) group atoms into beads for simulating larger systems.

Applications

Force fields are the basis of molecular dynamics simulations used to study protein folding, drug binding, and materials properties, trading the accuracy of quantum mechanics for the ability to simulate millions of atoms over microseconds.
