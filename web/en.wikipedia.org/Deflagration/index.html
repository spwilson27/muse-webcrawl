Deflagration

Deflagration
PropagationThermal conduction (subsonic flame front)
Typical speedFrom a few cm/s to tens of m/s in gases; well below the speed of sound in the unburnt material
ContrastDetonation (supersonic, shock-driven)
ExamplesGunpowder and propellant burning, gas and dust explosions in confined spaces

Deflagration (from Latin de- + flagrare, "to burn down") is a type of combustion in which a flame front propagates through a fuel–oxidiser mixture or an explosive at subsonic speed, driven by heat transfer: the hot products heat the adjacent unburned layer to ignition by conduction. It contrasts with a detonation, in which a supersonic shock wave compresses and ignites the material.

Characteristics

In a deflagration the pressure across the flame front changes comparatively little, and the reaction zone moves at speeds from centimetres per second (as in the laminar burning of a gas flame) up to hundreds of metres per second in turbulent or confined mixtures — still below the local speed of sound. Everyday examples include the burning of gunpowder and rocket propellants, the flame in a gas stove or Bunsen burner, and most accidental gas, vapour-cloud, and dust explosions. Firearm propellants are designed to deflagrate progressively, generating gas that accelerates the projectile without shattering the weapon.

Deflagration-to-detonation transition

Under confinement or over long run-up distances in pipes and galleries, a deflagration can accelerate: turbulence folds the flame, pressure waves pile up ahead of it, and the combustion can transition into a detonation — a phenomenon known as deflagration-to-detonation transition (DDT). DDT is a major hazard in mines, fuel–air systems, and hydrogen handling, and is also deliberately exploited in pulse-detonation engine research.

Applications and safety

Low explosives such as black powder and smokeless propellants function by deflagration, while high explosives detonate. Safety engineering for grain silos, coal mines, and chemical plants focuses on preventing deflagrations (through ventilation, inerting, and explosion venting/suppression systems) and on limiting conditions that could allow transition to detonation.
