The pupil is a hole located in the centre of the iris of the eye that allows light to strike the retina. It appears black because light entering the pupil is either absorbed by the tissues inside the eye or scattered within the eye and mostly does not exit. The term was created by Gerard of Cremona.

Structure

In humans the pupil is round, but other species, such as some cats, have vertical slit pupils, goats and sheep have horizontal pupils, and some aquatic animals have crescent-shaped pupils. The size of the pupil is controlled by two muscles in the iris: the sphincter pupillae, which constricts the pupil in bright light (miosis), and the dilator pupillae, which widens it in dim light or under sympathetic arousal (mydriasis). In humans the pupil diameter typically ranges from about 2 mm in bright light to about 8 mm in darkness.

Pupillary light reflex

The pupillary light reflex adjusts pupil size in response to light intensity falling on the retinal ganglion cells. It is mediated by the parasympathetic nervous system via the Edinger–Westphal nucleus and ciliary ganglion. Shining light in one eye normally causes both pupils to constrict (the direct and consensual responses), a property used in neurological examination.

Clinical significance

Abnormal pupil size or reactivity can indicate neurological injury, autonomic dysfunction or drug effects. Unequal pupils are termed anisocoria; a fixed dilated pupil can be a sign of raised intracranial pressure or oculomotor nerve palsy. Drugs such as opioids cause constriction (pinpoint pupils), while anticholinergics and sympathomimetics cause dilation.
