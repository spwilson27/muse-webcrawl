European option

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A European option is a type of financial derivative contract that gives the holder the right, but not the obligation, to buy (a call option) or sell (a put option) an underlying asset at a specified strike price — but only on the expiration date of the contract. This restriction distinguishes European options from American options, which may be exercised at any time up to and including expiration, and Bermudan options, which may be exercised on a set of specified dates. The names derive from geographical conventions and have nothing to do with where the options are traded; most index options in Europe and many in the United States are European-style.

Valuation

Because exercise is possible only at maturity, European options admit a closed-form valuation: the Black–Scholes formula. For a call on a non-dividend-paying stock, the price is C = S·N(d₁) − K·e^(−rT)·N(d₂), where S is the spot price, K the strike, r the risk-free rate, T the time to expiry, and N the cumulative standard normal distribution. European options satisfy put–call parity exactly, and they are generally worth the same as or less than otherwise identical American options, since the holder lacks the early-exercise right; for calls on non-dividend-paying stocks the two values coincide.

Uses

Cash-settled index options (such as those on the S&P 500 index, SPX) are European-style, avoiding assignment complications from early exercise. European options are also the standard building blocks in structured products and are used in hedging and speculation on equities, currencies, interest rates, and commodities.
