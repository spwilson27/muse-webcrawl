The Rosetta Stone is a granodiorite stele inscribed with a decree issued in Memphis, Egypt, in 196 BC during the Ptolemaic dynasty on behalf of King Ptolemy V Epiphanes. The decree appears in three scripts: the upper text in Ancient Egyptian hieroglyphs, the middle portion in Demotic, and the lower in Ancient Greek. Because the same text was given in three scripts, the stone provided the key to deciphering Egyptian hieroglyphs, which had been unreadable for over a thousand years.

Discovery

The stone was unearthed in July 1799 by French soldiers rebuilding a fort near the town of Rosetta (Rashid) in the Nile Delta during Napoleon's Egyptian campaign. After the French defeat, it passed to the British under the Treaty of Alexandria (1801) and has been displayed in the British Museum since 1802.

Decipherment

Scholars including Thomas Young established that the three texts were versions of one decree and that hieroglyphs recorded sound as well as meaning. Jean-François Champollion, building on knowledge of Coptic, announced the decipherment in 1822, demonstrating that hieroglyphic writing combined phonetic and ideographic elements. His work founded modern Egyptology. The phrase "Rosetta stone" has since become a generic term for anything that supplies a crucial key to understanding.

The decree

The text records honours voted by a council of priests for the young king Ptolemy V on the first anniversary of his coronation: tax remissions, gifts to temples, and the erection of royal statues and copies of the decree — of which the Rosetta Stone is one surviving fragment — in temples throughout Egypt.
