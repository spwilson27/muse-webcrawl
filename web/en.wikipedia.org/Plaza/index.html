Plaza

A traditional town plaza in a Spanish-speaking city

A plaza is an open public space, such as a city square, found in many towns and cities around the world. The word comes from Spanish, where it denotes the central square of a town, traditionally the site of the cathedral, the town hall (ayuntamiento) and other civic buildings, with commercial arcades around its edges. In Spanish America, colonial law — particularly the Laws of the Indies (1573) — prescribed the plaza mayor as the organising centre of every new settlement, from which a grid of streets would extend.

History

The plaza descends from the Roman forum and the Greek agora, open civic spaces where markets, assemblies and ceremonies took place. In Spain, the plaza mayor developed distinctive forms: Madrid's Plaza Mayor (completed 1619) is a closed, rectangular square surrounded by uniform residential buildings with balconies, designed to host bullfights, festivals, markets and autos-da-fé. Spanish colonists transplanted the form to the Americas and the Philippines, where the plaza complex — church, municipal palace, market — remains the social heart of many towns. In Mexico the central plaza is often called the zócalo, a name originating with Mexico City's main square.

Uses

Plazas function as places for markets, festivals, concerts, political demonstrations, promenading and everyday socialising. The evening paseo — a leisurely circuit of the plaza — is a longstanding custom in many Spanish and Latin American towns. Notable plazas include the Plaza Mayor in Madrid and Salamanca, the Zócalo in Mexico City, Plaza de Mayo in Buenos Aires, Plaza Bolívar in Bogotá, and St. Peter's Square in Vatican City. In modern urban planning the term has been extended to paved pedestrian spaces in front of landmark buildings, and in North America "plaza" is also commonly used for shopping centres and highway toll collection points (toll plazas) and rest stops (service plazas).

Design

Classic plaza design typically includes a central feature — a fountain, bandstand (quiosco), statue or garden — shaded seating, and enclosing buildings of a consistent height that define the space. Urban theorists such as William H. Whyte, who studied New York's plazas in The Social Life of Small Urban Spaces (1980), found that successful plazas offer abundant sittable space, food, water features, trees and a good relationship with the street.
