The University of Edinburgh (Scots Gaelic: Oilthigh Dhùn Èideann; abbreviated as Edin. in post-nominals) is a public research university based in Edinburgh, Scotland. Founded by the town council under the authority of a royal charter granted by King James VI in 1582 and officially opened in 1583, it is one of Scotland's four ancient universities and the sixth-oldest university in continuous operation in the English-speaking world.

History

The university played a central role in the Scottish Enlightenment of the 18th century, when figures such as philosopher David Hume, economist Adam Smith, and chemist Joseph Black taught or studied there, earning Edinburgh the nickname "Athens of the North". Its medical school became one of the most influential in the English-speaking world, pioneering teaching in anaesthesia-associated research and antiseptic surgery through figures such as James Young Simpson and Joseph Lister. Edinburgh was among the first British universities to admit women to degrees (from 1892), after the long campaign of the Edinburgh Seven.

Academics

The university is organized into three colleges — Arts, Humanities and Social Sciences; Science and Engineering; and Medicine and Veterinary Medicine — comprising over twenty schools. It consistently ranks among the top universities in the United Kingdom and the world, and counts numerous Nobel laureates among its alumni and staff. Modern achievements include the cloning of Dolly the sheep at the affiliated Roslin Institute in 1996 and Peter Higgs's work on the Higgs boson.

Campus

The university's buildings are spread across the city, including the historic Old College, the George Square and central area, King's Buildings for science and engineering, and the BioQuarter for medicine at Little France.

Student life

Edinburgh University Students' Association runs Teviot Row House, often cited as the oldest purpose-built student union building in the world (opened 1889). The university has over 300 student societies and a long sporting tradition.
