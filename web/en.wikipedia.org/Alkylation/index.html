Alkylation

Alkylation is a chemical reaction that entails transfer of an alkyl group. The alkyl group may be transferred as an alkyl carbocation, a free radical, a carbanion, or a carbene (or their equivalents). Alkylating agents are reagents for effecting alkylation. Alkyl groups can also be removed in a process known as dealkylation. Alkylating agents are often classified by their charge and type.

In organic chemistry

In the broadest sense, alkylation in organic chemistry covers any reaction that introduces an alkyl group onto a substrate molecule. The classic example is the Friedel–Crafts alkylation, in which an aromatic ring is alkylated by an alkyl halide in the presence of a strong Lewis acid catalyst such as aluminium chloride. Other important methods include the alkylation of enolates, organometallic reagents, and nucleophilic substitution reactions using alkyl halides or sulfonate esters.

In petroleum refining

In oil refining, alkylation is a process that combines light olefins, principally propylene and butylene, with isobutane in the presence of a strong acid catalyst — either sulfuric acid or hydrofluoric acid — to produce alkylate, a mixture of highly branched alkanes valued as a blending component for motor gasoline because of its high octane number and low vapour pressure.

In biology and medicine

In biochemistry, alkylation refers to the addition of alkyl groups to biomolecules; methylation, the addition of a methyl group, is a widespread regulatory modification of DNA and proteins. Alkylating agents that damage DNA are also used as chemotherapy drugs in cancer treatment, because rapidly dividing cells are especially sensitive to the resulting cross-links and strand breaks.
