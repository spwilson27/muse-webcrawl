Injection moulding

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Injection moulding (American spelling: injection molding) is a manufacturing process for producing parts by injecting molten material into a mould (die). It is most widely used with thermoplastic polymers, though it can also be applied to thermosets, elastomers, and metals. The process underpins the mass production of plastic goods from bottle caps and LEGO bricks to car bumpers and medical devices.

Process

Polymer pellets are fed from a hopper into a heated barrel, where a rotating reciprocating screw melts and conveys the plastic. The screw then rams forward, injecting the melt at high pressure through a nozzle and runner system into a closed, cooled steel or aluminium mould cavity. The part cools and solidifies in the shape of the cavity; the mould opens and ejector pins push the finished part out. Cycle times range from a few seconds to a few minutes.

Equipment and tooling

Machines are rated by clamping force, from a few tonnes to several thousand. The mould — typically machined from hardened tool steel — is by far the most expensive element, so injection moulding is economical mainly at high volumes; mould design must account for shrinkage, draft angles, parting lines, and cooling channels. Modern practice uses computer simulation ("mould flow analysis") to predict defects such as warping, sink marks, weld lines, and short shots.

History

The first injection moulding machine was patented in 1872 by John Wesley Hyatt and his brother Isaiah for moulding celluloid. James Hendry built the first screw-injection machine in 1946, enabling faster, more uniform production; screw machines now dominate the industry. Gas-assisted, multi-component (overmoulding), and micro-injection variants extend the basic process.
