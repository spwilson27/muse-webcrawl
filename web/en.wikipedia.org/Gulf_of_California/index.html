The Gulf of California, also known as the Sea of Cortés (Spanish: Mar de Cortés), is a marginal sea of the Pacific Ocean that separates the Baja California Peninsula from the Mexican mainland. Formed about 5.3 million years ago as tectonic forces tore the peninsula away from the mainland, it is one of the most biologically diverse seas on Earth — Jacques Cousteau called it "the world's aquarium".

Biodiversity

The gulf supports more than 5,000 species of micro-invertebrates, some 900 fish species (many endemic), and rich populations of whales, dolphins, sea lions and seabirds. Islands such as Isla Ángel de la Guarda and Isla Tiburón harbour unique reptiles and plants. The upper gulf is the only home of the vaquita, the world's smallest and most endangered porpoise, driven toward extinction by gillnets set for the totoaba fish.

Geology and human use

The gulf sits on the boundary of the Pacific and North American plates, extending the East Pacific Rise and the San Andreas fault system. Its waters support major shrimp and sardine fisheries and a growing tourism industry centred on La Paz, Loreto and San Carlos. The Islands and Protected Areas of the Gulf of California were inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2005.
