Hull (watercraft)

A hull is the watertight body of a ship, boat, submarine, or flying boat. The hull may open at the top (such as a dinghy), or it may be fully or partially covered with a deck. Atop the deck may be a deckhouse and other superstructures, such as a funnel, derrick, or mast. The line where the hull meets the water surface is called the waterline.

General features

A hull has several standard parts: the bow (front), stern (rear), port (left) and starboard (right) sides, the keel along the bottom centreline, and the bilge where the bottom curves up to the sides. Hulls are characterised by measurements including length overall, beam (width), draft (depth below the waterline), and freeboard (height of the deck above the water).

Hull forms

Hulls are broadly classified as displacement hulls, which move through the water by displacing it, and planing hulls, which at speed ride partly on top of the water. Vessel configurations include monohulls, catamarans (two hulls), trimarans (three), and semi-displacement forms. The shape of the hull strongly affects a vessel's speed, stability, seakeeping, and carrying capacity.

Construction

Hull structures may be built of wood, steel, aluminium, fibreglass-reinforced plastic, concrete, or composite materials. Historically, wooden hulls used carvel or clinker planking over frames; modern ship hulls typically use welded steel plating over internal framing, subdivided by bulkheads into watertight compartments.
