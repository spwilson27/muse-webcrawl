Well-being

Well-being, or wellbeing, also known as wellness, prudential value, prosperity or quality of life, is what is intrinsically valuable relative to someone. So the well-being of a person is what is ultimately good for this person, what is in the self-interest of this person. Well-being can refer to both positive and negative well-being. In its positive sense, it is sometimes contrasted with ill-being as its opposite.

Theories of well-being

Philosophers and psychologists have proposed several theories of what constitutes well-being. Hedonistic theories identify well-being with pleasure and the absence of pain. Desire theories hold that well-being consists in the satisfaction of a person's desires. Objective list theories hold that well-being depends on a plurality of goods, such as knowledge, friendship, achievement and health, which may contribute to a person's good independently of their desires. Eudaimonic approaches, originating with Aristotle, tie well-being to flourishing and living in accordance with virtue.

Subjective well-being

In psychology, subjective well-being (SWB) is how people experience and evaluate their lives, usually measured by life satisfaction, the presence of positive affect, and the absence of negative affect. Psychologist Ed Diener is a leading researcher in the field. Well-being is used by governments and organizations as an indicator of social progress, for example in the Human Development Index and various national well-being surveys.
