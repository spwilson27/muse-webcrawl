Deficient number

In number theory, a deficient number or deficient integer is a positive integer n for which the sum of its divisors σ(n) is less than twice the number: σ(n) < 2n. Equivalently, n is deficient if the sum of its proper divisors (its aliquot sum) is less than n itself.

Examples

The first deficient numbers are 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 13, 14, 15, 16 and 17. For example, the proper divisors of 8 are 1, 2 and 4, summing to 7, which is less than 8, so 8 is deficient. All prime numbers are deficient, since their only proper divisor is 1; all powers of primes, and all powers of two, are likewise deficient.

Related concepts

Deficient numbers contrast with perfect numbers, where the aliquot sum equals the number, and abundant numbers, where it exceeds the number. Most integers are deficient: the natural density of deficient numbers is between about 0.752 and 0.753, so roughly three quarters of all positive integers are deficient.
