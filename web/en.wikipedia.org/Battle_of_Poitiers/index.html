Battle of Poitiers

The Battle of Poitiers was fought on 19 September 1356 between an English-Gascon army commanded by Edward, the Black Prince, and a French army under King John II of France. It was one of the great English victories of the Hundred Years' War, following Crécy (1346) and preceding Agincourt (1415). The French army was destroyed and King John captured, an event that plunged France into political crisis.

Background

In 1356 Edward of Woodstock, Prince of Wales, led a great chevauchée (raid) northward from Bordeaux through France. King John II assembled a large army—modern estimates suggest 14,000–16,000 men against Edward's 6,000–7,000—and intercepted the Anglo-Gascon force near Poitiers as it tried to retire south laden with plunder. After negotiations mediated by Cardinal Talleyrand de Périgord failed, both sides prepared for battle.

The battle

Edward deployed defensively among hedges, vineyards and a marsh-covered flank, with longbowmen positioned along a hedged lane. The French attacked largely on foot in successive divisions (batailles). The first French attack, including a small mounted force meant to silence the archers, failed under arrow fire. The Dauphin's division engaged in hard fighting and then withdrew, followed by the Duke of Orléans's division, much of which left the field. King John's own division, the largest, advanced and fought fiercely, but a flanking counterattack by the Captal de Buch's Gascon force and a renewed charge led by the Black Prince broke French resistance. John II, fighting on foot with a poleaxe, was captured along with his young son Philip and thousands of nobles.

Aftermath

France lost some 2,500 men killed and many more captured, including the king himself. The disaster triggered the Jacquerie revolt, the uprising of Étienne Marcel in Paris, and ultimately the Treaty of Brétigny (1360), by which John was ransomed for three million crowns and England gained an enlarged Aquitaine in full sovereignty. Poitiers confirmed the battlefield effectiveness of the English tactical system: dismounted men-at-arms supported by longbowmen on a chosen defensive position.
