Machu Picchu

Machu Picchu is a 15th-century Inca citadel in the Cusco Region of Peru, set on a mountain ridge at about 2,430 metres (7,970 ft) above sea level above the Sacred Valley, roughly 80 kilometres northwest of Cusco. Built around 1450, most likely as a royal estate for the emperor Pachacuti, it was abandoned about a century later during the Spanish conquest. It is the most familiar icon of Inca civilisation and one of the New7Wonders of the World.

History

Radiocarbon dating places construction in the reign of Pachacuti Inca Yupanqui (1438–1472). Scholars debate whether Machu Picchu was a royal retreat, a religious sanctuary, or a ceremonial centre; most agree it housed the emperor's court during part of the year, supported by a permanent population of a few hundred retainers and nearby agricultural estates. The site was never reached by the Spanish and passed out of general knowledge, though local Quechua farmers always knew of it. Yale historian Hiram Bingham was guided to the ruins in 1911 by local farmer Melchor Arteaga and publicised them internationally; Bingham's removal of artefacts to Yale University led to a long dispute, resolved when Yale returned the collection to Peru in 2011–2012.

Architecture

The dry-stone walls, built of granite blocks cut and fitted without mortar (ashlar masonry), are celebrated for their precision and earthquake resistance. The site divides into an agricultural sector of terraces and an urban sector with temples, plazas, and houses with steep thatched roofs. Notable structures include the Temple of the Sun, the Intihuatana ritual stone, the Room of the Three Windows, and the Temple of the Condor. Structures are aligned to astronomical events such as the solstices, reflecting Inca cosmology centred on the sun god Inti.

Tourism and conservation

Machu Picchu is Peru's most visited attraction, reached by rail to Aguas Calientes and then by bus, or on foot via the multi-day Inca Trail trek. Visitor numbers are capped (about 4,500–5,600 per day in recent regulations) with timed entry circuits to limit erosion. UNESCO and Peruvian authorities monitor pressures from tourism, landslides, and development; the site was inscribed as a mixed cultural and natural World Heritage Site in 1983 within the Historic Sanctuary of Machu Picchu, which protects surrounding cloud forest rich in orchids and endemic birds.
