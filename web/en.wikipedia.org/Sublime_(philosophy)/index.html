Sublime (philosophy)

The sublime
FieldAesthetics
Key thinkersLonginus, Edmund Burke, Immanuel Kant, Friedrich Schiller
ContrastThe beautiful

In aesthetics, the sublime (from the Latin sublīmis) is the quality of greatness, whether physical, moral, intellectual, metaphysical, aesthetic, spiritual, or artistic. The term especially refers to a greatness beyond all possibility of calculation, measurement, or imitation — an experience of awe, terror, and exaltation before overwhelming vastness or power, as in storms, mountains, and the ocean.

Ancient origins

The foundational text is Peri Hypsous ("On the Sublime"), attributed to Longinus, a 1st- or 3rd-century Greek rhetorical treatise that analysed sublimity in literature as the effect of elevated language transporting the reader. Rediscovered in the Renaissance and translated by Boileau in 1674, it profoundly influenced 18th-century thought.

Burke and Kant

Edmund Burke's A Philosophical Enquiry into the Origin of Our Ideas of the Sublime and Beautiful (1757) contrasted the sublime — vast, obscure, powerful, and terrifying — with the beautiful — small, smooth, and pleasing. Burke grounded the sublime in pain and danger experienced at a safe remove.

Immanuel Kant, in the Critique of Judgment (1790), distinguished the mathematical sublime (overwhelming magnitude) from the dynamical sublime (overwhelming might). For Kant, sublimity resides not in nature but in the mind: confronted with nature's immensity, reason discovers its own supersensible vocation, and initial displeasure gives way to a higher pleasure.

Later developments

The sublime became central to Romanticism, shaping landscape painting (Caspar David Friedrich, J. M. W. Turner) and poetry (Wordsworth). In the 20th century, Jean-François Lyotard made the sublime a key concept of postmodern aesthetics, and the idea has been extended to technology and the digital age.
