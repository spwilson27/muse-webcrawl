Telomerase

Telomerase, also called terminal transferase, is a ribonucleoprotein that is a reverse transcriptase, carrying its own RNA molecule (the telomerase RNA component, TERC) which is used as a template when it elongates telomeres. Telomeres are the protective caps of repetitive DNA at the ends of linear chromosomes; they shorten with each cell division, and telomerase counteracts this shortening.

Discovery and structure

Telomerase was discovered by Carol Greider and Elizabeth Blackburn in the ciliate Tetrahymena in 1985; Blackburn, Greider, and Jack Szostak shared the 2009 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for discovering how chromosomes are protected by telomeres and the enzyme telomerase. The catalytic protein subunit, TERT, is a reverse transcriptase; the RNA component TERC provides the template for synthesizing the species-specific telomeric repeat (TTAGGG in vertebrates).

Function and significance

In most multicellular organisms, telomerase is active in germline and stem cells but repressed in most somatic cells, leading to the Hayflick limit of cellular division and replicative senescence. Most cancer cells reactivate telomerase — often through mutations in the TERT promoter — enabling unlimited proliferation, making telomerase an important target in oncology and in research on aging. Dyskeratosis congenita and some forms of aplastic anemia and pulmonary fibrosis result from inherited defects in telomerase components, causing prematurely short telomeres.
