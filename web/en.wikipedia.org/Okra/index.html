Okra

Okra (Abelmoschus esculentus), known in some English-speaking countries as lady's fingers, is a flowering plant in the mallow family native to East Africa. Cultivated in tropical, subtropical, and warm temperate regions around the world, okra is used as a vegetable. In some countries it is called bhindi, bamia, or gumbo.

Description

The plant is a perennial, often grown as an annual in temperate climates, growing to about 2 metres (6.6 ft) tall. Its leaves are broad and palmate; the flowers are hibiscus-like, yellow with a crimson centre. The fruit is a long, tapering capsule containing many seeds, harvested immature and eaten as a vegetable. When cut, the pods release a mucilage that thickens stews and soups.

Cultivation and production

Okra is grown throughout the tropics. India is by far the largest producer, followed by Nigeria and other West African countries. The plant is drought- and heat-tolerant but frost-sensitive.

Culinary use

Okra is a key ingredient in gumbo in Louisiana, in Indian dishes such as bhindi masala, in Middle Eastern bamia stews, and in West African soups. It may be fried, pickled, roasted, or stewed. The mucilage can be minimised by cooking the pods whole, frying, or adding acidic ingredients such as tomatoes or vinegar. Roasted okra seeds were historically used as a coffee substitute.

Nutrition

Raw okra is rich in dietary fibre, vitamin C, vitamin K, folate, and magnesium, and low in calories.
