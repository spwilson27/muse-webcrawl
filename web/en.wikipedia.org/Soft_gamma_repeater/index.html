Soft gamma repeaters (SGRs) are astronomical objects that emit large bursts of gamma rays and X-rays at irregular intervals. They are now understood to be magnetars: young, isolated neutron stars with magnetic fields of 1014–1015 gauss—the strongest known in the universe—whose decay and sudden rearrangement power the bursts. SGR spectra are "soft" (lower-energy) compared with classical gamma-ray bursts, and, unlike gamma-ray bursts, the sources repeat.

Discovery and history

The class began with the extraordinary event of 5 March 1979, when detectors on multiple spacecraft recorded an intense spike followed by decaying oscillations, later localised to the supernova remnant N49 in the Large Magellanic Cloud: SGR 0525−66. A handful of further sources were identified in the 1980s and 1990s. On 27 August 1998, SGR 1900+14 emitted a giant flare whose ionising radiation measurably affected Earth's upper atmosphere on the night side. On 27 December 2004, SGR 1806−20 produced the brightest giant flare ever recorded—releasing more energy in a tenth of a second than the Sun emits in roughly 150,000 years—and saturated gamma-ray detectors across the Solar System.

Emission mechanism

In the magnetar model proposed by Robert Duncan and Christopher Thompson, stresses in the neutron star crust, driven by the evolving magnetic field, cause starquakes that release magnetic energy as short bursts. Giant flares are attributed to large-scale reconnection of the external magnetic field. Between bursts the sources emit persistent X-rays, often pulsed at the star's rotation period of about 2–12 seconds. In April 2020, SGR 1935+2154 emitted a millisecond radio burst simultaneous with an X-ray burst—the first galactic fast radio burst—linking magnetars to at least some extragalactic FRBs.
