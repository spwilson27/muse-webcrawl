Glucose is a simple sugar (monosaccharide) with the molecular formula C6H12O6. It is the most abundant monosaccharide in nature and the primary source of energy for most living cells. The naturally occurring form, D-glucose, is also called dextrose; in animals it circulates in the blood as blood sugar.

Structure

Glucose is an aldohexose: a six-carbon sugar bearing an aldehyde group. In solution it exists mostly as a six-membered ring (glucopyranose) in two anomeric forms, α and β, which interconvert by mutarotation. Polymerisation of glucose yields the storage polysaccharides starch and glycogen and the structural polysaccharide cellulose.

Biological role

Plants produce glucose by photosynthesis; animals obtain it from digestion of carbohydrates. Cells extract energy from glucose through glycolysis and cellular respiration, yielding up to about 30 molecules of ATP per glucose molecule. In humans, blood glucose is tightly regulated by insulin and glucagon; normal fasting concentrations are roughly 70–99 mg/dL (3.9–5.5 mmol/L). Chronic elevation defines diabetes mellitus, and the glycated haemoglobin test (HbA1c) reflects average glucose over several months.

Uses

Commercial glucose is produced by hydrolysis of starch and is used in foods, confectionery, fermentation and medicine, including intravenous dextrose solutions for treating hypoglycaemia and dehydration.
