The Valley campaigns of 1864 were a series of military operations in the Shenandoah Valley of Virginia during the American Civil War. The valley, a rich agricultural region known as the "Breadbasket of the Confederacy" and a traditional invasion corridor, was contested repeatedly throughout the war. In 1864, Union forces under Maj. Gen. Philip Sheridan finally secured it and systematically destroyed its capacity to supply the Confederacy.

Early operations

In May 1864, Maj. Gen. Franz Sigel advanced up the valley but was defeated by Confederate forces under John C. Breckinridge at the Battle of New Market (May 15), where cadets from the Virginia Military Institute famously joined the fight. Sigel was replaced by Maj. Gen. David Hunter, who won at Piedmont (June 5), occupied Staunton and Lexington, and burned VMI. Confederate Lt. Gen. Jubal Early then took command, drove Hunter into West Virginia, and launched a daring raid down the valley, across the Potomac, threatening Washington, D.C. itself at the Battle of Fort Stevens (July 11–12).

Sheridan's campaign

Alarmed, Lt. Gen. Ulysses S. Grant created the Army of the Shenandoah under Philip Sheridan. Sheridan defeated Early at the Third Battle of Winchester (September 19) and Fisher's Hill (September 22), then conducted "The Burning" — the systematic destruction of barns, mills, crops, and livestock in the valley. Early's surprise dawn attack at Cedar Creek (October 19) initially routed much of Sheridan's army, but Sheridan's famous ride from Winchester rallied his men to a decisive counterattack that effectively ended Confederate resistance in the valley.

Legacy

The campaigns eliminated the Shenandoah as a Confederate supply source and invasion route, contributing materially to the Union victory in 1865.
