Pahoehoe (from Hawaiian pāhoehoe, meaning "smooth, unbroken lava") is basaltic lava characterised by a smooth, billowy, undulating or ropy surface. It forms from very fluid, hot, gas-rich lava that advances as a series of small lobes and toes, continually breaking out from a cooled crust. Pahoehoe is one of the principal surface morphologies of basaltic lava flows, the other common type being ʻaʻā, which has a rough, jagged, clinkery surface.

Formation

Pahoehoe flows form when lava has a low viscosity — typically because it is hot (around 1,100–1,200 °C), poor in silica, and rich in dissolved gas — and when eruption rates are moderate. The surface of the flow chills into a thin, still-plastic skin; continuing movement beneath drags and wrinkles this skin into the characteristic rope-like folds. Individual flow units are commonly small, from tens of centimetres to a few metres across, and stack upon one another as the flow advances.

Because pahoehoe insulates well, lava can travel long distances beneath an intact crust in lava tubes, emerging at flow fronts far from the vent. Prominent examples come from the shield volcanoes of Hawaii, including eruptions of Kīlauea and Mauna Loa, and from Icelandic eruptions such as those of the Fagradalsfjall system.

Transition to ʻaʻā

As pahoehoe lava moves away from its vent it loses heat and gas, its viscosity rises, and the flow may transform into ʻaʻā. The transition is one-way: ʻaʻā never reverts to pahoehoe. Morphological varieties of pahoehoe include ropy, slabby, and "entrail" pahoehoe, the last consisting of piles of elongated, overlapping toes.

Etymology

The term was introduced into geological literature from the Hawaiian language in the nineteenth century, together with ʻaʻā, and both are now used worldwide by volcanologists.
