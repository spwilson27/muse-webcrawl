Coal

Coal is a combustible black or brownish-black sedimentary rock, formed as rock strata called coal seams. It is mostly carbon with variable amounts of other elements, chiefly hydrogen, sulfur, oxygen and nitrogen. Coal is a type of fossil fuel, formed when dead plant matter decays into peat and is converted by burial heat and pressure over millions of years. It powered the Industrial Revolution and remains the largest single source of electricity generation worldwide, though its use is declining in many countries because burning coal is the fossil fuel most intensive in carbon dioxide emissions.

Formation and ranks

Most coal formed from the remains of plants that grew in vast tropical wetlands of the Carboniferous period (359–299 million years ago) and in later eras. Buried plant matter first becomes peat; with increasing depth, heat and time it is transformed through the ranks of lignite ("brown coal"), sub-bituminous and bituminous coal to anthracite, progressively richer in carbon and energy and poorer in moisture and volatiles. High-rank coals suitable for making coke ("metallurgical coal") are essential to steelmaking via the blast furnace.

Extraction and use

Coal is extracted by surface (open-pit/strip) mining where seams are shallow and by underground mining for deep seams. Coal mining has historically been among the most dangerous occupations, with hazards including roof falls, explosions of methane (firedamp) and coal dust, and the lung disease pneumoconiosis ("black lung"). Coal is burned in power stations for electricity and heat; converted to coke for steel; gasified or liquefied into syngas and liquid fuels; and used in cement kilns and industrial processes.

Environmental and health impact

Coal combustion produces more CO2 per unit of energy than oil or natural gas, and is the largest single source of anthropogenic carbon dioxide. It also emits sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides (acid rain), particulates, and heavy metals such as mercury, and coal ash is a major industrial waste stream. Uncontrolled underground coal-seam fires can burn for decades. These impacts drive policies to phase out unabated coal power as part of climate mitigation, as well as research into carbon capture and storage.

History

Coal was used in Bronze Age China and Roman Britain, but its large-scale adoption came with the steam engine in 18th–19th-century Britain, where coal underwrote industrialisation, railways and steam shipping. Coalfields shaped whole regions — from South Wales and the Ruhr to Appalachia and Silesia. Although oil and gas displaced it in many uses during the 20th century, global coal consumption reached record levels in the 2020s, driven by demand in Asia.
