Zitterbewegung

Zitterbewegung (German pronunciation: [ˈtsɪtɐbəˌveːɡʊŋ], "trembling motion") is the theoretical prediction of a rapid oscillatory motion of elementary particles, in particular electrons, that obey the Dirac equation. This interference phenomenon was first predicted by Erwin Schrödinger in 1930 as a result of his analysis of the wave packet solutions of the Dirac equation for relativistic electrons in free space, in which the interference between positive and negative energy states produces what appears to be a fluctuation (up to the speed of light) of the position of an electron around the median, with an angular frequency of 2mc²/ħ, or approximately 1.6 × 10²¹ radians per second for an electron.

For an electron, the amplitude of the oscillation is of the order of the Compton wavelength, about 10⁻¹³ metres, far too small and fast to be observed directly for a real electron. In the Foldy–Wouthuysen representation, the effect is transformed away, and in quantum field theory it is reinterpreted in terms of the interference of particle and antiparticle components of the wave function. The phenomenon is related to the Darwin term in atomic fine structure.

Simulation in other systems

Although zitterbewegung cannot be measured directly for free electrons, its mathematical analogue has been simulated in analogue systems, including trapped ions (reported in 2010), photonic lattices, and ultracold atoms, where the governing equations mimic the Dirac equation with experimentally accessible parameters.
