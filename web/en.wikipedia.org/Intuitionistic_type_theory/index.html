Intuitionistic type theory

Intuitionistic type theory (also known as constructive type theory, or Martin-Löf type theory) is a type theory and an alternative foundation of mathematics. Intuitionistic type theory was created by Per Martin-Löf, a Swedish mathematician and philosopher, who first published it in 1972. There are multiple versions of the theory as it has developed over time.

Overview

The theory is based on the principles of mathematical constructivism, influenced by the intuitionism of L. E. J. Brouwer. Judgements — such as "A is a type" and "a is a term of type A" — replace the set-membership statements of set theory. Every mathematical object is a term of some type, and propositions are represented as types under the Curry–Howard correspondence: a proof of a proposition is a term of the corresponding type, so that "propositions as types" unifies logic and computation.

Type formers

The theory includes dependent types, in which a type may depend on a term. Central type formers include the Π-type (dependent function type), the Σ-type (dependent pair type), identity types (whose terms are proofs of equality), inductive types such as the natural numbers, and a hierarchy of universes. The interpretation of logical connectives follows the BHK interpretation: a proof of A → B is a function converting proofs of A into proofs of B.

Relation to other foundations

Unlike classical set theory, intuitionistic type theory does not accept the law of excluded middle or the axiom of choice as general principles, although certain restricted forms are derivable. Because proofs are programs, the theory provides a direct basis for proof assistants: the systems Agda, Coq (in part), Idris, and Lean draw on Martin-Löf type theory or its extensions such as the calculus of constructions. Homotopy type theory is a later extension that adds the univalence axiom and higher inductive types.

History

Martin-Löf presented successive versions of the theory in the 1970s and 1980s, culminating in the 1984 monograph Intuitionistic Type Theory (the "Bibliopolis book"), based on lectures in Padua. An early impredicative version was shown inconsistent by Jean-Yves Girard (Girard's paradox), prompting the move to a predicative hierarchy of universes.
