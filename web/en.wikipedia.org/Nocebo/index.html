Nocebo

A nocebo effect is said to occur when a patient's negative expectations about a treatment cause the treatment to have a more negative effect than it otherwise would have. For example, when a patient anticipates a side effect of a medication, they can experience that effect even if the "medication" is actually an inert substance: the complementary concept to the placebo effect, in which positive expectations produce improvement.

Terminology and history

The term derives from the Latin nocēbō, "I shall harm", mirroring placebo, "I shall please". It was coined by Walter Kennedy in 1961. The nocebo effect is well documented in clinical trials, where patients receiving dummy treatments report side effects matching those they were warned about, and in everyday practice, where listing adverse effects can increase their incidence.

Mechanisms

Negative verbal suggestions, prior conditioning, anxiety and somatosensory amplification are thought to contribute. Neurobiological studies link nocebo hyperalgesia to cholecystokinin-mediated facilitation of pain. The phenomenon raises ethical questions in medicine: fully informing patients of side effects is required for consent, yet the information itself may cause harm.
