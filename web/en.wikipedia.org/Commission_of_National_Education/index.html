Commission of National Education

The Commission of National Education (Polish: Komisja Edukacji Narodowej, KEN) was the central educational authority of the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth, established by King Stanisław August Poniatowski and the Sejm on 14 October 1773. It is widely regarded as the first ministry of education in Europe, and one of the landmark achievements of the Polish Enlightenment.

Background

Education in the Commonwealth had been dominated by the Jesuits. When Pope Clement XIV suppressed the Society of Jesus in 1773, its schools and assets faced confiscation. The Commission was created to take over the Jesuit school network and its endowments and to reform education along Enlightenment lines.

Reforms

The Commission created a unified, three-tier school system supervised by reformed universities in Kraków and Vilnius, introduced instruction in the Polish language, and promoted the sciences, mathematics, history, geography, and modern languages alongside classical subjects. It commissioned new textbooks, notably through the Society for Elementary Books (founded 1775), which produced pioneering Polish-language texts in subjects such as mathematics, physics, and logic. Teacher training was systematised, and schooling was opened beyond the nobility, although in practice provision remained limited.

Legacy

The Commission operated until 1794, when the final partition of Poland ended the Commonwealth. Its reforms influenced education across partitioned Polish lands for generations, and 14 October is still celebrated in Poland as National Education Day and Teachers' Day.
