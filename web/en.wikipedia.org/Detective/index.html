A detective is an investigator, usually a member of a law enforcement agency, whose role is to gather information and evidence in order to solve crimes: identifying offenders, locating missing persons and recovering stolen property. In many police forces, detective is also a rank or title held by officers assigned to investigative work in plain clothes rather than uniform. Private detectives, commonly called private investigators or "private eyes," perform similar work for private clients and are typically licensed separately.

History

Before the nineteenth century, organised detective work barely existed; investigation was left to constables, thief-takers and private informers. The Bow Street Runners in London (from 1749) were an early proto-detective force. In France, Eugène-François Vidocq, a former criminal, founded the Sûreté in 1812 and pioneered many investigative methods, including undercover work and meticulous record-keeping. London's Metropolitan Police established a small Detective Branch in 1842, which grew into the Criminal Investigation Department (CID) in 1878. In the United States, Allan Pinkerton founded the Pinkerton National Detective Agency in 1850, famous for its unblinking-eye logo and slogan "We never sleep," from which the term "private eye" derives.

Duties and methods

Detectives investigate offences such as homicide, robbery, burglary, fraud, sexual offences and drug trafficking. Their work includes:

  • Securing and examining crime scenes and physical evidence
  • Interviewing witnesses, victims and suspects
  • Surveillance and undercover operations
  • Applying for and executing search warrants and arrest warrants
  • Analysing records, communications data and forensic results
  • Preparing case files and testifying in court

Modern detective work relies heavily on forensic science, databases (such as DNA and fingerprint databases), CCTV analysis and digital evidence from phones and computers.

Organisation and ranks

In the United Kingdom and Commonwealth forces, detective ranks mirror uniformed ranks with the prefix "Detective": Detective Constable (DC), Detective Sergeant (DS), Detective Inspector (DI), Detective Chief Inspector (DCI), Detective Superintendent and Detective Chief Superintendent. In the United States, detective is often a grade or assignment rather than a supervisory rank; large departments such as the NYPD have graded detective ranks, and detectives usually specialise in squads (homicide, narcotics, robbery, special victims).

Private detectives

Private investigators are hired by individuals, lawyers, insurers and businesses to conduct background checks, locate persons, investigate fraud or infidelity, and gather evidence for litigation. Most jurisdictions require licensing, and private detectives generally lack police powers of arrest beyond those of any citizen.

In fiction

The detective is one of the most enduring figures in popular culture. Detective fiction emerged in the nineteenth century with Edgar Allan Poe's C. Auguste Dupin, followed by Arthur Conan Doyle's Sherlock Holmes, and developed through the "golden age" writers such as Agatha Christie (Hercule Poirot, Miss Marple) and the hard-boiled school of Dashiell Hammett and Raymond Chandler (Sam Spade, Philip Marlowe). Police procedurals and television series have since made the detective a staple of global entertainment.
