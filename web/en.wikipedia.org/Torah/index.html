The Torah (Hebrew: תּוֹרָה, "instruction", "teaching", or "law") is the compilation of the first five books of the Hebrew Bible, namely the books of Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers and Deuteronomy. The Torah is the central reference of the religious Judaic tradition, and Jewish belief holds that it was revealed by God to Moses on Mount Sinai. In rabbinic literature the word "Torah" can also denote the entire corpus of Jewish law and teaching, including the Written Torah and the Oral Torah.

Contents

The Torah begins with the creation of the world and the stories of the patriarchs and matriarchs — Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, and their families — followed by the Exodus from slavery in Egypt under Moses, the giving of the law at Mount Sinai, the laws and rituals given to Israel, the wilderness wanderings, and concludes with the death of Moses before the entry into the Promised Land. It contains, by traditional count, 613 commandments (mitzvot).

Composition

Modern biblical scholarship generally regards the Torah as the product of multiple authors and editors over centuries, reaching its final form in the Persian period (roughly the 5th century BCE), drawing on earlier sources and traditions. Traditional Jewish and Christian interpretation attributes its authorship to Moses.

Ritual use

Torah scrolls (Sefer Torah) are handwritten on parchment by trained scribes and kept in the ark of the synagogue; portions are read publicly on a weekly cycle, completed each year at Simchat Torah. The Torah also forms the first part of the Christian Old Testament, where the five books are called the Pentateuch.
