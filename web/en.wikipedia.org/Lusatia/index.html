Lusatia (German: Lausitz; Upper Sorbian: Łužica; Lower Sorbian: Łužyca; Polish: Łużyce; Czech: Lužice) is a historical region in Central Europe, split between Germany and Poland. It stretches from the Bóbr and Kwisa rivers in the east to the Elbe in the west, and is further divided into Upper Lusatia and Lower Lusatia. Lusatia is the homeland of the Sorbs, a West Slavic people who maintain their own languages and culture.

Lusatia straddles the modern German–Polish border along the Neisse river.

Geography

Lower Lusatia, in the north, is a flat, partly forested lowland containing the Spree Forest (Spreewald) with its network of channels. Upper Lusatia, in the south, is hillier, rising to the Lusatian Highlands and the Lusatian Mountains on the Czech border. Major towns include Cottbus, Bautzen, Görlitz and Zittau.

History

Slavic tribes settled the region in the early Middle Ages. Lusatia was contested between the Holy Roman Empire, Bohemia, Saxony and Brandenburg; the Upper and Lower Lusatias passed to Saxony at the Peace of Prague in 1635. The Congress of Vienna in 1815 awarded Lower Lusatia and part of Upper Lusatia to Prussia. After the Second World War, the part east of the Oder–Neisse line became Polish.

The Sorbs

The Sorbs are a recognised national minority in Germany. Sorbian-language schools, media and cultural institutions are supported especially around Bautzen and Cottbus, and bilingual signage is common in the Sorbian settlement area.
