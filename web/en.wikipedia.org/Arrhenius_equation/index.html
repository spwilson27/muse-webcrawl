Arrhenius equation

The Arrhenius equation is a formula for the temperature dependence of reaction rates. It was proposed by the Swedish scientist Svante Arrhenius in 1889, building on work by J. J. Hood and van 't Hoff, and remains one of the foundational relations of chemical kinetics and of fields from semiconductor reliability to food science.

k = A e−Ea / (RT)

where k is the rate constant, A is the pre-exponential factor (frequency factor), Ea is the activation energy, R is the universal gas constant, and T is the absolute temperature. The exponential term represents the fraction of molecular collisions energetic enough to overcome the activation-energy barrier.

Interpretation

Taking logarithms gives ln k = ln A − Ea/(RT), so a plot of ln k against 1/T (an Arrhenius plot) is a straight line whose slope yields the activation energy. The pre-exponential factor embodies collision frequency and orientation (steric) effects. Transition state theory later refined the equation (the Eyring equation), and modified Arrhenius forms with a temperature-dependent pre-factor are used for many gas-phase reactions.

Applications

The equation governs the rule of thumb that reaction rates roughly double for each 10 °C rise in temperature, and underlies accelerated-ageing tests, the temperature dependence of diffusion and viscosity, semiconductor failure rates, and the thermal limits of biological processes.
