Dispersion relation

A dispersion relation relates the wavelength or wavenumber of a wave to its frequency. Given the dispersion relation, one can calculate the frequency-dependent phase velocity and group velocity of waves in the medium. For electromagnetic waves in a medium, the relation is often written ω(k), connecting angular frequency ω with wavenumber k; in vacuum it reduces to the linear law ω = ck, so phase and group velocities are equal and no dispersion occurs.

Examples

Deep-water surface waves obey ω = √(gk), giving group velocity half the phase velocity, while shallow-water waves are approximately non-dispersive. In optics, the dispersion relation is expressed through the refractive index n(ω), responsible for the splitting of white light by a prism. For matter waves in quantum mechanics, a free particle has E = p²/2m, i.e. ω = ℏk²/2m. Dispersion curves for phonons and electrons in solids determine thermal and electronic properties.

History

The term arose in optics from the dispersion of light, and Isaac Newton's prism experiments of the 1660s–1670s were an early study of the phenomenon. The general framework was developed through 19th-century wave theory and is now used across physics, from plasma oscillations to seismic waves.
