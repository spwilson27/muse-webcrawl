National Park Service

The National Park Service (NPS) is an agency of the United States federal government, within the U.S. Department of the Interior, that manages all national parks and most national monuments and other natural, historical, and recreational properties, with various title designations. The NPS is charged with a dual role of preserving the ecological and historical integrity of the places entrusted to its management and making them available for public use and enjoyment.

History

The United States authorised the first "public park or pleasuring-ground for the benefit and enjoyment of the people" at Yellowstone in 1872, but early parks were administered piecemeal by the Interior Department, the Army and others. After a campaign led by Stephen Mather and Horace Albright, President Woodrow Wilson signed the National Park Service Organic Act on 25 August 1916, creating the Service "to conserve the scenery and the natural and historic objects and the wild life therein and to provide for the enjoyment of the same in such manner and by such means as will leave them unimpaired for the enjoyment of future generations." Mather became its first director. In 1933 the system expanded greatly when national monuments, military parks and the national capital parks were transferred to the NPS under Franklin D. Roosevelt's government reorganisation.

The National Park System

The National Park System comprises more than 430 units — national parks, monuments, battlefields, military parks, historical parks, historic sites, lakeshores, seashores, recreation areas, scenic rivers and trails, and the White House — covering about 85 million acres in all 50 states, the District of Columbia, and U.S. territories. Famous parks include Yellowstone, Yosemite, Grand Canyon, Zion, Olympic and Great Smoky Mountains, the most visited unit. Annual visitation is on the order of 300 million recreation visits.

People and programmes

The Service employs roughly 20,000 people plus hundreds of thousands of volunteers. Uniformed park rangers — an image popularised by the flat campaign hat — perform protection, interpretation and emergency services. The NPS also administers programmes beyond park boundaries, including the National Register of Historic Places, National Historic Landmarks, historic preservation tax incentives, and rivers and trails conservation assistance. Its emblem, adopted in 1951, is an arrowhead whose elements — a sequoia tree, a mountain, a bison and water — symbolise vegetation, land, wildlife and recreation.

Challenges

The Service faces a large deferred-maintenance backlog, overtourism at popular parks, and the effects of climate change on glaciers, wildlife and coastal sites, all while operating under its dual mandate of conservation and public enjoyment.
