Strict conditional

In logic, a strict conditional (also called strict implication) is a conditional statement expressing that the consequent necessarily follows from the antecedent. It was developed by C. I. Lewis to improve on the material conditional of classical logic, whose paradoxes — such as a false proposition implying anything — make it a poor model of ordinary "if... then..." statements.

Definition

The strict conditional "if p then necessarily q" is written p ⥽ q and defined in modal logic as □(p → q): in every possible world where p is true, q is true. Equivalently, it is impossible for p to be true while q is false: ¬◇(p ∧ ¬q).

Paradoxes

Strict implication avoids the paradoxes of material implication but has paradoxes of its own: an impossible proposition strictly implies any proposition, and any necessary proposition is strictly implied by any proposition. Lewis responded by developing the modal systems S1–S5, work that founded modern modal logic.
