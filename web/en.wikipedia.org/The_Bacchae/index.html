The Bacchae

The Bacchae (Ancient Greek: Βάκχαι, Bákkhai) is an ancient Greek tragedy written by the Athenian playwright Euripides during his final years in Macedonia, at the court of Archelaus I. Written in 405 BC, it was first performed posthumously at the Theatre of Dionysus in Athens in 405 BC as part of a trilogy that also included Iphigeneia at Aulis and Alcmaeon in Corinth; the trilogy won first prize.

Plot

The young god Dionysus returns to Thebes, his mother's city, to avenge himself on a family that denies his divinity: his aunts have denied that Zeus fathered him, and his cousin King Pentheus forbids his worship. Disguised as a mortal priest, Dionysus drives the women of Thebes — including Pentheus's mother Agave — into ecstatic frenzy on Mount Cithaeron. Pentheus imprisons the stranger, who miraculously escapes as an earthquake shatters the palace. Lured by curiosity, Pentheus is persuaded to dress as a maenad and spy on the rites; discovered in a pine tree, he is torn limb from limb (sparagmos) by the possessed women, Agave leading them and carrying her son's head in triumph, believing it a lion's. Too late, she recognizes her deed; Cadmus's house is destroyed and its remnants exiled.

Themes

The play is often read as a confrontation between reason and ecstatic irrationality, civic control and religious frenzy, and has attracted very different interpretations: a devout warning against impiety, or a subtle critique of the god's cruelty. Its chorus of Asian maenads sings some of the most famous odes in Greek tragedy. Nietzsche, Freud and modern anthropologists have all drawn on it, and it remains one of the most frequently staged Greek tragedies.
