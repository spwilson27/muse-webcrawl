Muscle contraction

Muscle contraction is the activation of tension-generating sites within muscle cells. Contraction does not necessarily mean the muscle shortens: in an isometric contraction the muscle generates force without changing length, while in an isotonic contraction the muscle changes length, either shortening (concentric) or lengthening under load (eccentric). In vertebrates, skeletal muscle contraction is controlled by the nervous system and powers movement, posture, and breathing.

The cross-bridge cycle: myosin heads ratchet along actin filaments, sliding them toward the centre of the sarcomere.

Sliding filament mechanism

Muscle fibres are composed of myofibrils divided into repeating units called sarcomeres, containing thin actin filaments anchored at the Z-discs and thick myosin filaments at the centre. During contraction neither filament shortens; instead myosin heads bind to actin, flex, and pull the thin filaments inward so the sarcomere shortens. Each cycle of binding, power stroke, and detachment consumes one molecule of ATP. The model was proposed in 1954 by two independent teams including Andrew Huxley and Hugh Huxley.

Excitation–contraction coupling

A signal from a motor neuron releases acetylcholine at the neuromuscular junction, triggering an action potential that travels along the fibre membrane and down T-tubules. This opens calcium channels in the sarcoplasmic reticulum, and the released Ca²⁺ binds to troponin, shifting tropomyosin away from the binding sites on actin and allowing cross-bridges to form. When stimulation ends, calcium is pumped back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum and the muscle relaxes; after death, ATP depletion leaves cross-bridges attached, producing rigor mortis.

Types of contraction

Concentric contractions shorten the muscle (lifting a weight), eccentric contractions lengthen it under tension (lowering a weight, downhill running) and are a potent stimulus for strength gains but also a cause of soreness, and isometric contractions hold tension without movement (planking, gripping). A single stimulus produces a twitch; rapid repeated stimuli summate into sustained tetanus, while the gradation of whole-muscle force is achieved by recruiting additional motor units.

Smooth and cardiac muscle

Smooth muscle uses calcium–calmodulin activation of myosin light-chain kinase rather than troponin, contracts slowly, and can maintain tone with little energy expenditure. Cardiac muscle contraction is triggered by calcium-induced calcium release and is coordinated across the heart by gap junctions in intercalated discs.
