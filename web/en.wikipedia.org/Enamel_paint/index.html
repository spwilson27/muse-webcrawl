Enamel paint

Enamel paint is paint that air-dries (or is cured) to a hard, usually glossy finish, used for coating surfaces that need a durable, protective, and washable layer. Despite the name, modern enamel paints contain no true enamel (powdered glass); the term instead describes their resemblance to the smooth, hard surface of vitreous enamel. Enamel paints are used on metal, wood, and masonry, in applications from household trim and appliances to automobiles, signage, and scale models.

Enamel paints dry to a hard, glossy, enamel-like finish

Composition

Traditional enamel paints are oil-based, using alkyd resins dissolved in a solvent; they dry slowly by oxidation to a tough film with excellent adhesion and levelling. Water-based ("acrylic enamel" or latex enamel) formulations have become common for easier clean-up and lower volatile organic compound emissions, trading some hardness for convenience. Two-part epoxy and polyurethane enamels, cured by a hardener, provide the toughest coatings for industrial and marine use.

Properties and uses

Enamel finishes resist moisture, abrasion, chemicals, and staining better than ordinary flat paints, and their gloss range runs from satin to high-gloss. Typical uses include doors, window frames, kitchen cabinets, radiators, garden furniture, bicycles, machinery, and boats. Small jars of solvent-based enamel — popularised by brands such as Humbrol and Testors — are a standard material in scale modelling, valued for their opacity and fine finish.

Application

Surfaces are cleaned, sanded, and usually primed before enamelling. Enamel can be brushed, rolled, or sprayed; it levels out as it dries, minimising brush marks. Oil-based enamels require mineral spirits for clean-up and have long recoat times, while water-based products dry quickly but may need more coats. Because the cured film is brittle relative to flexible substrates, enamels perform best on rigid surfaces.
