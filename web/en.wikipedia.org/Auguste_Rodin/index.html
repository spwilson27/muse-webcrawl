François Auguste René Rodin (12 November 1840 – 17 November 1917) was a French sculptor, generally considered the founder of modern sculpture. He was schooled traditionally and took a craftsman-like approach to his work. Rodin possessed a unique ability to model a complex, turbulent, and deeply pocketed surface in clay.

Many of Rodin's most notable sculptures were criticised during his lifetime. They clashed with predominant figurative sculpture traditions, in which works were decorative, formulaic, or highly thematic. Rodin's most original work departed from traditional themes of mythology and allegory, modelling the human body with naturalism, and celebrating individual character and physicality.

Early life and career

Born in Paris to a working-class family, Rodin was largely self-educated. Three times rejected by the École des Beaux-Arts, he earned his living as a decorative craftsman and ornamenter. A trip to Italy in 1875, where he studied Michelangelo, transformed his art; his Age of Bronze (1877) was so naturalistic that he was accused of having cast it from a living model.

Major works

In 1880 Rodin received the commission for The Gates of Hell, a monumental portal inspired by Dante's Inferno, which occupied him for the rest of his life and generated many individual figures, including The Thinker (originally representing Dante himself) and The Kiss. The Burghers of Calais (1889) and the controversial monument to Balzac (1898) are among his most celebrated public works.

Legacy

By 1900 Rodin was world-famous. He bequeathed his studio and works to the French state; the Musée Rodin in Paris opened in 1919 at the Hôtel Biron, his former residence. His influence on 20th-century sculpture, through pupils and assistants such as Camille Claudel and Constantin Brâncuși, was immense.
