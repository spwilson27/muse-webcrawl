Revealed preference theory is a method of analysing choices made by individuals, developed by the economist Paul Samuelson. Rather than assuming consumers maximise a given utility function, it infers their preferences from observed purchasing behaviour: if a consumer chooses bundle A when bundle B was affordable, A is "revealed preferred" to B.

Motivation

Samuelson proposed the approach (first in 1938, fully in his 1948 Foundations of Economic Analysis) to free demand theory from unobservable psychological notions of utility, grounding it instead in observable choice. It fitted the behaviourist and positivist temper of mid-20th-century economics and remains central to empirical consumer analysis and welfare measurement.

Axioms

The Weak Axiom of Revealed Preference (WARP) requires that if A is revealed preferred to B, then B must never be chosen when A is affordable. The Strong Axiom (SARP), developed by H. S. Houthakker (1950), extends consistency to indirect comparisons through chains of choices. The Generalised Axiom (GARP) handles observations in which the consumer may be indifferent between chosen and affordable bundles.

Rationalisability

Afriat's theorem (Sydney Afriat, 1967) gives the central result: a finite set of price and quantity observations satisfies GARP if and only if there exists a well-behaved (continuous, increasing, concave) utility function that the consumer could have been maximising. Revealed preference analysis is accordingly used to test whether data are consistent with utility maximisation, and to construct bounds on welfare changes, cost-of-living indices, and demand responses without specifying utility forms.

Criticisms and extensions

Critics note that choices may be inconsistent in ways documented by behavioural economics — framing effects, defaults, inattention — and that the theory can become tautological if inconsistent data are simply attributed to changing preferences. The framework has nonetheless been extended to household bargaining models, production, and social choice, and it inspired related work in philosophy on preference and belief.
