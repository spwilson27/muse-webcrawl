Banjo

Banjo
Type: String instrument
Origin: African-American and Caribbean traditions, North America
Common types: 5-string, tenor, plectrum

The banjo is a stringed instrument with a thin membrane stretched over a frame or cavity to form a resonator. It was created by enslaved Africans in the Americas, adapting West African instruments such as the akonting, and became central to American folk music, bluegrass, country and old-time music.

Construction

The banjo consists of a circular body (the "pot") with a membrane head, a long neck with frets, and typically five strings — four full-length strings plus a short fifth "thumb" string. Modern banjos often have a wooden or metal resonator back; open-back models are preferred for clawhammer playing.

Playing styles

Two major techniques dominate: clawhammer (frailing), a down-picking style used in old-time music, and bluegrass picking, popularised by Earl Scruggs, using fingerpicks and forward rolls. Four-string tenor and plectrum banjos feature in traditional jazz and Irish music, where the tenor banjo is standard.

History

References to banjo-like instruments in the Caribbean date to the 17th century. In the 19th century the banjo spread through minstrel shows and parlour music; Joel Sweeney is often credited with adding the fifth string. In the 20th century it became emblematic of bluegrass through Bill Monroe's band and players such as Scruggs and Béla Fleck, who extended its range into jazz and classical repertoire.
