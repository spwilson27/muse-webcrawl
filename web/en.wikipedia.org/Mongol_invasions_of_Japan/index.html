The Mongol invasions of Japan (Japanese: Genkō) were two major military expeditions launched by Kublai Khan, founder of China's Yuan dynasty, against Kamakura-period Japan in 1274 and 1281. Both invasions failed, largely because powerful typhoons destroyed much of the Mongol fleets — storms that entered Japanese tradition as the kamikaze, or "divine wind".

Background

After conquering Korea (Goryeo), Kublai Khan repeatedly sent envoys demanding Japanese submission and tribute. The Kamakura shogunate, under the regent Hōjō Tokimune, refused. Kublai therefore assembled an invasion fleet of Yuan and Korean ships and troops.

The invasions of 1274 and 1281

The first invasion (the Battle of Bun'ei) landed in Hakata Bay in November 1274; after a day of hard fighting the fleet withdrew and was wrecked by a storm. The second and far larger invasion came in 1281 (the Battle of Kōan), with two fleets totalling perhaps 140,000 men. Japanese defenders, fighting from coastal fortifications built after 1274, held the invaders at bay for weeks until a massive typhoon in August destroyed the Southern Route fleet at anchor near Takashima Island; most surviving invaders were killed or captured.

Consequences

The failed invasions were among the most significant events in Japanese history: they preserved Japan's independence, strained the Kamakura shogunate's finances (it could not reward its warriors with confiscated land, since no territory had been conquered), and contributed to the shogunate's decline. The "divine wind" legend reinforced a belief in Japan's sacred protection that echoed into the 20th century.
