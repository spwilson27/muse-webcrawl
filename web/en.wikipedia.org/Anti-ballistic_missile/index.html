Anti-ballistic missile

An anti-ballistic missile (ABM) is a surface-to-air missile designed to counter ballistic missiles. Ballistic missiles are used to deliver nuclear, chemical, biological or conventional warheads in a ballistic flight trajectory. The term "anti-ballistic missile" is a generic term conveying a system designed to intercept and destroy any type of ballistic threat; however, it is commonly used for systems specifically designed to counter intercontinental ballistic missiles (ICBMs).

History

The first operational ABM systems were deployed in the 1960s and 1970s: the Soviet A-35 system around Moscow and the US Safeguard system, some of which used nuclear-tipped interceptors such as the Spartan and Sprint missiles. The 1972 Anti-Ballistic Missile Treaty between the US and USSR sharply limited ABM deployments; the United States withdrew from the treaty in 2002.

Modern systems

Contemporary systems rely on hit-to-kill kinetic interceptors guided by radar and infrared sensors. Examples include the US Ground-Based Midcourse Defense and Aegis BMD systems, THAAD, the Patriot PAC-3, Israel's Arrow, India's PAD/AAD, and Russia's A-135 and S-400/S-500 systems. Interception may occur in the boost, midcourse or terminal phase of the target's flight.
