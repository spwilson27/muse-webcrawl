Invasive species in Australia

Notable invadersRabbits, cane toads, red foxes, feral cats, camels, carp
Introduced since1788 (European colonisation)
Estimated costBillions of dollars annually in control and lost production

Invasive species in Australia are introduced plants and animals that have established self-sustaining populations and caused significant ecological and economic damage. Australia's long geographic isolation left its native fauna and flora poorly adapted to compete with species introduced after European colonisation began in 1788.

Animals

The European rabbit, released for hunting in 1859, spread across the continent within decades, competing with native herbivores and degrading rangelands; control measures have included the rabbit-proof fence, the myxoma virus, and rabbit haemorrhagic disease. Cane toads, introduced to Queensland in 1935 to control cane beetles, proved ineffective against the beetle but highly toxic to native predators. Feral cats and red foxes are implicated in the extinction or decline of many small native mammals, and feral camels, pigs, goats, deer, and water buffalo damage arid and tropical ecosystems. In freshwater systems, European carp dominate many rivers, while the red-eared slider and other exotic species pose emerging threats.

Plants

Weeds such as prickly pear (largely controlled by the cactoblastis moth in the 1920s), lantana, rubber vine, and mimosa pigra invade pastures and bushland, altering fire regimes and displacing native vegetation.

Management

Australia operates strict biosecurity controls at its borders, and governments fund control programs including baiting, fencing of conservation reserves, and research into biological controls. Despite these efforts, invasive species remain one of the leading causes of biodiversity loss in Australia.
