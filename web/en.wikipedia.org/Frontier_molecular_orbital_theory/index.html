Frontier molecular orbital theory

Frontier molecular orbital theory is an application of molecular orbital theory describing chemical interactions in terms of the HOMO (highest occupied molecular orbital) and LUMO (lowest unoccupied molecular orbital) of the reacting molecules. The approach was developed by Kenichi Fukui in the 1950s and later shared the 1981 Nobel Prize in Chemistry with Roald Hoffmann's work on orbital symmetry.

Principles

Fukui observed that chemical reactions are dominated by the electrons in the highest occupied orbital and the empty lowest unoccupied orbital, because these "frontier" electrons are the most weakly bound and most readily donated or accepted. A small energy gap between one molecule's HOMO and another's LUMO favours interaction; the shapes and phases of the orbitals determine where bonds form. The theory underlies the concepts of nucleophilicity and electrophilicity in orbital terms, and explains the regiochemistry of cycloadditions such as the Diels–Alder reaction, where the largest HOMO and LUMO coefficients align.

Applications

Frontier orbital arguments are widely used qualitatively in organic chemistry to rationalize reactivity and selectivity, and quantitatively in computational chemistry through related concepts such as Fukui functions within conceptual density functional theory.
