Vishnu as Narasimha protects his devotee Prahlada from the demon king Hiranyakashipu.

Prahlada (Sanskrit: प्रह्लाद, Prahlāda) is a saintly figure in Hindu tradition, an asura prince celebrated for his unwavering devotion (bhakti) to the god Vishnu despite the persecution of his father, the demon king Hiranyakashipu. His story appears principally in the Bhagavata Purana, the Vishnu Purana, and the Mahabharata, and is the narrative foundation of the festival of Holi and of the Narasimha avatar of Vishnu.

Legend

Hiranyakashipu had obtained a boon making him nearly invulnerable and, in his arrogance, demanded to be worshipped as god. His young son Prahlada, who had learned devotion to Vishnu while still in the womb (having heard the sage Narada's teachings), refused, insisting that Vishnu was omnipresent and supreme. Enraged, the king subjected the boy to a series of torments — trampling by elephants, venomous snakes, poison, fire, and being thrown from a cliff — each of which Prahlada survived through Vishnu's protection. The attempt to burn him with his aunt Holika, who was immune to fire, backfired: Holika burned while Prahlada emerged unharmed, an episode commemorated in the Holika Dahan bonfires of Holi.

Finally, Hiranyakashipu demanded whether Vishnu was in a nearby pillar; when Prahlada affirmed it, the king struck the pillar, and Vishnu burst forth in the man-lion form of Narasimha. At twilight (neither day nor night), on the threshold (neither inside nor outside), placing the king on his lap (neither earth nor sky), Narasimha slew Hiranyakashipu with his claws (neither weapon nor tool), circumventing every condition of the boon. Prahlada then succeeded his father and ruled righteously. In later tradition he is counted among the foremost exemplars of devotion, and his grandson was Bali, another famed devotee-king.
