The Brundtland Commission, formally the World Commission on Environment and Development (WCED), was a sub-organization of the United Nations established in 1983 to examine the long-term environmental strategies of the international community. Chaired by former Norwegian prime minister Gro Harlem Brundtland, it is best known for its 1987 report, Our Common Future (also called the Brundtland Report), which popularized the concept of sustainable development.

Founding and work

UN Secretary-General Javier Pérez de Cuéllar appointed Brundtland to establish and chair the commission in 1983. The commission held public hearings on five continents, hearing from scientists, farmers, industrialists, and indigenous peoples, and worked to reconcile economic development — particularly poverty in the developing world — with environmental protection. Its members included prominent officials from both developed and developing countries, among them vice-chairman Mansour Khalid of Sudan.

Our Common Future

The commission's report, published by Oxford University Press in 1987, defined sustainable development as "development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs." The report argued that poverty and environmental degradation are linked, called for revived but qualitatively different economic growth, and urged changes in energy, industry, food security, and international cooperation. It directly led to the 1992 United Nations Conference on Environment and Development (the Rio Earth Summit), which produced Agenda 21, the Rio Declaration, and the framework conventions on climate change and biodiversity.

Legacy

The Brundtland definition remains the most widely quoted formulation of sustainable development and underpins later frameworks including the Millennium Development Goals and the Sustainable Development Goals adopted in 2015.
