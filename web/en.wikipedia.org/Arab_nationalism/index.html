Arab nationalism

Arab nationalism (Arabic: القومية العربية, al-qawmīya al-ʿarabīya) is a political ideology asserting that Arabs constitute a single nation. As a traditional nationalist ideology, it promotes Arab culture and civilization, celebrates Arab history, the heroes and virtues of the Arab people, and uses the Arabic language as a unifying factor.

The ideology emerged in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, partly in reaction to the decline of the Ottoman Empire and the growth of Turkish nationalism within it. It gained significant momentum after the Arab Revolt of 1916–1918 during the First World War, led by Sharif Hussein of Mecca in alliance with the British against the Ottomans.

History

The roots of Arab nationalism lie in the Nahda (Arab Renaissance) of the 19th century, a cultural and literary revival centered in Beirut and Cairo. Thinkers such as Butrus al-Bustani promoted Arab identity and the Arabic language. Secret societies such as al-Fatat, founded in Paris in 1911, began agitating for Arab independence from Ottoman rule.

Following the First World War, the Sykes–Picot Agreement and the Balfour Declaration frustrated Arab aspirations for a unified state. During the interwar period, Arab nationalist movements developed in the French and British mandates. The ideology reached its zenith in the mid-20th century under Egyptian president Gamal Abdel Nasser, whose pan-Arab vision briefly produced the United Arab Republic (1958–1961), a union of Egypt and Syria. The Ba'ath Party, founded by Michel Aflaq and Salah al-Din al-Bitar, combined Arab nationalism with socialism and governed in Syria and Iraq.

Decline

The Arab defeat in the Six-Day War of 1967 is widely seen as a turning point that undermined the prestige of secular Arab nationalism. The rise of Islamism, the Iran–Iraq War, and the 2003 invasion of Iraq further weakened pan-Arab movements, although Arab identity remains a powerful cultural and political force.
