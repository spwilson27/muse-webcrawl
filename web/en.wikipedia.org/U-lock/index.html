U-lock

A U-lock is a rigid bicycle lock consisting of a hardened steel shackle bent into the shape of the letter U, with the two ends of the shackle fitting into a locking crossbar. The U-shaped design allows the lock to secure a bicycle's frame and wheel to a fixed object such as a bike rack or railing, while leaving relatively little room inside the shackle for prying tools to be inserted.

U-locks are widely considered among the most secure portable bicycle locks, and they are a common choice in cities with high rates of bicycle theft. They are also used, in larger forms, to secure motorcycles and other equipment.

History

The U-lock was developed in the early 1970s. Michael Zane, founder of the Kryptonite lock company, is generally credited with popularising the design after devising a lock in 1971–1972 inspired by earlier prototypes, including a lock made by Stan Kaplan. Kryptonite's early success came after a widely publicised demonstration in New York City, where a bicycle locked with the new lock reportedly survived several days of attempted theft on a street where unprotected bicycles were quickly stolen.

Design

A typical U-lock consists of:

  • A U-shaped shackle made of hardened steel, often 13–18 mm thick, sometimes coated in vinyl or rubber to protect the bicycle's paint.
  • A crossbar containing the locking mechanism, usually operated by a key or, less commonly, a combination dial.
  • A mounting bracket for carrying the lock on the bicycle frame, though many riders carry the lock in a bag or through a belt loop because of its weight.

Higher-end models use disc-detainer cylinders or other pick-resistant mechanisms, shackles resistant to bolt cutters and angle grinders, and designs that require both sides of the shackle to be cut before the lock can be removed.

Security and attacks

U-locks resist many common attacks, including cutting with hand tools and hammering, but they are not invulnerable. Known attack methods include:

  • Leverage attacks, in which a pipe or car jack is inserted into the shackle and twisted or forced open; minimising empty space inside the U reduces this risk.
  • Cutting with hydraulic cutters or an angle grinder, which can defeat nearly any portable lock given enough time and noise.
  • Freezing the lock with canned refrigerant and shattering it, effective mainly on cheaper locks.
  • Lock picking of inexpensive tubular or wafer cylinders.

In 2004 it was widely reported that many locks using tubular pin tumbler cylinders — including some Kryptonite models — could be opened with the barrel of a Bic ballpoint pen pressed into the keyway. The disclosure led to recalls, free lock exchanges, and a shift toward flat-key and disc-detainer designs.

Ratings

Independent organisations such as Sold Secure in the United Kingdom and the ART Foundation in the Netherlands test and rate bicycle locks, and some insurers require a lock meeting a minimum rating. U-locks frequently occupy the top rating categories alongside heavy chain locks.
