Missouri Compromise

The Missouri Compromise (also known as the Compromise of 1820) was federal legislation that stopped northern attempts to abolish slavery in the territory of Missouri and set a precedent of balancing the admission of slave and free states. It admitted Missouri as a slave state and Maine as a free state, and prohibited slavery in the Louisiana Purchase lands north of latitude 36°30′ except within Missouri.

Background

In 1819 Missouri, a territory with some 10,000 enslaved people, applied for statehood. Representative James Tallmadge of New York proposed amendments gradually abolishing slavery there, provoking a fierce sectional debate: the Union then stood evenly divided between eleven free and eleven slave states, and the balance of the Senate was at stake.

The settlement

Brokered largely by Henry Clay, the compromise passed in 1820: Maine, formerly part of Massachusetts, entered as a free state while Missouri entered as a slave state, preserving the Senate balance; and an amendment proposed by Senator Jesse B. Thomas of Illinois barred slavery from Louisiana Purchase territory north of 36°30′ north latitude. A second compromise in 1821 resolved the dispute over Missouri's constitution, which had purported to exclude free Black people from the state.

Repeal and legacy

The arrangement held for three decades until the Kansas–Nebraska Act of 1854 repealed the 36°30′ restriction in favour of popular sovereignty, igniting "Bleeding Kansas" and helping found the Republican Party. In Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857), the Supreme Court declared the compromise unconstitutional. Thomas Jefferson had feared it from the start, calling the Missouri question "a fire bell in the night".
