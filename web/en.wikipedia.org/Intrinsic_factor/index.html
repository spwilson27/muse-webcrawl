Intrinsic factor

Intrinsic factor
Also known as: Gastric intrinsic factor (GIF)
Produced by: Parietal cells of the stomach
Function: Enables vitamin B12 absorption

Intrinsic factor, also called gastric intrinsic factor, is a glycoprotein produced by the parietal cells of the stomach. It is necessary for the absorption of vitamin B12 later on in the ileum of the small intestine.

Mechanism

As food is digested, vitamin B12 is released from dietary protein and initially bound by haptocorrin in saliva and gastric juice. In the duodenum, pancreatic enzymes free the vitamin, which then binds intrinsic factor. The intrinsic factor–B12 complex travels to the ileum, where receptors (the cubam complex) on enterocytes absorb it.

Clinical significance

Deficiency of intrinsic factor—most often from autoimmune gastritis in which parietal cells are destroyed, or after gastrectomy—causes vitamin B12 malabsorption and pernicious anaemia, a megaloblastic anaemia that can also produce neurological symptoms. Historically, William Castle's experiments in the 1920s established the existence of an "intrinsic factor" in gastric juice distinct from the "extrinsic factor" (B12) in food. Treatment is by B12 injections or high-dose oral supplements.

Genetics

Intrinsic factor is encoded by the CBLIF gene (formerly GIF) in humans. Rare inherited defects cause juvenile pernicious anaemia. See also Vitamin B12 and Pernicious anemia.
