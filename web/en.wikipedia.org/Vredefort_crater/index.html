Vredefort crater

LocationFree State Province, South Africa
Diameter160–300 km (original rim)
Age2,023 ± 4 million years (Paleoproterozoic)
ImpactorAsteroid estimated 10–15 km across
ExposedCentral uplift (Vredefort Dome) at surface
UNESCO statusWorld Heritage Site (2005)

The Vredefort crater is the largest verified impact crater on Earth. It was formed about 2.023 billion years ago, during the Paleoproterozoic Era, when an asteroid struck the Earth in what is now the Free State province of South Africa. The crater's original rim is estimated to have been between 160 and 300 kilometres in diameter; only the central uplift, the Vredefort Dome, remains readily visible today.

Formation and geology

The impactor, probably an asteroid about 10–15 km in diameter, released an enormous amount of energy, excavating a transient cavity and driving a rebound of deep crustal rocks to form the central dome. The impact deformed the strata of the Witwatersrand Basin, tilting and overturning gold-bearing conglomerates around the crater rim—an accident of geology that protected the Witwatersrand goldfields from erosion and made them accessible to mining. Shock features such as shatter cones, impact melt rocks (pseudotachylite), and shocked quartz confirm the impact origin, though for many years some geologists argued the structure was of volcanic origin; the debate was settled in the 1990s in favour of impact.

Significance

Vredefort is one of only a handful of known multi-ringed impact basins on Earth and is more than twice the age of the next-oldest large crater, Sudbury in Canada. Because of its size, age, and state of preservation, it is a key site for understanding large impacts and early Earth history. The event occurred at a time when Earth's atmosphere was becoming oxygenated, though no direct link to biological change has been established. In 2005, the Vredefort Dome was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List. The crater is named after the town of Vredefort, which lies near its centre.
