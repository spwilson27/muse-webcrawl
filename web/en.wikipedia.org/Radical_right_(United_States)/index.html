Radical right (United States)

The radical right in the United States refers to far-right political movements and ideologies that have appeared throughout American history, characterized by strong opposition to communism, immigration, and social change, and by appeals to nationalism, traditional values, and sometimes conspiracy theories. Scholars distinguish the radical right from mainstream conservatism by its more extreme rhetoric, its suspicion of democratic institutions, and its willingness to entertain anti-democratic ideas.

Political rallies have been a recurring feature of radical right mobilization in the United States.

History

Historians trace elements of the American radical right to nativist movements of the 19th century, such as the Know Nothing party, and to the second Ku Klux Klan of the 1920s. In the 1930s, figures such as Father Charles Coughlin used radio broadcasts to spread anti-communist and antisemitic messages to millions of listeners.

During the Cold War, the radical right coalesced around anti-communism. The John Birch Society, founded in 1958 by Robert Welch, alleged that communist conspiracies had infiltrated the highest levels of the American government. The 1964 presidential campaign of Barry Goldwater drew support from radical right groups even as mainstream Republicans distanced themselves from the John Birch Society.

Scholarly analysis

The term "radical right" was popularized by a group of social scientists, including Daniel Bell, Richard Hofstadter, and Seymour Martin Lipset, whose 1955 collection The New American Right analyzed McCarthyism and related movements. Hofstadter's 1964 essay "The Paranoid Style in American Politics" argued that radical right movements were driven by status anxiety and a conspiratorial worldview.

Later scholars, including Chip Berlet and Matthew N. Lyons, defined the radical right as movements that combine extreme nationalism with attacks on egalitarianism and that scapegoat oppressed groups for social problems while remaining formally within the framework of electoral politics.

Contemporary movements

In the late 20th and early 21st centuries, the radical right has included militia movements, Christian nationalist groups, and the alt-right, a loose coalition that gained prominence in the 2010s through internet platforms. Analysts continue to debate the boundaries between the radical right and the mainstream conservative movement.
