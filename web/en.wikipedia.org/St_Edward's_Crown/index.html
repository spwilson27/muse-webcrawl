St Edward's Crown, used at the crowning of British monarchs

St Edward's Crown is the crown used at the moment of coronation of the monarchs of the United Kingdom, and is the centrepiece of the Crown Jewels of the United Kingdom. Named after Edward the Confessor, it is a recreation of the medieval crown that was melted down in 1649 during the English Civil War, and is traditionally employed only at the coronation ceremony itself.

History

The original crown was said to date from the 11th century and was kept at Westminster Abbey; it was among the regalia destroyed by order of Parliament after the execution of Charles I. For the coronation of Charles II in 1661, the royal goldsmith Sir Robert Vyner made the present crown of solid gold. Because of its considerable weight — over 2.2 kilograms — several monarchs from Queen Victoria onward chose lighter crowns for the ceremony, and St Edward's Crown was carried in procession rather than worn. The tradition of its use was revived by George V in 1911, and it was used for the coronations of George VI (1937), Elizabeth II (1953), and Charles III (2023).

Description

The crown consists of a solid gold frame decorated with four crosses pattée and four fleurs-de-lis, supporting two arches topped by a monde and cross. For coronations it is set with hundreds of precious and semi-precious stones, which are hired for the occasion and removed afterward, leaving the jewelled wreaths empty. A stylised depiction of St Edward's Crown is used in royal cyphers, coats of arms, and emblems throughout the Commonwealth realms.
