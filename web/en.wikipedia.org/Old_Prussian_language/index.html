Old Prussian was a Western Baltic language spoken by the Old Prussians, a Baltic people inhabiting the region between the lower Vistula and the lower Nemunas (in present-day north-eastern Poland and the Kaliningrad Oblast). It is the only Western Baltic language with substantial written records, and it died out around the beginning of the 18th century as its speakers were assimilated into German- and Polish-speaking populations.

Records

The language is known from a small corpus. The oldest connected evidence is the Basel Epigram (1369), a two-line rhymed text. The Elbing Vocabulary (c. 1400) lists some 800 Old Prussian words with German glosses, and the vocabulary of the monk Simon Grunau (c. 1517–1526) adds about 100 more. The three Lutheran catechisms printed at Königsberg (two in 1545 and Abel Will's translation of Luther's Enchiridion in 1561) provide the only extended texts; their Prussian was translated from German and Polish models and likely already differed from everyday speech.

Linguistic features

Old Prussian is of great importance to Indo-European studies because it preserves very archaic features — including traces of the neuter gender (later lost in Lithuanian and Latvian), a rich case system, mobile accent and conservative vocabulary. Its nominal declension included nominative, genitive, dative, accusative and vocative cases. Scholars have debated its closest affinities: traditionally grouped with Lithuanian and Latvian in a Baltic branch, Old Prussian also shares innovations with the Slavic languages, contributing to the theory of a Balto-Slavic proto-language.

Decline

The Teutonic Order's conquest of Prussia in the 13th century began German colonisation; the Reformation made German the church language, the plagues of 1709–1711 devastated the remaining Prussian-speaking countryside, and the last fluent speakers are thought to have died around that time. Toponyms and loanwords survived in local German dialects.

Revival

Since the late 20th century, enthusiasts in Lithuania and elsewhere have attempted to reconstruct and revive a "New Prussian" language from the catechisms and vocabularies, producing grammars, dictionaries, translations and a small community of speakers and learners.
