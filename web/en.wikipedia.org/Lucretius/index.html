Titus Lucretius Carus (c. 99 – c. 55 BC) was a Roman poet and philosopher. His only known work is the philosophical poem De rerum natura ("On the Nature of Things"), a comprehensive exposition of Epicurean physics written in some 7,400 dactylic hexameters. It is one of the principal surviving sources for the atomist philosophy of Democritus, Leucippus and Epicurus, and a masterpiece of Latin literature.

Life

Almost nothing is known of Lucretius's life with certainty. Jerome, writing centuries later, claimed that Lucretius was driven mad by a love potion and died by suicide — a story most scholars treat as a hostile Christian fabrication or unreliable tradition. The poem is addressed to a Memmius, possibly the politician Gaius Memmius, and Cicero praised the work's brilliance in a letter to his brother Quintus.

De rerum natura

The poem's six books explain a universe composed solely of atoms and void: nothing is created from nothing; the soul is mortal, being a compound of fine atoms; the gods exist but do not intervene in human affairs; natural phenomena such as thunder and plague have natural, not divine, causes; and the world itself arose by chance concourses of atoms — including the famous clinamen, the unpredictable "swerve" of atoms that breaks deterministic chains and makes free will possible. Book 5 gives an account of the origin of the world, of life, and of human civilization, and the poem ends abruptly with a harrowing description of the plague of Athens.

Transmission and influence

Nearly lost during the Middle Ages, the poem survived through a handful of Carolingian manuscripts. In 1417 the humanist Poggio Bracciolini obtained a copy from a German monastery, an event dramatised in Stephen Greenblatt's The Swerve (2011). The rediscovery helped transmit atomism into early modern thought, influencing writers and thinkers from Montaigne and Shakespeare to Gassendi, Newton and Thomas Jefferson, who owned several editions.
