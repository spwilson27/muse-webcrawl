Guinea pig

The guinea pig or domestic guinea pig (Cavia porcellus), also known as the cavy or domestic cavy, is a species of rodent belonging to the genus Cavia in the family Caviidae. Despite their common name, guinea pigs are not pigs and do not come from Guinea; they originated in the Andes of South America, where they were domesticated from a wild cavy species, likely the montane guinea pig (Cavia tschudii), as early as 5000 BC. They remain an important food source in parts of the Andes (where they are called cuy), and are widely kept elsewhere as pets and used as laboratory animals.

Biology and behaviour

Guinea pigs are stout, tailless rodents with continuously growing teeth. They are herbivores requiring abundant hay and a dietary source of vitamin C, which — like humans — they cannot synthesise. They are social, crepuscular animals with a rich vocal repertoire, including the loud whistle or "wheek" used to solicit food, purring, and teeth-chattering. Young are precocial — born furred, with eyes open, after a long gestation of about 59–72 days — and litters average two to four pups. When excited, guinea pigs may leap straight up in the air repeatedly, a behaviour called "popcorning".

History and uses

Andean peoples domesticated guinea pigs for food over millennia, and the animals also feature in traditional medicine and religious ceremonies. Spanish traders brought them to Europe in the 16th century, where they became exotic pets of the upper classes — Queen Elizabeth I is said to have kept them — and the name "guinea" may derive from their cost of one guinea, or from confusion with Guiana or the Guinea coast trade. Since the 18th century they have been used extensively in scientific research, giving rise to the phrase "guinea pig" for any experimental subject; notable work includes the discovery of vitamin C and early studies of infectious disease. Their use in laboratories has declined in favour of mice and rats but continues in some fields.

As pets

Guinea pigs are popular household pets, especially for children, though they require considerable care: spacious housing, companionship of their own kind (in Switzerland it is illegal to keep a single guinea pig), unlimited hay, fresh vegetables and gentle handling. Numerous breeds are recognised, differing in coat length and type, including the smooth-coated American, the rosetted Abyssinian, the long-haired Peruvian and Silkie, and the hairless Skinny pig.
