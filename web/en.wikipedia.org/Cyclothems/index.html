Cyclothems

In geology, cyclothems are alternating stratigraphic sequences of marine and non-marine sediments, sometimes interbedded with coal seams. The term was coined by J. Marvin Weller in 1930 for the repeated rock successions of Pennsylvanian age in Illinois. Individual cyclothems are typically several metres to tens of metres thick, and dozens of them may be stacked in a single basin succession.

A typical cyclothem

An idealised Illinois cyclothem begins with a sandstone (often filling a channel), followed by shale, an underclay (fossil soil), a coal seam, and then marine limestone and black shale, before the cycle repeats. The sequence records the advance and retreat of a shallow sea across a low coastal plain: soils and coal swamps formed on land, and marine muds and limestones were deposited when the sea flooded in.

Origin

The classic explanation invokes glacio-eustatic sea-level change: as ice sheets of the late Palaeozoic ice age grew and melted on Gondwana, global sea level rose and fell rhythmically, alternately flooding and exposing the coal-forming lowlands of North America and Europe. The cycles have periods comparable to Milankovitch cycles, and their regularity across whole continents supports an external, climate-driven control rather than local tectonics alone.

Significance

Cyclothems are economically important because they contain many of the world's great coal seams, and scientifically important as records of icehouse climate dynamics and early terrestrial ecosystems preserved in the underclays and coal balls.
