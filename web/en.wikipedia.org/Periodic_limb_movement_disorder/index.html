Periodic limb movement disorder

Polysomnography is used to confirm periodic limb movements during sleep.

Periodic limb movement disorder (PLMD) is a sleep disorder in which the patient moves limbs involuntarily and periodically during sleep, and has symptoms or problems related to the movement. PLMD should not be confused with restless legs syndrome (RLS), in which a person voluntarily moves in response to an uncomfortable urge in the legs while awake. PLMD, by contrast, is involuntary, and the patient is often unaware of the movements altogether.

Signs and symptoms

People with PLMD often report excessive daytime sleepiness, falling asleep during the day, trouble falling asleep at night, and difficulty staying asleep throughout the night. Patients may also exhibit involuntary limb movements that occur at periodic intervals, generally 20 to 40 seconds apart, during sleep. The movements often involve the lower extremities — extension of the big toe and dorsiflexion of the ankle, knee, and hip — though they can occasionally occur in the upper extremities as well. A bed partner commonly notices the kicking movements before the patient does.

Causes

The exact cause of PLMD is unknown. It frequently co-occurs with other conditions, including restless legs syndrome, narcolepsy, obstructive sleep apnea, REM sleep behavior disorder, Parkinson's disease, and end-stage renal disease. Low iron stores (ferritin) have also been associated with increased severity. Certain medications, including some antidepressants and antihistamines, may worsen the movements.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis requires polysomnography (a sleep study), which records the periodic limb movements and relates them to sleep fragmentation. A periodic limb movement index (PLMI) of more than 15 movements per hour in adults, together with clinical sleep disturbance that cannot be explained by another disorder, supports the diagnosis.

Treatment

Treatment typically addresses underlying causes, such as iron deficiency, and may include dopaminergic agents, gabapentinoids, or benzodiazepines. Adjusting medications that exacerbate limb movements, improving sleep hygiene, and treating co-occurring sleep disorders are also common approaches.
