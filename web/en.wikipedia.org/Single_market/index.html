Single market

A single market removes internal barriers to trade between participating states.

A single market is a type of trade bloc in which most trade barriers have been removed (for goods, services, capital and labour) with some common policies on product regulation and freedom of movement of the factors of production. The goal is that the movement of capital, labour, goods, and services between the members is as easy as within them.

The four freedoms

The best-known example, the European Single Market of the European Union, rests on the "four freedoms": free movement of goods, services, capital and persons. It was formally completed on 1 January 1993 under the programme launched by the Single European Act of 1986 and the 1985 Cockfield White Paper. Non-EU states Norway, Iceland and Liechtenstein participate through the European Economic Area, and Switzerland through bilateral agreements.

Features

Unlike a mere free trade area, a single market requires extensive harmonisation or mutual recognition of regulations: the landmark Cassis de Dijon ruling of the European Court of Justice established that goods lawfully sold in one member state must generally be accepted in another. A single market may also include common competition policy, state-aid rules and public procurement rules. It is a deeper stage of integration than a customs union, but does not by itself entail a common currency or fiscal union.

Other examples

Other projects described as single markets include the CARICOM Single Market and Economy in the Caribbean and the ASEAN Economic Community in Southeast Asia, though these are generally less complete than the European model.
