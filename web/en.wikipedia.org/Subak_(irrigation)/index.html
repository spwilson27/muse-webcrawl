Subak (irrigation)

Subak is the water management (irrigation) system for the paddy fields on Bali, Indonesia. It was developed in the 9th century. For the Balinese, irrigation is not simply the provision of water for the plant's roots, but water is used to construct a complex, pulsed artificial ecosystem. The system consists of five terraced rice fields and water temples on nearly 20,000 hectares (ha). The temples are the main focus of this cooperative water management, known as subak.

The subak system is a manifestation of the Balinese philosophical concept of Tri Hita Karana — harmony among people, nature and the gods — and in 2012 the "Cultural Landscape of Bali Province: the Subak System as a Manifestation of the Tri Hita Karana Philosophy" was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Organisation

A subak is a cooperative association of farmers who share water from a common source — a spring, river or weir — and who coordinate planting schedules, water allocation and the maintenance of canals, tunnels, weirs and terraces. Decisions are made democratically at meetings held in pavilions (bale subak), and officers such as the pekaseh (head) oversee operations. Water temples at each level of the system, from the great temple of Pura Ulun Danu Batur at Lake Batur down to small field shrines, integrate irrigation with ritual: offerings and temple festivals schedule the agricultural calendar.

Ecology

Research, notably by anthropologist J. Stephen Lansing, showed that subak coordination achieves near-optimal water sharing and pest control: simultaneous fallow periods across a watershed deprive pests of habitat, while staggered planting balances water demand. Lansing's computer models demonstrated that the self-organised temple networks outperform centralised management, a finding that helped restore the system after "green revolution" policies in the 1970s had disrupted it and caused pest outbreaks and water conflicts.
