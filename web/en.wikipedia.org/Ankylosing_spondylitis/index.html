Ankylosing spondylitis

Ankylosing spondylitis
SpecialtyRheumatology
SymptomsChronic inflammatory back pain and stiffness, reduced spinal mobility, fatigue
OnsetUsually before age 45, often in the 20s
CausesUnknown; strong genetic association with HLA-B27
TreatmentExercise and physiotherapy, NSAIDs, TNF inhibitors and other biologics

Ankylosing spondylitis (AS) is a long-term type of arthritis in which chronic inflammation affects the joints of the spine and the sacroiliac joints, and can lead to progressive stiffening and fusion (ankylosis) of the vertebrae. It is the archetype of the spondyloarthropathies and is now classified within the spectrum of axial spondyloarthritis. The name comes from the Greek ankylos (bent/stiff) and spondylos (vertebra).

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms usually begin insidiously before the age of 45 with inflammatory back pain: pain and stiffness in the lower back and buttocks that improve with activity rather than rest, are worse in the second half of the night, and are accompanied by prolonged morning stiffness. Over years, inflammation at vertebral edges promotes new bone formation (syndesmophytes), producing the "bamboo spine" appearance and loss of spinal flexibility, sometimes with a stooped posture. Peripheral joints, entheses (such as the Achilles tendon), eyes (anterior uveitis in roughly a third of patients), skin and bowel may also be involved.

Cause and diagnosis

The cause is unknown but involves immune-mediated inflammation with a strong genetic component: about 85–90% of patients of European ancestry carry the HLA-B27 antigen, although most HLA-B27 carriers never develop the disease. Diagnosis is based on the clinical picture, laboratory tests (raised inflammatory markers in many patients, HLA-B27) and imaging: plain X-rays show sacroiliitis and later spinal changes, while MRI can detect early inflammation before structural damage appears (the basis for diagnosing non-radiographic axial spondyloarthritis).

Treatment and prognosis

There is no cure, but regular exercise and physiotherapy are the cornerstone of management, preserving posture and mobility. Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) are first-line medication; for persistent active disease, biologic therapies — tumour necrosis factor inhibitors and interleukin-17 inhibitors — and targeted synthetic drugs (JAK inhibitors) are effective. Surgery is occasionally needed for severely damaged hip joints. Life expectancy is modestly reduced, mainly through cardiovascular disease, but early diagnosis and modern therapy have greatly improved outcomes.
