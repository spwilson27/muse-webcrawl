New World monkeys are the five families of primates classified in the parvorder Platyrrhini, found in the tropical forests of Mexico, Central America, and South America. The name "platyrrhine" refers to their broad noses with widely separated, outward-facing nostrils, in contrast to the narrow-nosed Old World monkeys (catarrhines) of Africa and Asia. New World monkeys are almost exclusively arboreal and include marmosets, tamarins, capuchins, squirrel monkeys, howler monkeys, spider monkeys, and many others—more than 170 species in total.

Characteristics

New World monkeys range from the tiny pygmy marmoset, the smallest monkey in the world, to howler monkeys among the largest. Many atelids possess fully prehensile tails that function as a fifth limb, an adaptation absent in Old World monkeys. Unlike catarrhines, platyrrhines have three premolar teeth in each quadrant, lack cheek pouches and sitting pads, and most species lack trichromatic colour vision in males—although howler monkeys independently evolved full colour vision. Marmosets and tamarins are unusual in normally giving birth to twins and in having claws rather than nails on most digits.

Evolution

Fossil and molecular evidence indicates that the ancestors of New World monkeys reached South America from Africa around 40 million years ago, most plausibly by rafting across the Atlantic on mats of vegetation when the ocean was narrower than today. The oldest known fossil, Perupithecus, dates to about 36 million years ago in Peru. Once in South America, the group radiated into a diversity of niches without competition from other primates.

Families

The five living families are the Callitrichidae (marmosets and tamarins), Cebidae (capuchins and squirrel monkeys), Aotidae (night monkeys, the only nocturnal anthropoids), Pitheciidae (titis, sakis, and uakaris), and Atelidae (howler, spider, woolly, and muriqui monkeys).
