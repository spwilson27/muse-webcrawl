Ecological stability

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An ecosystem is said to possess ecological stability (or equilibrium) if it is capable of returning to its equilibrium state after a perturbation (a capacity known as resilience) or does not experience unexpected large changes in its characteristics across time. Although the terms community stability and ecological stability are sometimes used interchangeably, community stability refers only to the characteristics of communities. It is possible for an ecosystem or a community to be stable in some of their properties and unstable in others. For example, a vegetation community in response to a drought might conserve biomass but lose biodiversity.

An ecosystem returning to equilibrium after a disturbance

Stable ecological systems are especially prevalent in literature. Ecological stability studies have a long history, with many early papers in ecology outlining the properties of stable communities and mathematical models developed subsequently to formalise these ideas. Nature seems particularly adept at recovering from localised perturbations, and the idea that ecological systems tend towards a stable state (or a stable cycle of states) has been persistent in ecology.

Stability analysis

When the species abundances of an ecosystem are represented as vectors in a multi-dimensional space, with each axis representing the abundance of a particular species, the dynamics of the system can be studied by examining how the system state moves over time. If the mathematical model has an equilibrium point and the eigenvalues of the system's Jacobian at the equilibrium all have negative real parts, then the equilibrium is locally stable: small perturbations decay and the state returns to the equilibrium.

Types of stability

Ecological stability is studied at many levels, from individual populations to whole ecosystems. Different notions include:

  • Constancy — the ability of a system to maintain a constant state over time.
  • Resistance — the ability of a system to remain unchanged when subjected to a disturbance.
  • Resilience — the speed with which a system returns to equilibrium after a disturbance.
  • Persistence — the ability of a system or species to survive over time.
  • Variability — the inverse of the amount of fluctuation in system properties.

Stability and diversity

It had long been thought that greater species diversity generally promotes stability, an idea articulated by Charles Elton and Robert MacArthur. However, in the 1970s Robert May showed that randomly assembled complex model ecosystems tend to become less stable as diversity and connectance increase, sparking a long-running diversity–stability debate. Later work showed that real food webs have structural features (such as many weak interactions) that can reconcile high diversity with stability.
