Geissler tube

A Geissler tube is an early gas-discharge tube used to demonstrate the principles of electrical glow discharge. Invented in 1857 by German physicist and glassblower Heinrich Geissler, it consists of a sealed glass tube, often elaborately shaped, containing a rarefied gas (such as neon, argon or carbon dioxide vapour) at low pressure, with metal electrodes at each end. When a high voltage is applied, the gas ionises and glows with a colour characteristic of the gas.

History and use

Geissler's skill as a glassblower, combined with his improved mercury vacuum pump, made it possible to produce tubes with far lower internal pressures than before. The tubes became popular scientific demonstrations and parlour amusements in the later 19th century; ornate tubes with fluorescent liquids and twisted glass spirals were manufactured commercially. Julius Plücker and Johann Hittorf used Geissler tubes to study electrical discharges in gases, observing that the glow was deflected by magnets.

Legacy

Geissler tubes were the direct ancestors of the Crookes tube and cathode-ray tube, of neon lighting and of modern gas-discharge lamps. Experiments with such tubes led to the discovery of cathode rays, and ultimately to J. J. Thomson's identification of the electron and Röntgen's discovery of X-rays.
