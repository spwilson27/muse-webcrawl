Borosilicate glass

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Borosilicate glass is a type of glass with silica and boron trioxide as the main glass-forming constituents. Borosilicate glasses are known for having very low coefficients of thermal expansion (≈3 × 10−6 K−1 at 20 °C), making them more resistant to thermal shock than any other common glass. Such glass can withstand temperature differentials without fracturing and is widely used for laboratory glassware, cookware, telescope mirrors, and high-quality flashlights.

Composition and properties

A typical borosilicate composition (the classic "Borosilicate 3.3" formulation) is about 80% silica (SiO2), 13% boron trioxide (B2O3), 4% sodium oxide, and 2% alumina. The boron content, derived from materials such as borax and boric acid, both lowers thermal expansion and increases chemical durability. Borosilicate glass softens at higher temperatures than soda-lime glass and is more expensive and harder to work, requiring higher-temperature torches for lampworking.

History

Borosilicate glass was first developed by German glassmaker Otto Schott in the late 19th century and sold under the brand name Duran since 1893. In 1915, Corning Glass Works introduced Pyrex in the United States, initially made of borosilicate glass; it became famous for laboratory ware and oven cookware. Other major producers include Schott (Duran, Borofloat), and manufacturers of the Simax and Bomex brands.

Applications

  • Laboratory glassware — beakers, flasks, test tubes, and condenser columns.
  • Cookware — oven dishes, measuring cups, and teapots that tolerate sudden temperature change.
  • Optics and astronomy — mirror blanks for large telescopes, including early casts for major observatory mirrors.
  • Other — glass piping, high-intensity discharge lamps, nuclear waste vitrification, and artistic lampworked glass.
