Synthon

In retrosynthetic analysis, a synthon is a hypothetical unit within a target molecule that represents a potential starting reagent in the retroactive synthesis of that molecule. The term was coined in 1967 by the organic chemist E. J. Corey in his development of retrosynthetic analysis, for which he later received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

A synthon and the corresponding commercially available synthetic equivalent are related in the same way that a retron and a synthetic precursor are related in a proposed forward synthesis. Each disconnection of a bond in the target molecule generates two synthons — typically an electrophilic synthon and a nucleophilic synthon — which must then be matched to real reagents.

Common synthons

Examples include the cyanide anion as a synthon for a carboxylic acid group, the acylium ion equivalent supplied by acyl chlorides in Friedel–Crafts acylation, and enolate synthons supplied by ketones and esters. Synthons derived from sugars, amino acids and other members of the "chiral pool" are used to introduce stereochemistry into synthetic targets.

Retrosynthetic analysis

In a retrosynthetic plan, the target molecule is transformed iteratively through disconnections, functional group interconversions and strategic bond formations until simple, available starting materials are reached. Synthons are the conceptual fragments produced by each disconnection step.
