Revelations of Divine Love

Revelations of Divine Love is a medieval book of Christian mystical devotions. It was written by Julian of Norwich between 1373 and 1393, and is believed to be the earliest surviving book written in the English language by a woman. It is also the earliest surviving work written by an English anchorite or anchoress.

Julian, who lived all her life in the English city of Norwich, wrote about the sixteen mystical visions or "shewings" she received in 1373, when she was in her early thirties and suffering from a severe illness. After a dramatic recovery, she became an anchoress in a cell attached to St Julian's Church, Norwich, and wrote first a short account and then — after some twenty years of contemplation — the much longer, theologically richer version known as the Long Text.

The showings

On the night of 13 May 1373, Julian, believing herself on her deathbed, received a series of sixteen visions centred on the Passion of Christ, in which she saw his bleeding head in the presence of the crucifix brought by her curate. The visions ended with an assurance of divine love. Among the most famous passages is her vision of a hazelnut-sized object in her palm, representing all creation, which endures because God made it, loves it, and keeps it. Her most quoted words are Christ's assurance: "All shall be well, and all shall be well, and all manner of thing shall be well."

Themes

The Revelations is notable for its emphasis on God's love rather than wrath: Julian reports finding no anger in God toward sinners, interpreting sin as a necessary, though painful, part of growth ("sin is behovely"). She develops a striking theology of Christ as Mother, describing Jesus as our true Mother in whom we are born again through the Passion. She also recounts the parable of the Lord and the Servant, a vision she pondered for decades, reconciling the Fall with universal divine compassion. Her theology of prayer, and her serene treatment of suffering, plague, and doubt, were written against the background of the Black Death's devastation of Norwich.

Manuscripts and publication

The Long Text survives in a few manuscripts, including the Paris Manuscript and two Sloane manuscripts; the Short Text survives in a single mid-15th-century manuscript. The Long Text was first printed by Serenus Cressy in 1670. Modern critical editions and translations in the 20th century brought Julian a wide readership, and T. S. Eliot quoted "all shall be well" in Little Gidding.

Influence

Julian is regarded as one of the greatest English mystics. Her theology of divine love as motherly and her optimistic doctrine of universal well-being have influenced modern Christian thought and interfaith dialogue. She is commemorated in the Church of England and the Episcopal Church on 8 May, alongside her contemporary Margery Kempe, whose Book records a visit to Julian for spiritual counsel.
