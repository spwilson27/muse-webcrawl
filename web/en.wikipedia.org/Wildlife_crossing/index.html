Wildlife crossing

Wildlife crossings are structures that allow animals to cross human-made barriers — especially roads and highways — safely. They are a central tool of habitat conservation, intended to reconnect fragmented habitats, prevent wildlife-vehicle collisions, and maintain genetic connectivity between populations.

Types

Crossings include landscape bridges and wildlife overpasses (such as the vegetated bridges of Banff National Park in Canada), underpasses and viaducts, amphibian and fish tunnels, canopy bridges for arboreal species, and modified culverts. They are usually combined with fencing that funnels animals toward the crossing. France built some of the first dedicated animal bridges in the 1950s, and the Netherlands has constructed hundreds of crossings, including the Natuurbrug Zanderij Crailoo, one of the world's longest.

Effectiveness

Studies in Banff found that grizzly bears, elk, deer, moose, and wolves used the park's crossings hundreds of thousands of times, reducing wildlife-vehicle collisions by more than 80 percent. Because wide-ranging species need to move across landscapes to find food, mates, and new territory, crossings are increasingly built into highway projects; the Wallis Annenberg Wildlife Crossing over U.S. Route 101 in California, designed to help mountain lions cross a ten-lane freeway, is among the largest such projects.
