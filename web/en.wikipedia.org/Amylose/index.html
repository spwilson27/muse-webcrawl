Amylose

Amylose is a polysaccharide made of α-D-glucose units bonded by α(1→4) glycosidic bonds. It is one of the two components of starch, making up approximately 20–30% of its structure, the other being amylopectin. Because of its tightly packed helical structure, amylose is more resistant to digestion than amylopectin and is an important form of resistant starch.

Structure and properties

Unlike the highly branched amylopectin, amylose is essentially linear, typically containing 300 to 3,000 glucose units, and coils into a helix with about six glucose monomers per turn. The interior of the helix is hydrophobic, allowing amylose to form a deep blue complex with iodine — the basis of the classic iodine test for starch. Amylose is less soluble in water than amylopectin and tends to form firm gels and films; the retrogradation of amylose is responsible for the staling of bread.

Occurrence and applications

Amylose content varies by source: high-amylose maize may contain more than 50%, while waxy starches contain almost none. Long-grain rice, relatively high in amylose, cooks up fluffier and less sticky than low-amylose short-grain rice. Industrially, amylose is used in biodegradable films and as a resistant starch ingredient thought to benefit gut health and glycaemic control.
