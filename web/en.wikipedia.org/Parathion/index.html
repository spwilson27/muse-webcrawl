Parathion

Parathion, also called parathion-ethyl or diethyl parathion and sold as Folidol, is an organophosphate insecticide and acaricide. It was originally developed by the German chemical company IG Farben in the 1940s, under the direction of Gerhard Schrader, whose research programme also produced nerve agents such as sarin. It is highly toxic to non-target organisms, including bees, fish, birds, and humans.

Parathion works by inhibiting the enzyme acetylcholinesterase; in the body it is converted to paraoxon, an even more potent inhibitor. Exposure causes the signs of cholinergic poisoning — pinpoint pupils, salivation, convulsions, and respiratory failure — and can be fatal. Treatment involves atropine and oxime reactivators such as pralidoxime.

Regulation

Because of its extreme toxicity, parathion has been banned or severely restricted in most countries. It is classified by the World Health Organization as Class Ia, "extremely hazardous", listed under the Rotterdam Convention, and banned in the European Union and the United States for most uses. Its less acutely toxic methyl analogue, methyl parathion, has also been widely restricted. Parathion was responsible for many fatal occupational and accidental poisonings, particularly among agricultural workers in developing countries, and for the notorious 1958 Kerala food-poisoning incident in India, in which contaminated flour killed more than one hundred people.
