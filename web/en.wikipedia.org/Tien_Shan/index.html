The Tien Shan (Chinese: 天山, "Heavenly Mountains"; Kyrgyz: Теңир-Тоо) is a large system of mountain ranges in Central Asia, stretching about 2,500 km from east to west across the borders of China, Kyrgyzstan, Kazakhstan, and Uzbekistan. It is one of the great mountain systems of Asia, with peaks rising above the surrounding deserts and steppes like islands of ice.

Geography

The range's highest point is Jengish Chokusu (Victory Peak, 7,439 m) on the Kyrgyz–Chinese border, followed by the marble pyramid of Khan Tengri (7,010 m including its ice cap). The Tien Shan is separated from the Pamirs to the southwest by the Fergana Valley region, and its western ranges enclose the high Issyk-Kul basin in Kyrgyzstan, whose large lake never freezes despite its altitude. Major glaciers, notably the Engilchek Glacier system, feed rivers such as the Syr Darya and the Ili that sustain irrigation in the dry lowlands.

Geology and ecology

The range is an active intracontinental orogen, uplifted by the collision of the Indian plate with Eurasia even though it lies far from the plate boundary, as crustal blocks were reactivated along ancient sutures. Its slopes host one of the world's most important wild fruit forests — walnut and wild apple woodland in Kyrgyzstan, the genetic ancestor of the domestic apple — along with spruce forests, alpine meadows, and snow leopards. Parts of the range in both China and Kyrgyzstan are inscribed as UNESCO World Heritage Sites.

History

Branches of the Silk Road crossed the Tien Shan through passes such as the Torugart and Irkeshtam, linking the Tarim Basin with Central Asian cities. The Chinese monk Xuanzang crossed the range on his 7th-century pilgrimage to India and described its glaciers and the "Thousand Springs" region.
