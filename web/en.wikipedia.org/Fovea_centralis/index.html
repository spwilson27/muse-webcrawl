Fovea centralis

Schematic of the fovea, a small pit in the macula packed with cone photoreceptors

The fovea centralis is a small, central pit composed of closely packed cones in the eye. It is located in the centre of the macula lutea of the retina and is responsible for sharp central vision, which is necessary for reading, driving, and any activity in which visual detail is of primary importance.

Structure

The fovea is approximately 1.5 mm in diameter. Its central portion, the foveola, contains only cone photoreceptors — no rods and almost no blood vessels — with inner retinal layers displaced aside to form the foveal pit, minimising light scattering. Humans typically have trichromatic foveal cones sensitive to red, green, and blue light. The density of cones here is the highest in the retina, giving the fovea the greatest visual acuity; each cone connects to its own output pathway in the central foveola.

Function

Because of its high acuity, the eyes constantly make small movements (fixations and saccades) to place images of interest onto the fovea. Although it covers only a tiny fraction of the visual field, roughly half of the visual cortex is devoted to processing foveal information. Damage to the fovea, as in macular degeneration, severely impairs detailed central vision while leaving peripheral vision relatively intact.
