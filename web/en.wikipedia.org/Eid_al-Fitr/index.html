Eid al-Fitr (Arabic: عيد الفطر, "Festival of Breaking the Fast") is one of the two main festivals in Islam, celebrated by Muslims worldwide on the first day of Shawwal, the month following Ramadan. It marks the end of the month-long dawn-to-sunset fasting of Ramadan. The day begins with a special congregational prayer (salat al-eid) held in mosques and open grounds, and it is forbidden to fast on Eid al-Fitr itself.

Observances

Before the Eid prayer, Muslims give zakat al-fitr (also called fitrana), a charitable donation of food or its value intended to allow the poor to share in the feast. After prayers, families and friends visit one another, exchange greetings of "Eid Mubarak" ("Blessed Eid"), wear new or best clothes, and share festive meals. Children commonly receive money or gifts (eidi). Regional traditions include special sweets such as ma'amoul (filled shortbread) in the Levant and Egypt, kahk in Egypt, sheer khurma in South Asia, and rendang with ketupat in Indonesia and Malaysia, where the festival is known as Hari Raya Puasa. Public celebrations typically last one to three days, and Eid al-Fitr is an official holiday in Muslim-majority countries.

Date

Because the Islamic calendar is lunar, Eid al-Fitr moves about eleven days earlier each solar year, cycling through all seasons over roughly 33 years. The precise day is determined by the sighting of the Shawwal crescent moon, so dates can differ by a day between countries.
