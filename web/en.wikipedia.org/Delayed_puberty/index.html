Delayed puberty

Delayed puberty is when a person lacks or has incomplete development of specific sexual characteristics past the usual age of onset of puberty. It is conventionally defined as the absence of testicular enlargement in boys by age 14, or the absence of breast development in girls by age 13, or the absence of menstruation by age 15 or 16. Puberty may also be considered delayed when progression through its stages is unusually slow. The condition affects a small percentage of adolescents and is more commonly diagnosed in boys.

Causes

The most common cause is constitutional delay of growth and puberty, a benign variation of normal development that often runs in families and resolves spontaneously. Other causes fall into two broad groups. Hypogonadotropic hypogonadism reflects inadequate secretion of the gonadotropin hormones LH and FSH, and may be caused by chronic illness, malnutrition or eating disorders, excessive exercise, thyroid disease, pituitary tumours, or genetic conditions such as Kallmann syndrome. Hypergonadotropic hypogonadism reflects failure of the gonads themselves, as in Klinefelter syndrome in boys and Turner syndrome in girls, or following chemotherapy, radiation, or surgery.

Diagnosis and treatment

Evaluation typically involves a medical history, growth charts, physical examination including Tanner staging, a bone-age X-ray of the hand, and blood tests for gonadotropins and sex steroids. Treatment depends on the cause: underlying conditions are treated where possible; constitutional delay often needs only reassurance and monitoring, though a short course of low-dose testosterone in boys or estrogen in girls may be offered to relieve psychological distress and accelerate development. Long-term sex-hormone replacement is required when hypogonadism is permanent. Delayed puberty can cause significant anxiety and social difficulties for adolescents even when it has no serious underlying cause.
