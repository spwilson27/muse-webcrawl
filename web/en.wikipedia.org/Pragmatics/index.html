Pragmatics

In linguistics and related fields, pragmatics is the study of how context contributes to meaning. The field of study evaluates how human language is utilised in social interactions, as well as the relationship between the interpreter and the interpreted.

The concept originates with the philosopher Charles Morris, who distinguished syntax (relations among signs), semantics (relations between signs and what they refer to), and pragmatics (relations between signs and their users). Unlike semantics, which examines meaning conventionally encoded in language, pragmatics studies meaning as inferred by speakers and listeners in context.

Key topics

Central topics in pragmatics include implicature, the meaning a speaker conveys beyond what is literally said, as developed in H. P. Grice's theory of conversational maxims and the cooperative principle; speech acts, following J. L. Austin and John Searle, which analyse utterances as actions (asserting, promising, ordering); deixis, expressions such as "I", "here" and "tomorrow" whose reference depends on context; presupposition; and politeness, formalised in Brown and Levinson's account of face.

Approaches

Major theoretical frameworks include Gricean pragmatics, relevance theory (Sperber and Wilson), which treats comprehension as an inferential search for optimal relevance, and neo-Gricean approaches. Pragmatics overlaps with sociolinguistics, discourse analysis, cognitive linguistics and the philosophy of language. Clinical and developmental pragmatics study how pragmatic abilities are acquired by children and impaired in conditions such as autism spectrum disorder.
