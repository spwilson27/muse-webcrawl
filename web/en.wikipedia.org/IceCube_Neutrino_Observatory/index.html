IceCube Neutrino Observatory

The IceCube Neutrino Observatory (or simply IceCube) is a neutrino observatory constructed at the Amundsen–Scott South Pole Station in Antarctica. Occupying roughly a cubic kilometre of ice, it is the world's largest neutrino detector, built to observe neutrinos from the most violent astrophysical sources: supernovae, gamma-ray bursts, and colliding black holes and neutron stars.

Construction and design

Construction ran from 2004 to 2010. Holes were melted in the ice with hot-water drills, and 86 strings, each carrying 60 digital optical modules, were lowered to depths between 1,450 and 2,450 metres. When a neutrino interacts in the ice, the resulting charged particles emit Cherenkov radiation, which the modules record, allowing reconstruction of the neutrino's direction and energy. A surface array, IceTop, studies cosmic rays.

Discoveries

In 2013 IceCube announced the first detection of high-energy astrophysical neutrinos. In 2017 a neutrino, IceCube-170922A, was traced back to the flaring blazar TXS 0506+056, the first identified source of high-energy neutrinos and a landmark for multi-messenger astronomy.
