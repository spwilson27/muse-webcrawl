Treaty of Apamea

Treaty of Apamea
Signed188 BC, Apamea, Phrygia
PartiesRoman Republic; Seleucid Empire (Antiochus III)
ContextRoman–Seleucid War (192–188 BC)
EffectSeleucid power expelled from Asia Minor; huge indemnity imposed

The Treaty of Apamea was the peace settlement of 188 BC that ended the Roman–Seleucid War between the Roman Republic and the Seleucid king Antiochus III the Great. Concluded at Apamea in Phrygia after Rome's decisive victory at the Battle of Magnesia (190 BC), it permanently removed Seleucid power from Asia Minor and established Rome as the arbiter of the eastern Mediterranean.

Terms

Antiochus was required to:

  • Evacuate all territory west and north of the Taurus Mountains, surrendering his possessions in Asia Minor, which Rome distributed mainly to its allies Pergamum under Eumenes II and Rhodes.
  • Pay an enormous indemnity of 15,000 Euboean talents — 3,000 immediately and the rest in twelve annual instalments — one of the largest war indemnities of the ancient world.
  • Surrender his elephants, reduce his navy to ten warships, and refrain from sailing warships beyond specified limits or recruiting mercenaries from Roman-controlled territory.
  • Deliver twenty hostages chosen by Rome, among them his younger son, the future king Antiochus IV Epiphanes; Hannibal, who had taken refuge at the Seleucid court, was also demanded, though he escaped into exile.

Consequences

The indemnity strained Seleucid finances for generations and drove later kings to desperate revenue-raising, including Antiochus IV's plundering of temples — a background to the Maccabean revolt. Pergamum and Rhodes emerged enlarged but increasingly dependent on Roman favour. The treaty is seen by historians as a landmark in Rome's rise to Mediterranean hegemony: achieved without annexing territory in the east, it shows Rome dictating terms across the Hellenistic world.
