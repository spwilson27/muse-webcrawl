Julius Rosenberg (1918 – 1953) and Ethel Rosenberg (née Greenglass; 1915 – 1953) were an American married couple convicted of conspiring to pass secrets about the atomic bomb to the Soviet Union during and after the Second World War. They were executed in the electric chair at Sing Sing Prison on 19 June 1953 — the only American civilians put to death for espionage during the Cold War.

Arrest and trial

Julius, an electrical engineer employed by the Army Signal Corps until his dismissal over Communist Party membership, was arrested in July 1950 after the confession of the spy Klaus Fuchs led investigators through Harry Gold to Ethel's brother, David Greenglass, a machinist at Los Alamos. Greenglass testified that Julius had recruited him and that Ethel had typed his notes — testimony he later partly recanted. At their 1951 trial before Judge Irving Kaufman, the Rosenbergs maintained their innocence and invoked the Fifth Amendment about Communist affiliation. Sentencing them to death, Kaufman blamed them for the Korean War and Soviet atomic capability.

Controversy and later evidence

The case became a worldwide cause célèbre; figures including Jean-Paul Sartre, Pablo Picasso and Pope Pius XII appealed for clemency, while many Americans supported the verdict amid McCarthy-era fears. Decades later, decrypted Soviet cables (the Venona project, released from 1995) confirmed that Julius had run an espionage ring passing military and industrial secrets, though the value of his atomic information is debated, and indicated Ethel knew of his activities but was likely not a recruited agent herself. Their sons, Michael and Robert Meeropol, long campaigned over the case; in 2015 they petitioned for Ethel's exoneration.
