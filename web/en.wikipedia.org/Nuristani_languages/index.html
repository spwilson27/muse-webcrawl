The Nuristani languages, previously known as Kafiri languages, are a small group of languages spoken by the Nuristani people in the Hindu Kush valleys of northeastern Afghanistan and adjacent parts of Chitral, Pakistan. They form one of the three branches of the Indo-Iranian languages, alongside Indo-Aryan and Iranian, though their exact position has long been debated; Georg Morgenstierne's fieldwork in the early 20th century established them as a distinct third group.

Languages

The principal Nuristani languages are Kati (the largest, also called Kamkata-vari), Waigali, Ashkun, Prasun (Paruni), Tregami, and Zemiaki. They are spoken in isolated mountain valleys, and mutual intelligibility between them is limited. Prasun is notably divergent, retaining archaic features and lacking some sound changes found in the others. Nuristani languages preserve some Proto-Indo-Iranian distinctions lost elsewhere, and retain a three-way contrast in certain fricatives.

History and culture

The region was known as Kafiristan ("land of the unbelievers") because its people practised an ancient polytheistic religion until they were forcibly converted to Islam in 1895–96 by Emir Abdur Rahman Khan, after which it was renamed Nuristan, "land of light". The languages were first described in detail by Western scholars in the late 19th and early 20th centuries.

Status

None of the Nuristani languages has official status, and most have only recently been written, usually in a Perso-Arabic script. All are considered vulnerable, with Dari (Persian) and Pashto dominant in education and administration.
