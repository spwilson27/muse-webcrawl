Shape-memory polymer

A thermally triggered shape-memory polymer recovering its permanent shape.

Shape-memory polymers (SMPs) are polymeric smart materials that have the ability to return from a deformed state (a temporary shape) to their original (permanent) shape when induced by an external stimulus, most commonly a change in temperature. They are part of the broader family of shape-memory materials, which also includes shape-memory alloys such as nitinol.

The shape-memory effect in polymers arises from a network architecture combining permanent crosslinks ("netpoints"), which define the permanent shape, with reversible switching segments whose mobility changes across a transition temperature (a glass transition or melting point). Below the transition the polymer can be deformed and the temporary shape fixed by cooling; reheating above the transition restores chain mobility and the network recovers its original form.

Properties and comparison with alloys

Compared with shape-memory alloys, SMPs are lighter, cheaper, and capable of much larger recoverable strains—up to several hundred percent—but provide lower recovery stresses and slower actuation. They can be processed by conventional techniques, are often biocompatible, and can be triggered not only by heat but also by light, electric or magnetic fields, moisture, or solvents, depending on their design.

Applications

Applications include self-deploying structures for spacecraft, smart textiles and wrinkle-recovering fabrics, biomedical devices such as self-tightening sutures and expandable stents, self-healing coatings, and reconfigurable or 4D-printed components whose shapes evolve after fabrication.
