Peanut


Peanuts (Arachis hypogaea) ripen underground.

The peanut (Arachis hypogaea), also known as the groundnut, is a legume crop grown mainly for its edible seeds. Unusually for a crop plant, its flower pedicels bend down after pollination and the fruit develops underground. Peanuts are rich in protein, oil, and niacin, and are eaten roasted, boiled, as peanut butter, and as oil.

History

The species was domesticated in South America, probably in the valleys of Peru and Bolivia, thousands of years ago. Portuguese and Spanish traders carried it to Africa and Asia in the 16th century, and it became a major crop in West Africa and, later, the southern United States, where George Washington Carver promoted its use in crop rotation in the early 20th century.

Cultivation and uses

China and India are the largest producers, followed by the United States and Nigeria. Peanuts fix nitrogen and are valuable in rotations. Derivatives include peanut oil, flour, and protein products; lower grades are used for animal feed. Peanut allergy is among the most common severe food allergies, prompting labelling laws and school restrictions in many countries. Aflatoxin contamination from Aspergillus moulds is a major food-safety concern in warm climates.
