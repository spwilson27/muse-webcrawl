Foot-in-the-door technique

The foot-in-the-door technique is a compliance tactic in social psychology that aims at getting a person to agree to a large request by first setting them up with a smaller, related request. Once a person has complied with the small request, they are significantly more likely to also comply with the larger one.

Classic study

The technique was demonstrated by Jonathan Freedman and Scott Fraser in a 1966 experiment. Householders who first agreed to a trivial request — signing a petition or displaying a small sign about safe driving — were far more likely a few weeks later to allow a large, unattractive "Drive Carefully" billboard to be installed on their front lawns (about 76% versus 17% in a control group asked only the large request).

Explanations

The leading explanation is self-perception theory: after agreeing to the small request, people infer that they are the kind of person who supports the cause, and this altered self-image makes refusing the larger request feel inconsistent. Consistency and commitment principles in Robert Cialdini's work on persuasion develop the same idea.

Applications and related tactics

The technique is used in sales, fundraising, marketing, and health campaigns — for example, asking first for a small donation or a free trial before a larger commitment. It is contrasted with the door-in-the-face technique, in which an initial request is deliberately so large that it will be refused, making the subsequent target request seem reasonable by comparison.
