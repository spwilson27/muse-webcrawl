Houston Ship Channel

The Houston Ship Channel is a 52-mile (84 km) dredged waterway in Texas connecting Houston to the Gulf of Mexico through Galveston Bay. Completed in its modern form in 1914, it transformed landlocked Houston into one of the busiest ports in the United States and hosts the Port of Houston and one of the world's largest petrochemical complexes.

History

Navigation on Buffalo Bayou and Galveston Bay dates to the founding of Houston in 1836. After the devastating Galveston hurricane of 1900, Houston businessmen pushed for a deep, protected inland port; federal and local funding created a 25-foot channel, ceremonially opened in November 1914. Successive deepening and widening projects have brought the channel to roughly 45 feet deep, and a further expansion programme ("Project 11") began in the 2020s to widen it for larger vessels.

Economic role and environment

More than 200 public and private terminals line the channel, handling petrochemicals, containers, steel, and bulk cargo; the surrounding refining and chemical plants constitute the largest such complex in the United States. The concentration of industry has also produced chronic air and water pollution concerns for neighbouring communities, and the low-lying channel corridor is vulnerable to hurricane storm surge, a risk studied since Hurricane Ike in 2008.
