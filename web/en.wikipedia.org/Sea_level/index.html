Sea level

Mean sea level (MSL) is the average height of the surface of the sea over a defined period, used as a standard reference (a vertical datum) for measuring elevations on land and depths in the ocean. It is typically computed from hourly tide-gauge measurements averaged over a full lunar cycle of about 19 years, removing the effects of tides and weather.

Measurement

Sea level varies from place to place: the ocean surface is not level in the geometric sense but follows the geoid, and local sea level is affected by winds, currents, water temperature and ongoing vertical land motion — formerly glaciated regions such as Scandinavia and Canada are still rebounding upward after the last ice age (post-glacial rebound). Since 1992, satellite altimeters (beginning with TOPEX/Poseidon) have measured global sea surface height precisely, complementing the tide-gauge network.

Sea level change

Over geological time, sea level has swung by more than a hundred metres with the growth and decay of ice sheets; at the Last Glacial Maximum it stood about 120 metres lower than today. Since the late 19th century global mean sea level has risen by roughly 20–25 cm, and the rate has accelerated to more than 3.5 mm per year in recent decades, driven by thermal expansion of warming seawater and melting glaciers and ice sheets in Greenland and Antarctica. Continued rise threatens low-lying coasts and island nations with flooding, erosion and saltwater intrusion.

Uses

Mean sea level underlies national height systems used in maps and engineering, aviation altimetry (via standard pressure references) and chart datums for navigation.
