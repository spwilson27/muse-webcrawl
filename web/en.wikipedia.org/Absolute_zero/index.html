Absolute zero

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Absolute zero is the lowest limit of the thermodynamic temperature scale; a state at which the enthalpy and entropy of a cooled ideal gas reach their minimum values, taken as zero kelvin. The fundamental particles of nature have minimum vibrational motion, retaining only quantum mechanical, zero-point energy-induced particle motion. By international agreement, absolute zero is defined as 0  K on the Kelvin scale and as −273.15 degrees on the Celsius scale, and as −459.67 degrees on the Fahrenheit scale (exactly −273.15 °C and 0 on the Rankine scale, respectively).

Scientists and technologists routinely achieve temperatures close to absolute zero, where matter exhibits quantum effects such as Bose–Einstein condensation, superconductivity and superfluidity.

Thermodynamics near absolute zero

Near absolute zero, the behaviour of matter is dominated by quantum mechanics. The third law of thermodynamics, formulated by Walther Nernst, states that the entropy of a perfect crystal approaches zero as the temperature approaches absolute zero, and — in its unattainability formulation — that it is impossible by any procedure, no matter how idealized, to reduce the temperature of a system to absolute zero in a finite number of steps.

At temperatures near 0 K, nearly all molecular motion ceases and the entropy change for any isothermal process approaches zero. However, quantum mechanical systems retain zero-point energy even at absolute zero, as required by the Heisenberg uncertainty principle.

History of the concept

One of the first to discuss the possibility of an "absolute cold" was Robert Boyle. In 1665, in his New Experiments and Observations touching Cold, he raised the question of a primum frigidum. The argument was later taken up by Guillaume Amontons, who in 1699 extrapolated the behaviour of his air thermometer to estimate the temperature at which air pressure would vanish, arriving at roughly −240 °C on the modern scale — the first published estimate of absolute zero.

In 1848, William Thomson (Lord Kelvin) proposed an absolute temperature scale independent of any particular substance, with its zero at the point where no more heat could be removed from a body. The exact value, −273.15 °C, was established in the 20th century through gas thermometry and, since the 2019 redefinition of SI base units, the kelvin is defined by fixing the value of the Boltzmann constant.

Very low temperatures

The average temperature of the universe, based on measurements of the cosmic microwave background, is about 2.7 K. Laboratory techniques have gone far below this. Laser cooling and magnetic refrigeration have produced temperatures below one nanokelvin for dilute atomic gases, and nuclear demagnetization of metals has reached the picokelvin range for nuclear spins. In 2021, researchers reported cooling a rubidium gas to 38 picokelvin (38 trillionths of a kelvin above absolute zero) in a drop-tower experiment — among the coldest temperatures ever recorded.

The coldest known natural place is the Boomerang Nebula, at about 1 K, although laboratories on Earth routinely achieve far lower temperatures.

Matter at low temperature phenomena

  • Bose–Einstein condensation — first achieved in 1995 with dilute gases of rubidium and sodium atoms at temperatures near 100 nK.
  • Superfluidity — liquid helium-4 below the lambda point (2.17 K) flows with zero viscosity.
  • Superconductivity — discovered by Heike Kamerlingh Onnes in 1911 when mercury was cooled to about 4.2 K.
