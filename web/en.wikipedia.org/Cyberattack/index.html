Cyberattack

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A cyberattack (or cyber attack) is any offensive manoeuvre that targets computer information systems, computer networks, infrastructures, personal computer devices, or smartphones. An attacker is a person or process that attempts to access data, functions, or other restricted areas of the system without authorisation, potentially with malicious intent. Depending on context, cyberattacks can be part of cyber warfare or cyberterrorism, and can be employed by sovereign states, individuals, groups, or organisations, from anonymous sources or state-backed advanced persistent threat groups.

Types

Common attack methods include malware (viruses, worms, trojans, and ransomware), phishing and other social engineering, denial-of-service (DoS and DDoS) attacks that overwhelm systems with traffic, man-in-the-middle attacks, SQL injection and other exploitation of software vulnerabilities, credential theft and password attacks, and zero-day exploits of previously unknown flaws. Supply-chain attacks compromise trusted software or vendors to reach their targets indirectly, as in the 2020 SolarWinds incident, which affected numerous US government agencies and companies.

Notable incidents

The Stuxnet worm, discovered in 2010, damaged Iranian uranium-enrichment centrifuges and demonstrated that malware could cause physical destruction. In 2017 the WannaCry ransomware cryptoworm spread to hundreds of thousands of computers worldwide, disrupting hospitals in the UK's National Health Service, while the NotPetya attack, attributed to Russia and aimed at Ukraine, caused an estimated US$10 billion in global damage to firms such as Maersk and Merck. The 2021 Colonial Pipeline ransomware attack in the United States prompted fuel shortages and a state of emergency.

Motives and actors

Motives range from financial gain (the dominant motive in reported breaches), through espionage and intellectual-property theft by states, to hacktivism, sabotage, and geopolitical coercion. Defenders respond with layered security: patching, network segmentation, multi-factor authentication, monitoring and incident response, and threat-intelligence sharing. Governments have established cyber commands and agencies — such as US Cyber Command and the UK's National Cyber Security Centre — to defend critical infrastructure and, in some doctrines, to conduct offensive operations.

Legal aspects

Most jurisdictions criminalise unauthorised computer access under statutes such as the US Computer Fraud and Abuse Act and the UK Computer Misuse Act 1990. Internationally, the Budapest Convention on Cybercrime (2001) harmonises national laws, while scholars debate how international humanitarian law applies to state cyber operations, a question explored by the Tallinn Manual.
