History of the Jews in Ukraine

The history of the Jews in Ukraine goes back over a thousand years. Jewish communities have existed in the territory of modern Ukraine since the time of Kievan Rus', and at their height — before the Holocaust — Ukrainian lands were home to one of the largest Jewish populations in the world, numbering in the millions. Ukraine was the birthplace of Hasidic Judaism and a major centre of Yiddish culture, Hebrew letters and Zionism.

Early history

Evidence of Jewish presence in the northern Black Sea region reaches back to antiquity, including Greek colonies and the Khazar Khaganate, whose ruling class converted to Judaism in the 8th or 9th century. In Kievan Rus' a Jewish community existed in Kyiv by the 10th–11th centuries, with a "Jewish gate" recorded in the city. After the Mongol invasion of 1240, Jewish settlement in Kyiv declined, while communities grew in the west under Polish rule.

Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth

From the 14th century, Jews settled in growing numbers in Ukrainian towns within the Kingdom of Poland and later the Commonwealth, working as merchants, craftsmen and estate leaseholders. The catastrophe of 1648–49, when Bohdan Khmelnytsky's Cossack uprising swept Ukraine, brought massacres in which tens of thousands of Jews were killed. In the 18th century, the Haidamak uprisings culminated in the 1768 massacre at Uman. In the same era, Israel ben Eliezer, the Baal Shem Tov, founded Hasidism in Podolia; movements centred on tzaddikim at Medzhybizh, Uman (followers of Nachman of Breslov) and elsewhere spread across Eastern Europe.

Russian Empire and the Pale of Settlement

After the partitions of Poland (1793–95), most Ukrainian Jews became subjects of the Russian Empire and were confined to the Pale of Settlement. Jews formed large shares of town populations — in Odesa, founded in 1794, they comprised a third or more of the inhabitants and built a cosmopolitan centre of commerce and culture. They faced the 1827 cantonist decrees, the May Laws of 1882 and waves of pogroms in 1881–82 and 1903–06 (notably Kishinev, in neighbouring Bessarabia, and Kyiv and Odesa). Mass emigration to America, and smaller flows to Palestine, followed. Ukraine nurtured Yiddish writers such as Sholem Aleichem, who was born near Kyiv, and Hebrew and Zionist activism in Odesa.

Revolution and civil war

The Russian Revolution of 1917 abolished the Pale, but the civil war of 1918–21 devastated Ukrainian Jewry: forces of the Ukrainian People's Republic, White armies, Red forces and independent warlords perpetrated pogroms that killed tens of thousands — estimates range from 50,000 to over 100,000. Under Symon Petliura's government some pogroms were condemned, but troops under its nominal authority committed many of them.

Soviet Ukraine

In the 1920s the Soviet regime briefly promoted Yiddish culture and Jewish agricultural colonies in the south, and Jews urbanised rapidly, moving into industry, education and administration. The 1930s brought repression of religious life, the closure of synagogues and, during the Great Terror, purges that struck Jewish cultural institutions. By 1939 about 1.5 million Jews lived in the Ukrainian SSR; western Ukrainian territories annexed from Poland added hundreds of thousands more.

The Holocaust

Following the German invasion of June 1941, Einsatzgruppen, German police and auxiliaries carried out mass shootings across Ukraine — the "Holocaust by bullets". At Babi Yar, a ravine outside Kyiv, 33,771 Jews were murdered on 29–30 September 1941 alone. Ghettos in the west were liquidated, and camps such as Janowska in Lviv murdered their inmates. In total, roughly 1.5 million Jews were killed on Ukrainian territory; the pre-war Jewish civilisation of the region was destroyed.

Postwar and independent Ukraine

Survivors returned to find communities shattered; postwar Soviet antisemitism, including the "Doctors' Plot" era and suppression of Jewish culture, pushed many toward emigration when it became possible in the 1970s and again after 1989. Independent Ukraine after 1991 saw a revival of communal and religious life, alongside continued large-scale emigration to Israel, the United States and Germany. In 2019 Volodymyr Zelenskyy, of Jewish descent, was elected president. The Russian invasion of 2022 displaced many of the remaining community, estimated at a few tens of thousands, and damaged historic sites, even as Ukrainian Jewish soldiers and volunteers joined the country's defence.
