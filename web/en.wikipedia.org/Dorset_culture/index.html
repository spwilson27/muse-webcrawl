Dorset culture

The Dorset was a Paleo-Eskimo culture, lasting from 500 BC to between AD 1000 and 1500, that followed the Pre-Dorset and preceded the Thule people (the proto-Inuit) in the North American Arctic. The culture takes its name from Cape Dorset (now Kinngait) in Nunavut, Canada, where the first evidence of its existence was found.

Characteristics

The Dorset people lived in the Canadian Arctic and Greenland, hunting seals, walrus, narwhal, caribou, and polar bears. They are noted for their distinctive small triangular stone end blades, soapstone lamps, and finely carved miniature ivory and bone artwork, including the famous early depictions of bears and human figures. The Dorset appear to have lacked dogs, bows and arrows, and large boats.

Decline

The Dorset disappeared as the Thule, ancestors of the modern Inuit, spread eastward from Alaska after about AD 1000. The causes of the Dorset disappearance remain debated — climate change during the Medieval Warm Period, competition or displacement by the Thule, and disease have all been proposed. Inuit oral traditions remember a gentle, shy people called the Tuniit, often identified with the Dorset.

Genetics

Ancient DNA studies have shown that the Dorset were genetically distinct from both their predecessors and the Thule, representing a separate migration from Siberia around 3000 BC, with little interbreeding with the later Thule population.
