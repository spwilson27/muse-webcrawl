Forest Stewardship Council

The FSC label identifies products from responsibly managed forests.

The Forest Stewardship Council (FSC) is an international non-profit, multi-stakeholder organization established in 1993 that promotes responsible management of the world's forests via timber certification. It is headquartered in Bonn, Germany. The FSC sets standards for forest management, accredits independent certifiers, and licenses the use of its labels on wood and paper products, allowing consumers to identify products sourced from well-managed forests.

History

The FSC was founded following the 1992 Earth Summit in Rio de Janeiro, at which governments failed to agree on a global forest convention. Environmental groups, timber traders, foresters and representatives of indigenous peoples met in Toronto in 1993 to create a voluntary, market-based alternative. A rival scheme, the Programme for the Endorsement of Forest Certification (PEFC), was established by European forest owners in 1999.

Standards and certification

FSC certification rests on ten principles covering legal compliance, workers' and indigenous peoples' rights, community relations, environmental values, management planning, monitoring and high conservation value forests. Two main certificates exist: Forest Management certification for forest operations, and Chain of Custody certification, which tracks certified material through the supply chain. Labels include FSC 100%, FSC Mix and FSC Recycled. By the 2020s, more than 150 million hectares of forest worldwide were FSC certified.

Governance

Membership is divided into environmental, social and economic chambers, each holding equal voting weight, with further balance between members from the global North and South. This structure distinguishes the FSC from industry-led schemes.

Criticism

The FSC has been criticized by some environmental groups, including FSC Watch, for certifying controversial logging operations and for weaknesses in auditing. In 2018, Greenpeace International declined to renew its membership, arguing the scheme was not doing enough to protect intact forest landscapes, though several other major NGOs remain supportive members.
