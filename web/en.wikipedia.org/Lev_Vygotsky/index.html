Lev Vygotsky

Lev Semyonovich Vygotsky (Russian: Лев Семёнович Выго́тский; November 17, 1896 – June 11, 1934) was a Soviet psychologist, known for his work on psychological development in children and creating the framework known as cultural-historical activity theory. His major works span the fields of developmental psychology, child development, and education. He introduced concepts including the zone of proximal development and argued that learning is fundamentally a social, culturally mediated process, with language — particularly inner speech — playing a central role in the development of higher mental functions.

Life

Vygotsky was born in Orsha (now Belarus) and raised in Gomel. He studied law at Moscow University while also attending courses in history and philosophy, and taught literature and psychology in Gomel. His 1924 presentation on the methods of reflexological and psychological investigation brought him to the Institute of Psychology in Moscow, where he gathered a circle of collaborators — including Alexander Luria and Alexei Leontiev — known as the Vygotsky Circle. He died of tuberculosis in 1934, aged only 37. After his death his works were suppressed in the Soviet Union until the mid-1950s, and his major texts reached Western readers only from the 1960s, producing a delayed but enormous influence.

Main ideas

Vygotsky argued that children's learning takes place first on a social plane, between people, and only later inside the child (the "general genetic law of cultural development"). The zone of proximal development is the gap between what a learner can do unaided and what they can do with guidance from a more knowledgeable other; teaching, he argued, should target this zone. He also studied concept formation, the egocentric/private speech of children (which he saw as a tool of thought rather than mere immaturity, contrary to Piaget), and the education of children with disabilities, founding Soviet defectology.

Major works

Thought and Language (1934, published posthumously) analysed the relation between speech and thinking; Mind in Society (1978) assembled his essays for English readers. His theory underpins modern approaches such as scaffolding and social constructivism in education.
