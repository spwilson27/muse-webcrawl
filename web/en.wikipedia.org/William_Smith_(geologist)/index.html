William Smith (geologist)

William Smith (March 23, 1769 – August 28, 1839) was an English geologist, credited with creating the first nationwide geological map of any country. Working as a surveyor for canals and coal mines, he recognized that rock strata could be identified and dated by the distinctive fossils they contain — the principle of faunal succession — and in 1815 published his great geological map of England, Wales, and part of Scotland.

Early life and surveying career

Smith was born in Oxfordshire, the son of a blacksmith. He became a surveyor's assistant and worked on the Somerset Coal Canal and other canal projects in the 1790s. Observing the layered rocks exposed by canal cuts and mines around Bath, he noticed that each stratum occurred in a consistent order and contained characteristic fossils.

Faunal succession and the map

By 1799 Smith had dictated a table of the strata near Bath and sketched early geological maps. His insight — that sedimentary layers can be identified across the country by their fossil contents — provided the practical basis for stratigraphy. After years of travel gathering data, in 1815 he published A Delineation of the Strata of England and Wales with Part of Scotland, a large hand-colored map that founded geological mapping.

Hardship and recognition

Smith's map was plagiarized by contemporaries, and financial difficulties landed him in debtors' prison in 1819. Recognition came late: in 1831 the Geological Society awarded him the first Wollaston Medal, with its president Adam Sedgwick calling him "the Father of English Geology." He received a government pension and died in 1839.
