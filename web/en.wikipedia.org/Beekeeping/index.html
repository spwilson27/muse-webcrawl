Beekeeping

Beekeeping (or apiculture) is the maintenance of bee colonies, commonly in man-made beehives, by humans. Most such bees are honey bees in the genus Apis, but other honey-producing bees such as Melipona stingless bees are also kept. A beekeeper (or apiarist) keeps bees in order to collect their honey and other products that the hive produces (including beeswax, propolis, flower pollen, bee pollen, and royal jelly), to pollinate crops, or to produce bees for sale to other beekeepers. A location where bees are kept is called an apiary or "bee yard".

History

Depictions of humans collecting honey from wild bees date to 15,000 years ago; efforts to domesticate them are shown in Egyptian art around 4,500 years ago. Simple hives and smoke were used, and honey was stored in jars, some of which were found in the tombs of pharaohs such as Tutankhamun. It was not until the 18th century that European natural philosophers undertook the scientific study of bee colonies and began to understand the biology of bees. The invention of the movable-frame hive, notably the Langstroth hive patented in 1852, together with the discovery of "bee space", revolutionised beekeeping by allowing inspection and honey harvest without destroying the colony.

The colony

A honey bee colony normally consists of a single queen, several thousand worker bees, and, seasonally, several hundred drones. The queen is the mother of all the bees in the colony and can lay up to 2,000 eggs a day at the height of the season. Worker bees are sterile females that forage, build comb, feed larvae, and defend the hive. Drones are males whose only role is to mate with a virgin queen.

Modern practice

Modern beekeepers use hives with removable frames, wear protective clothing, and use a smoker to calm the bees during inspections. Colonies are managed to prevent swarming, monitored for diseases and parasites — most seriously the mite Varroa destructor — and moved to follow nectar flows or provide pollination services. Managed bees are the most economically valuable pollinators of crops worldwide, and commercial pollination is a major part of the industry in countries such as the United States.

Products

The primary product is honey, which bees produce from nectar and store as a winter food source; surplus honey is harvested by the beekeeper. Beeswax is used in candles, cosmetics, and food. Propolis, pollen, and royal jelly are collected for dietary supplements and traditional medicine. Bees also supply pollination, whose economic value greatly exceeds that of hive products.

Threats

Bee populations face pressure from habitat loss, pesticides, climate change, and pests and diseases including Varroa mites, tracheal mites, American foulbrood, and colony collapse disorder. These pressures have increased interest in sustainable and natural beekeeping methods.
