Project Tiger is a wildlife conservation programme launched by the Government of India on 1 April 1973 to protect the Bengal tiger and its habitats. One of the largest species-conservation efforts in the world, it established a network of dedicated tiger reserves administered under a core–buffer strategy, in which core areas are kept free of human disturbance while buffer zones allow regulated, conservation-oriented land use. Since 2006 the project has been administered by the statutory National Tiger Conservation Authority (NTCA).

When Project Tiger began, India's wild tiger population had collapsed from an estimated 40,000 animals at the turn of the twentieth century to under 2,000, a decline driven by sport hunting in the colonial and early independence periods, poaching, and the conversion of forests to agriculture. The 1972 census, which counted about 1,827 tigers, shocked conservationists and the government of Prime Minister Indira Gandhi, whose administration passed the Wildlife (Protection) Act of 1972 and then launched Project Tiger the following year, with initial support from the World Wide Fund for Nature (WWF) and the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN).

Background

The tiger was once abundant across the Indian subcontinent, and tiger hunting was a celebrated ritual of both Mughal and British imperial elites and Indian princes. Tens of thousands of tigers were shot between the late nineteenth century and the mid-twentieth. Post-independence, forest clearance, hydroelectric and mining projects, and a thriving illegal market for skins eroded tiger habitat further. Naturalists such as Kailash Sankhala — who became Project Tiger's first director — documented the crisis and argued that only a dedicated, centrally sponsored programme could save the species. India hosted an international tiger symposium in 1969 at which the IUCN called for a total ban on tiger hunting; India banned tiger hunting in 1970 and incorporated wildlife protection into national law in 1972.

Objectives and strategy

The stated aim of Project Tiger is to ensure a viable population of tigers in India for scientific, economic, aesthetic, cultural and ecological values, and to preserve areas of biological importance as natural heritage for the benefit, education and enjoyment of the people. Its management model divides each tiger reserve into:

  • Core (critical tiger habitat) — an inviolate zone managed strictly for tiger conservation, from which villages have been relocated on a voluntary, compensated basis; and
  • Buffer (peripheral area) — a multiple-use zone where habitat restoration, eco-development for local communities and regulated tourism reduce pressure on the core.

The Directorate also coordinates anti-poaching patrols, fire and habitat management, prey-base augmentation and, from time to time, the reintroduction of tigers to reserves where they had been wiped out, most famously at Sariska (2008) and Panna (2009).

Tiger reserves

The project began with nine reserves — including Corbett (then in Uttar Pradesh), Bandipur, Kanha, Manas, Melghat, Palamau, Ranthambore, Similipal and Sunderbans — covering some 18,278 square kilometres. The network has grown steadily; by the early 2020s there were more than fifty tiger reserves spread across eighteen states and covering over 75,000 square kilometres, roughly 2.3 percent of India's land area. Well-known reserves include Corbett, Kanha, Bandhavgarh, Ranthambore, Kaziranga, Sundarbans, Bandipur and Periyar. Several, such as the Nagarahole–Bandipur–Mudumalai–Wayanad complex in the Western Ghats and the Terai Arc across northern India and Nepal, form some of the largest connected tiger landscapes in the world.

Administration

Project Tiger was originally run as a centrally sponsored scheme of the Ministry of Environment and Forests, with funding shared between the central government and the states. Following the amendment of the Wildlife (Protection) Act in 2006 — a response to the Sariska crisis, in which local extinction of tigers went officially unnoticed — the National Tiger Conservation Authority was created as a statutory body to approve tiger conservation plans, set standards for tourism and habitat management, and oversee the reserves. Each reserve is managed day to day by a field director under the state's forest department.

Population monitoring

For decades India estimated tiger numbers by the unreliable pugmark (footprint) census. A scandal in 2004–2005, when an official review confirmed that Sariska Tiger Reserve had lost all its tigers to poaching despite healthy official counts, forced a methodological revolution. Since the 2006 national estimation, India has used a scientific protocol of camera trapping, occupancy surveys, prey counts and statistical capture–recapture modelling, developed with the Wildlife Institute of India, to produce country-wide estimates every four years. The estimates recorded about 1,411 tigers in 2006, 1,706 in 2010, 2,226 in 2014 and 2,967 in 2018; the 2022 estimation reported a minimum of about 3,167 tigers, with a modelled figure around 3,682, consolidating India's position as the home of roughly three-quarters of the world's wild tigers.

Achievements and criticism

Project Tiger is widely credited with pulling the Indian tiger back from the brink and building one of the world's most extensive protected-area networks. Tiger numbers have more than doubled since the 2006 low point, and the project has protected whole ecosystems and watersheds far beyond the tiger itself — an illustration of the "umbrella species" principle.

At the same time the programme has been criticised on several grounds. The creation of inviolate cores has entailed the relocation of forest-dwelling communities, and conservationists and social scientists dispute the scale, voluntariness and compensation of some relocations, particularly affecting Adivasi (tribal) peoples whose rights under the Forest Rights Act of 2006 must be recognised before relocation. Tigers continue to be lost to poaching for the illegal trade in skins and body parts, to retaliatory killing after livestock depredation and human deaths, and to habitat fragmentation by roads, railways, mines and dams outside reserves. Critics also note conflict between tourism revenue and conservation priorities in heavily visited reserves, and the challenge of maintaining genetic connectivity between isolated populations. The NTCA and state governments respond with corridor protection, compensation schemes, and community-based programmes such as eco-development committees, but human–tiger coexistence remains the central long-term problem of Indian tiger conservation.
