Fermat's little theorem states that if p is a prime number, then for any integer a, the number ap − a is an integer multiple of p. In modular arithmetic this is written ap ≡ a (mod p). If a is not divisible by p, the theorem is equivalent to ap−1 ≡ 1 (mod p).

History

Pierre de Fermat first stated the result in a letter to Bernard Frénicle de Bessy dated 18 October 1640, without giving a proof. The first published proof was given by Leonhard Euler in 1736; an earlier proof by Leibniz survives in unpublished manuscripts.

Applications

The theorem underlies probabilistic primality tests such as the Fermat primality test, in which numbers that fail the congruence are certainly composite. Numbers that pass for every base coprime to them yet are composite are called Carmichael numbers. Fermat's little theorem is also fundamental to the RSA public-key cryptosystem, via its generalization, Euler's theorem.

Proof idea

A classic proof considers the multiples a, 2a, …, (p−1)a modulo p. When a is not divisible by the prime p, these are a permutation of 1, 2, …, p−1 modulo p; equating the two products yields (p−1)! · ap−1 ≡ (p−1)! (mod p), and cancelling gives ap−1 ≡ 1 (mod p).
