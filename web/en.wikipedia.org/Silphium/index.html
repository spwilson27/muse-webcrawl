Silphium

Silphium, also known as silphion, laserwort, or laser, is an unidentified plant that was used in classical antiquity as a seasoning, perfume, aphrodisiac, and medicine. It was also used as a contraceptive by ancient Greeks and Romans. It was the essential item of trade from the ancient North African city of Cyrene in Libya, and was so critical to the Cyrenian economy that most of their coins bore the image of the plant.

The exact identity of silphium is unclear. It is believed to have been a member of the genus Ferula (giant fennels). The plant was so valuable that it was worth its weight in silver, and attempts to cultivate it outside its narrow native range around Cyrene failed.

Extinction

Silphium is commonly regarded as the first recorded example of a species extinction caused by human overexploitation. Demand was so great, and the plant reportedly so resistant to cultivation, that harvesting outstripped natural regeneration; by the reign of Nero, according to Pliny the Elder, only a single stalk could be found, which was presented to the emperor. Overgrazing by livestock and desertification of its habitat may have contributed to its disappearance.

Uses

Ancient sources attribute to silphium a wide range of uses: its resin seasoned food, its sap treated coughs, sore throats, fevers, and snake bites, and it was famously employed as a contraceptive and abortifacient. The plant's heart-shaped seed has been suggested as one origin of the heart symbol's association with love.
