The Abu Simbel temples are two massive rock-cut temples in Nubia, southern Egypt, on the western bank of Lake Nasser. They were carved out of the mountainside in the thirteenth century BC, during the reign of Pharaoh Ramesses II of the Nineteenth Dynasty, as a lasting monument to himself and his queen, Nefertari, and to commemorate Egyptian dominance over Nubia, traditionally associated with his victory at the Battle of Kadesh.

The temples

The Great Temple is dominated by four colossal seated statues of Ramesses II, each about 20 metres high, carved into the cliff face. Inside, a hypostyle hall lined with Osiride pillars leads to a sanctuary where statues of the gods Ptah, Amun-Ra, the deified Ramesses and Ra-Horakhty sit in a row; the temple was aligned so that twice a year, around 22 February and 22 October, the sun's rays penetrated the sanctuary and illuminated all but Ptah, god of darkness. The smaller temple is dedicated to the goddess Hathor and to Nefertari, whose statues stand as tall as the king's — an honour exceptional in Egyptian art.

Relocation

The temples were largely forgotten, buried in sand, until the Swiss explorer Johann Ludwig Burckhardt found them in 1813. In the 1960s the construction of the Aswan High Dam threatened to submerge them beneath Lake Nasser. In one of the greatest feats of archaeological engineering, an international UNESCO-led campaign cut the temples into large blocks between 1964 and 1968 and reassembled them 65 metres higher and 200 metres back from the river, inside an artificial hill — a project that helped inspire the World Heritage Convention.
