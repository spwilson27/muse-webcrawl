Spontaneous human combustion (SHC) is the pseudoscientific concept of the combustion of a living (or recently deceased) human body without an apparent external source of ignition. In addition to reported cases, examples appear in literature, and both types have received popular attention. There have been about 200 cited reports worldwide over a period of around 300 years.

Reported characteristics

Alleged cases often share features: the body is largely reduced to ash while extremities (feet, a leg) survive; damage to the room is oddly localised; and the victim is frequently elderly, alone, and near a fire source such as a hearth or cigarette. Famous cases include Mary Reeser of St. Petersburg, Florida (1951), whose remains were found after a fire that left much of her room untouched, and cases described by Charles Dickens, who used SHC to kill the character Krook in Bleak House (1853) — and defended the phenomenon in his preface against critics such as George Henry Lewes.

Scientific explanations

No plausible mechanism is known by which a body could ignite spontaneously, and investigated cases have generally involved an external source. The leading explanation is the wick effect: clothing acts as a wick and rendered body fat as fuel, allowing a body ignited by a dropped cigarette or ember to burn slowly for hours like a candle, consuming the torso while leaving extremities and surroundings comparatively undamaged. A BBC demonstration with a pig carcass in 1998 reproduced the classic features of SHC cases in this way. Many victims were likely incapacitated or deceased before burning began.

In culture

SHC has appeared in novels, films and documentaries and featured in popular treatments of unexplained phenomena since the 18th century, when cases were often attributed to alcoholism — victims were said to be saturated with flammable spirits. In 2011, an Irish coroner recorded a verdict of spontaneous combustion in the death of Michael Faherty, a rare official use of the term, though investigators noted a nearby open fire.
