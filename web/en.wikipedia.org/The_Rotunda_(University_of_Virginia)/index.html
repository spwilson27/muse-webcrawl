The Rotunda (University of Virginia)

ArchitectThomas Jefferson
Built1822–1826; rebuilt after fire 1895–1898
StyleNeoclassical
LocationUniversity of Virginia, Charlottesville

The Rotunda is a building at the University of Virginia in Charlottesville, Virginia, United States, designed by Thomas Jefferson as the architectural and academic heart of his "Academical Village." Modelled on the Pantheon in Rome at half its scale, it originally housed the university library, symbolising Jefferson's conviction that learning, rather than religion, should stand at the centre of the institution.

Design and construction

Completed in 1826, the year of Jefferson's death, the brick Rotunda crowns the north end of the Lawn, the terraced green flanked by the Pavilions and student rooms. Its great Dome Room held the library's books on two levels, while the oval rooms below served as lecture and examination spaces.

Fire and rebuilding

On 27 October 1895 a fire destroyed the Rotunda's dome and interior and its annex. The New York architect Stanford White of McKim, Mead & White rebuilt it, controversially redesigning the Dome Room as a single great space; his annex was removed in the twentieth century. For the United States Bicentennial, the Rotunda was restored in 1973–76 to a configuration closer to Jefferson's original, reinstating the two-storey Dome Room. It is part of a UNESCO World Heritage Site together with Monticello.

Use today

The Rotunda hosts doctoral defences, lectures, ceremonies and the meetings of the university's Board of Visitors, and remains the university's enduring symbol, depicted on its materials and imprinted on its institutional identity. A comprehensive renovation completed in 2016 returned historic classrooms to daily teaching use.
