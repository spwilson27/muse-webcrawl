Acetylcholinesterase

EC number3.1.1.7
SubstrateAcetylcholine
ProductsAcetate + choline
GeneACHE (human)

Acetylcholinesterase (AChE) is an enzyme that catalyses the breakdown of the neurotransmitter acetylcholine into acetate and choline. It is found mainly at neuromuscular junctions and in chemical synapses of the cholinergic nervous system, where it terminates synaptic transmission almost immediately after acetylcholine is released. AChE is one of the fastest enzymes known, approaching diffusion-limited rates.

Function

After acetylcholine activates receptors on the postsynaptic membrane, acetylcholinesterase in the synaptic cleft hydrolyses it within microseconds, preventing continuous stimulation of muscle or nerve cells and allowing precise, repeated signalling. A related enzyme, butyrylcholinesterase, is found in plasma and other tissues.

Inhibitors

Inhibiting AChE causes acetylcholine to accumulate, overstimulating cholinergic receptors. Irreversible inhibitors include organophosphate insecticides (such as malathion and parathion) and nerve agents (sarin, VX); poisoning produces the cholinergic crisis summarised by the SLUDGE symptoms and can be fatal. Reversible inhibitors are used medically: physostigmine, neostigmine and pyridostigmine treat myasthenia gravis and reverse neuromuscular blockade, while donepezil, rivastigmine and galantamine are used to modestly improve symptoms in Alzheimer's disease.
