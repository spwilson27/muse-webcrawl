Golden toad

The golden toad (Incilius periglenes) is an extinct species of true toad that was once abundant in a small, high-altitude region of about 4 square kilometres (1.5 sq mi) in the Monteverde Cloud Forest Reserve of Costa Rica. The species was first described in 1966 by herpetologist Jay Savage. The last sighting of a single male golden toad was on May 15, 1989, and it has since been classified as extinct by the IUCN.

Description

The golden toad showed extreme sexual dimorphism. Males were a brilliant orange-gold, smooth-skinned, and about 39–48 mm long, whereas females were dark olive to black with scarlet spots edged in yellow, and larger, at 42–56 mm. The toads were explosive breeders: during a few weeks in the rainy season, hundreds gathered in temporary pools in the forest floor's root systems, where males greatly outnumbered females and competed to mate.

Extinction

In 1987, over 1,500 adults were counted at breeding pools; by 1988 only about ten were found, and in 1989 a single male. No golden toads have been seen since. The causes remain debated. Proposed explanations include climate change associated with El Niño warming and drying of the cloud forest, the spread of the amphibian chytrid fungus Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis, and interactions between the two: unusually warm, dry conditions may have stressed the toads while favoring the fungus. The golden toad became an icon of the global amphibian decline crisis.
