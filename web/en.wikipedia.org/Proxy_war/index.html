Proxy war

In a proxy war, rival powers back opposing local belligerents instead of fighting directly.

A proxy war is an armed conflict in which two opposing countries or parties instigate or play a major role in supporting a war fought by other, usually local, actors. The instigators use third-party fighting forces — regular armies of smaller states, insurgent movements or militias — to pursue their own strategic goals while avoiding, or limiting, direct confrontation with one another.

Cold War examples

Proxy wars became a hallmark of the Cold War, when nuclear deterrence made direct war between the United States and the Soviet Union too dangerous. The Korean War, the Vietnam War, the Soviet–Afghan War, and conflicts in Angola, Ethiopia, Nicaragua and El Salvador all involved superpower sponsorship of opposing sides, ranging from money and weapons to advisers and, at times, combat troops fighting an adversary's client rather than the adversary itself.

After the Cold War

The pattern persisted after 1991: regional powers have backed rival factions in the Syrian and Yemeni civil wars, and external states supplied competing Libyan factions after 2011. Analysts debate how much control sponsors actually exercise: proxies have their own agendas, and support can create blowback, empower extremist groups, or draw sponsors deeper into conflicts than intended.

Characteristics

Sponsorship may include funding, arms, training, intelligence, logistics or diplomatic cover. Because the sponsors are not formally at war, proxy wars complicate the application of international law on aggression and intervention, and they often prolong conflicts by resupplying sides that would otherwise be forced to settle.
