Hard science fiction is a category of science fiction characterized by concern for scientific accuracy and logic. The term was first used in print by P. Schuyler Miller in a 1957 book review in Astounding Science Fiction. Hard SF stories typically attempt to keep their technology and natural phenomena consistent with known science, or with plausible extrapolations of it, and often devote considerable attention to engineering detail.

History

The tradition is associated with the Golden Age of science fiction under editor John W. Campbell, who demanded scientifically literate stories. Hal Clement's Mission of Gravity (1953), set on a rapidly rotating high-gravity planet, is a classic of world-building rigour, while Arthur C. Clarke's work popularized realistic spaceflight. Later practitioners include Poul Anderson, Larry Niven, Greg Bear, Kim Stanley Robinson, and, in the twenty-first century, Andy Weir, whose novel The Martian was praised for its problem-solving realism.

Debates

There is no agreed boundary for the category: some readers measure hardness by the number of speculative liberties taken, and informal scales rank fictional technologies by plausibility. Critics from within the genre, including some "hard" authors, have argued that character and story matter more than rigour, and the distinction between hard and soft SF is often treated as a spectrum rather than a division.
