The Kentucky Derby is an American horse race held annually in Louisville, Kentucky, on the first Saturday in May. Contested by three-year-old Thoroughbreds over 1¼ miles at Churchill Downs, it is the first leg of the American Triple Crown and is often called "The Most Exciting Two Minutes in Sports" and "The Run for the Roses."

History

The Derby was first run in 1875, founded by Meriwether Lewis Clark Jr., who had attended the Epsom Derby in England and modelled his race on it. Fifteen three-year-olds contested the inaugural running over 1½ miles before about 10,000 spectators; the distance was shortened to its present length in 1896. The race has been run every year since, without interruption.

Traditions

The winner is draped with a blanket of red roses, a tradition dating to the 1890s, and the crowd sings "My Old Kentucky Home" as the horses parade to the post. Mint juleps are the traditional drink of the meeting, and the infield and grandstands together draw crowds exceeding 150,000. The week-long Kentucky Derby Festival precedes the race.

The Triple Crown

The Derby is followed two weeks later by the Preakness Stakes and three weeks after that by the Belmont Stakes. Thirteen horses have won all three, most famously Secretariat (1973), whose Derby time of 1:59⅖ remains the record, and American Pharoah (2015), who ended a 37-year drought.

Records

Eddie Arcaro and Bill Hartack share the record of five Derby wins as jockeys; trainer Ben A. Jones saddled six winners. The fastest winning time is Secretariat's 1:59.40 in 1973. Fillies have won the race three times: Regret (1915), Genuine Risk (1980), and Winning Colors (1988).
