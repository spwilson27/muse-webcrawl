Alfonso X (23 November 1221 – 4 April 1284), called the Wise (el Sabio), was King of Castile and León from 1252 until his death. One of the great scholar-kings of medieval Europe, he sponsored a remarkable programme of translation and original scholarship in law, astronomy, history and literature, and made Castilian the language of his court and administration.

Reign

Alfonso succeeded his father Ferdinand III and continued the Reconquista on a smaller scale, taking Jerez, Cádiz and Niebla, while his imperial ambitions led him to pursue election as Holy Roman Emperor (the fecho del imperio) — a costly claim he eventually renounced in 1275. His later reign was troubled by noble revolts, the Mudéjar revolt of 1264, succession disputes after the death of his heir Fernando de la Cerda, and finally a civil war in which his son Sancho seized power, leaving Alfonso effectively confined to Seville until his death.

Scholarship and law

Alfonso's scriptorium, drawing on Christian, Muslim and Jewish scholars, produced the Alfonsine Tables of planetary motion, the Libros del saber de astronomía, the histories Estoria de España and Grande e general estoria, and the Cantigas de Santa Maria, over 400 songs to the Virgin in Galician-Portuguese. His greatest legal monument, the Siete Partidas, a comprehensive code drawing on Roman law, became foundational for Spanish and Latin American law and remains cited in parts of the United States deriving law from Spain.

Legacy

Though his political projects largely failed, Alfonso's cultural achievement shaped the Castilian language and Iberian learning for centuries. The lunar crater Alphonsus is named after him.
