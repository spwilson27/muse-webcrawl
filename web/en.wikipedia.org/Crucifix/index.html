Crucifix

A crucifix (from Latin cruci fixus meaning "(one) fixed to a cross") is a cross with an image of Jesus on it, as distinct from a bare cross. The representation of Jesus himself on the cross is referred to in English as the corpus (Latin for "body"). The crucifix is a principal symbol for many groups of Christians, and one of the most common forms of the Crucifixion in the arts. It is used especially in the Catholic Church, Lutheranism, the Eastern Orthodox Church, and Oriental Orthodoxy.

A crucifix with the corpus of Christ.

History

Early Christians were reluctant to depict the Crucifixion directly; the crucifix with corpus became common only from the early Middle Ages. Byzantine and medieval western crucifixes evolved from triumphant depictions of Christ reigning from the cross (Christus triumphans) toward the suffering Christ (Christus patiens) of Gothic art. Above the corpus, crucifixes often bear the inscription INRI, from the Latin of the titulus on the cross, "Jesus of Nazareth, King of the Jews".

Use

Crucifixes are used in processions, on altars, in homes, and as jewellery; Catholic rosaries typically end with a crucifix. Processional and altar crucifixes are standard in Catholic, Orthodox, and many Lutheran and Anglican churches. During the Reformation, Lutheran churches retained crucifixes, while Reformed traditions generally preferred the empty cross; the contrast between crucifix and bare cross remains a visible marker of differing emphases — Christ's sacrifice versus his resurrection — though both symbols are used across many traditions.
