Stevens Inquiries

The Stevens Inquiries were a series of three investigations led by Sir John Stevens, Commissioner of the Metropolitan Police, into allegations of collusion between the security forces in Northern Ireland and loyalist paramilitaries during the Troubles. They were the largest criminal investigations in British history at the time.

Background

Allegations had long circulated that members of the Royal Ulster Constabulary, the British Army's Force Research Unit and intelligence agencies had passed information to loyalist groups such as the Ulster Defence Association (UDA), enabling them to target republicans and Catholic civilians. The murder of solicitor Pat Finucane in 1989, and the role of agent Brian Nelson, became central cases.

The three inquiries

Stevens 1 (1989–90) began after the killing of Loyalist Loughlin Maginn, whose murderers possessed security-force documents. Stevens' team's incident room was destroyed by fire in January 1990, in circumstances widely regarded as suspicious. The inquiry nonetheless led to dozens of convictions and confirmed that documents had leaked to loyalists.

Stevens 2 (1999) re-examined the Finucane case and Nelson's handling by military intelligence.

Stevens 3 (1999–2003) was the most far-reaching. Its summary report, published in April 2003, concluded that collusion had taken place: the murders of Pat Finucane and student Adam Lambert could have been prevented, and that members of the security forces had obstructed the inquiries, including through the fire at Stevens' offices.

Legacy

The inquiries led to numerous arrests and prosecutions, and a core recommendation that a public inquiry be held into the Finucane murder — a demand the Finucane family pursued for decades. Stevens later described obstruction of his work by elements within the state, and the findings remain central to debates about the legacy of the conflict.
