Linotype machine

The Linotype machine is a "line casting" machine used in printing, which casts an entire line of metal type at once from individually assembled letter matrices. Invented by the German-American inventor Ottmar Mergenthaler, it was first used commercially by the New-York Tribune in 1886 and marketed by the Mergenthaler Linotype Company.

Operation

An operator types copy on a 90-character keyboard; each keystroke releases a brass matrix (die) from a magazine, and the matrices assemble into a line with wedge-shaped spaces. The assembled line is justified automatically, then used as a mould into which molten type metal is injected, producing a solid slug — a "line o' type" — after which the matrices are mechanically returned to their channels for reuse.

Impact

Linotype composition was roughly six times faster than hand typesetting, dramatically cutting the cost of newspapers and books and transforming the printing industry for nearly a century. Thomas Edison reportedly called it the "eighth wonder of the world". From the 1960s onward it was displaced by phototypesetting and then by digital typesetting.
