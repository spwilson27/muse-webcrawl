Unified English Braille

Unified English Braille (UEB) is a single braille code for the English language encompassing literary text, mathematics, science notation and computing symbols. Developed from the 1990s by the International Council on English Braille (ICEB), it replaced the separate codes previously used in English-speaking countries — in particular English Braille (EBAE) for literature and the Nemeth Code for mathematics in the United States.

Development and adoption

Work on a unified code began in 1991, driven by inconsistencies between national codes and the desire that a braille reader meet the same symbols in a novel, a mathematics textbook and a computer manual. ICEB adopted UEB as its standard in 2004, and member countries followed at different speeds: Australia, New Zealand, South Africa, Nigeria and Canada adopted it in the mid-2000s; the United Kingdom completed adoption around 2011–2015; and the Braille Authority of North America voted in 2012 to adopt UEB in the United States, effective 4 January 2016, while retaining Nemeth Code as an option within a UEB framework.

Features

UEB keeps the familiar contracted-braille core of EBAE but removes nine contractions that conflicted with technical notation, adds explicit symbols for print characters such as the ampersand, asterisk, bullet and currency signs, and introduces typeform indicators for italics, bold and underline, as well as a consistent system for transcribing mathematics without switching codes. Its rules are codified in The Rules of Unified English Braille, published by ICEB.

Reception

Supporters emphasize one code to learn, easier back-translation by computer and consistency across countries. Some readers and teachers objected to the loss of familiar contractions and the lengthening of some texts, and the continued use of Nemeth in US mathematics education reflects an ongoing debate about technical notation.
