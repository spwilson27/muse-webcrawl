Concordance (publishing)

A concordance is an alphabetical list of the principal words used in a book or body of work, listing every instance of each word with its immediate context. Concordances have been compiled for works of special importance, such as the Bible, the Qur'an, and the works of Shakespeare.

History

The first Bible concordance was compiled in the 13th century under the direction of Hugh of Saint-Cher, with the help of some 500 Dominican friars; it covered the Latin Vulgate. Famous English concordances include Alexander Cruden's A Complete Concordance to the Holy Scriptures (1737) and James Strong's Exhaustive Concordance of the Bible (1890), which assigned the numbering system ("Strong's numbers") still used in Bible study software.

Modern use

In corpus linguistics, a concordance is a list of all occurrences of a word or phrase in a text or corpus, typically displayed in keyword-in-context (KWIC) format, with the search term centred and a fixed width of surrounding context on either side. Computer-generated concordances are fundamental tools for lexicography and language teaching.
