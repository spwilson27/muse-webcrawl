Ransomware

Ransomware is a type of malicious software (malware) that threatens to publish a victim's personal data or permanently block access to it unless a ransom is paid. Modern crypto-ransomware encrypts the victim's files with strong cryptography and demands payment — usually in cryptocurrency such as Bitcoin — in exchange for the decryption key. Some variants merely lock the device's screen.

History

The first documented ransomware, the AIDS Trojan (PC Cyborg), appeared in 1989; its author, Joseph Popp, demanded payment by postal mail. Ransomware grew sharply in the 2010s with anonymous payment systems. Notable outbreaks include CryptoLocker (2013), and the 2017 WannaCry attack, which exploited the EternalBlue vulnerability to infect hundreds of thousands of computers in about 150 countries, severely affecting hospitals, railways and factories. NotPetya, also in 2017, masqueraded as ransomware but was designed to destroy data.

Business model

Since about 2019, many gangs have practised double extortion: data is stolen before encryption, and the criminals threaten to leak it even if the victim restores from backups. Ransomware as a service (RaaS) lets developers lease their malware to affiliates for a share of the profits. Targets have included hospitals, schools, pipelines and municipal governments, prompting governments to discourage or ban ransom payments.

Defences

Recommended protections include prompt patching, network segmentation, phishing-resistant authentication, endpoint detection, and maintaining offline or immutable backups that are regularly tested. Paying the ransom does not guarantee recovery and may fund further crime.
