London dispersion forces (LDF), also known as dispersion forces or instantaneous dipole–induced dipole forces, are a type of intermolecular force arising from the quantum mechanical fluctuations of electron density within atoms and molecules. They are the weakest intermolecular force but are present in all matter, including nonpolar substances. The German-American physicist Fritz London gave a quantum mechanical explanation in 1930.

Mechanism

At any instant, the electron cloud of an atom may be unevenly distributed, producing a temporary dipole. This dipole induces a corresponding dipole in a neighbouring atom, and the two attract each other. Averaged over time these fluctuating interactions yield a net attractive force that falls off rapidly with distance, varying roughly as the inverse sixth power of separation.

Importance

Dispersion forces are the only attraction between nonpolar molecules such as noble gases and hydrocarbons, explaining why they can condense into liquids. Their strength increases with molecular size and polarizability, which is why boiling points of the halogens and noble gases rise down their groups. They also contribute to phenomena such as gecko adhesion to smooth surfaces.
