Puebloans

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Puebloans, or Pueblo peoples, are Native American peoples in the Southwestern United States, in the states of New Mexico, Arizona and Texas, who speak languages in four different language families and who traditionally lived in compact permanent settlements known as pueblos. The term is principally applied to their modern descendants and to archaeological cultures of the region.

There are 19 federally recognised Pueblo tribes in New Mexico, one in Arizona (the Hopi) and one in Texas (Ysleta del Sur Pueblo). Their languages belong to the Tanoan, Keresan, Zuni and Hopi (Uto-Aztecan) families.

History

The Pueblo peoples descend from the Ancestral Puebloans, who built the great cliff dwellings and great houses of places such as Mesa Verde and Chaco Canyon between roughly AD 900 and 1350. Drought, social change and migration in the late thirteenth century led to the abandonment of many northern settlements and consolidation along the Rio Grande and in western pueblos such as Acoma, Zuni and the Hopi mesas.

Spanish colonisation from 1598 brought missionisation and forced labour, provoking the Pueblo Revolt of 1680 under the leadership of Popé, which expelled the Spanish for twelve years — the most successful Indigenous uprising against a European colonial power in North America. Spain reconquered the region in the 1690s, after which a modus vivendi developed. The Pueblos passed to Mexican and then, in 1848, to United States sovereignty.

Culture

Pueblo society is traditionally organised around clan systems, ceremonial societies and the kiva, a round ceremonial chamber. Agriculture centred on maize, beans and squash, raised with sophisticated dry-farming and irrigation techniques adapted to an arid environment. Pueblo pottery, weaving, jewellery and feast-day dances remain living traditions, and each pueblo is a sovereign community with its own government and ceremonial calendar.
