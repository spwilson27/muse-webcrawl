Geographical centre

The geographical centre (or geographic center) of a region is the point determined by some averaging method from the region's extent — informally, its "middle". Many countries, states, and continents have officially or popularly recognised geographical centres, often marked by monuments and promoted as tourist attractions.

Methods of determination

There is no single correct method. Common approaches include the centroid (centre of mass) of the region's area computed from its boundary on a map projection; the midpoint of the extremes of latitude and longitude (the centre of the smallest axis-aligned rectangle enclosing the region); and the point minimising the average distance to all other points in the region. Results differ according to whether outlying islands are included, how coastlines are resolved, and which projection is used — a sensitivity sometimes called the "geographical centre problem".

Examples

Well-known centres include the geographic centre of the contiguous United States near Lebanon, Kansas (determined by the U.S. Coast and Geodetic Survey in 1918 by balancing a cardboard cut-out of the country), the centre of North America, long promoted by Rugby, North Dakota, and the contested "centre of Europe", claimed by sites in Lithuania, Poland, Ukraine, Belarus, Slovakia, and elsewhere depending on the method and the boundaries used. Australia's centre is commonly placed at the Lambert Centre, south of Alice Springs.

Significance

Although of little scientific consequence, geographical centres hold cultural and promotional value, and disputes over their location reflect differing definitions of the regions themselves.
