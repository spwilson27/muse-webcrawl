Ancient Greek temple

An ancient Greek temple was a structure built to house a cult statue of a god or goddess within a Greek sanctuary. Temples were not places of congregational worship; sacrifices and rituals took place at an outdoor altar, while the temple served as the deity's dwelling and a treasury for votive offerings.

Architecture

The typical temple stood on a stepped platform (crepidoma) and consisted of a central room, the cella or naos, often preceded by a porch (pronaos). Around the cella ran a colonnade (peristyle). Greek temples were built in the architectural orders — Doric, Ionic, and later Corinthian — each with characteristic column capitals and entablature.

Development

Early temples of the 8th century BC were built of wood and mudbrick; by the 6th century BC they were being translated into stone. Famous examples include the Parthenon in Athens (447–432 BC), the Temple of Hephaestus, the Temple of Olympian Zeus, and the temples of Paestum in Italy and Agrigento in Sicily.

Decoration

Pediments, metopes, and friezes carried sculptural programmes depicting myths and battles, and were originally painted in vivid colours. Subtle refinements — slight curvature of horizontal lines and inward inclination of columns (entasis) — corrected optical illusions.
