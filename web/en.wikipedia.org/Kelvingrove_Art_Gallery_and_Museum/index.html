Kelvingrove Art Gallery and Museum is a museum and art gallery in Glasgow, Scotland, and one of the city's most popular visitor attractions. It reopened in July 2006 after a three-year refurbishment and houses one of Europe's great civic art collections, with 22 galleries displaying around 8,000 objects.

Building

The building, constructed in red sandstone in a Spanish Baroque style, was opened in 1901 as the centrepiece of the Glasgow International Exhibition in Kelvingrove Park. A popular urban myth claims the building was erected back-to-front — with its grand entrance intended to face the park — and that the architect leapt from a tower in despair; in fact the main entrance was always intended to face Argyle Street.

Collections

The collections include natural history specimens (famously Sir Roger the elephant), arms and armour, and art by the Old Masters, French Impressionists, the Dutch Renaissance, the Scottish Colourists and the Glasgow Boys. Highlights include Salvador Dalí's Christ of Saint John of the Cross and works by Rembrandt, Monet, Van Gogh and Picasso. The centre hall features a concert pipe organ used for daily recitals, and displays a suspended Spitfire over the west court.
