Arminius (18/17 BC – AD 21) was a chieftain of the Germanic Cherusci tribe who commanded an alliance of Germanic tribes at the Battle of the Teutoburg Forest in AD 9, in which three Roman legions under Publius Quinctilius Varus were destroyed. The defeat halted Rome's expansion into Germania east of the Rhine and is regarded as one of the most consequential battles in European history.

Early life and Roman service

Arminius, whose Germanic name is unknown (the form "Hermann" dates only to the Reformation era), was the son of the Cheruscan chief Segimer. Taken to Rome as a youth, he gained Roman citizenship and equestrian rank, served with distinction as an officer of auxiliary troops, and learned Roman tactics and Latin — knowledge he turned against Rome when he returned to Germania as a trusted adviser to Varus.

Teutoburg Forest and aftermath

While outwardly loyal, Arminius secretly organised a coalition of the Cherusci, Marsi, Chatti, Bructeri and others. In September AD 9 he lured Varus's army — the Seventeenth, Eighteenth and Nineteenth legions, some 20,000 men — into an ambush in the Teutoburg Forest during stormy weather over several days of fighting; Varus killed himself and the legions were annihilated. Rome recovered the lost eagles only decades later, and although Germanicus campaigned in Germania in AD 14–16, inflicting defeats on Arminius at Idistaviso and the Angrivarian Wall, Emperor Tiberius chose to abandon the conquest. Arminius later warred against Maroboduus, king of the Marcomanni, and was murdered in AD 21 by members of his own family who feared his growing power.

Legacy

Tacitus called Arminius "unquestionably the liberator of Germania". Rediscovered with Tacitus's works in the Renaissance, he became a German national hero — Hermann — celebrated in literature and in the colossal Hermannsdenkmal statue near Detmold, completed in 1875.
