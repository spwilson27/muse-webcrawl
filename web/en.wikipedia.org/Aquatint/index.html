Aquatint

Aquatint
TypeIntaglio printmaking technique
DevelopedMid-18th century
Key practitionersJean-Baptiste Le Prince, Francisco Goya
EffectTonal areas resembling watercolour washes

Aquatint is an intaglio printmaking technique, a variant of etching, that produces areas of tone rather than lines. It is used to create prints with broad, even tonal effects similar to watercolour washes or ink washes, and is frequently combined with line etching on the same plate so that an artist can combine drawn lines with shaded areas.

Technique

In aquatint, powdered rosin is dusted onto a metal plate (traditionally copper or zinc) and heated so that the grains fuse to the surface. When the plate is immersed in acid, the acid bites tiny pits around each rosin grain. These pits hold ink and print as a fine, even tone. By "stopping out" areas with varnish between successive bitings, the printmaker builds a scale of tones from pale grey to near-black: the longer an area is bitten, the darker it prints.

History

Experiments with tonal intaglio processes date to the seventeenth century, but aquatint was perfected in the 1760s, notably by Jean-Baptiste Le Prince in France, and was widely used for reproducing watercolours and for topographical and satirical prints in Britain. Francisco Goya raised the technique to an expressive art form in series such as Los Caprichos (1799) and The Disasters of War, combining aquatint's deep tones with etched line.

Use

Aquatint remained a standard reproductive technique through the nineteenth century before photography displaced it commercially. It continues to be used by fine-art printmakers, and modern variants (such as sugar-lift aquatint, used by Picasso) expand its painterly possibilities.
