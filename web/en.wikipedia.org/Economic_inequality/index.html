Economic inequality

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Countries shaded by Gini coefficient, a common measure of income inequality.

Economic inequality is the difference found in various measures of economic well-being among individuals in a group, among groups in a population, or among countries. Economists generally study three metrics: wealth inequality, income inequality and consumption inequality. The topic has gained prominence since the 1970s, as inequality within many countries rose after a period of decline that followed the Second World War.

Measurement

The Gini coefficient, ranging from 0 (perfect equality) to 1 (maximal inequality), is the most widely used single measure. Other tools include the Lorenz curve, the Palma ratio comparing the richest 10% to the poorest 40%, and percentile ratios such as the income share of the top 1%.

Causes

Proposed causes of rising inequality include technological change that rewards skilled labour, globalisation and declining union membership, changes in tax policy, the growth of the financial sector, and — in Thomas Piketty's influential account — a rate of return on capital persistently exceeding the growth rate of the economy as a whole.

Effects and policy

Inequality has been linked in research to slower growth, poorer health and social outcomes, and political instability, though the strength and direction of these relationships remain debated. Policy responses include progressive taxation, minimum wages, education and transfers.
