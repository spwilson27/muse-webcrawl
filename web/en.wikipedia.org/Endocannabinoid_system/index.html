Endocannabinoid system

The endocannabinoid system (ECS) is a biological system of neurotransmitters and receptors found throughout the bodies of humans and other animals. It consists of endocannabinoids — endogenous lipid-based retrograde neurotransmitters — the cannabinoid receptors they bind to, and the enzymes that synthesize and degrade them. The system is involved in regulating a wide variety of physiological and cognitive processes, including appetite, pain sensation, mood, memory, fertility, and immune function.

Components

The two best-studied endocannabinoids are anandamide (N-arachidonoylethanolamine) and 2-arachidonoylglycerol (2-AG). The two principal receptors are:

  • CB1 receptors — located mainly in the brain and central nervous system, and responsible for the psychoactive effects of cannabis; they are among the most abundant G protein-coupled receptors in the brain.
  • CB2 receptors — located mainly in the peripheral nervous system and immune cells, where they modulate immune responses and inflammation.

Function

Endocannabinoids are unusual neurotransmitters: they are synthesized on demand from membrane lipids and travel backwards (retrograde) across synapses, binding presynaptic CB1 receptors to suppress further neurotransmitter release. This makes the ECS a fine-tuning mechanism for other signalling systems. Enzymes such as FAAH (which degrades anandamide) and MAGL (which degrades 2-AG) terminate the signal.

Clinical relevance

The system is the target of the plant cannabinoid THC, which mimics anandamide at CB1 receptors. The CB1-blocking drug rimonabant was briefly marketed for obesity before being withdrawn over psychiatric side effects, and drugs modulating endocannabinoid enzymes remain an active area of research for pain and neurological disorders.
