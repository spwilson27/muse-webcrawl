Khosrow I

Khosrow I (Middle Persian: Xusrō; 501–579), traditionally known by his epithet of Anushirvan ("the immortal soul"; Persian: انوشیروان, Anūšīrvān), was the Sasanian King of Kings of Iran from 531 to 579. He was the son and successor of Kavad I. Khosrow is remembered as one of the greatest Sasanian monarchs — a reformer of taxation and the army, a builder, a patron of learning, and the formidable rival of the Byzantine emperor Justinian I.

Reforms

Inheriting an empire shaken by the Mazdakite movement and noble factionalism, Khosrow fixed the land tax as a measured survey-based assessment payable in cash, replacing arbitrary levies; reformed the military by creating a salaried cavalry equipped by the crown and dividing the empire's defence among four regional commanders (spahbeds); curbed the great aristocratic houses; and codified law and court etiquette. His long reign is regarded as the high point of Sasanian administration and culture.

Wars with Byzantium

Khosrow signed the "Eternal Peace" with Justinian in 532, then invaded Syria in 540, sacking Antioch and deporting its population to a new city near Ctesiphon. Wars resumed over Lazica on the Black Sea until the Fifty-Year Peace of 562, by which Byzantium paid an annual subsidy. In the 570s his forces intervened in Yemen, expelling the Aksumites, and in 572 war with Byzantium broke out again in Armenia and Mesopotamia, continuing at his death.

Culture and legacy

Khosrow welcomed Greek philosophers after Justinian closed the Academy of Athens in 529, founded the Academy of Gondishapur's golden age of medicine and translation, and had the Sanskrit Panchatantra rendered into Middle Persian as Kalila wa Dimna by his physician Borzuya. Chess arrived from India at his court. The colossal audience hall of Taq Kasra at Ctesiphon, whose great arch still stands, is attributed to his reign. In Persian memory "Anushirvan the Just" became the model of the philosopher-king of justice; his counsels were preserved in later mirrors-for-princes literature.
