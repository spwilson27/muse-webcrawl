Cornwall

Cornwall (Cornish: Kernow) is a ceremonial county in South West England, comprising the Cornish peninsula and the Isles of Scilly. It is bordered by the Celtic Sea to the north and west, the English Channel to the south, and the county of Devon to the east, across the River Tamar. Its administrative centre and only city is Truro. Cornwall is known for its rugged coastline, mild oceanic climate, and distinctive cultural identity, rooted in its Celtic heritage and the Cornish language, which has been revived after dying out as a community language in the 18th century.

Geography and economy

Cornwall is the westernmost part of the South West Peninsula, exposed to the full force of the Atlantic. Its long coastline — around 700 km (430 mi) — alternates between dramatic cliffs and sandy beaches popular with tourists and surfers. Historically, tin and copper mining were central to the Cornish economy: the Cornwall and West Devon Mining Landscape is a UNESCO World Heritage Site, and engine houses dot the coast. China clay extraction, fishing, and agriculture also remain important, though tourism now dominates employment. Iconic attractions include the Eden Project, St Michael's Mount, Tintagel Castle, and Land's End.

Culture and identity

The Cornish are recognised as a national minority in the United Kingdom. The Cornish language (Kernewek), a Brittonic Celtic language closely related to Welsh and Breton, is taught in some schools and appears on many signs. Cornish symbols include the flag of Saint Piran — a white cross on a black field — and the chough, a red-billed crow. The Cornish pasty, protected by geographical indication status, and events such as Padstow's 'Obby 'Oss festival reflect a rich folk tradition. Many Cornish people regard Cornwall as a distinct nation or duchy — the Duchy of Cornwall, held by the Prince of Wales, owns much land in the county.
