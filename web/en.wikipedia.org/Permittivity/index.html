Permittivity

In electromagnetism, the absolute permittivity, often simply called permittivity and denoted by the Greek letter ε, is a measure of the electric polarisability of a dielectric material: it quantifies how much an electric field is reduced inside the medium compared with a vacuum, or equivalently how readily the material polarises in response to the field.

Definition

Permittivity relates the electric displacement field D to the electric field E by D = εE. The permittivity of vacuum, ε0 ≈ 8.854 × 10−12 F/m, is a fundamental physical constant (the vacuum permittivity). The dimensionless ratio ε/ε0 is the relative permittivity, historically called the dielectric constant; it is about 1 for air, roughly 2–4 for common plastics and paper, and about 80 for liquid water at room temperature.

Behaviour

In general, permittivity is a function of frequency: at high frequencies a dielectric's dipoles cannot reorient quickly enough, so the permittivity decreases and energy is dissipated as loss, represented by making ε complex. It can also depend on temperature, field strength (nonlinearity) and direction in anisotropic crystals. High-permittivity materials are used to increase capacitance in capacitors, while low-permittivity ("low-κ") materials are sought as insulators in integrated circuits to reduce cross-talk.
