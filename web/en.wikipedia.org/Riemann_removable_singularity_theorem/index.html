Riemann removable singularity theorem

The Riemann removable singularity theorem is a fundamental result in complex analysis named after the German mathematician Bernhard Riemann. It characterises isolated singularities of holomorphic functions that can be "removed" by redefining the function at a single point.

A function with a removable singularity can be extended holomorphically across the missing point.

Statement

Let D be an open subset of the complex plane, let a be a point of D, and let f be a holomorphic function on the punctured set D \ {a}. If there exists a neighbourhood of a on which f is bounded, then f can be extended to a holomorphic function on all of D: there is a holomorphic function g on D that agrees with f away from a. The isolated singularity at a is then called a removable singularity.

Proof sketch

One standard proof defines an auxiliary function h(z) = (z − a)2f(z) for z ≠ a and h(a) = 0. Boundedness of f near a implies h is differentiable at a with derivative zero, so h is holomorphic on all of D and has a double zero at a. Dividing by (z − a)2 then yields the desired holomorphic extension of f. An equivalent approach uses the Cauchy integral formula and estimates showing that all negative coefficients in the Laurent series of f about a must vanish.

Consequences

The theorem implies that a holomorphic function on a punctured neighbourhood which omits a suitable limiting behaviour cannot blow up near an isolated singularity unless it genuinely has a pole or an essential singularity there. Together with the Casorati–Weierstrass theorem and Picard's great theorem, it forms part of the classical classification of isolated singularities into removable singularities, poles, and essential singularities.

A classical application is the extension of the function sin(z)/z, initially undefined at the origin, to an entire function by assigning the value 1 at z = 0.
