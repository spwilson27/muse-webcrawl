Sword and sorcery

Sword and sorcery (S&S) is a subgenre of fantasy characterised by sword-wielding heroes engaged in exciting and violent adventures, usually involving supernatural elements, romance and magic. Unlike the "high fantasy" of epic world-threatening quests, sword and sorcery stories are typically personal in scale, driven by the hero's own interests — vengeance, treasure or survival — and set in exotic, often decadent worlds.

Origins

The genre originated in the pulp magazines of the 1920s and 1930s, above all Weird Tales. Its defining figure is Robert E. Howard, whose Conan the Barbarian stories, beginning in 1932, set the template; other pioneers included Clark Ashton Smith, C. L. Moore (with Jirel of Joiry) and Fritz Leiber, whose Fafhrd and the Gray Mouser tales gave the subgenre its name in 1961, when Leiber proposed "sword-and-sorcery" as a popular label for the field. Michael Moorcock's Elric stories inverted the archetype with a frail, brooding anti-hero.

Characteristics

Sword and sorcery features fast-paced action, morally ambiguous protagonists, ancient and often sinister magic, monstrous creatures, and settings modelled on the ancient or medieval world. Stories are usually short or episodic, and the stakes are personal rather than cosmic.

Influence

The subgenre influenced fantasy role-playing games such as Dungeons & Dragons, heavy metal music, video games and films, including the 1982 film Conan the Barbarian, which sparked a wave of sword-and-sorcery cinema in the 1980s.
