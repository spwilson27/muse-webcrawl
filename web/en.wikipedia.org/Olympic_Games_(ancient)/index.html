Ancient Olympic Games

Ancient Olympic Games
Site: Olympia, Elis, Greece
First recorded: 776 BC
Last held: 4th century AD
Prize: Olive wreath (kotinos)

The ancient Olympic Games (Greek: Ὀλυμπιακοὶ ἀγῶνες) were a series of athletic competitions among representatives of Greek city-states, held in honour of Zeus at the sanctuary of Olympia in Elis. Traditionally dated from 776 BC, when Koroibos of Elis won the stadion footrace, they were held every four years — the period known as an Olympiad, which the Greeks used as a unit of time reckoning.

History

For centuries the Games were a religious festival centred on the sanctuary, which housed Phidias's colossal gold-and-ivory statue of Zeus, one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World. A sacred truce (ekecheiria) allowed athletes and spectators to travel safely. The Games continued under Roman rule but were suppressed in the late 4th century AD, traditionally by the Christian emperor Theodosius I in AD 393 (or by Theodosius II's decrees in the 420s).

Events

The programme grew from the single stadion sprint (about 192 metres) to include the diaulos, the long-distance dolichos, wrestling, boxing, the pankration (a brutal mixed combat sport), the pentathlon (running, jumping, discus, javelin and wrestling), and equestrian events including four-horse chariot racing. Competitors were free Greek males who trained and competed nude; married women were barred even as spectators, though a separate festival, the Heraean Games, existed for women.

Prizes and fame

Victors received an olive wreath cut from the sacred tree at Olympia and immense honour at home: statues, odes by poets such as Pindar, civic privileges and sometimes free meals for life. Famous champions included the wrestler Milo of Croton and Leonidas of Rhodes, whose twelve victories stood as a record for over two millennia.
