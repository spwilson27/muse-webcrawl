The Wedding at Cana (Les Noces de Cana) is a monumental oil painting by the Italian Renaissance artist Paolo Veronese, representing the biblical story of the Marriage at Cana, at which Jesus miraculously converts water into red wine (John 2:1–11). Executed in 1563, it was painted for the refectory of the Benedictine monastery of San Giorgio Maggiore in Venice. At nearly 70 square metres, it is the largest painting in the Louvre's collection, where it hangs opposite the Mona Lisa.

Description

The canvas shows a lavish Venetian banquet in classical architectural surroundings, with some 130 figures. Christ sits serenely at the centre of the U-shaped wedding table, flanked by the Virgin Mary and his disciples; the bride and groom sit at the far left end. Guests are portrayed in contemporary 16th-century dress, and the crowd of onlookers is said to include portraits of Eleanor of Austria, Francis I of France, Suleiman the Magnificent, and the artists Titian, Tintoretto, and Veronese himself — traditionally identified among the musicians in the foreground. Servants carve meat, pour wine, and present dishes, while a servant in the right foreground draws wine from a cask and another tastes it from a glass — the moment the miracle is discovered.

History

Commissioned in 1562 by the Benedictine monks of San Giorgio Maggiore, the painting was completed in about fifteen months with the help of Veronese's brother Benedetto. In 1797 Napoleon's troops seized the canvas as war loot; it was cut in two for transport to Paris and reassembled at the Louvre. Unlike other looted works it was not returned after 1815. Restoration in the early 1990s was marked by controversy when restorers uncovered that a figure's mantle, long thought green, was originally a different tone. A full-scale facsimile produced by Factum Arte was installed in the original refectory on San Giorgio Maggiore in 2007.
