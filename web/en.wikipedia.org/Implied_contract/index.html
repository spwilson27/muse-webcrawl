Implied contract

Ordering work or accepting services can create a binding contract even when nothing is signed.
Area of lawContract law
SubtypesImplied-in-fact; implied-in-law
Based onConduct of the parties, or operation of law
Contrasts withExpress contract
Related doctrineUnjust enrichment, quantum meruit

An implied contract is a legally binding obligation that arises not from explicit words, spoken or written, but from the conduct of the parties or from the operation of law. Legal systems distinguish the implied-in-fact contract, inferred from behaviour showing a real meeting of minds, from the implied-in-law contract — better known as a quasi-contract — which the law imposes regardless of the parties' intentions to prevent unjust enrichment.

Implied-in-fact contracts

An implied-in-fact contract is a genuine contract; only the way it is proved differs from an express contract. When a customer sits in a barber's chair and accepts a haircut, or hands a watch to a jeweller for repair, the circumstances show an offer, acceptance and an understanding that payment will be made, even though no price was ever mentioned. Courts infer the terms from the parties' actions, the surrounding circumstances and any prior course of dealing. Because a real agreement exists, an implied-in-fact contract can in principle be displaced: clear evidence that the service was offered as a gift defeats it.

Implied-in-law contracts (quasi-contracts)

An implied-in-law "contract" is not a contract at all in the consensual sense, but an obligation imposed by law to do justice between the parties. If a doctor renders emergency aid to an unconscious stranger, the stranger may be liable for the reasonable value of the services (quantum meruit) although he agreed to nothing. The doctrine prevents one party from profiting at another's expense where retention of the benefit would be unjust. Unlike implied-in-fact contracts, quasi-contracts cannot be contradicted by proof that the recipient never intended to pay — liability follows from the circumstances, not from assent.

Implied terms

A related but distinct topic is the implication of terms into an express contract: terms implied in fact, to give the agreement business efficacy or because they are so obvious they "go without saying", and terms implied in law, read into whole classes of contracts — for example, in many jurisdictions, the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing, or statutory implied terms that goods sold are of satisfactory quality.
