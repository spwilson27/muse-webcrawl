First pass effect

First pass effect
FieldPharmacology, pharmacokinetics
Also calledFirst-pass metabolism, presystemic metabolism
SitePrimarily the liver; also gut wall and lung

The first pass effect (also known as first-pass metabolism or presystemic metabolism) is a phenomenon of drug metabolism whereby the concentration of a drug, specifically when administered orally, is greatly reduced before it reaches the systemic circulation. It is the fraction of drug lost during the process of absorption which is generally related to the liver and gut wall.

After a drug is swallowed, it is absorbed by the digestive system and enters the hepatic portal system. It is carried through the portal vein into the liver before it reaches the rest of the body. The liver metabolizes many drugs, sometimes to such an extent that only a small amount of active drug emerges from the liver to the rest of the circulatory system. This first pass through the liver thus greatly reduces the bioavailability of the drug.

Mechanism

The four primary systems that affect the first pass effect of a drug are the enzymes of the gastrointestinal lumen, gut wall enzymes, bacterial enzymes, and hepatic enzymes. Drugs that are extensively metabolized on first pass include propranolol, lidocaine, morphine, nitroglycerin and imipramine. In some cases the metabolites produced by hepatic enzymes are themselves active, or even more potent than the parent compound — a strategy exploited deliberately in prodrugs such as codeine, which is converted to morphine.

Alternative routes of administration

Alternative routes of administration, such as suppository, intravenous, intramuscular, inhalational aerosol, transdermal, or sublingual, avoid the first pass effect because they allow drugs to be absorbed directly into the systemic circulation. Sublingual administration of nitroglycerin is a classic clinical example: the drug diffuses through the oral mucosa directly into systemic veins and avoids immediate hepatic inactivation.

Factors affecting first pass metabolism

First pass metabolism can vary widely between individuals and is influenced by liver disease (such as cirrhosis, which reduces enzymatic capacity and creates portosystemic shunts), age, enzyme induction or inhibition by other drugs, and genetic polymorphisms of metabolic enzymes such as the cytochrome P450 family.
