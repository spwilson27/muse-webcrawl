Coal-seam fire

A coal-seam fire is a burning of an outcrop of coal or a coal seam underground. Such fires can be ignited by lightning, wildfires, spontaneous combustion, or human activity such as mining and refuse burning. Because they burn in an oxygen-poor environment, coal-seam fires can smoulder for decades or even centuries, advancing slowly along a seam, baking the rock above them, emitting smoke, subsiding the ground, and releasing large quantities of carbon dioxide and toxic gases. Thousands are estimated to be burning worldwide at any time, notably in China, India and the United States.


Gas vents over a coal-seam fire, where the heated ground cracks and smokes.

Notable fires

  • Centralia, Pennsylvania: a fire ignited in 1962 in a landfill above an anthracite seam spread through abandoned mine workings beneath the borough. Toxic gases and subsidence led to the demolition of the town and the buy-out and relocation of nearly all its residents; the fire is expected to burn for many more decades.
  • Burning Mountain (Mount Wingen), Australia: a naturally burning seam estimated to have been alight for about 6,000 years, advancing roughly a metre per year — the oldest known coal fire.
  • Jharia, India: dozens of fires, first recorded in 1916, burn in one of the world's densest coalfields, undermining homes, railways and the health of hundreds of thousands of people.
  • Wuda and other fields, China: fires across northern China are estimated to consume tens of millions of tonnes of coal annually and are major sources of greenhouse-gas emissions.

Causes and behaviour

Coal oxidises at ambient temperature; if the heat generated cannot dissipate — in a stockpile, a broken seam or abandoned workings — the coal can self-heat to ignition. Once a seam is burning, the fire creates its own chimney effect, drawing air through cracks and old workings. Extinguishing a large underground fire is extremely difficult: methods include excavating the burning seam, flooding, grouting and sealing vents, and injection of inert gas or fly-ash slurry, but re-ignition is common.

Impacts

Coal-seam fires waste resources, render land unusable, poison local air with carbon monoxide, sulfur compounds and mercury, and are a significant, difficult-to-quantify source of global CO2 emissions. They also create hazards of sudden ground collapse above burned-out voids.
