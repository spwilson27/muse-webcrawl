Glagolitic script

The Glagolitic script (ⰳⰾⰰⰳⱁⰾⰹⱌⰰ, glagolica) is the oldest known Slavic alphabet. It was created in the 9th century by the Byzantine monks Saints Cyril and Methodius to translate the Bible and other texts into Old Church Slavonic for their mission to Great Moravia in 863.

Glagolitic originally had around 40 letters, corresponding closely to the sounds of the Slavic language of the time. Its letterforms are distinctive and geometric, and their origin is debated — they appear to be largely original creations by Cyril, possibly drawing on Greek cursive and other scripts. The alphabet was soon supplemented and, in Bulgaria, gradually replaced by the Cyrillic script, which was developed by Cyril and Methodius's disciples and named in Cyril's honour.

Use in Croatia

Remarkably, Glagolitic survived for a thousand years in Croatia, where it was used in liturgical books written in Croatian Church Slavonic — Croatia was the only Catholic country permitted to celebrate the liturgy in a vernacular language and script. Two forms developed: the rounded type used in early manuscripts, and the angular Croatian Glagolitic used in inscriptions such as the Baška tablet (c. 1100), among the oldest Croatian written monuments, and in printed books from 1483 onward. Glagolitic remained in liturgical use in parts of coastal Croatia into the 20th century.

Legacy

The script is included in Unicode (block U+2C00–U+2C5F, with a supplement at U+1E000). It is a prominent symbol of Croatian cultural heritage, appearing on monuments, public signage in some regions, and commemorative coinage.
