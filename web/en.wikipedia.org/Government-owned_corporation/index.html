Government-owned corporation

A government-owned corporation, also called a state-owned enterprise (SOE) or government business enterprise, is a legal entity that undertakes commercial activities on behalf of an owner government. Their legal status varies from being a part of government to stock companies with the state as a regular or dominant stockholder.

Characteristics

There is no standard definition of a government-owned corporation, but such entities typically share several features: the state owns a majority stake or exercises effective control; the entity is established under company or public law with some operational autonomy; and it pursues a mix of commercial returns and public-policy objectives, such as providing public services, operating natural monopolies, or developing strategic industries.

Examples and scale

Government-owned corporations operate in sectors including energy, transport, banking, telecommunications, postal services, and defence. Notable examples include Saudi Aramco, the United States Postal Service, the Tennessee Valley Authority, Norway's Equinor, and China's large state-owned enterprises supervised by SASAC. State-owned enterprises account for a substantial share of global market capitalization and employment, particularly in emerging economies.

Debates

Economists debate the efficiency of state ownership. Supporters argue SOEs can correct market failures and deliver universal services; critics argue they suffer from soft budget constraints, political interference, and weaker incentives than private firms. Waves of privatization since the 1980s have reduced state ownership in many countries, though governments have also created new SOEs through sovereign wealth funds and industrial policy.
