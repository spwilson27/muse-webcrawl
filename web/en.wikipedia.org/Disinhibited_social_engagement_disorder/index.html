Disinhibited social engagement disorder

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Disinhibited social engagement disorder (DSED), or disinhibited attachment disorder, is an attachment disorder in which a child has little or no fear of unfamiliar adults and actively approaches and engages with them. It is classified in the DSM-5 as a trauma- and stressor-related disorder, arising as a consequence of severe early social neglect and deprivation, such as growing up in institutions with high child-to-caregiver ratios or repeated changes of primary caregivers.

Signs and symptoms

Children with DSED show reduced or absent reticence in interacting with unfamiliar adults, overly familiar verbal and physical behaviour inconsistent with culturally sanctioned boundaries, diminished checking back with an adult caregiver in unfamiliar settings, and a willingness to go off with an unfamiliar adult with little or no hesitation. The behaviour is not limited to impulsivity (as in attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder) but includes socially disinhibited behaviour.

Diagnosis and history

Before the DSM-5 (2013), DSED was considered a subtype of reactive attachment disorder (RAD), termed the "disinhibited type". DSM-5 separated the two conditions: children with the inhibited, emotionally withdrawn pattern retain the diagnosis of RAD, while the socially disinhibited pattern became DSED. Diagnosis requires evidence that the child experienced extremes of insufficient care thought to be responsible for the disturbance, and the disorder is considered rare outside of severely deprived populations, such as children raised in orphanages.

Treatment

Treatment centres on providing the child with a stable, emotionally available caregiving environment. Interventions aim to strengthen the child's attachment to a consistent caregiver and to teach appropriate social boundaries; symptoms often improve once adequate caregiving is established, although signs may persist in some children into adolescence.
