G cells or gastrin cells are a type of cell in the stomach and duodenum that secretes the peptide hormone gastrin. They are found mainly in the pyloric antrum, with smaller populations in the gastric fundus and, to a lesser extent, the duodenum and pancreas. Gastrin released from G cells travels through the bloodstream to stimulate parietal cells to secrete hydrochloric acid and chief cells to secrete pepsinogen.

Function

G cells are neuroendocrine cells that sense the chemical contents of the gastric lumen. They are stimulated by peptides and amino acids in the stomach, by distension of the antrum, and by the vagus nerve via gastrin-releasing peptide (GRP). Their open-type morphology, with an apical surface reaching the lumen, allows them to sample luminal contents directly. Gastrin acts on histamine-producing enterochromaffin-like cells and directly on parietal cells to drive the gastric phase of acid secretion.

Regulation and inhibition

G cell secretion is inhibited when the pH of gastric contents falls below about 3, largely through somatostatin released from neighbouring D cells — an important negative-feedback control on acid production. The hormone secretin, released from the duodenum, also suppresses gastrin secretion. Chronic hypochlorhydria, for example from long-term proton-pump inhibitor use or atrophic gastritis, removes this feedback and causes hypergastrinaemia and G cell hyperplasia.

Clinical significance

Tumours of G cells, called gastrinomas, secrete excessive gastrin and cause Zollinger–Ellison syndrome, characterised by severe recurrent peptic ulcers. Gastrinomas occur in the pancreas and duodenum and may be part of multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 (MEN 1).
