Vibrato

Vibrato is a musical effect consisting of a regular, pulsating change of pitch, used to add expression and warmth to vocal and instrumental music. It is produced by periodic variation of the fundamental frequency of a note, typically at a rate of about 5–7 cycles per second, with an extent of up to a quarter-tone or semitone in expressive singing.

Technique

String players produce vibrato by rocking the fingertip on the string, driven from the wrist or arm. Singers' vibrato arises from oscillation in the vocal mechanism and is generally considered a natural product of a free, well-supported voice rather than something deliberately manufactured. Wind players may use diaphragm, embouchure, or jaw movements; guitarists bend the string or use a vibrato arm ("whammy bar"); electronic keyboards and synthesisers apply pitch modulation, often controlled by a mod wheel. Vibrato should be distinguished from tremolo, which is a fluctuation in volume rather than pitch (though the terms are often confused, not least in amplifier marketing).

History and aesthetics

Vibrato was described as an ornament by early music writers and used selectively in Baroque performance; historical performance practice debates how continuous it was. In the 19th and early 20th centuries, continuous vibrato became the norm for string playing and operatic singing, popularised by figures such as violinist Fritz Kreisler, and it became a hallmark of jazz, blues, and rock singing and guitar playing. Preferences continue to vary: choral directors and early-music specialists often ask for a straighter tone, while in much popular music a pronounced vibrato is a signature of individual style.
