Polysynthetic language

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Polysynthetic words pack sentence-like information into a single word

A polysynthetic language is a type of synthetic language characterised by a very high number of morphemes per word, often combining a verb root with many affixes and incorporated elements. In such languages a single word can express what other languages require a whole sentence to say — including subject, object, tense, manner and incorporated nouns.

Characteristics

Polysynthetic languages are typically both highly agglutinative or fusional and feature noun incorporation, in which a noun stem is compounded with a verb to form a single word (e.g. the equivalent of "I-hunt-seals" as one word). They commonly mark multiple arguments on the verb (polypersonal agreement) and may have elaborate systems of affixes encoding direction, evidentiality, instrument and aspect. Because so much information is carried inside words, word order is often relatively free.

Distribution and examples

Classic examples include many Indigenous languages of the Americas — Inuktitut and other Inuit languages, Greenlandic, Mohawk and other Iroquoian languages, Nahuatl, and many languages of the Pacific Northwest and Amazonia — as well as the language isolate Basque in some analyses, Chukchi in Siberia, and Ainu in Japan. The Yupik word often cited in textbooks, tuntussuqatarniksaitengqiggtuq ("he had not yet said again that he was going to hunt reindeer"), illustrates the type.

Classification debate

"Polysynthesis" is one pole of the traditional morphological typology (isolating – agglutinative – fusional – polysynthetic) associated with Wilhelm von Humboldt and Edward Sapir. Modern linguists note that the category bundles several independent features, so definitions vary; some reserve the term for languages combining noun incorporation with polypersonal agreement. Many polysynthetic languages are endangered, as speaker communities shift to dominant languages.
