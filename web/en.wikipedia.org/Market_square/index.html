Market square

A market square (or market place) is a square meant for trading, in which a market is held. It is an important feature of many European and other towns worldwide, and historically was the centre of commercial and civic life. A market square is usually an open space surrounded by buildings and often features a market cross, town hall, church or other prominent civic structure.

History

Market squares developed in medieval Europe as towns obtained charters granting the right to hold a market. The square hosted stalls and booths on market days, and permanent shops and inns grew up around its edges. In planned towns of Central and Eastern Europe, such as those founded under Magdeburg law, the market square (Rynek in Polish, Ring in Silesian usage) formed the centre of a regular street grid. The Grand-Place in Brussels, the Rynek Główny in Kraków — one of the largest medieval squares in Europe — and the squares of many English market towns are notable examples.

Functions

Beyond trade, market squares served as sites for public proclamations, elections, punishments, festivals and fairs. Many retain regular markets, while others have become pedestrianised spaces for cafés, events and tourism. In North America the analogous space is often the town square or courthouse square.
