The American Mafia, commonly referred to in North America as the Mafia, Cosa Nostra or the Mob, is an Italian-American criminal organisation that developed from Sicilian Mafia traditions among Italian immigrants in the United States in the late 19th century. At its height it comprised some two dozen crime families across the country — most famously the Five Families of New York — coordinated loosely by a national Commission, and dominated much of American organised crime.

Little Italy in New York, an early centre of Italian-American organised crime.

Origins and Prohibition

Italian-American gangs grew in immigrant neighbourhoods from the late 1800s, engaging in extortion rackets such as the Black Hand. Prohibition (1920–1933) made the Mafia rich and powerful: bootlegging liquor generated enormous profits and financed corruption of police and politicians. Gang wars culminated in the Castellammarese War (1930–1931), after which Charles "Lucky" Luciano reorganised the underworld, abolishing the old "boss of bosses" title and creating the Commission to arbitrate disputes. The modern structure — boss, underboss, consigliere, caporegimes and soldiers — dates from this settlement.

Post-war power

After the Second World War the families extended into Las Vegas casinos, labour unions, the garment and construction industries, and narcotics. Public awareness grew through the televised Kefauver hearings (1950–51), the 1957 Apalachin meeting raid, and the testimony of informant Joseph Valachi in 1963, which confirmed the organisation's structure and the name "La Cosa Nostra". The Mafia Commission Trial and other prosecutions followed.

Decline

From the 1970s, the Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations (RICO) Act allowed prosecutors to dismantle entire families, while informants — culminating in underboss Sammy Gravano's testimony against John Gotti in 1992 — broke the code of silence (omertà). Competition from other criminal groups, demographic change in Italian-American communities and relentless federal enforcement reduced the Mafia to a shadow of its former power, though families in New York, Chicago, Philadelphia and elsewhere remain active on a smaller scale.

Structure and culture

Membership was restricted to men of Italian descent, inducted through a secret initiation ritual and bound by rules of loyalty and silence. The organisation's blend of violence, kinship and ritual has made it an enduring subject of American popular culture, from The Godfather to The Sopranos.
