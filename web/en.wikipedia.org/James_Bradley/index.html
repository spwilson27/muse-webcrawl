James Bradley FRS (1693 – 13 July 1762) was an English astronomer who served as the third Astronomer Royal from 1742. He is best known for two fundamental discoveries in observational astronomy: the aberration of light (1728), which provided the first direct observational proof of the Earth's motion around the Sun, and the nutation of the Earth's axis (1748).

James Bradley (1693–1762), Astronomer Royal

Early life

Bradley was born at Sherborne in Gloucestershire and educated at Balliol College, Oxford, where he took holy orders. His interest in astronomy was encouraged by his uncle, the Reverend James Pound, a noted observer. Bradley was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1718 and became Savilian Professor of Astronomy at Oxford in 1721.

Aberration of light

Working with Samuel Molyneux at Kew, Bradley attempted to measure the parallax of the star Gamma Draconis. He found instead an unexpected annual displacement of about 20 arcseconds, in the wrong direction for parallax. Bradley realised the effect arose from combining the finite speed of light with the Earth's orbital velocity: the telescope must be tilted slightly forward, just as an umbrella is tilted into the rain. Announced in 1728, the aberration of light confirmed the Copernican picture of a moving Earth and yielded an estimate of the speed of light close to the modern value.

Nutation

Continued observations over a full 18.6-year cycle of the Moon's nodes allowed Bradley to detect nutation, a small nodding of the Earth's axis caused by the Moon's gravitational pull on the Earth's equatorial bulge. He announced the discovery in 1748 and received the Copley Medal of the Royal Society.

Astronomer Royal

Succeeding Edmond Halley in 1742, Bradley modernised the Royal Observatory at Greenwich and made thousands of highly precise star observations. His catalogues, reduced after his death by Friedrich Bessel, remained foundational for stellar astronomy well into the nineteenth century.
