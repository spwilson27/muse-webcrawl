Ecology

Field: Biology

Key concepts: Ecosystem, biodiversity, niche, food web

Term coined: 1866, by Ernst Haeckel

Ecology (from Ancient Greek οἶκος oîkos 'house' and -λογία -logía 'study of') is the natural science of the relationships among living organisms, including humans, and their physical environment. Ecology considers organisms at the level of the individual, the population, the community, the ecosystem, and the biosphere. It overlaps with the closely related sciences of biogeography, evolutionary biology, genetics, ethology, and natural history.

Scope and scale

Ecology is a branch of biology, and it is not synonymous with environment, environmentalism, natural history, or environmental science. Among its central topics are the distribution and abundance of organisms, the interactions that determine them, energy flow and nutrient cycling through ecosystems, biodiversity, and the adaptations of organisms to their environments.

History

The term was coined in 1866 by the German scientist Ernst Haeckel. Early foundations were laid by naturalists such as Alexander von Humboldt, and the field matured in the twentieth century with the ecosystem concept introduced by Arthur Tansley in 1935, Eugene Odum's systems ecology, and the development of population and community ecology by figures such as G. Evelyn Hutchinson and Robert MacArthur.

Levels of organization

Ecological study proceeds from organisms and populations through communities — assemblages of interacting species — to ecosystems, landscapes, biomes, and the biosphere as a whole. Processes such as predation, competition, mutualism, succession, and disturbance shape these systems across spatial and temporal scales.

Applications

Applied ecology informs conservation biology, natural resource management, restoration ecology, agriculture, and responses to global change, including climate change and biodiversity loss.
