The Roman legion (Latin: legio, "levy") was the largest unit of the Roman army and the military instrument with which Rome built its empire. From citizen militias of the early Republic, the legion evolved — especially after the Marian reforms of the late 2nd century BC — into a professional, long-service heavy infantry force that endured for over a thousand years.

Organisation

In the Principate a legion numbered roughly 5,200 men: ten cohorts, each of six centuries of 80 men led by centurions, with the first cohort double-strength. It was commanded by a senatorial legatus legionis, seconded by tribunes and the camp prefect. Legionaries were Roman citizens signing on for 25 years; non-citizen auxiliary units of comparable size supplied cavalry, archers and light infantry, earning citizenship on discharge. The legion's sacred standard was the aquila, a golden eagle whose loss — as in the Teutoburg Forest disaster of AD 9 — was a catastrophe of honour.

Equipment and tactics

The legionary carried the curved rectangular shield (scutum), a short stabbing sword (gladius), a dagger (pugio) and two heavy javelins (pila) designed to bend on impact and encumber enemy shields. Battle tactics emphasised disciplined volleys of pila followed by close-order sword work, with reserves fed in by cohort. Equally important was engineering: legions built fortified marching camps nightly, along with roads, bridges and siege works — skills on display from the siege ramps of Alesia to the campaigns of the Roman conquest of Britain. Victories were celebrated with triumphs along the Via Sacra of the Roman Forum.

Later history

Under Diocletian and Constantine the army was reorganised into smaller, more numerous legions split between frontier (limitanei) and mobile field armies (comitatenses). In the West the legion system dissolved with the empire in the 5th century AD; in the East it evolved into the Byzantine military system.
