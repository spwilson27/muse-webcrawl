The Khmer Rouge (French for "Red Khmers") is the name popularly given to the followers of the Communist Party of Kampuchea and the regime through which they ruled Cambodia between 1975 and 1979. Led by Pol Pot, the movement seized Phnom Penh on 17 April 1975 after a civil war against the US-backed Khmer Republic of Lon Nol, and renamed the country Democratic Kampuchea.

Rule and genocide

Immediately upon taking power, the Khmer Rouge emptied the cities, abolished money, markets, private property, religion, and formal education, and forced the population into agricultural cooperatives. Intellectuals, professionals, ethnic minorities (Vietnamese, Cham Muslims, Chinese), and anyone suspected of disloyalty were persecuted. Mass executions, forced labour, starvation, and disease killed an estimated 1.5 to 2 million people — roughly a quarter of Cambodia's population — in what became known as the Cambodian genocide. The regime's most notorious sites include the S-21 prison at Tuol Sleng and the Killing Fields of Choeung Ek.

Fall

Border clashes with Vietnam escalated into a full invasion in December 1978; Phnom Penh fell on 7 January 1979. The Khmer Rouge retreated to the Thai border and waged a guerrilla war, retaining Cambodia's UN seat with Western and Chinese support. Pol Pot died in 1998, and the movement dissolved in 1999. A UN-backed tribunal, the Extraordinary Chambers in the Courts of Cambodia, later convicted senior leaders including Kaing Guek Eav ("Duch"), Nuon Chea, and Khieu Samphan.
