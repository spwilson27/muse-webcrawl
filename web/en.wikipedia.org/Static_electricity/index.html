Static electricity

Static charge accumulated on a person can make hair repel and stand on end

Static electricity is an imbalance of electric charges within or on the surface of a material, which remains until it is able to move away by means of an electric current or electrical discharge. It is named in contrast with current electricity, delivered by wires. Static charge is most commonly created by the triboelectric effect: contact and separation of two materials transfers electrons between them, leaving one positively and the other negatively charged.

Causes and effects

Everyday examples include a balloon sticking to a wall after being rubbed on hair, clothes clinging after tumble-drying, and the small shock felt when touching a metal doorknob after walking across a carpet — a discharge of several thousand volts, though with tiny energy. Lightning is static discharge on a vast scale, between charged regions of clouds or between cloud and ground. Humidity reduces static build-up because surface moisture lets charges leak away.

Hazards and applications

Electrostatic discharge can damage sensitive electronic components, ignite flammable vapours in industry and at fuel pumps, and cause dust explosions in grain silos and coal mines; countermeasures include grounding, bonding, antistatic sprays, wrist straps, and humidity control. Useful applications include electrostatic precipitators for air pollution control, xerography (photocopying and laser printing, which use charged toner), powder coating, and electrostatic spraying of paint and crops.

History

The phenomenon was known to the ancient Greeks: Thales of Miletus noted around 600 BC that rubbed amber (Greek elektron) attracts light objects — the origin of the word "electricity". Systematic study began with William Gilbert's De Magnete (1600), and continued through Otto von Guericke's friction generator, Benjamin Franklin's experiments, and Coulomb's law of electrostatic force.
