Ukko

Ukko (Finnish: [ˈukːo]), also known as Äijä or Äijö, is the chief god in Finnish mythology, the god of the sky, weather, thunder, lightning and the harvest. His name derives from the Finnish word ukkonen, meaning a thunderstorm — literally "Ukko's (weather)" — and in modern Finnish ukko is also a respectful word for an old man or grandfather.

Ukko corresponds closely to the Estonian Taara (Tharapita) and shares traits with Baltic and Norse thunder gods such as Perkūnas, Perun and Thor, reflecting ancient contacts around the Baltic Sea.

Attributes and worship

Ukko's weapon was a hammer, axe or sword, with which he struck lightning. He was said to drive a chariot across the sky, the noise of which was thunder, and he was associated with the rowan tree and animals such as the white wagtail and the ladybird (called ukonlehmä, "Ukko's cow"). Snakes, too, were connected with him, and the appearance of a snake could be taken as an omen.

In the agricultural calendar, Ukko was honoured at a spring or early-summer festival known as Ukon juhla or Ukon vakat, at which a communal ale was brewed and drunk from birch-bark vessels to secure rain and a good harvest, and to promote fertility of crops and livestock. Sacred groves and stones were dedicated to him, and offerings of food and drink were left for him.

In the Kalevala and later tradition

In the Kalevala, Elias Lönnrot's 19th-century compilation of Finnish oral poetry, Ukko appears as the supreme god, addressed in charms and prayers, though the narrative centres on heroes such as Väinämöinen, Ilmarinen and Lemminkäinen. Much of what is known of pre-Christian Finnish religion comes from folklore, charms (loitsut), and lists of gods recorded by Mikael Agricola in the 16th century, in which Ukko heads the pantheon of Tavastia.

With Christianisation, many of Ukko's functions passed to God and the saints — in Finnish, God is sometimes called Ukko in old hymns and folk poetry — but his name survives in everyday language for thunder, in place names across Finland, and in modern Finnish neopaganism (Taarausko/Ukonusko), where he is again venerated.
