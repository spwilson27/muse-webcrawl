Patchwork


Patchwork quilt with assorted fabric squares

Patchwork or "pieced work" is a form of needlework that involves sewing together pieces of fabric into a larger design. The larger design is usually based on repeating patterns built up with fabric pieces of different colors, shapes, and sizes, which together form a geometric or pictorial composition.

Patchwork is most often associated with quilting, where patched layers are stitched together with a soft insulating material between them to form a blanket. The craft has ancient roots: patchwork techniques appear in Egyptian and Chinese textiles thousands of years old, and the practice became widespread in Europe and North America, where scarcity of fabric encouraged economical reuse of scraps.

History

Evidence of patchwork has been found in Egyptian tombs and in Chinese artifacts from the Northern and Southern dynasties period. In medieval Europe, patched textiles were used for clothing, banners, and bed coverings. In the United States, patchwork quilting flourished in the 18th and 19th centuries; settlers pieced together worn garments and feed sacks into quilts that were both practical and decorative. Signature quilts, friendship quilts, and commemorative designs became important folk art forms.

Techniques

Basic patchwork units include squares, rectangles, triangles, and other shapes cut to precise templates and joined by hand or machine with consistent seam allowances, traditionally a quarter inch. Common block patterns include the nine-patch, log cabin, flying geese, and Dresden plate. Modern quilters also use foundation paper piecing and improvisational methods.

Contemporary use

Today patchwork is practiced as a hobby and an art form worldwide, with guilds, exhibitions, and a large pattern and fabric industry. Studio art quilts use patchwork techniques to produce wall hangings and textile art far removed from their utilitarian origins.
