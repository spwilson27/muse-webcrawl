Stereoscopy

Principle: Binocular disparity
Invented: 1830s, Charles Wheatstone
Applications: Photography, cinema, virtual reality

Stereoscopy (also called stereoscopics or stereo imaging) is a technique for creating or enhancing the illusion of depth in an image by means of stereopsis for binocular vision. Two offset images, corresponding to the views of the left and right eyes, are presented separately to each eye, and the brain combines them into a perception of three-dimensional depth.

History

Charles Wheatstone demonstrated the principle in 1838 with a mirror stereoscope used with drawn images; David Brewster's lenticular stereoscope and the advent of photography made stereo views a mass entertainment of the Victorian era. Stereoscopic photography found scientific uses in astronomy and aerial surveying, and stereo cinema enjoyed waves of popularity with anaglyph and polarised 3D films in the 1950s and again in the 2000s.

Methods

Images can be separated for the two eyes by colour (anaglyph), polarised light, shutter glasses, lenticular screens, or head-mounted displays as used in virtual reality. Free-viewing techniques — parallel or cross-eyed viewing of side-by-side pairs — require no equipment. The technique relies on the horizontal disparity between the two viewpoints, separated by roughly the interocular distance of about 6.5 cm.
