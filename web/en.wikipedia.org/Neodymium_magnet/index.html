Neodymium magnet

A neodymium magnet (also known as an NdFeB, NIB, or Neo magnet) is a type of rare-earth magnet made from an alloy of neodymium, iron, and boron with the chemical formula Nd2Fe14B. Developed independently in 1984 by General Motors and Sumitomo Special Metals, it is the strongest type of permanent magnet commercially available, with a magnetic energy product far exceeding that of ferrite or alnico magnets.

Properties

The Nd2Fe14B compound has a tetragonal crystal structure with exceptionally high magnetocrystalline anisotropy, giving the magnet both high saturation magnetisation and high coercivity. Small neodymium magnets can exert powerful forces; a magnet a few centimetres across can lift more than a thousand times its own weight. The magnets are, however, brittle, prone to corrosion — they are usually plated with nickel, zinc, or epoxy — and lose strength at relatively modest temperatures, with maximum operating temperatures typically between 80 °C and 200 °C depending on grade. Their Curie temperature is about 310–400 °C.

Applications

Neodymium magnets have displaced other magnet types in countless applications: electric motors in cordless tools, hybrid and electric vehicles, and wind turbine generators; magnetic bearings and couplings; hard disk drives; headphones and loudspeakers; jewellery clasps and toys; and magnetic resonance imaging equipment. Their strength-to-size ratio has enabled the miniaturisation of many devices.

Safety

Large neodymium magnets pose handling hazards: two magnets snapping together can pinch or crush fingers and shatter into sharp fragments, and swallowed small magnets (from toys) can attract each other through intestinal walls, causing serious injury — a hazard that has led to product recalls and regulations in several countries.
