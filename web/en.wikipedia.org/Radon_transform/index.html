Radon transform

The Radon transform of an object shown as a sinogram.

In mathematics, the Radon transform is the integral transform introduced by Johann Radon in 1917, which takes a function f defined on the plane and gives, for each line, the integral of f over that line. The set of all such line integrals, as a function of line angle and offset, is called a sinogram when displayed as an image.

Inverse transform and tomography

Radon also proved that a function can be reconstructed from its transform — the inverse Radon transform. This result is the mathematical foundation of computed tomography (CT): a scanner measures the attenuation of X-rays along many lines through the body, and algorithms such as filtered back-projection invert the transform to recover a cross-sectional image. Related inversion ideas are used in PET and SPECT imaging, electron microscopy, geophysics and non-destructive testing.

Properties

The Fourier slice theorem links the Radon transform to the Fourier transform: the one-dimensional Fourier transform of a projection at a given angle equals a central slice of the two-dimensional Fourier transform of the object. The Radon transform is closely related to the Hough transform used in image analysis for detecting lines.
