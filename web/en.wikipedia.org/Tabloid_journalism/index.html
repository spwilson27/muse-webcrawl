Tabloid journalism

Tabloid journalism is a style of journalism that emphasizes sensational crime stories, gossip about celebrities and sports figures, and lurid headlines. The term derives from the tabloid newspaper format—a smaller page size—but has come to describe a style of content, distinguished from the more sober reporting of broadsheet newspapers.

Tabloid newspapers are known for oversized headlines and an emphasis on sensation

Characteristics

Tabloid stories typically feature large, eye-catching headlines, extensive photography, short and simply written articles, and a focus on crime, scandal, sex, and celebrity. In Britain, the "red tops"—so called for their red mastheads—such as The Sun, the Daily Mirror, and the Daily Star exemplify the style. In the United States, supermarket tabloids such as the National Enquirer are noted for celebrity gossip and sensational or paranormal claims.

History

The roots of tabloid journalism lie in the "yellow journalism" of the late 19th century and the penny press before it, which prized mass circulation and human-interest stories. The modern tabloid format took hold in Britain with the Daily Mirror in the early 20th century and in the United States with papers such as the New York Daily News (founded 1919). Television and later online news adopted many tabloid techniques, blurring the line between tabloid and mainstream coverage.

Criticism

Tabloid journalism is frequently criticized for factual inaccuracy, invasion of privacy, and unethical newsgathering practices such as phone hacking, which in Britain led to the closure of the News of the World in 2011 and the Leveson Inquiry into press standards. Defenders argue that the best tabloids combine popular appeal with campaigning and investigative journalism.
