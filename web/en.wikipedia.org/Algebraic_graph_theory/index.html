Algebraic graph theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Algebraic graph theory is a branch of mathematics in which algebraic methods are applied to problems about graphs. It is traditionally divided into three main areas: the use of linear algebra (spectral graph theory), the use of group theory (graph symmetry), and the study of algebraic invariants of graphs.

Spectral graph theory

Spectral graph theory studies graphs through the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of matrices associated with them, principally the adjacency matrix and the graph Laplacian. The multiset of eigenvalues, called the graph's spectrum, encodes structural information: the largest eigenvalue relates to the average and maximum degree and to bipartite character, the second-largest eigenvalue governs expansion and the speed at which random walks mix, and the Laplacian spectrum counts spanning trees (Kirchhoff's matrix tree theorem). Spectral methods underlie expander graphs, which are central to computer science, coding theory and network design, and results such as the Alon–Boppana bound and Ramanujan graphs.

Graph symmetry

The group-theoretic branch studies the automorphism group of a graph — the permutations of its vertices that preserve adjacency. Highly symmetric families such as vertex-transitive and distance-transitive graphs are analysed with permutation group methods. Frucht's theorem shows that every finite group is the automorphism group of some graph, while the famous Petersen graph serves as a counterexample in many conjectures about symmetric graphs.

Graph invariants

The third branch studies polynomials and other algebraic invariants attached to a graph, notably the chromatic polynomial, which counts proper vertex colourings, and its generalisation the Tutte polynomial, which connects graph theory with statistical mechanics and knot theory.
