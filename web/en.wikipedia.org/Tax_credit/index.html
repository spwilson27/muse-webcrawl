Tax credit

A tax credit is a tax incentive that allows certain taxpayers to subtract the amount of the credit they have accrued from the total amount they owe the state. It may also be a credit granted in recognition of taxes already paid, or a form of state support for particular behavior. Unlike a tax deduction, which reduces taxable income, a credit reduces the tax itself dollar for dollar, making it more valuable to low-bracket taxpayers than an equivalent deduction.

Refundable and non-refundable credits

A non-refundable credit can reduce tax liability to zero but not below. A refundable credit can exceed the tax owed, generating a payment to the taxpayer; prominent United States examples include the Earned Income Tax Credit and the refundable portion of the Child Tax Credit. Credits may also be carried forward or backward to other tax years in some systems.

Examples

Governments use credits to pursue policy goals: encouraging research and development, investment in renewable energy and energy-efficient property, hiring from targeted groups, providing relief for foreign taxes paid (the foreign tax credit), and supporting families through child and education credits such as the American Opportunity Tax Credit. In the United Kingdom, tax credits historically formed a major part of the working-age welfare system before being largely replaced by Universal Credit.
