Vocal cords

The vocal cords, also known as vocal folds, are two bands of muscle tissue in the larynx (voice box) that vibrate to produce the sound of the human voice. During normal breathing they sit open; when speaking or singing, they are brought together and vibrate as air from the lungs passes between them, a process called phonation.

Anatomy

Each vocal fold extends from the thyroid cartilage at the front of the larynx to the arytenoid cartilages at the back. The folds consist of the vocalis muscle covered by layers of lamina propria and epithelium. Above them lie the vestibular folds (false vocal cords), which play little role in normal voicing. The opening between the true vocal folds is called the glottis.

Function

Pitch is controlled by the tension and length of the folds, adjusted by the cricothyroid and other intrinsic laryngeal muscles; longer, thicker folds produce lower pitch. Disorders of the vocal folds include laryngitis, vocal fold nodules, polyps and paralysis, which can cause hoarseness or loss of voice.
