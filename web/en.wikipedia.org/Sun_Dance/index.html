Sun Dance

The Sun Dance is a ceremony practiced by a number of Indigenous peoples of the Great Plains, most famously the Lakota, Cheyenne, Arapaho, Blackfeet, and others. It is one of the most sacred rituals of Plains cultures, traditionally held in the late spring or early summer when the chokecherries ripen.

Ceremony

The ceremony centers on a sacred cottonwood tree, cut and erected at the center of a circular arbor. Participants dance facing the tree for several days, fasting from food and water, while giving thanks and offering prayers for the renewal of the earth and the well-being of the people. Among the Lakota the ceremony is called Wiwanyag Wachipi, "dance looking at the sun."

In some traditions, male dancers made flesh offerings, piercing the skin of the chest with skewers tied to the central pole, or dragging buffalo skulls. The rite is understood as a sacrifice and a means of seeking visions, healing, and renewal.

History

The U.S. and Canadian governments suppressed the Sun Dance during the assimilation era; the 1883 Religious Crimes Code in the United States effectively banned it, and it was outlawed under Canada's Indian Act from 1895. Practice continued in secret in some communities. Legal protection came with the American Indian Religious Freedom Act of 1978 in the United States and the lifting of the Canadian ban in 1951. Today the Sun Dance is again held openly by many nations, though most ceremonies are closed to non-Indigenous observers.
