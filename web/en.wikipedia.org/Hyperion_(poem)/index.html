Hyperion (poem)

Hyperion, a fragment, is an epic poem by the English Romantic poet John Keats. Written in blank verse modelled on Milton's Paradise Lost, it tells of the fall of the Titans and the rise of the Olympian gods, centring on the grief of the fallen sun-god Hyperion and the transformation of Apollo into the god of poetry. Keats began the poem in late 1818 but abandoned it in April 1819, dissatisfied with its Miltonic style.

The Fall of Hyperion

Keats reworked the material as The Fall of Hyperion: A Dream, a first-person vision in which the poet-narrator is questioned by the goddess Moneta about the difference between poets and dreamers. This second, more personal version was also left unfinished at Keats's death in 1821. Both versions were published posthumously in part; Hyperion appeared in the 1820 volume Lamia, Isabella, The Eve of St. Agnes, and Other Poems.

Themes and reception

The poem explores suffering, progress and the succession of beauty by a greater beauty: Oceanus counsels the Titans that "first in beauty shall be first in might." Critics regard the fragments as among Keats's greatest achievements despite being unfinished, notable for their majestic diction and the Apollo passage in which the young god "dies into life" through knowledge of sorrow.
