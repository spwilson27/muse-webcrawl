Simvastatin

Simvastatin, sold under the brand name Zocor among others, is a statin, a type of lipid-lowering medication. It is used along with a proper diet and exercise to decrease elevated lipid levels. It is also used to decrease the risk of heart problems in those at high risk. It is taken by mouth.

Common side effects include constipation, headaches, and nausea. Serious side effects may include muscle breakdown, liver problems, and increased blood sugar levels. A lower dose may be needed in people with kidney problems. There is evidence of harm to the unborn baby when taken during pregnancy and it should not be used by those who are breastfeeding. It is in the statin class of medications and works by decreasing the production of cholesterol by the liver.

Medical uses

Simvastatin is used to decrease elevated cholesterol and triglycerides, and to reduce the risk of heart attack, stroke, and angioplasty in those at high risk. It is prescribed for hypercholesterolemia, including familial hypercholesterolemia, and for cardiovascular disease prevention in people with diabetes or multiple risk factors.

Mechanism of action

Simvastatin is a prodrug: after oral administration it is hydrolysed to the active beta-hydroxyacid form, which is a potent inhibitor of HMG-CoA reductase, the enzyme that catalyses an early and rate-limiting step in cholesterol biosynthesis. Inhibition of cholesterol synthesis in the liver leads to increased expression of LDL receptors and increased clearance of LDL particles from the blood.

History

Simvastatin was developed by Merck from a closely related compound, lovastatin. It was patented in 1980 and came into medical use in 1992. It is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines and is available as a generic medication. In the United Kingdom it is available over the counter in low doses. Brand names include Zocor, and it is combined with ezetimibe in the product Vytorin.

Adverse effects

The most common adverse effects are headache, constipation, nausea and abdominal pain. More serious but rare effects include myopathy and rhabdomyolysis, particularly at higher doses or in combination with certain other medicines. In 2011 the US Food and Drug Administration restricted the use of the 80 mg dose because of the increased risk of muscle damage.
