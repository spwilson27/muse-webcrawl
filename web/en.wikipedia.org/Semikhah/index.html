Semikhah

Semikhah (Hebrew: סמיכה, "leaning [of the hands]"), also semicha, plural semakhot, is the traditional term for ordination of a rabbi in Judaism. The original semikhah was the formal "transmission" of authority from Moses through the generations. This form of semikhah ceased between 358 and 425 CE. Since then semikhah has continued in a less formal way. Throughout history there have been several attempts to revive the classical semikhah.

The word semikhah derives from the Hebrew root samekh, meaning "to rely on", "to support", or "to lay" (hands). The noun literally means "laying on" (of the hands).

Biblical origins

According to tradition, Moses ordained Joshua by laying his hands upon him, as described in Numbers 27 and Deuteronomy 34, and ordained the seventy elders. The elders in turn ordained their successors, forming a chain of transmission of judicial and teaching authority reaching the members of the Sanhedrin. Rabbis ordained with classical semikhah had the authority to decide cases involving fines and, in the Sanhedrin, capital cases.

Classical and later semikhah

The classical ordination could only be conferred in the Land of Israel by someone who had himself received it. The chain was broken in late antiquity amid Roman persecution. In 1538, Rabbi Jacob Berab of Safed attempted to renew semikhah, ordaining several scholars including Joseph Karo, but the attempt faltered amid opposition from Jerusalem's rabbinate and Berab's exile.

Modern ordination

Today, semikhah usually signifies a rabbinical school's certification that a graduate is qualified to serve as a rabbi, issue rulings on questions of halakha, and serve a congregation. Ordaining institutions (yeshivot and seminaries such as Hebrew Union College and the Jewish Theological Seminary) grant it after examinations in Talmud and codes of Jewish law. The requirements and curriculum differ among the Orthodox, Conservative, and Reform movements.
