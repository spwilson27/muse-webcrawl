Bible

The Bible is a collection of religious texts or scriptures, some of which are considered sacred in Christianity, Judaism, Samaritanism, Islam, the Bahá'í Faith, and other Abrahamic religions. The Bible is an anthology — a compilation of texts originally written in Hebrew (with some portions in Aramaic) and Greek — whose canons vary between traditions. The Christian Bible has two main parts: the Old Testament, which largely corresponds to the Hebrew Bible, and the New Testament, written in Koine Greek in the first century CE.

Contents

The Hebrew Bible (Tanakh) contains 24 books in three divisions: the Torah (Law), Nevi'im (Prophets), and Ketuvim (Writings). The Protestant Old Testament has the same content arranged as 39 books; Catholic and Orthodox canons include additional deuterocanonical books such as Tobit and Maccabees, giving 46 books. The New Testament's 27 books comprise the four Gospels, the Acts of the Apostles, the epistles (many attributed to Paul), and the Book of Revelation.

Development and transmission

The texts were composed over roughly a millennium, from about the 12th century BCE to the 2nd century CE, and were transmitted by scribes; the oldest substantial manuscripts include the Dead Sea Scrolls (3rd century BCE onwards). The Greek Septuagint translation was produced in Alexandria from the 3rd century BCE. Jerome's Latin Vulgate became the standard Western text. Johannes Gutenberg's printed Bible (c. 1455) was the first major book printed with movable type in Europe. Influential English translations include the King James Version (1611).

Influence

The Bible has profoundly shaped Western law, literature, art, music, and language, and its narratives and ethics are shared — with differences — across the Abrahamic faiths. It remains the most translated book in the world, available in full in more than 700 languages.
