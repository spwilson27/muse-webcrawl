Book of Ezekiel

The Book of Ezekiel is the third of the Latter Prophets in the Tanakh (Hebrew Bible) and one of the major prophetic books of the Old Testament, following Isaiah and Jeremiah. It records the visions and oracles of the priest-prophet Ezekiel, who was exiled to Babylon with King Jehoiachin in 597 BCE and ministered among the exiles by the Kebar River (Chebar Canal) near Tel Abib from about 593 to 571 BCE.

Structure and contents

The book divides into three broad parts. Chapters 1–24 contain judgements against Judah and Jerusalem before the city's fall in 586 BCE, beginning with Ezekiel's inaugural vision of the divine chariot (the merkabah, with its four living creatures and wheels) in chapter 1, and including symbolic acts — lying on his side, shaving his head, and the departure of the glory of God from the Temple. Chapters 25–32 are oracles against the foreign nations, including Tyre, Egypt, and Ammon. Chapters 33–48, after Jerusalem's fall, turn to consolation and restoration: the watchman oracle, the shepherds of Israel, the vision of the valley of dry bones (chapter 37) symbolising national revival, the defeat of Gog of Magog (chapters 38–39), and a long closing vision (chapters 40–48) of a restored temple, priesthood, and divided land, ending with the city's new name: "The LORD is there" (YHWH Shammah).

Themes

Distinctive themes include individual moral responsibility ("the soul who sins shall die", chapter 18), the holiness and transcendence of God, the new heart and new spirit promised to Israel, and elaborate symbolic actions and visions that have made Ezekiel among the most vivid and strange of biblical books. His imagery influenced later apocalyptic literature, including the Book of Revelation's four living creatures and New Jerusalem, as well as Jewish merkabah mysticism.
