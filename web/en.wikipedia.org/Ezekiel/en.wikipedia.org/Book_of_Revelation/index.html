Book of Revelation

The Book of Revelation, also known as the Apocalypse of John, is the final book of the New Testament and therefore of the Christian Bible. Written in Koine Greek, its title derives from the first word of the text, apokalypsis (ἀποκάλυψις), meaning "unveiling" or "revelation". The author names himself John and says he was on the island of Patmos; tradition identified him with John the Apostle, though most modern scholars regard him as a distinct figure, John of Patmos. The book is usually dated to the reign of Domitian, around 95 CE.

Contents

After a prologue and letters to the seven churches of Asia Minor (chapters 2–3), John describes a vision of heaven: God on the throne and the Lamb (Christ) opening a scroll sealed with seven seals, releasing judgements and the Four Horsemen — conquest, war, famine, and death. Cycles of seven trumpets and seven bowls of wrath follow, populated by famous imagery: the woman and the dragon, the Beast from the sea and the number 666, the Whore of Babylon, Armageddon, the millennium and the binding of Satan, the Last Judgment, and finally a new heaven and new earth with the New Jerusalem descending "as a bride adorned for her husband".

Interpretation

Interpretive schools include preterism (the prophecies mainly concern the 1st century and Rome), historicism, futurism (the events lie in the end times), and idealism (the imagery symbolises the perennial struggle between good and evil). Revelation's canon status was disputed in the early church, especially in the East, and Martin Luther initially questioned it. Its imagery has saturated Western culture, from medieval art and the hymns of the church to modern fiction and the vocabulary of "Armageddon" and "apocalypse" itself.
