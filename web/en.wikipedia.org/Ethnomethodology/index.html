Ethnomethodology is an approach within sociology that studies the ways in which people make sense of their world, display this understanding to others, and produce the mutually understood order of social life. The term was coined by American sociologist Harold Garfinkel, whose 1967 book Studies in Ethnomethodology laid out the programme.

Core ideas

Where conventional sociology treats social order as an external fact that explains action, ethnomethodology asks how members themselves accomplish that order in everyday practices. Its central concepts include indexicality — the dependence of an expression's meaning on its context — and accountability, the idea that actions are designed to be observable and reportable. Members' methods are studied as ongoing practical achievements rather than as rule-following.

Breaching experiments

Garfinkel famously used "breaching experiments", in which students deliberately violated taken-for-granted expectations — for example by acting as a lodger in their own home or insisting on clarification of trivial remarks — to reveal the invisible background expectancies that sustain ordinary interaction.

Influence

Ethnomethodology gave rise to conversation analysis, developed by Harvey Sacks, Emanuel Schegloff and Gail Jefferson, and has influenced studies of work, science and technology, and human–computer interaction. It remains a distinctive, sometimes controversial, strand of social inquiry.
