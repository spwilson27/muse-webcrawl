Great Seal of Scotland

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Great Seal of Scotland is the state seal used to authenticate the most solemn documents of the Scottish Crown and, since devolution, of the Scottish Government. Like other great seals, it is used for instruments such as royal proclamations, commissions, and grants of land or office, and its custody has traditionally been a high office of state.

History

Scottish kings used a great seal from at least the reign of Edgar (1097–1107) onward; early seals show the king enthroned on one side and mounted in arms on the other, following European practice. Custody belonged to the Chancellor of Scotland and later to the Lord Chancellor. After the Treaty of Union in 1707, the Acts of Union provided for a Great Seal for Scotland distinct from that of Great Britain, used for Scottish affairs. A notable episode occurred in 1708 when Jacobite sympathisers stole or disposed of the old seal, forcing the striking of a new one. Until 1999 the seal was kept by the Secretary of State for Scotland.

Present use

Since the Scotland Act 1998, the Great Seal of Scotland is kept by the First Minister of Scotland ex officio, as Keeper of the Great Seal, and is administered by the Registers of Scotland. It seals documents including the letters patent appointing judges and the royal warrant for the signing of Acts of the Scottish Parliament, which are sealed after receiving royal assent. The seal's design bears the reigning sovereign's image and arms.
