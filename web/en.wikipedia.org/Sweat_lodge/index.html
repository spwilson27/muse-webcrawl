Sweat lodge

Image placeholder: a traditional sweat lodge structure.

A sweat lodge is a hut or structure built for a ceremonial sauna-like ritual of purification practised by some Indigenous peoples of North America. The ceremony, known among the Lakota as inípi ("to live again"), involves prayer, singing and intense heat generated by pouring water over heated stones inside a dark, enclosed lodge.

Structure and ceremony

The lodge is typically a low, dome-shaped frame of willow or other flexible saplings covered with blankets, tarps or historically animal hides, with a central pit for heated stones. Stones are heated in an external fire and carried in, and water is ladled over them to produce steam. A ceremony is led by a lodge leader and may consist of several rounds separated by opening the door flap.

Traditions

Sweat lodges are used by many Plains and other Native nations for spiritual purification, prayer and healing. Analogous heat-bathing traditions exist in other cultures, such as the Mesoamerican temazcal, the Finnish sauna and the Russian banya.

Safety and appropriation concerns

Improperly conducted sweat lodges run by untrained outsiders have caused deaths from heat stroke and asphyxiation, most notoriously in a 2009 incident near Sedona, Arizona. Many Native leaders object to the commercial appropriation of the ceremony by non-Native practitioners. See also sweat gland.
