Utility

Utility

In economics, utility is a measure of the satisfaction or benefit an agent gains from consuming a good or service. The concept was central to classical utilitarian philosophy and became a foundational tool of neoclassical economics, where consumers are modelled as choosing the bundle of goods that maximises their utility subject to a budget constraint.

History

Daniel Bernoulli introduced the idea of diminishing marginal utility of money in 1738 to resolve the St. Petersburg paradox. Jeremy Bentham and John Stuart Mill made utility the basis of the ethical theory of utilitarianism — "the greatest happiness of the greatest number". In the marginalist revolution of the 1870s, William Stanley Jevons, Carl Menger and Léon Walras placed marginal utility at the centre of value theory.

Cardinal and ordinal utility

Early economists treated utility as cardinally measurable, in units sometimes called "utils". Modern consumer theory instead uses ordinal utility: a utility function merely ranks bundles, and any monotonic transformation represents the same preferences. Indifference curves and the marginal rate of substitution express these preferences geometrically.

Expected utility

John von Neumann and Oskar Morgenstern's expected utility theorem (1944) axiomatised rational choice under uncertainty, showing that preferences satisfying certain axioms can be represented by maximising the expected value of a utility function. Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky's prospect theory (1979) documented systematic deviations from expected-utility behaviour.

Criticism

Critics note that utility is not directly observable, that interpersonal comparisons are problematic, and that real choices often violate the axioms of rational choice, as documented by behavioural economics.
