Joan of Arc (French: Jeanne d'Arc; c. 1412 – 30 May 1431), nicknamed "the Maid of Orléans," is a patron saint of France honoured as a heroine for her role in the siege of Orléans and the coronation of Charles VII during the Hundred Years' War. Claiming to be guided by visions of saints, she persuaded the Dauphin to let her lead his army, lifted the siege of Orléans in 1429 and escorted Charles to his coronation at Reims, turning the course of the war.

Early life

Joan was born to a peasant family at Domrémy in north-east France. From about the age of thirteen she experienced visions and voices of Saint Michael, Saint Catherine and Saint Margaret instructing her to drive the English from France and bring the Dauphin to Reims for coronation.

Military career

In 1429 Joan travelled through enemy territory to Chinon, where she identified the disguised Charles VII and convinced churchmen who examined her at Poitiers. Sent with a relief army to Orléans, she raised the morale of the French and the siege was lifted within days of her arrival, on 8 May 1429. A string of victories followed, culminating in the Battle of Patay, and on 17 July 1429 Charles was crowned in Reims Cathedral with Joan at his side. Her attempt to liberate Paris in September failed, and she was wounded.

Capture, trial and death

On 23 May 1430 Joan was captured by Burgundian forces at Compiègne and sold to the English. Put on trial for heresy in Rouen before a pro-English court under Bishop Pierre Cauchon, she was condemned — the charges centred on her visions and her wearing of men's clothing — and burned at the stake on 30 May 1431, aged about nineteen.

Rehabilitation and canonization

A posthumous retrial in 1456 annulled the verdict, declaring her innocent. Joan became a symbol of French national identity, was beatified in 1909 and canonized by Pope Benedict XV in 1920. Her life has inspired countless works of art, literature and film.
