Dagon

Dagon (Hebrew: דָּגוֹן, Dāgōn) was a god worshipped in ancient Syria and the Levant, attested from the third millennium BCE. He was a major deity at Ebla, Mari and Ugarit, associated with grain and agriculture, and father of the god Baal in Ugaritic tradition. Dagon is best known in popular culture as a god of the Philistines in the Hebrew Bible.

Origins and worship

The etymology of Dagon's name is debated: it is most plausibly related to the Semitic word dgn, "grain" or "corn", fitting his role as a grain and fertility god, though later Jewish and Christian commentators linked it to dag, "fish", leading to depictions of Dagon as a merman-like figure. Texts from Ebla (c. 2300 BCE) already name Dagon as chief god of Tuttul on the middle Euphrates. At Mari he had a major temple and gave oracles to King Zimri-Lim. In the Ugaritic Baal Cycle, Baal is repeatedly called "son of Dagon".

In the Hebrew Bible

The Bible presents Dagon as the national god of the Philistines, with temples at Gaza and Ashdod. In the Book of Judges, Samson destroys the temple of Dagon at Gaza, killing the assembled Philistine lords and himself. In 1 Samuel, the captured Ark of the Covenant is placed in Dagon's temple at Ashdod, where the statue is found fallen and broken before it. 1 Samuel also records that the Philistines offered sacrifices to Dagon after capturing Samson, and 1 Chronicles states that they fixed Saul's head in Dagon's temple after the battle of Mount Gilboa.

Later influence

Dagon appears in Milton's Paradise Lost among the fallen angels, and in modern popular culture most famously in H. P. Lovecraft's short story "Dagon" (1917), where the name attaches to a deep-sea fish-god of the Cthulhu Mythos.
