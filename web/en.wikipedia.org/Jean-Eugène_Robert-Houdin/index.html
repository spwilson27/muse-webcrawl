Jean-Eugène Robert-Houdin

Jean-Eugène Robert-Houdin (7 December 1805 – 13 June 1871) was a French watchmaker, magician and illusionist, widely considered the father of the modern style of conjuring. He transformed magic from a fairground entertainment into an elegant theatrical art performed in evening dress, and his name was later adopted (with an added "i") by the American magician Harry Houdini.

Early life

Robert-Houdin was born Jean-Eugène Robert in Blois, the son of a watchmaker. Trained in his father's trade, he developed a lifelong fascination with clockwork mechanisms and automata. A chance purchase of a two-volume work on conjuring, the Scientific Amusements, mistakenly delivered instead of a book on clockmaking, is said to have sparked his interest in magic. He took lessons from a local amateur conjurer and practised sleight of hand while continuing his horological career.

Career

After marrying Josèphe Cécile Houdin, he adopted the hyphenated name Robert-Houdin. In 1845 he opened a small theatre in Paris, the Théâtre Robert-Houdin, where his "Soirées fantastiques" presented illusions built on ingenious clockwork and early electrical apparatus. His signature effects included the Ethereal Suspension, in which his son appeared to float in mid-air after inhaling ether, and an automaton orange tree that blossomed and produced real fruit on stage.

In 1856, at the request of the French government, he travelled to Algeria to demonstrate that his "miracles" exceeded those of the marabouts whose influence France wished to counter, a celebrated episode in the history of stage magic.

Later life and legacy

Robert-Houdin retired to Saint-Gervais, near Blois, where he built a villa fitted with electrical gadgets of his own design. His memoirs, Confidences d'un prestidigitateur (1858), remain a classic of magic literature. He died in 1871. The young Erik Weisz later took his stage name "Houdini" in tribute to Robert-Houdin, though he subsequently wrote a critical book, The Unmasking of Robert-Houdin (1908).
