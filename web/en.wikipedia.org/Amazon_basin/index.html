Amazon basin

The Amazon basin is the part of South America drained by the Amazon River and its tributaries. With an area of approximately 7 million km² — about 35% of the South American continent — it is the largest drainage basin in the world. It spans Brazil, Peru, Colombia, Ecuador, Bolivia, Venezuela, Guyana and Suriname, and is bounded by the Andes to the west, the Guiana Highlands to the north and the Brazilian Highlands to the south.

Geography and climate

Most of the basin is a vast, low-lying sedimentary plain covered by the Amazon rainforest, the world's largest tropical forest. The climate is hot and humid, with rainfall exceeding 2,000 mm per year in many areas. Rivers are classified by water type: nutrient-poor blackwater (e.g. Rio Negro), sediment-rich whitewater (e.g. the Amazon main stem and Madeira) and clearwater rivers (e.g. Tapajós and Xingu). Seasonal flooding creates extensive várzea floodplain forests.

Biodiversity and threats

The basin harbours perhaps 10% of the world's known species — some 40,000 plant species, thousands of birds, mammals and fish — and vast carbon stores. Since the 1970s large areas have been cleared for cattle ranching, soy farming, mining and logging, and deforestation threatens regional rainfall recycling and the global climate. Indigenous peoples, whose territories cover much of the remaining forest, play a key role in its protection.
