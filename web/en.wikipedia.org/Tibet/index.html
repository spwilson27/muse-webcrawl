Tibet is a region in the western part of East Asia, covering much of the Tibetan Plateau, the highest region on Earth with an average elevation of about 4,000–5,000 metres. It is the homeland of the Tibetan people and is known for its distinctive form of Mahayana and Vajrayana Buddhism and its most famous landmark, the Potala Palace in Lhasa.

Geography

The plateau is ringed by the Himalayas to the south, the Karakoram to the west and the Kunlun to the north. It is the source of many of Asia's great rivers, including the Yarlung Tsangpo (Brahmaputra), the Yangtze, the Yellow River, the Mekong, the Salween and the Indus. Yaks, adapted to high altitude and cold, are central to pastoral life.

History

The Tibetan Empire (7th–9th centuries CE), founded under Songtsen Gampo, unified the plateau and contested Central Asia with Tang China; Buddhism was introduced from India and Nepal. After the empire's fragmentation, Mongol patronage elevated the Sakya school, and in 1642 the Fifth Dalai Lama established the Ganden Phodrang government with Mongol support. Tibet came under Qing influence in the 18th century. Following the fall of the Qing in 1911, Tibet functioned de facto independently until 1951, when the Seventeen Point Agreement incorporated it into the People's Republic of China. In 1959, after a failed uprising, the 14th Dalai Lama fled to India, where the Central Tibetan Administration remains in exile at Dharamshala.

Culture

Tibetan Buddhism, with its monastic universities, prayer wheels, prayer flags and pilgrimage circuits (kora), shapes daily life. Major festivals include Losar (New Year) and Saga Dawa. The political status of Tibet remains contested internationally.
