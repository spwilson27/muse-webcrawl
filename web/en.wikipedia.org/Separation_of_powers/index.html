Separation of powers

The separation of powers principle states that a state's government is divided into branches, each with separate, independent powers and responsibilities, so that the powers of one branch are not in conflict with those of the others. A typical division into three branches — sometimes called the trias politica model — is a legislature, an executive, and a judiciary.

History

Early notions appear in Aristotle's Politics and in the mixed constitution of the Roman Republic. John Locke, in his Second Treatise of Government (1689), distinguished legislative, executive and "federative" powers. The doctrine was most influentially formulated by Montesquieu in De l'esprit des lois (1748), who argued that liberty is protected when legislative, executive and judicial powers are held by different bodies.

The model in practice

In presidential systems such as the United States, the separation is strict: the legislature (Congress) makes laws, the president executes them, and independent courts interpret them, with each branch holding checks on the others — vetoes, overrides, impeachment, and judicial review. In parliamentary systems the executive is drawn from and accountable to the legislature, so separation is partial ("fusion of powers"), though judicial independence is usually preserved. Some constitutions add further branches, such as Taiwan's five-power constitution, or electoral and audit institutions in Latin America.

Criticism

Critics note that complete separation is neither possible nor always desirable: modern administration requires coordination, and strict separation can produce gridlock, while defenders reply that diffusing power remains the surest guard against tyranny.
