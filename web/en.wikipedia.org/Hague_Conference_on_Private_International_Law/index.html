The Hague Conference on Private International Law (HCCH) is an intergovernmental organisation seated in The Hague, Netherlands, whose purpose is "to work for the progressive unification of the rules of private international law". It develops multilateral treaties — the Hague Conventions — governing cross-border legal matters such as service of process, taking of evidence, child abduction, adoption, and the recognition of judgments.

History

The Conference was first convened in 1893 on the initiative of the Dutch jurist Tobias Asser, who later received the Nobel Peace Prize. Early sessions produced conventions on civil procedure and family law. After interruptions during the world wars, the seventh session in 1951 drafted a statute that entered into force in 1955, transforming the Conference into a permanent organisation with a Permanent Bureau in The Hague.

Major conventions

  • Apostille Convention (1961) — abolishes consular legalisation of public documents, replacing it with a single apostille certificate.
  • Service Convention (1965) — channels for serving judicial documents abroad.
  • Evidence Convention (1970) — procedures for taking evidence in another state.
  • Child Abduction Convention (1980) — prompt return of children wrongfully removed across borders.
  • Intercountry Adoption Convention (1993) — safeguards for international adoptions.
  • Judgments Convention (2019) — recognition and enforcement of foreign civil and commercial judgments.

Structure and work

Member states meet in plenary sessions, while the Permanent Bureau serves as secretariat, conducting comparative-law research and supporting the practical operation of the conventions through guides to good practice, judicial networks and the INCADAT database on child abduction case law. The HCCH works in English and French, and increasingly in Spanish.
