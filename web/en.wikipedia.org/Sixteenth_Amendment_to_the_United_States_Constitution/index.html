Sixteenth Amendment to the United States Constitution

The Sixteenth Amendment to the United States Constitution allows Congress to levy an income tax without apportioning it among the states on the basis of population. It was passed by Congress on July 2, 1909, and ratified by the requisite number of states on February 3, 1913.

The Congress shall have power to lay and collect taxes on incomes, from whatever source derived, without apportionment among the several States, and without regard to any census or enumeration.

Background

Before the twentieth century, federal revenue came mainly from tariffs and excise taxes. An income tax enacted during the Civil War was allowed to lapse, and the Wilson–Gorman Tariff Act of 1894 imposed a two percent tax on incomes over $4,000. In Pollock v. Farmers' Loan & Trust Co. (1895), the Supreme Court held that taxes on income from property were direct taxes that had to be apportioned among the states by population — a requirement that made a general income tax impractical.

Adoption and effect

The amendment was proposed as part of a political compromise during debate over the Payne–Aldrich Tariff Act. Ratification was completed in February 1913, Delaware, Wyoming, and New Mexico providing the decisive approvals. Congress promptly enacted the Revenue Act of 1913, imposing a one percent tax on personal income above $3,000 with surtaxes rising to seven percent. The Sixteenth Amendment underpins the modern federal income tax system, administered by the Internal Revenue Service.
