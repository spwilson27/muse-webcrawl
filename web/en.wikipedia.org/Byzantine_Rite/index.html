Byzantine Rite

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Byzantine Rite, also known as the Greek Rite or the Rite of Constantinople, is a liturgical tradition that originated in the city of Constantinople in the Byzantine Empire. It is used by the Eastern Orthodox Church and by the Eastern Catholic Churches that were formerly Orthodox, making it one of the most widely practised liturgical rites in Christianity.

Services

The centrepiece of the rite is the Divine Liturgy, celebrated in the forms attributed to St John Chrysostom (used on most days), St Basil the Great (used on certain fast days and Sundays in Lent), and the Liturgy of the Presanctified Gifts during Great Lent. Around it revolves a full daily cycle of services — Vespers, Compline, the Midnight Office, Matins and the Hours — especially developed in monasteries.

Features

The rite is noted for its rich hymnography (troparia, kontakia and canons), the use of icons and the iconostasis separating the sanctuary from the nave, chant without instrumental accompaniment, frequent use of incense, and an elaborate calendar of feasts and fasts governed by the Paschal cycle. Its liturgical languages include Greek, Church Slavonic, Arabic and many vernaculars.

Churches using the rite

Besides the Eastern Orthodox churches, the rite is used by Byzantine Catholic churches such as the Ukrainian Greek Catholic Church, the Melkite Greek Catholic Church and the Ruthenian Greek Catholic Church.
