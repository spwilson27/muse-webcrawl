Occipital bone

The occipital bone is a cranial dermal bone and the main bone of the occiput (back and lower part of the skull). It is trapezoidal in shape and curved on itself like a shallow dish. The occipital bone overlies the occipital lobes of the cerebrum and the cerebellum. At the lower part of the bone, there is a large oval aperture, the foramen magnum, through which the spinal cord passes into the vertebral canal.

The occipital bone forms the back of the cranium.

Structure

The occipital bone is divided into four parts arranged around the foramen magnum: the large, curved squamous part behind and above; two lateral parts (bearing the occipital condyles) on either side; and the basilar part in front, which joins the body of the sphenoid bone. Externally, the squamous part bears the external occipital protuberance (inion) and the superior and inferior nuchal lines, to which neck muscles attach. Internally, grooves cradle the transverse and sigmoid venous sinuses, and the internal occipital protuberance separates the cerebral and cerebellar fossae.

Articulations

The occipital bone articulates with the two parietal bones at the lambdoid suture, with the temporal bones at the occipitomastoid sutures, with the sphenoid at the spheno-occipital synchondrosis, and with the first cervical vertebra (atlas) at the atlanto-occipital joints, which permit nodding of the head.

Development

The bone develops from several ossification centres in both membrane and cartilage; in young children the four parts are separate and fuse during early childhood. The fusion of the basilar part with the sphenoid, completed in adolescence, is used in estimating age of skeletal remains.

Clinical significance

Fractures of the occipital bone can accompany basilar skull fractures. Congenital fusion anomalies at the craniocervical junction, and Chiari malformations involving herniation of the cerebellar tonsils through the foramen magnum, are clinically significant conditions involving this bone.
