Placentalia
Scientific classification: Kingdom Animalia; Phylum Chordata; Class Mammalia; Infraclass Placentalia
Fossil range: Late Cretaceous – present

Placentalia is one of the three surviving subdivisions of the class of animals originating with the common ancestor of the mammals, along with monotremes and marsupials. Placental mammals are distinguished from other mammals in that the fetus is carried in the uterus of its mother to a relatively late stage of development, nourished through an organ called the placenta. The name is something of a misnomer, since marsupials also nurture their fetuses with a placenta, though for a shorter period and with less involved connection.

Characteristics

Anatomical features distinguishing placentals include the absence of epipubic bones, a corpus callosum connecting the cerebral hemispheres, and a placenta formed from the chorion and allantois that permits prolonged gestation. The young are born at a comparatively advanced developmental stage, although the degree varies greatly: precocial species such as horses can stand shortly after birth, while altricial species such as mice are born blind and hairless.

Evolution

Molecular studies suggest the placental orders diverged during the Cretaceous period, roughly 90–100 million years ago, while the fossil record shows the group diversifying explosively after the Cretaceous–Palaeogene extinction event 66 million years ago that eliminated the non-avian dinosaurs. Living placentals are grouped into several superorders, including Euarchontoglires (which includes primates and rodents), Laurasiatheria (including bats, carnivorans, and ungulates), Xenarthra, and Afrotheria.

Diversity

With more than 5,000 described species in about 20 orders, placentals make up the great majority of living mammals, ranging in size from the bumblebee bat to the blue whale, the largest animal known to have existed.
