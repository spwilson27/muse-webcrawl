Mono language (California)

The Mono language is a Uto-Aztecan language of the Numic group spoken by the Mono people of California, in the western foothills and eastern slopes of the Sierra Nevada. It is closely related to Northern Paiute, and the two are sometimes considered dialects of a single language, though they are conventionally treated as distinct.

Mono
Native to: United States (California)
Region: Sierra Nevada
Native speakers: fewer than 40 (severely endangered)
Language family: Uto-Aztecan > Numic > Western Numic

Dialects

Mono is traditionally divided into two dialect groups: Western Mono (also called Monachi), spoken on the western slope of the Sierra Nevada near the headwaters of the San Joaquin and Kings rivers, and Eastern Mono (also called Owens Valley Paiute), spoken in the Owens Valley east of the Sierra crest. The Owens Valley variety shares many features with Northern Paiute and is often classified with it.

Phonology and grammar

Like other Numic languages, Mono has a system of final vowel devoicing, a set of grammaticalized instrumental prefixes used with verbs of handling and affecting objects, and a rich system of demonstratives distinguishing distance, visibility, and whether the referent is sitting, standing, or lying. Noun incorporation and compounding are productive.

Status

Mono is severely endangered. Most fluent speakers are elderly, and transmission to children largely ceased in the twentieth century under pressure from English-language schooling. Tribal communities in North Fork, Big Sandy Rancheria, Cold Springs Rancheria, and the Owens Valley have undertaken documentation and revitalization efforts, including language classes and master-apprentice programs.
