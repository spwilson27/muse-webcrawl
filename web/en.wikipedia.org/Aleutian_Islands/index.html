Aleutian Islands

The Aleutian Islands (Aleut: Tanam Unangaa, literally "land of the Aleuts") are a chain of 14 large volcanic islands and 55 smaller islands. Most of the chain is part of the U.S. state of Alaska; the far western Commander Islands belong to Russia. The islands form the Aleutian Arc in the Northern Pacific Ocean, stretching about 1,200 miles (1,900 km) westward from the Alaska Peninsula toward the Kamchatka Peninsula, and marking the boundary between the Bering Sea to the north and the Pacific Ocean to the south.

Geography

The islands are divided into groups: the Fox Islands, Islands of Four Mountains, Andreanof Islands, Rat Islands, Near Islands, and the Commander Islands. There are 57 volcanoes in the chain, part of the Pacific Ring of Fire. The climate is oceanic, with moderate and fairly uniform temperatures, abundant rainfall, fog, and strong winds; the treeless terrain is covered in grasses, heath, and tundra plants.

History

The islands have been inhabited by the Aleut (Unangax̂) people for thousands of years. Russian explorers and fur traders arrived from 1741, beginning a period of exploitation of sea otter pelts and severe population decline among the Aleuts. The islands passed to the United States with the Alaska Purchase of 1867. During the Second World War, the Battle of the Aleutian Islands (1942–43) saw Japanese forces occupy Attu and Kiska — the only battles fought on incorporated American soil — before being expelled by American and Canadian forces.

Economy

Fishing and fish processing dominate the economy, with Dutch Harbor on Unalaska Island among the largest fishing ports in the United States by volume. Much of the chain lies within the Alaska Maritime National Wildlife Refuge, and the islands are important breeding grounds for seabirds and marine mammals.
