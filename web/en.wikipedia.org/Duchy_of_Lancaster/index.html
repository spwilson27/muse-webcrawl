The Duchy of Lancaster is a private estate of the British sovereign, held in trust for future monarchs as a source of income independent of the Crown Estate. The sovereign is styled Duke of Lancaster — including queens regnant, who are also called "Duke" — and the estate is administered on their behalf by the Chancellor of the Duchy of Lancaster and a council.

History

The duchy's origins lie in lands granted to Edmund Crouchback, son of Henry III, from 1265, and in the county palatine created for Henry of Grosmont in 1351. It passed to John of Gaunt and, when his son became Henry IV in 1399, the duchy merged with the Crown but was kept legally separate from the other royal estates, a status it retains.

Holdings

The estate includes rural estates in Lancashire, Yorkshire, Cheshire, Staffordshire and Derbyshire — including the Foreshore and the Savoy Estate in London — together with commercial property and a financial investment portfolio. Its best-known assets include Lancaster Castle, held by the duchy and used as a prison until 2011 and now open as a visitor attraction.

Administration and finance

The duchy's net income is paid to the sovereign as part of the Privy Purse, funding official and private expenditure not met by the Sovereign Grant. In the County Palatine of Lancaster, the duchy retains certain legal rights, including the appointment of the High Sheriff and the administration of bona vacantia for charitable distribution.
