Mount Rainier, also known as Tahoma, is a large active stratovolcano in the Cascade Range of the Pacific Northwest, located about 95 km (59 mi) south-southeast of Seattle in Mount Rainier National Park, Washington. With a summit elevation of 4,392 m (14,410 ft), it is the highest mountain in Washington and the most topographically prominent peak in the contiguous United States.

Glaciers and hazards

Rainier is the most heavily glaciated peak in the contiguous United States, with about 26 named glaciers covering some 93 km² (36 sq mi). The volcano is considered one of the most dangerous in the world because of the huge volume of ice on its flanks: a major eruption or flank collapse could melt the ice and send lahars (volcanic mudflows) down river valleys toward the densely populated Puget Sound lowlands. The Osceola Mudflow, about 5,600 years ago, reached Puget Sound and covered some 550 km².

History and climbing

Indigenous peoples knew the mountain as Tahoma (Tacoma), "mother of waters". Captain George Vancouver named it in 1792 for Rear Admiral Peter Rainier. The first recorded ascent was in 1870 by Hazard Stevens and P. B. Van Trump. Today roughly 8,000–13,000 people attempt the summit each year, with about half reaching it, mainly via the Disappointment Cleaver and Emmons Glacier routes.
