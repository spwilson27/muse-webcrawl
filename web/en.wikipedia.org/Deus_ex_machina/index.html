Deus ex machina

Deus ex machina (Latin for "god from the machine") is a plot device in which a seemingly unsolvable problem in a story is abruptly resolved by an unexpected and unlikely occurrence. The term derives from the crane (mechane) used in ancient Greek theatre to lower actors playing gods onto the stage.

Origin

In classical Greek drama, especially the tragedies of Euripides, a god would often be lowered by crane at the end of the play to resolve the conflict and decree the characters' fates. The Latin phrase translates the Greek theos apo mechanes. Horace, in his Ars Poetica, advised against resorting to a god unless the knot of the plot truly deserved such a solution.

Modern use and criticism

Today the term is generally pejorative: critics use it when a resolution feels unearned — a sudden rescue, coincidence, or new power that the narrative has not set up. Writers such as Aristotle had already argued in the Poetics that a resolution should arise from the plot itself rather than from an external mechanism. Nevertheless, the device persists across literature, film, and games, sometimes used deliberately for comedy, satire, or divine intervention consistent with a work's themes.
