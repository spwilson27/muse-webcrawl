Galvanic skin response

The galvanic skin response (GSR), also known as electrodermal activity (EDA) and skin conductance response (SCR), is the phenomenon whereby the skin's ability to conduct electricity changes with its moisture level. Because sweat glands are controlled by the sympathetic nervous system, skin conductance is an indication of psychological or physiological arousal: if the sympathetic branch of the autonomic nervous system is highly aroused, sweat gland activity increases, which in turn increases skin conductance.

Measurement

EDA is measured by placing two electrodes, usually on the fingers or palm, and applying a small constant voltage; changes in conductance are recorded in microsiemens. The signal has a tonic component (skin conductance level, SCL), reflecting slow background changes, and a phasic component (skin conductance responses, SCRs), short-lived peaks following stimuli such as startling sounds, emotional images, or effortful decisions.

Applications

Electrodermal activity is used in psychological research on emotion, attention, and conditioning; as one channel in the polygraph; in biofeedback; and in usability and consumer research. It indicates arousal intensity but not emotional valence — fear, excitement, and cognitive effort can all raise conductance — and it cannot by itself demonstrate deception.

History

The effect was studied in the late 19th century by Charles Féré and Ivan Tarchanoff, and was widely adopted in 20th-century psychophysiology. The Hub-designed variable is also central to the "Iowa gambling task" literature on anticipatory skin conductance responses in decision-making.
