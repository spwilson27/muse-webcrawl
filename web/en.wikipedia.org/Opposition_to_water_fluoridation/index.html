Opposition to water fluoridation

Opposition to water fluoridation is opposition to the addition of fluoride to public drinking water supplies to reduce tooth decay. Although community water fluoridation began in the United States in 1945 and is endorsed by major public-health bodies, it has attracted persistent controversy, and many municipalities — especially outside North America, Australia and Ireland — have declined to adopt it or have discontinued it after public votes.

Grounds of opposition

Opponents raise several arguments: that fluoridation is mass medication without individual consent, infringing personal liberty; that fluoride exposure above optimal levels causes dental and skeletal fluorosis; that possible links to other health effects warrant precaution; that topical fluoride (as in toothpaste) delivers the benefit without ingestion; and that the practice disproportionately affects communities that cannot afford alternatives. Historically, opposition in the 1950s and 1960s sometimes overlapped with conspiracy theories — memorably satirised in the film Dr. Strangelove — though most contemporary campaigns rest on ethical and precautionary arguments.

Scientific and policy context

Health authorities including the WHO, the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and numerous national academies hold that fluoridation at recommended levels (about 0.7 mg/L in the US) is safe and effective, and reviews find the principal established adverse effect at those levels is mild dental fluorosis. Nonetheless, scientific debate continues over effect sizes in the era of fluoride toothpaste, and the balance of benefit and consent has remained a live political question, with referendums, court cases and legislative bans recurring in several countries.
