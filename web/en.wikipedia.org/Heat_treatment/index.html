Heat treatment

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Heat treatment is a group of industrial, thermal and metalworking processes used to alter the physical, and sometimes chemical, properties of a material. The most common application is metallurgical. Heat treatments are also used in the manufacture of many other materials, such as glass. The process involves heating or chilling, usually to extreme temperatures, to achieve a desired result such as hardening or softening.

Processes

Well-known techniques include annealing, case hardening, precipitation strengthening, tempering, carburising and quenching. Annealing heats a metal and lets it cool slowly, relieving internal stresses and improving ductility. Quenching cools a heated workpiece rapidly, typically in water, oil or air, to lock in a hard microstructure such as martensite in steel. Tempering then reheats the quenched metal to a lower temperature to reduce brittleness while retaining much of its hardness. Case hardening processes, such as carburising and nitriding, harden only the surface layer, leaving a tougher core.

Physical basis

The effects of heat treatment arise from changes in a material's microstructure: diffusion of atoms within the solid, phase transformations between crystal structures — for example between ferrite, austenite and martensite in steel — and the formation or dissolution of precipitates. Time-temperature-transformation (TTT) and continuous-cooling-transformation (CCT) diagrams are used to predict the outcome of a given thermal cycle.

Applications

Heat-treated components are found in gears, shafts, cutting tools, springs, bearings and structural parts across the automotive, aerospace and toolmaking industries. Glass is heat treated by tempering to increase strength and produce characteristic safe-fracture behaviour.
