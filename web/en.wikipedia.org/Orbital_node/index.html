Orbital node

An orbital node is one of the two points where an orbit crosses a plane of reference to which it is inclined. A non-inclined orbit has no nodes. The ascending node is where the object crosses the reference plane moving northward (in the direction of increasing coordinates), and the descending node is where it crosses moving southward. The line joining the two nodes — the line of nodes — is the intersection of the orbital plane with the reference plane.


Ascending and descending nodes

Reference planes

For orbits around Earth, the reference plane is usually the equatorial plane, and the position of the ascending node is measured by the longitude (right ascension) of the ascending node, one of the six Keplerian orbital elements. For heliocentric orbits the reference plane is the ecliptic. The Moon's nodes, where its orbit crosses the ecliptic, are traditionally called the dragon's head and dragon's tail, and eclipses can occur only when the Sun is near a node.

Nodal precession

Because central bodies are not perfect spheres, nodes do not stay fixed: Earth's oblateness causes the line of nodes of satellites to regress, an effect exploited by Sun-synchronous orbits, whose nodal precession of about one degree per day keeps the orbit plane at a constant angle to the Sun. The Moon's nodes precess with a period of about 18.6 years, producing the cycle of lunar standstills and influencing eclipse seasons and nutation.
