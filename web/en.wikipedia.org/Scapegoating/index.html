Scapegoating

ConceptAssigning blame for collective problems to one person or group
Origin of termBiblical ritual in Leviticus 16 (goat for Azazel)
FieldsPsychology, sociology, anthropology, political science
Key theoristsRené Girard; frustration–aggression theory

Scapegoating is the practice of singling out a person or group for unmerited blame and negative treatment, so that responsibility for problems, failures or misfortunes is displaced onto them and away from those actually responsible — or from unbearable circumstances themselves. The scapegoat typically bears punishment, exclusion or hostility while the community that blames them experiences relief and restored solidarity.

Origin of the term

The word derives from the ritual described in Leviticus 16 for the Israelite Day of Atonement (Yom Kippur). Two goats were presented: one was sacrificed, while over the second the high priest confessed all the sins of the people, symbolically laying them on the animal, which was then driven into the wilderness "for Azazel". William Tyndale rendered this in his 1530 English Bible as the "scapegoat" — the goat that escapes. Ancient Greece had a parallel custom, the pharmakos, in which a marginal person was expelled from the city to carry away pollution or avert disaster.

Psychological and social mechanisms

Psychologists explain scapegoating through displaced aggression: frustration–aggression theory holds that when the true source of frustration is too powerful, too vague or too abstract to attack — an economic collapse, an epidemic, a military defeat — the resulting hostility is redirected toward a weaker, safer target. Social identity processes reinforce this: blaming an out-group strengthens in-group cohesion and self-esteem. Targets are often visible minorities, immigrants, political opponents or subordinate individuals within an organisation. Scapegoating also serves individuals: leaders or family members may habitually blame one member (in family therapy, the "identified patient") for tensions generated by the whole system.

Girard's scapegoat theory

The French thinker René Girard placed scapegoating at the centre of his anthropology. In works such as Violence and the Sacred (1972) and The Scapegoat (1982), Girard argued that human communities threatened by mimetic rivalry and internal violence restore order through unanimous violence against an arbitrary victim — the "scapegoat mechanism" — which, because it must be believed to work, becomes hidden and mythologised, founding rituals and institutions. Girard read the Passion narratives as uniquely exposing the victim's innocence and thereby undermining the mechanism.

Historical examples

Scapegoating has repeatedly followed disasters. During the Black Death of 1348–51, Jewish communities across Europe were falsely accused of poisoning wells and massacred. Witch hunts blamed marginal, often elderly women for crop failures and disease. In modern politics, immigrants and minorities have been blamed for unemployment and crime, losing sides in wars have resorted to "stab-in-the-back" myths, and organisations under stress have singled out whistle-blowers or junior employees for collective failures.
