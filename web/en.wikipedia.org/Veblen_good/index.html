Veblen good

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A Veblen good is a type of luxury good for which demand increases as the price increases, in apparent contradiction of the law of demand. The phenomenon is named after economist Thorstein Veblen, who introduced the idea of conspicuous consumption in The Theory of the Leisure Class (1899).

Luxury goods can function as Veblen goods: their high price is part of their appeal.

Mechanism

Because a Veblen good is consumed partly as a status signal, a higher price makes it a more exclusive and desirable symbol, so quantity demanded rises with price over some range. Classic examples include luxury watches, designer handbags, fine wines, and sports cars. The effect is related to, but distinct from, Giffen goods, which are inferior staples whose demand rises with price through an income effect among the poor.

Demand curve

For a Veblen good, the demand curve slopes upward over the relevant price range: D(P) increases as P increases, until prices become so extreme that even status value cannot sustain demand.

Marketing implications

Brands of Veblen goods deliberately maintain high prices, restrict distribution, and avoid discounting, since lowering price can reduce perceived prestige and total revenue.
