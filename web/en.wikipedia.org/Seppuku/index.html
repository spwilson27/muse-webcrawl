Seppuku

Seppuku (切腹, "cutting [the] belly"), also called harakiri (腹切り), is a form of Japanese ritual suicide by disembowelment. It was originally reserved for samurai in their code of honour (bushido), practised voluntarily to die with honour rather than fall into the hands of enemies, as a form of capital punishment for samurai who had committed serious offences, or to atone for shame and failure.

The ritual

In its formalised version, the samurai bathed, dressed in a white kimono, and was served a last meal. The short blade (tantō or wakizashi) was placed before him on a lacquered tray. After composing a death poem, the samurai plunged the blade into his abdomen and drew it across. A trusted second, the kaishakunin, stood ready to decapitate him with a katana at the moment of the cut, sparing him a prolonged death and the loss of composure. The ceremony was performed before witnesses, often in the garden of a temple or daimyo's mansion.

History

The first recorded acts of seppuku date from the 12th century, including the death of Minamoto no Tametomo's retainer tradition and, canonically, Minamoto no Yorimasa in 1180. During the Edo period seppuku became a prescribed judicial punishment for samurai, as in the famous case of the forty-seven rōnin (1703), ordered to perform seppuku for avenging their lord — a story immortalised as Chūshingura. Seppuku by women of the samurai class, called jigai, was performed by cutting the throat rather than the abdomen.

Modern era

Judicial seppuku was abolished in 1873. The practice persisted anachronistically: General Nogi Maresuke performed junshi (following one's lord in death) on the death of the Emperor Meiji in 1912; Japanese officers and pilots chose suicide during the Second World War; and the writer Yukio Mishima performed seppuku in 1970 after a failed coup attempt, an act that shocked modern Japan. Today seppuku survives mainly as a cultural and literary symbol of honour, loyalty, and atonement.
