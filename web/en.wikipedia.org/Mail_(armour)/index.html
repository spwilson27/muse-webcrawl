Mail (armour)

Mail (also called chainmail) is a type of armour consisting of small metal rings linked together in a pattern to form a mesh. It was in use from at least the 3rd century BCE, possibly invented by the Celts, and remained a principal form of body armour in Europe and Asia for well over a thousand years.

Construction

Mail garments are made from thousands of individual rings, each interlinked with four neighbours in the common "European 4-in-1" weave. In high-quality armour the rings were riveted shut (or welded) to resist being pulled open by weapons; cheaper mail used butted rings whose ends merely touched. A full hauberk (a knee-length mail shirt) could contain 20,000–45,000 rings and weigh 10–25 kg, with the weight borne largely on the hips by a belt.

History and effectiveness

The Romans adopted mail as the lorica hamata. It was the dominant armour of early medieval Europe, worn by the knights depicted in the Bayeux Tapestry, and used across the Islamic world, Persia and India, where it was often combined with plates (mail and plate). Mail resists cutting blows very well and was usually worn over a padded garment (gambeson) to absorb impact. It declined as plate armour developed in the 14th–15th centuries, though it continued to protect joints and as a secondary defence. Mail is still used today in butchers' gloves and shark-resistant diving suits.
