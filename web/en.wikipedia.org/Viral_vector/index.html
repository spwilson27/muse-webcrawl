Viral vector

Viral vectors
Common typesRetrovirus, lentivirus, adenovirus, adeno-associated virus (AAV), vaccinia
UsesGene therapy, vaccines, basic research, oncolytic therapy
First developed1970s–1980s

Viral vectors are tools derived from viruses that have been engineered to deliver genetic material into cells. By replacing viral genes needed for replication with a gene of interest, scientists exploit the virus's natural ability to enter cells, using the vector in gene therapy, vaccine development and laboratory research.

Types

Retroviral and lentiviral vectors integrate their cargo into the host genome, giving long-term expression in dividing cells; lentiviruses can also transduce non-dividing cells and are used to make CAR T-cells. Adenoviral vectors deliver large payloads with high efficiency but provoke strong immune responses and do not integrate. Adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors persist as episomes in non-dividing cells and underpin approved gene therapies such as Luxturna and Zolgensma. Other platforms include herpes simplex virus, vaccinia and vesicular stomatitis virus vectors.

Applications

Viral vectors deliver corrective genes in gene therapy, present antigens in vaccines — adenovirus-vectored vaccines were widely used against Ebola and COVID-19 — and create transgenic model organisms and cell lines in research. Oncolytic viruses are engineered to infect and destroy cancer cells while stimulating anti-tumour immunity.

Safety considerations

Key concerns include insertional mutagenesis (implicated in leukaemia cases in an early gene-therapy trial), immunogenicity that limits repeat dosing, pre-existing immunity to the parent virus, and manufacturing scale. Modern vector design removes replication capacity and uses tissue-specific promoters to reduce these risks.
