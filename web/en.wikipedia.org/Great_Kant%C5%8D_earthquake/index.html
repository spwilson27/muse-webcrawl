The Great Kantō earthquake (Japanese: 関東大地震) struck the Kantō Plain on the Japanese island of Honshu at 11:58 JST on Saturday, 1 September 1923. The earthquake, with a moment magnitude of 7.9, devastated Tokyo, the port city of Yokohama, and the surrounding prefectures, and at the time it was the deadliest earthquake in Japanese history.

Damage

Shaking lasted between four and ten minutes and triggered widespread fires, since the quake struck near lunchtime when many people were cooking over open flames. Firestorms raged across Tokyo; in one notorious incident, tens of thousands of evacuees sheltering at an open space in Honjo were killed by a fire whirl. A tsunami with waves up to 12 metres struck the coast of Sagami Bay. Kamakura's Great Buddha statue was shifted by the shaking. The estimated death toll ranges from 105,000 to 142,000 people.

Aftermath

In the chaos following the quake, rumors spread that ethnic Koreans were poisoning wells and committing arson, leading to the Kantō Massacre, in which vigilante groups, police, and military killed thousands of Koreans, as well as Chinese residents and Japanese socialists. Reconstruction planning under Home Minister Gotō Shinpei reshaped Tokyo with wider roads and parks designed as fire refuges. Since 1960, 1 September has been observed in Japan as Disaster Prevention Day.
