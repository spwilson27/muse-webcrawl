Army of Flanders

The Army of Flanders (Spanish: Ejército de Flandes) was a multinational army in the service of the kings of Spain that was based in the Netherlands during the 16th to 18th centuries. It was notable for being one of the longest-serving standing armies of the early modern period and one of the first professional armies.

The army was created in 1567 when Fernando Álvarez de Toledo, 3rd Duke of Alba, marched from Italy along the route that became known as the Spanish Road with some 10,000 men to suppress the Dutch Revolt. It fought in the Eighty Years' War against the Dutch Republic and at various times against France and England as well.

Composition

The army's core was the Spanish tercio, a combined-arms infantry formation mixing pikemen, arquebusiers and later musketeers. Alongside Spaniards served Walloons, Germans, Italians, Burgundians, English and Irish soldiers. The Army of Flanders is often credited as the finest military force in Europe in the late 16th and early 17th centuries.

History

The Dutch Revolt

Under the Duke of Alba, the army imposed harsh repression, including the Council of Troubles. Alba's successors included Luis de Requesens and Don John of Austria. Alexander Farnese, Duke of Parma, achieved notable successes in the 1580s, recapturing much of the southern Netherlands. The army's finances were chronically strained, leading to repeated mutinies; the most infamous consequence was the Sack of Antwerp in 1576, the "Spanish Fury".

Decline

The army gradually declined in the 17th century as Spain's resources waned. The defeat at the Battle of Rocroi in 1643 symbolized the end of Spanish military dominance in Europe. The Army of Flanders was eventually disbanded in the early 18th century following the War of the Spanish Succession.
