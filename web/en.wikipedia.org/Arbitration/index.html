Arbitration

Arbitration
FieldAlternative dispute resolution
Decision-makerArbitrator or arbitral tribunal
OutcomeBinding (or non-binding) arbitral award
Key instrumentsNew York Convention (1958); UNCITRAL Model Law

Arbitration is a method of alternative dispute resolution in which parties to a dispute agree to submit their case to one or more neutral arbitrators, whose decision — the arbitral award — is usually legally binding and enforceable by courts. Unlike mediation, in which a neutral assists negotiation without deciding, arbitration is an adjudicative process that resolves the dispute.

Arbitration agreements

Arbitration normally rests on consent: commercial contracts frequently contain arbitration clauses committing future disputes to arbitration, while parties may also agree to arbitrate after a dispute has arisen. Under the doctrine of separability, the arbitration clause is treated as distinct from the main contract, and under kompetenz-kompetenz the tribunal may rule on its own jurisdiction. Courts in most legal systems will stay litigation brought in breach of a valid arbitration agreement.

Procedure

The parties have wide freedom to choose the seat of arbitration, the applicable rules (such as those of the ICC, LCIA, SIAC or ICDR), the number of arbitrators and the governing law. Hearings are private and usually less formal than court proceedings. Awards can be enforced internationally under the 1958 New York Convention, ratified by more than 170 states, though courts may refuse enforcement on narrow grounds such as lack of due process or public policy. The UNCITRAL Model Law provides a template adopted by many jurisdictions to modernise arbitration statutes.

Types and criticism

Arbitration is used in international commerce, construction, shipping, investment treaty disputes between investors and states (ISDS), labour relations and consumer and employment contracts. Critics argue that mandatory arbitration clauses in consumer and employment contracts can deprive individuals of access to courts and class actions, while supporters emphasise speed, confidentiality, neutrality and expertise.
