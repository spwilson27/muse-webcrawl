Historiography


The study of historical writing

Historiography is the study of the methods used by historians in developing history as an academic discipline. By extension, the term historiography is any body of historical work on a particular subject. The historiography of a specific topic covers how historians have studied that topic by using particular sources, techniques of analysis, and theoretical approaches. Scholars discuss historiography topically—such as that of the United Kingdom, of WWII, of the British Empire, of early Islam, and of China—and in terms of approaches, such as political history or social history.

Development

Herodotus and Thucydides founded history-writing in the Greek world in the 5th century BC, while China developed a continuous official historiography from Sima Qian's Records of the Grand Historian. Medieval chronicles, Islamic historical scholarship such as Ibn Khaldun's Muqaddimah (1377)—an early philosophy of history—and Renaissance humanism each reshaped the craft. In the 19th century, Leopold von Ranke professionalised history around archival source criticism and the ideal of showing the past "as it essentially was" (wie es eigentlich gewesen).

Schools and approaches

  • Marxist historiography emphasised class struggle and economic structures; its British practitioners (Hill, Hobsbawm, E. P. Thompson) reshaped social history.
  • The French Annales School (Bloch, Febvre, Braudel) championed the longue durée, geography, and total history over political narrative.
  • Whig history, named by Herbert Butterfield (1931), reads the past as progress toward the present and is now generally a term of criticism.
  • Later 20th-century turns brought women's and gender history, postcolonial and subaltern studies, microhistory, oral history, and the cultural and linguistic turns, while cliometrics applied quantitative economics.

Debates continue over objectivity, narrative, and the relationship between history and memory, with works such as E. H. Carr's What Is History? (1961) remaining standard reference points.
