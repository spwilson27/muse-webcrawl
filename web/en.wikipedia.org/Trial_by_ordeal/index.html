Trial by ordeal
A judicial practice in which guilt or innocence was determined by subjecting the accused to a painful or dangerous experience

Trial by ordeal was a practice in mediaeval Europe and other societies by which the guilt or innocence of the accused was determined by subjecting them to a painful, or at least an unpleasant, usually dangerous experience. The test was one of life or death, and the premise was that God would save the truly innocent.

Forms

Common forms included the ordeal of hot iron, in which the accused carried a heated iron bar and the burned hand was inspected after several days; the ordeal of hot water, retrieving a stone from boiling water; and the ordeal of cold water, in which the bound accused was lowered into water — sinking was taken to indicate innocence, floating guilt.

Decline

In 1215 the Fourth Lateran Council forbade clergy from participating in ordeals, removing their religious sanction in much of Europe. Ordeals were gradually replaced by jury trial in England and by inquisitorial procedure and judicial torture on the Continent.
