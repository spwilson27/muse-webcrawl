Public relations

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Public relations (PR), broadly, is the practice of managing and disseminating information from an individual or an organisation (such as a business, government agency or a non-profit) to the public in order to influence public perception. Public relations and publicity differ in that PR is controlled internally, whereas publicity is not controlled and contributed by external parties.

The Public Relations Society of America defines the field as "a strategic communication process that builds mutually beneficial relationships between organizations and their publics". PR practitioners work with the news media, social media, investors, employees, government and other stakeholders, and perform tasks such as writing press releases and speeches, organising press conferences, managing crises, and conducting campaigns.

History

Modern public relations developed in the early twentieth century in the United States. Ivy Lee, who issued a "Declaration of Principles" in 1906 promising openness with the press, and Edward Bernays, Sigmund Freud's nephew and author of Propaganda (1928), are regarded as founders of the profession. Bernays popularised the term "public relations" and the role of the "public relations counsel". Government information campaigns during the two world wars greatly expanded the practice, and the post-war growth of corporations, mass media and consumer culture made PR a major industry.

Methods

Common techniques include media relations and earned media, press releases, events, sponsorship, corporate social responsibility programmes, public affairs and lobbying, internal communications, and crisis management. The rise of social media has shifted much PR work toward continuous digital engagement and influencer relations. Practitioners sometimes describe the strategic framework as PESO: paid, earned, shared and owned media.

Criticism

PR is frequently criticised as a form of propaganda or manipulation, and the derogatory term "spin" — and "spin doctor" for practitioners — reflects suspicion of the trade's persuasive intent. Front groups, astroturfing and undisclosed paid advocacy are recurrent ethical controversies, addressed by industry codes of ethics.
