Hyper-IgM syndrome

SpecialtyImmunology
SymptomsRecurrent infections, failure to thrive
CausesGenetic defects in class-switch recombination
Class-switch recombination, defective in hyper-IgM syndromes.

Hyper-IgM syndrome is a group of rare primary immunodeficiency disorders characterised by defective immunoglobulin class-switch recombination (CSR), resulting in low or absent levels of IgG, IgA, and IgE with normal or elevated levels of IgM. Because the antibody response cannot mature beyond IgM, patients suffer recurrent bacterial and opportunistic infections beginning in early childhood.

Types and genetics

Several genetic forms are recognised. The most common is X-linked hyper-IgM syndrome, caused by mutations in CD40LG, the gene encoding CD40 ligand on activated T cells; without CD40–CD40L signalling, B cells cannot receive the signal to switch antibody class. Autosomal recessive forms result from mutations in CD40 itself or in enzymes of the class-switch machinery, notably activation-induced cytidine deaminase (AID, encoded by AICDA) and uracil-DNA glycosylase (UNG).

Clinical features

Affected children typically present in the first years of life with recurrent sinopulmonary infections, chronic diarrhoea, and failure to thrive. The X-linked form is also notable for susceptibility to opportunistic infections such as Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia and Cryptosporidium infection of the biliary tract, which can lead to sclerosing cholangitis and liver disease. Many patients have neutropenia and enlarged lymphoid tissue with giant germinal centres that lack normal architecture.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on immunoglobulin levels (low IgG and IgA with normal or high IgM), flow cytometry for CD40 ligand expression, and confirmatory genetic testing. Treatment includes regular immunoglobulin replacement therapy, prophylactic antibiotics (including trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole against Pneumocystis), and management of infections. Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation can be curative, particularly in the X-linked form, and is generally recommended before irreversible liver damage occurs.
