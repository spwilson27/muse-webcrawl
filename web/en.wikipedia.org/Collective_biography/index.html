Collective biography

Collective biography is a method in history, and a genre of writing, that studies the lives of a defined group of people together in order to identify common patterns of background, career, behaviour and experience. It is closely related to prosopography, the systematic investigation of a historical population through the combined study of its members' life histories.

Method

Instead of treating individuals in isolation, the collective biographer defines a population — for example, members of a parliament, officers of an army, students of a university, or saints of a period — and compiles standardised information about each member: birth, family, education, offices held, wealth, marriage, death. Aggregating this data can reveal the social composition of an elite, routes of recruitment, networks of kinship and patronage, and typical career trajectories that are invisible in single biographies.

History

The genre has ancient roots in works such as Plutarch's Parallel Lives and Suetonius's Lives of the Caesars, and in medieval collections of saints' lives. In modern scholarship, collective biography was developed for political history by writers such as Lewis Namier, whose studies of eighteenth-century British MPs reconstructed politics through members' backgrounds and connections, and has since been applied by social historians, historical sociologists and compilers of national biographical dictionaries such as the Oxford Dictionary of National Biography.
