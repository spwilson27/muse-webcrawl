British Commandos

The British Commandos were elite special-forces units formed by the British Army during the Second World War, at the urging of Winston Churchill after the evacuation of Dunkirk in June 1940. Modelled in name on the Boer kommandos of the South African War, they were all-volunteer raiding forces intended to strike German-occupied Europe from the sea — "to set Europe ablaze" in small-scale form — raising enemy costs and British morale. They served in every theatre, pioneered modern amphibious special operations, and their green beret, fighting knife and methods were inherited by the postwar Royal Marines Commandos and by special forces worldwide.

Formation and training

The idea was drafted by Lieutenant-Colonel Dudley Clarke and approved within days of Dunkirk; the first, hastily mounted raid (Operation Collar, June 1940) landed in France with negligible results, but the concept matured under Combined Operations headquarters, successively led by Admiral Sir Roger Keyes and Lord Louis Mountbatten. Training was centralised at Achnacarry Castle in the Scottish Highlands under Charles Vaughan, whose regime of live-fire exercises, demolitions, cliff and boat work, and punishing speed marches — the "death ride" culture of Achnacarry — became legendary and was copied by the US Army Rangers, who trained alongside the Commandos.

Operations

Early successes included the Lofoten Islands raid (March 1941), which destroyed fish-oil factories and captured Enigma materials, and Operation Archery at Vaagso (December 1941). The St Nazaire Raid (Operation Chariot, March 1942) — in which HMS Campbeltown rammed the Normandie dry dock — won five Victoria Crosses and put the only dock able to service the Tirpitz out of action. Commandos spearheaded the landings in Madagascar, North Africa (Torch), Sicily and Italy (including the costly Salerno fighting), the D-Day assaults in Normandy (notably Lord Lovat's No. 4 Commando and the Free French commandos at Ouistreham, and No. 47 (RM) Commando at Port-en-Bessin), the Walcheren operation opening Antwerp, and the final Rhine crossing; in the Far East, No. 3 Commando Brigade fought in Burma, including the hill battles at Kangaw. Ten Commandos fielded by the end included allied volunteers of No. 10 (Inter-Allied) Commando — French, Dutch, Belgian, Norwegian, Polish and others — and the Jewish X Troop.

Disbandment and legacy

The Army Commandos were disbanded in 1946, but the commando role passed permanently to the Royal Marines, who retain the green beret, the Commando dagger badge and the Achnacarry-descended Commando course at Lympstone. The wartime Commandos' example shaped the US Rangers, French naval commandos (whose founder, Philippe Kieffer, served in No. 4 Commando), Dutch and Belgian special units, and postwar British special forces selection. The Commando Memorial at Spean Bridge commemorates the more than 1,700 commandos killed in the war.
