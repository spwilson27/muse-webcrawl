Doggerland

Doggerland is an area of land, now submerged beneath the southern North Sea, that connected Great Britain to continental Europe. It was flooded by rising sea levels around 6500–6200 BCE. Geological surveys have suggested that it stretched from what is now the east coast of Great Britain to what are now the Netherlands, the western coast of Germany and the Danish peninsula of Jutland. It was probably a rich habitat with human habitation in the Mesolithic period, although rising sea levels gradually reduced it to low-lying islands before its final submergence, possibly following the Storegga Slide tsunami.

Formation and flooding

During the Last Glacial Maximum, sea levels were roughly 120 m lower than today, and the North Sea basin was dry land crossed by rivers: the Thames and Rhine were tributaries of a great Channel River. As ice sheets melted, the plain was progressively inundated; the area takes its name from the Dogger Bank, a shallow fishing bank that was one of its last high areas — itself named after Dutch fishing boats called doggers.

Archaeology

Fishing trawlers have dredged up Mesolithic artefacts — harpoons, flints, mammoth and lion bones — since the early 20th century; a barbed antler point trawled in 1931 first suggested a lost landscape. Modern seismic surveys (notably the University of Birmingham's "Europe's Lost Frontiers" project) have mapped submerged river valleys, lakes and coastlines, and sediment cores have yielded ancient DNA and pollen revealing a temperate woodland environment inhabited by hunter-gatherers.

Cultural references

Doggerland features in novels and documentaries as an "Atlantis of the North Sea", and research on its drowning informs debate about human responses to rapid sea-level rise.
