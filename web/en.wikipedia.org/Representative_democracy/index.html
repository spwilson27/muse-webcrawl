Representative democracy is a type of democracy in which citizens elect representatives to make political decisions on their behalf, as opposed to direct democracy, where citizens vote on laws themselves. It is the dominant form of democracy in the modern world, found in both republics and constitutional monarchies, and is typically combined with universal suffrage, free and fair elections, and protections for civil liberties in what is called liberal democracy.

Principles

In a representative democracy, elected officials — usually members of a legislature or parliament — deliberate and enact laws, approve budgets and oversee the executive. Elections held at regular intervals make representatives accountable: voters can reward or remove them. Representation may be organised through single-member districts, proportional representation, or mixed systems, each shaping how faithfully the legislature mirrors voters' preferences. Theories of representation distinguish the delegate model, in which representatives mirror constituents' wishes, from the trustee model associated with Edmund Burke, in which they exercise independent judgement.

History

Representative institutions grew out of medieval assemblies such as the English Parliament and the Icelandic Althing, but modern representative democracy emerged with the American and French revolutions and the gradual extension of the franchise during the 19th and 20th centuries. Thinkers such as James Madison defended representation as a remedy for the instability of pure democracy, arguing in The Federalist that elected representatives could refine public views and govern large territories that direct democracy could not.

Varieties

Representative democracies include parliamentary systems (the United Kingdom, Germany, India), where the executive depends on the legislature's confidence, and presidential systems (the United States, Brazil), where the executive is separately elected. Some incorporate direct-democratic elements such as referendums and citizen initiatives, producing hybrid or semi-direct systems.

Criticisms

Critics point to democratic deficits: unrepresentative electoral systems, the influence of money and lobbying, low participation between elections, and representatives who diverge from voters' preferences. Proposed remedies include electoral reform, deliberative citizens' assemblies, recall elections, and stronger transparency — debates that continue across established and emerging democracies.
