An interchange fee is a fee paid between banks for the acceptance of card-based transactions. In a typical card payment, the merchant's bank (the acquirer) pays a fee to the cardholder's bank (the issuer). The fee is deducted as part of the merchant service charge and is set by the card networks, such as Visa and Mastercard, or by regulation.

How it works

When a customer pays by card, the total merchant discount is divided among the acquirer, the card network (scheme fees), and the issuer (interchange). Interchange compensates issuers for processing costs, the interest-free period on credit cards, fraud losses, and funding cardholder rewards. Rates vary by card type — premium and commercial cards usually carry higher interchange — and by transaction channel and region.

Regulation

Interchange has been controversial because merchants argue it raises retail prices for all consumers. The European Union capped consumer card interchange in 2015 at 0.2% for debit and 0.3% for credit transactions. In the United States, the Durbin Amendment (2010) capped debit interchange for large issuers, while credit interchange remains unregulated and commonly around 1.5–2.5%. Australia also regulated interchange through its central bank.
