Ethmoid sinus

LocationEthmoid bone, between the eyes and nose
SystemRespiratory system
DrainageMiddle and superior meatus of the nasal cavity
Part ofParanasal sinuses

The ethmoid sinuses, or ethmoidal air cells, are one of the four paired paranasal sinuses of the human skull. Unlike the other sinuses, which are single cavities, the ethmoid sinuses consist of numerous thin-walled air cells — typically between 3 and 18 — housed in the ethmoidal labyrinth of the ethmoid bone, between the orbits and the upper nasal cavity.

Structure

The air cells are divided into anterior, middle, and posterior groups. The anterior and middle cells drain into the middle meatus of the nose (the middle cells often forming the bulge called the ethmoid bulla), while the posterior cells drain into the superior meatus. The paper-thin lateral wall of the labyrinth, the lamina papyracea, separates the sinuses from the orbit, a proximity that matters in both infection and surgery.

Development

The ethmoid sinuses are present at birth — the only paranasal sinuses besides the maxillary sinuses that are — and continue to grow through childhood, reaching adult size around puberty.

Clinical significance

Inflammation of the ethmoid sinuses (ethmoid sinusitis) can cause pain between or behind the eyes. Because of the thin lamina papyracea, infection can spread from the ethmoid cells into the orbit, causing orbital cellulitis, a potentially serious complication particularly in children. The ethmoid sinuses are a common focus of functional endoscopic sinus surgery (FESS) for chronic rhinosinusitis and nasal polyps.
