Reflection symmetry

Reflection symmetry (also called line symmetry, mirror symmetry, or bilateral symmetry) is a type of symmetry in which one half of an object is the mirror image of the other half. A shape has reflection symmetry if there exists at least one line — the axis of symmetry or line of symmetry — that divides it into two congruent halves that coincide when folded along the line.

Formal description

Mathematically, a figure in the plane has reflection symmetry if reflecting it across some line leaves it unchanged; the reflection is an isometry with the points of the axis fixed. A square has four lines of symmetry, a rectangle two, an equilateral triangle three, and a circle infinitely many. In three dimensions the corresponding symmetry is reflection across a plane. Together with rotation and translation, reflection generates the Euclidean symmetry (isometry) groups studied in geometry and crystallography.

In nature and art

Bilateral symmetry is the dominant body plan of animals (the Bilateria), including humans, whose external form is approximately mirror-symmetric about the sagittal plane — a trait associated with directed locomotion. Reflection symmetry is also pervasive in architecture, design and art, from the Taj Mahal to Gothic cathedrals, where it conveys balance and order. Some letters of the Latin alphabet (A, H, I, O, X) possess vertical or horizontal symmetry, a fact exploited in ambigrams and palindromic designs.
