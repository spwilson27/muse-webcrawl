Sack of Rome (1527)

Date6 May 1527
LocationRome, Papal States
BelligerentsMutinous Imperial (Habsburg) army vs. Papal States
ResultRome sacked; Pope Clement VII besieged, then captured

The Sack of Rome on 6 May 1527 was the capture and looting of Rome by mutinous troops of the Holy Roman Emperor Charles V during the War of the League of Cognac. It is conventionally regarded as the end of the Italian High Renaissance.

Background

Pope Clement VII had joined the League of Cognac with France, Venice and Florence against Charles V in the Italian Wars. The Imperial army in Italy — a mixture of Spanish tercios and German Landsknechte under Charles III, Duke of Bourbon — was unpaid and restive, and marched south on Rome in the hope of plunder and extorted payment.

The assault

On the morning of 6 May 1527 the Imperial troops stormed the walls of the Borgo in thick fog. Bourbon was killed early in the attack, reportedly by a shot fired by the goldsmith and memoirist Benvenuto Cellini, which removed the one commander able to restrain the army. The Swiss Guard made a famous last stand on the steps of St Peter's Basilica: of 189 guardsmen, 147 died, while 42 escorted Clement VII along the Passetto to Castel Sant'Angelo.

The sack

For days the city was subjected to murder, rape, looting and desecration, with German Lutheran landsknechts directing particular violence at clergy and churches. Thousands of inhabitants were killed and many more died of disease; the population of Rome fell dramatically, from around 55,000 before the sack to perhaps 10,000 within a year. Clement surrendered in June and, after paying a huge ransom, escaped in December. Imperial and papal forces were reconciled in 1529, and Charles was crowned by the Pope at Bologna in 1530.

Consequences

The sack shattered Rome's position as the centre of Renaissance culture: artists fled or were killed, patronage collapsed, and the psychological shock was felt across Europe. Politically it confirmed Habsburg dominance in Italy for a generation and pushed the Papacy toward the defensive posture of the Counter-Reformation.
