Logicism

Logicism is a programme in the philosophy of mathematics, comprising one of the schools of thought in the foundations of mathematics, that holds that mathematics is an extension of logic and that all mathematical concepts and truths can be derived from purely logical concepts and principles.

History

The thesis was advanced by Gottlob Frege in his Grundgesetze der Arithmetik (1893–1903), and taken up by Bertrand Russell and Alfred North Whitehead in Principia Mathematica (1910–1913), which derived large parts of mathematics from a small set of logical axioms and rules. Frege's system was undermined by Russell's paradox, which Russell's theory of types was designed to avoid.

Assessment

Gödel's incompleteness theorems of 1931 showed that any consistent formal system strong enough for arithmetic cannot prove all arithmetic truths, dashing the strongest form of the logicist programme. Nevertheless, work by later "neo-logicists" has revived a restricted version, showing that arithmetic can be derived from Hume's Principle together with second-order logic.
