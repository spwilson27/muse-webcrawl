Protectionism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Protectionism, sometimes referred to as trade protectionism, is the economic policy of restricting imports from other countries through methods such as tariffs on imported goods, import quotas, and a variety of other government regulations. Proponents argue that protectionist policies shield the producers, businesses, and workers of the import-competing sector of the country from foreign competitors and help maintain domestic employment. Opponents argue that protectionist policies reduce trade, raise prices for consumers, and harm the overall economy by reducing the benefits of specialization and comparative advantage.

Historically, protectionism was associated with economic theories such as mercantilism, which argued that a nation should export more than it imports to accumulate wealth, and import substitution industrialization, which sought to develop domestic industries behind trade barriers. In the modern era, protectionist measures are often justified on the grounds of national security, infant industry development, environmental protection, or unfair competition such as dumping.

History

Protectionist policies have a long history. In the 18th and 19th centuries, most industrializing nations protected their domestic industries with tariffs. Alexander Hamilton's Report on Manufactures (1791) argued that the young United States should protect its infant industries. The United States maintained among the highest tariff rates in the world through much of the 19th and early 20th centuries, including the Tariff of 1828, the Morrill Tariff of 1861, and the Smoot–Hawley Tariff Act of 1930, which raised tariffs on over 20,000 imported goods and is widely blamed for worsening the Great Depression by provoking retaliation.

After the Second World War, the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT), signed in 1947, and its successor, the World Trade Organization (WTO), founded in 1995, progressively reduced tariff levels worldwide. Nevertheless, protectionist sentiment resurfaced periodically, notably during the trade tensions of the 2010s, when the United States imposed tariffs on steel, aluminum, and a wide range of goods from China.

Policies

Tariffs

A tariff is a tax imposed on imported goods and services. Tariffs raise the price of foreign goods, making domestic products more competitive. They also generate government revenue. Specific tariffs are levied as a fixed charge per unit, while ad valorem tariffs are charged as a percentage of the value of the good.

Import quotas

An import quota is a direct quantitative restriction on the amount of a good that may be imported. Quotas limit supply and raise domestic prices without generating tariff revenue; the rents accrue instead to whoever holds the import licenses or to foreign exporters under voluntary export restraints.

Non-tariff barriers

Non-tariff barriers include subsidies to domestic producers, government procurement preferences, technical standards and regulations that foreign producers find costly to meet, export controls, and currency manipulation. Such measures can protect domestic industry without explicit taxes on imports.

Arguments for and against

Supporters of protectionism cite the protection of jobs, the nurturing of infant industries until they can compete internationally, national defense (ensuring domestic capacity in steel, semiconductors, and other strategic goods), and the prevention of a "race to the bottom" in labor and environmental standards. Critics counter that protectionism raises consumer prices, invites retaliation, reduces efficiency by shielding uncompetitive firms, distorts resource allocation, and disproportionately harms poorer consumers who spend a larger share of income on traded goods.

Most mainstream economists hold that free trade increases aggregate welfare through comparative advantage, though many acknowledge that the gains are unevenly distributed and that adjustment costs for displaced workers can be substantial and long-lasting.
