Lamian

Lamian (simplified Chinese: 拉面; traditional Chinese: 拉麵; pinyin: lāmiàn) is a type of soft wheat-flour Chinese noodle that is particularly common in northern China. Lamian is made by twisting, stretching and folding dough into strands, using the weight of the dough itself, a process repeated until the desired length and thickness are achieved. Literally, lā means "pull" and miàn means "noodle".

Preparation

The dough, made from soft wheat flour and water — sometimes with alkaline agents or oil — is rolled, pulled and folded repeatedly. Skilled makers separate a single piece of dough into many thin strands in seconds. Thickness grades range from "dragon's hair" (very thin) to flat, belt-like strands.

Dishes

The best-known lamian dish is Lanzhou beef noodle soup (Lanzhou lamian), from Lanzhou, Gansu, served in a clear beef broth with sliced beef, radish, chilli oil, coriander and garlic chives, and traditionally attributed to Hui Muslim cooks. Hand-pulled noodles are also served stir-fried or in other soups throughout China and worldwide.

History

Hand-pulled noodles have a long history in northern China, where wheat is the staple grain. Lanzhou lamian is said to have been developed in the Qing dynasty and popularised by Ma Baozi in the early 20th century.
