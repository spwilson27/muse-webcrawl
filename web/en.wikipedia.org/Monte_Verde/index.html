Monte Verde

Monte Verde is a Paleolithic archaeological site in the Llanquihue Province of southern Chile, near Puerto Montt. One layer of the site, Monte Verde II, has been dated to about 14,800 years ago (calibrated), making it one of the oldest known sites of human habitation in the Americas. Its discovery, and eventual acceptance, overturned the "Clovis first" consensus that the first humans in the Americas arrived via the Clovis culture around 13,000 years ago.

Discovery and excavation

The site was discovered in 1975 when a veterinary student, alerted by local farmers, found an unusual bone — later identified as belonging to a gomphothere (an extinct elephant-like animal) — eroding out of the bank of Chinchihuapi Creek. Beginning in 1977, archaeologist Tom Dillehay of the University of Kentucky led excavations. Because the site was covered by a peat bog, organic materials were exceptionally well preserved.

Findings

Monte Verde II preserved the remains of a small settlement: tent-like wooden structures, hearths, wooden tools, knotted cords, stone tools, mastodon meat and bone, wild potato skins, and medicinal plants gathered from as far away as the coast. A human footprint was also preserved in the clay. A deeper, more controversial layer, Monte Verde I, has been dated to roughly 33,000 years ago but is not widely accepted.

Significance

In 1997 a delegation of archaeologists visited the site and accepted the dating of Monte Verde II, ending decades of dispute. The site implies that people reached southern Chile well before the end of the last ice age, supporting models of early coastal migration into the Americas.
