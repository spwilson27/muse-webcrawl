Interstellar space is the physical space within a galaxy not occupied by stars and their planetary systems — the region between stars. It is filled with the interstellar medium (ISM): tenuous gas (mostly hydrogen and helium), microscopic dust grains, cosmic rays and magnetic fields. Although often pictured as empty, the ISM contains most of a galaxy's baryonic mass outside stars and is the reservoir from which new stars and planets form.

The local interstellar environment

The Solar System sits within the Local Bubble, a cavity of hot, very low-density gas a few hundred light-years across, and is currently passing through the Local Interstellar Cloud. The Sun's heliosphere — the bubble of solar wind — shields the inner Solar System from much of the interstellar medium; its boundary, the heliopause, lies at roughly 120 astronomical units from the Sun.

Exploration

NASA's Voyager 1 crossed the heliopause in August 2012, becoming the first human-made object to enter interstellar space, followed by Voyager 2 in November 2018. Both spacecraft returned direct measurements of interstellar plasma density and cosmic-ray intensity. No other probe is yet on an interstellar trajectory, though concepts such as light-sail missions to Alpha Centauri (Breakthrough Starshot) aim to send gram-scale probes across interstellar distances.

In science and culture

Interstellar space separates the stars by light-years, making interstellar travel a staple of science fiction and a formidable physical challenge: at Voyager's speed, reaching the nearest star would take tens of thousands of years. Passing interstellar objects — the asteroid-like 'Oumuamua (2017) and comet 2I/Borisov (2019) — have given astronomers their first samples of material from other planetary systems.
