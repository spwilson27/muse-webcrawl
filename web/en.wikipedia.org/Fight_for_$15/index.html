The Fight for $15 is an American political and labour movement, organised with support from the Service Employees International Union (SEIU), that advocates raising the minimum wage to $15 per hour and extending union rights to low-wage workers, initially fast-food employees. It began in November 2012, when about 200 fast-food workers in New York City walked off the job demanding $15 an hour and a union — at a time when the federal minimum wage was $7.25.

History and actions

The campaign spread rapidly: strikes and one-day walkouts by fast-food, retail, home care, airport and other service workers took place in hundreds of cities, culminating in coordinated national days of action from 2013 onward. The movement also broadened its demands to issues including paid sick leave, predictable scheduling and racial justice, and spread internationally to dozens of countries as part of a global fast-food workers' campaign.

Policy outcomes

Fight for $15 is credited with shifting the policy debate on minimum wages in the United States. Seattle (2014) and SeaTac, Washington, were among the first jurisdictions to adopt $15 minimums; San Francisco, New York City and the state of California followed, and states including New York, Massachusetts, New Jersey, Illinois, Maryland, Connecticut and Florida subsequently enacted paths to $15. Organisers estimate the movement helped win well over $100 billion in raises for tens of millions of workers. Although the US federal minimum wage remained $7.25, the demand entered mainstream Democratic politics and many large employers, including Amazon and Target, adopted $15 starting wages.

Criticism

Business groups and some economists opposed the campaign, arguing that rapid increases would reduce employment, accelerate automation or raise prices, and criticised its union-backed strategy of organising through public pressure rather than workplace elections. Supporters countered with studies finding modest employment effects and emphasised gains in living standards.
