Goulash communism


Relative consumer abundance distinguished Kádár-era Hungary.

Goulash communism (Hungarian: gulyáskommunizmus), also known as Kadarism, was the variety of state socialism practised in Hungary under János Kádár from the suppression of the Hungarian Revolution of 1956 until the end of communist rule in 1989. Named jokingly after the Hungarian dish goulash — a mixture of disparate ingredients — it combined one-party political control with unusually tolerant cultural policies and a partially market-oriented economy, giving Hungarians the highest living standards in the Eastern Bloc.

Characteristics

Kádár reversed the Stalinist terror of his predecessors, proclaiming in 1961 that "whoever is not against us is with us." Political repression continued but at far lower intensity; travel to the West, though restricted, was comparatively easy, and censorship was administered through the "three T's" categories of supported (támogatott), tolerated (tűrt) and banned (tiltott) works. The New Economic Mechanism of 1968 introduced limited market elements, enterprise autonomy and legal scope for a "second economy" of private sideline work, which kept shops supplied and produced the style of life satirized as "the happiest barrack in the camp."

Decline

The system was financed increasingly by Western loans in the 1970s, and mounting hard-currency debt, stagnating growth and Gorbachev's reforms in the USSR eroded it in the 1980s. Kádár was removed as party leader in 1988, and Hungary negotiated a peaceful transition to multiparty democracy in 1989.
