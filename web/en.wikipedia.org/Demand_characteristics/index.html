Demand characteristics

In social research, especially psychology, demand characteristics are cues in an experiment that convey to participants the purpose of the study or the behaviour that is expected of them. The term was coined by psychologist Martin Orne in 1962. Participants may respond to these cues by trying to be "good subjects", altering their behaviour in ways that threaten the validity of the experiment's conclusions.

Experimental settings can signal to participants what responses are expected

The good subject effect

Orne proposed that participants in psychological experiments enter a special social situation in which they try to discern the experimenter's hypothesis and then behave in a way that supports it. This cooperative tendency, sometimes called the good subject effect, is a form of reactivity: the act of measurement changes the thing measured.

Sources of demand characteristics

Demand characteristics can arise from the experimental setting, the procedures, the instructions given, the behaviour of the experimenter, and communication between participants. Rumours about a study circulating on a campus, for example, can shape expectations before a session begins.

Control strategies

Researchers attempt to limit demand effects through deception and cover stories, the use of unobtrusive measures, post-experimental interviews probing what participants believed the study was about, and designs in which experimenters are kept blind to condition. Orne also advocateded "quasi-controls" — studies that simulate the demand structure of an experiment without administering the treatment — to estimate how much of an effect demands alone could produce.
