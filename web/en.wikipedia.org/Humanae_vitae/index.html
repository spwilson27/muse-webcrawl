Humanae vitae (Latin for "Of Human Life") is an encyclical written by Pope Paul VI and dated 25 July 1968. Subtitled On the Regulation of Birth, it re-affirmed the Catholic Church's traditional teaching that artificial contraception is intrinsically wrong, while permitting natural family planning, and it addressed abortion, sterilisation, and the dignity of married love.

[Image: Pope Paul VI, author of Humanae vitae]

Background

The encyclical followed years of study by the Pontifical Commission on Birth Control, appointed by John XXIII and expanded by Paul VI. A majority of the commission favoured revising the teaching on the contraceptive pill, but Paul VI decided to uphold the traditional prohibition, reasoning from natural law and the inseparability of the unitive and procreative meanings of the marital act.

Teaching

Humanae vitae teaches that each marital act must remain open to the transmission of life, condemns direct abortion and sterilisation, and warns that widespread contraception could lead to marital infidelity, a lowering of moral standards, and state coercion in population policy. It calls married couples to responsible parenthood and encourages scientists to develop methods of natural regulation.

Reception

The encyclical proved highly controversial, prompting public dissent from many theologians and widespread non-observance among lay Catholics in developed countries, while being defended by later popes, notably John Paul II, whose "theology of the body" developed its themes. It remains a defining document of modern Catholic moral teaching.
