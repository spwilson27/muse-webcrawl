Codeine: 3-methylmorphine, an opiate alkaloid of the opium poppy

Codeine is an opiate and prodrug of morphine mainly used to treat pain, coughing, and diarrhea. It is also commonly used as a recreational drug. It is found naturally in the sap of the opium poppy, Papaver somniferum. It is typically used to treat mild to moderate degrees of pain. Greater benefit may occur when combined with paracetamol (acetaminophen) or a nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) such as aspirin or ibuprofen.

Pharmacology

Codeine itself has weak affinity for the μ-opioid receptor; its analgesic effect depends largely on conversion by the liver enzyme CYP2D6 to morphine. Its peak plasma concentration (Cmax) after oral dosing occurs at about one hour. Genetic variation in CYP2D6 means that poor metabolizers obtain little analgesia, while ultrarapid metabolizers can convert codeine dangerously fast, which has led to warnings against its use in children after tonsillectomy and during breastfeeding.

History and regulation

Codeine was isolated in 1832 by the French chemist Pierre Jean Robiquet. Its name derives from the Greek kodeia, "poppy head". It is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines and is the most widely used opiate in the world. Because of its abuse potential — including in codeine-based cough syrups — many countries restrict its sale, requiring prescriptions or pharmacist supervision.
