Sobibor extermination camp

The memorial at the site of the Sobibor camp

Sobibor (German: Sobibór) was a German Nazi extermination camp built and operated by the SS as part of Operation Reinhard, the secret programme to murder the Jews of German-occupied Poland. It was located in the forest near the village of Sobibór, close to the Bug River in what is today eastern Poland, near the borders with Ukraine and Belarus. Between May 1942 and October 1943, at least 170,000 people — most estimates range from 170,000 to 250,000 — were murdered there, almost all of them Jews from Poland, the Netherlands, France, Germany, Austria, Czechoslovakia and the Soviet Union.

Operation of the camp

Victims arrived by train at a ramp disguised as an ordinary station. They were told they were at a transit camp, forced to undress, and driven along a fenced path — cynically called the "Road to Heaven" — to gas chambers disguised as showers, where they were killed with carbon monoxide from a tank engine. Bodies were buried in mass graves and later exhumed and burned on open pyres to erase evidence. A small number of prisoners were kept alive as forced labourers (Sonderkommandos) to process the victims' belongings.

The uprising

On 14 October 1943, prisoners led by the Jewish-Polish officer Leon Feldhendler and the Soviet POW Alexander Pechersky rose in revolt, killing a number of SS men and guards and enabling around 300 prisoners to escape into the forest. About 100 were recaptured, but roughly 50 survived the war. After the revolt, the SS shot the remaining prisoners, demolished the camp, and planted the site with trees to conceal it. Sobibor is remembered as one of the very few successful mass uprisings in a Nazi death camp, alongside Treblinka. A museum and memorial now occupy the site.
