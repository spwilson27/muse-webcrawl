Miranda v. Arizona, 384 U.S. 436 (1966), was a landmark decision of the United States Supreme Court in which the Court ruled that statements made in response to custodial interrogation are admissible at trial only if the prosecution can show that the defendant was informed of the right to consult an attorney and of the right against self-incrimination prior to questioning by police, and that the defendant not only understood these rights, but voluntarily waived them. The decision gave rise to the "Miranda warnings" now routinely given by American police to suspects in custody.

Background

Ernesto Miranda was arrested in Phoenix, Arizona, in 1963 in connection with the kidnapping and rape of an 18-year-old woman. After two hours of interrogation by police, during which he was not informed of his right to counsel or his right to remain silent, Miranda signed a written confession. The confession was admitted at his trial and he was convicted. The Arizona Supreme Court affirmed, holding that Miranda's constitutional rights had not been violated because he had not specifically requested counsel. The case was consolidated with three others: Vignera v. New York, Westover v. United States, and California v. Stewart.

Decision

In a 5–4 decision written by Chief Justice Earl Warren, the Court held that the Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination requires that a person subjected to custodial interrogation be advised of the right to remain silent, that anything said can and will be used against them in court, that they have the right to an attorney, and that if they cannot afford an attorney one will be appointed. Without these procedural safeguards, statements obtained during interrogation are presumed compelled and inadmissible. Miranda himself was retried without the confession and was convicted again on other evidence in 1967.

Aftermath and legacy

The decision had a profound impact on American criminal procedure and popular culture; the phrase "you have the right to remain silent" became widely known through police dramas. Congress attempted to overrule the decision by statute in 1968 (18 U.S.C. § 3501), but in Dickerson v. United States (2000) the Court held that Miranda announced a constitutional rule that Congress could not legislatively supersede. Ernesto Miranda was paroled in 1972 and was stabbed to death in a Phoenix bar fight in 1976.
