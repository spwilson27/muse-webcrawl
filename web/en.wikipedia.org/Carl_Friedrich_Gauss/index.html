Johann Carl Friedrich Gauss (30 April 1777 – 23 February 1855) was a German mathematician, astronomer, and physicist widely regarded as one of the greatest mathematicians of all time — often called the Princeps mathematicorum ("Prince of Mathematicians"). His work influenced number theory, algebra, statistics, analysis, differential geometry, geodesy, geophysics, mechanics, electrostatics, and astronomy.

Early life and breakthroughs

A child prodigy from a poor family, Gauss was supported by the Duke of Brunswick. At 19 he proved that the regular 17-sided polygon (heptadecagon) could be constructed with compass and straightedge — the first such advance in 2,000 years. His Disquisitiones Arithmeticae (1801) systematised number theory, introducing modular arithmetic and proving the law of quadratic reciprocity.

Career

In 1801 Gauss computed the orbit of the asteroid Ceres from limited observations, using the method of least squares, which brought him fame. He became professor of astronomy and director of the observatory at Göttingen in 1807, remaining there for life. He directed the geodetic survey of Hanover, invented the heliotrope, contributed to the theory of surfaces (Theorema Egregium), developed non-Euclidean geometry (which he did not publish), and with Wilhelm Weber built one of the first electric telegraphs while studying terrestrial magnetism. The normal distribution in statistics is often called the Gaussian distribution.
