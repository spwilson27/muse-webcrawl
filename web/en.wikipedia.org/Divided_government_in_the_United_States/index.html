Divided government in the United States

In the United States, a divided government is a situation in which one party controls the presidency (the executive branch), while another party controls one or both houses of Congress (the legislative branch). A unified government, by contrast, exists when a single party controls the presidency, the House of Representatives and the Senate—sometimes called a "trifecta" at the state level. Divided government is a product of the separation of powers in the US Constitution, which provides for separate elections for the presidency and for members of Congress.

History

Divided government was relatively rare in the early republic but became more common in the twentieth century, particularly after the Second World War. Between 1945 and the early twenty-first century, the government was divided more often than not. Notable examples include the presidencies of Dwight D. Eisenhower (with a Democratic Congress after 1954), Richard Nixon and Gerald Ford (with Democratic Congresses), Ronald Reagan (with a Democratic House), George H. W. Bush, Bill Clinton (after the Republican takeover of Congress in 1994), George W. Bush (briefly, and again after 2006), Barack Obama (after 2010), and Donald Trump (after 2018). Divided government can also arise at the state level between a governor and the state legislature.

Effects

Political scientists debate the consequences of divided government. It is often associated with legislative gridlock, in which fewer significant laws are enacted, negotiations are harder, and conflicts over budgets can lead to government shutdowns. Critics also argue that divided government blurs accountability, allowing each side to blame the other for inaction.

Others, such as David Mayhew in his book Divided We Govern (1991), have argued that major legislation is passed at roughly similar rates under divided and unified governments, and that divided government can encourage moderation, compromise and oversight, since congressional committees controlled by the opposition have incentives to investigate the executive branch. Empirical findings suggest that divided government tends to matter most in areas such as budgeting, confirmations and investigations.

Causes

Suggested causes of divided government include voters deliberately balancing the parties ("split-ticket voting"), the different electoral calendars of the House, Senate and presidency, midterm elections in which the president's party typically loses seats, and the incumbency advantage of sitting members of Congress. As split-ticket voting has declined in recent decades, periods of unified government have alternated with divisions produced mainly by midterm swings.
