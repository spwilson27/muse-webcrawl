Oedipus (Greek: Οἰδίπους, Oidípous) is a tragic hero of Greek mythology, king of Thebes, whose story is among the most famous of all classical myths. As prophesied, he unknowingly killed his father Laius and married his mother Jocasta. His name — "swollen foot" — recalls the injury inflicted when he was exposed as an infant.

Myth

Warned by an oracle that his son would kill him, Laius had the baby Oedipus pierced through the ankles and left to die on Mount Cithaeron. A shepherd rescued the child, who was raised in Corinth by King Polybus and Queen Merope. As a young man, Oedipus heard a rumour that he was adopted and consulted the oracle at Delphi; told he would kill his father and marry his mother, he fled Corinth — and on the road to Thebes quarrelled with and killed an older man, Laius, at a place where three roads met.

Arriving at Thebes, Oedipus solved the riddle of the Sphinx — "What creature walks on four feet in the morning, two at noon, and three in the evening?" Answer: man. Rewarded with the throne and the widowed queen Jocasta, he ruled prosperously for years and fathered four children. When a plague struck Thebes, the search for Laius's murderer led Oedipus to the truth. Jocasta hanged herself; Oedipus blinded himself with her brooches and went into exile, accompanied by his daughter Antigone, dying at Colonus near Athens.

Literary treatments

The myth is treated in the lost epic Oedipodea, in Aeschylus's Theban trilogy, and most famously in Sophocles' plays Oedipus Rex and Oedipus at Colonus. Aristotle in the Poetics held Oedipus Rex to be the perfect tragedy. In the twentieth century Freud named the Oedipus complex after the myth.
