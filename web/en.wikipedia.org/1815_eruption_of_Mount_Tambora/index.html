1815 eruption of Mount Tambora

The 1815 eruption of Mount Tambora, on the island of Sumbawa in present-day Indonesia, was the most powerful volcanic eruption in recorded human history. Reaching a Volcanic Explosivity Index (VEI) of 7, it ejected an estimated 150 cubic kilometres of material, destroyed the mountain's summit, and caused global climate anomalies that made 1816 the "Year Without a Summer."

The eruption

After several years of increasing activity, Tambora erupted violently on 5 April 1815, with an explosion heard on Sumatra, more than 2,000 km away. The climactic eruption on the evening of 10 April produced pyroclastic flows that destroyed villages around the volcano and reached the sea, generating tsunamis. Ash fell as far away as Borneo, Sulawesi and Java. The summit, formerly about 4,300 m high, was reduced to about 2,850 m, leaving a caldera 6–7 km wide and 600–700 m deep.

Human toll

At least 10,000–11,000 people were killed directly by the eruption on Sumbawa; tens of thousands more died in the following months from famine and disease as crops were buried under ash. Total deaths are commonly estimated at about 71,000, the deadliest volcanic disaster in recorded history.

Global climate effects

Sulfur dioxide injected into the stratosphere formed a global aerosol veil that lowered average global temperatures by roughly 0.4–0.7 °C. In 1816, killing frosts occurred through the summer in New England and eastern Canada, harvests failed across Europe and Asia, and food prices soared, contributing to widespread famine and unrest. The gloomy weather of that summer at the Villa Diodati in Switzerland famously inspired Mary Shelley's Frankenstein.
