Slaughterhouse

A slaughterhouse, also called an abattoir, is a facility where animals are slaughtered to provide food. Slaughterhouses supply meat, which then becomes the responsibility of a packaging facility. They customarily slaughter animals on a large scale, processing cattle, pigs, sheep and poultry, and may also handle specialty meats such as horse meat.

Process

Animals are received in stockyards, inspected ante-mortem, then stunned — commonly by captive bolt, electrical stunning or carbon dioxide — to render them unconscious before exsanguination (bleeding). Carcasses are then skinned or scalded and dehaired, eviscerated, inspected post-mortem, split, washed and chilled before cutting and packaging. By-products are directed to rendering. Rigorous hygiene, veterinary inspection and animal welfare rules govern operations in most jurisdictions.

History and reform

The modern regulated abattoir developed in 19th-century France, where Napoleon ordered municipal slaughterhouses in Paris to replace scattered private slaughtering; La Villette opened in 1867. In the United States, the mechanised "disassembly lines" of Cincinnati and Chicago packinghouses inspired industrial production methods and, after exposes such as Upton Sinclair's novel The Jungle (1906), led to federal meat inspection legislation. Slaughterhouses remain subjects of debate over animal welfare, worker safety and environmental impact.
