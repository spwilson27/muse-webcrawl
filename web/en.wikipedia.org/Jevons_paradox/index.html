Jevons paradox

In ecological economics, the Jevons paradox (sometimes the Jevons effect) occurs when technological progress increases the efficiency with which a resource is used, but the rate of consumption of that resource rises because the falling effective cost increases demand. It is the extreme form of the rebound effect, in which efficiency gains are more than offset by increased use—"backfire".

History

The paradox is named after the English economist William Stanley Jevons, who in his 1865 book The Coal Question observed that improvements in steam-engine efficiency, beginning with James Watt's engine, had not reduced Britain's coal consumption but were followed by greatly increased consumption, as cheaper steam power found ever wider applications. Jevons concluded that "it is wholly a confusion of ideas to suppose that the economical use of fuel is equivalent to a diminished consumption. The very contrary is the truth."

Modern discussion

The paradox has been revived in debates over energy policy: whether efficiency standards for vehicles, lighting, or computing actually reduce total energy use or merely shift and expand it. Economists distinguish direct rebound (cheaper use of the improved service) from indirect and economy-wide effects; whether full backfire occurs depends on the elasticity of demand for the resource's services. Variants such as the Khazzoom–Brookes postulate restate the argument in modern terms, and the paradox informs arguments that efficiency policy must be paired with caps, taxes, or other conservation measures.
