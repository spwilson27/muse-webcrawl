Pharmacophore

FieldMedicinal chemistry, cheminformatics, drug design
Feature typesHydrogen-bond donors/acceptors, hydrophobic regions, aromatic rings, positive/negative charges
UsesVirtual screening, de novo design, lead optimization

A pharmacophore is an abstract description of the molecular features necessary for a molecule to interact optimally with a specific biological target and trigger (or block) its biological response. According to the IUPAC definition, a pharmacophore is "the ensemble of steric and electronic features that is necessary to ensure the optimal supramolecular interactions with a specific biological target structure and to trigger (or to block) its biological response."

Features

Typical pharmacophoric features include hydrogen-bond donors and acceptors, hydrophobic groups, aromatic rings, positively and negatively charged groups, and metal-binding groups, arranged in three dimensions with defined distances, angles, and tolerance spheres. Excluded volumes may represent regions where atoms would clash with the target protein.

History

The concept traces to Paul Ehrlich's early-twentieth-century ideas about the parts of a molecule responsible for its activity, and the term was developed further by researchers such as Lemont Kier in the 1960s–70s. Pharmacophore modelling became a core technique of computer-aided drug design with the spread of molecular modelling software in the 1980s and 1990s.

Applications

Ligand-based pharmacophores are derived from the common features of known active compounds when the target structure is unknown; structure-based pharmacophores are derived from a protein–ligand complex, identifying interaction points in the binding site. Pharmacophore queries are used to search large compound databases in virtual screening, to align molecules for 3D-QSAR, and as input to de novo design. The approach abstracts away from specific chemical scaffolds, so it can retrieve structurally novel "scaffold-hopped" hits that a similarity search would miss.

Limitations

Pharmacophore models depend on correct conformations and alignment of the training compounds; they may miss binding determinants such as water-mediated interactions, protein flexibility, and entropy effects, and can produce false positives that require follow-up by docking or experiment.
