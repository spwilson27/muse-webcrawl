Document delivery

Document delivery is a service by which researchers obtain copies of articles, book chapters, reports, conference papers and other publications that their own library does not hold. It is closely related to interlibrary loan, but whereas interlibrary loan traditionally involves lending a physical item that must be returned, document delivery supplies a copy— historically a photocopy and today usually a scanned or born-digital file—that the recipient keeps. It is a core service of academic, medical, legal and corporate libraries and of specialised national and commercial suppliers.

History

Libraries supplied copies of documents by post from the late nineteenth century; the growth of photocopying in the 1960s and 1970s made article-level supply routine. Large national suppliers became prominent, such as the British Library's document supply centre at Boston Spa, once among the world's largest, and the Canadian Institute for Scientific and Technical Information (CISTI). With the spread of electronic journals in the 1990s and 2000s, delivery moved to fax and then email and web-based services, and requests increasingly were fulfilled directly from publishers' platforms.

Services and suppliers

Requests are commonly mediated through library systems using protocols such as ISO ILL (ISO 10160/10161) and handled by networks including OCLC's WorldShare and the RapidILL system. Commercial document delivery services supply articles to pharmaceutical, legal and research organisations for fees that include publisher royalties. Copyright compliance is managed through licensing schemes and collecting societies (for example, the Copyright Clearance Center in the United States), and under doctrines such as fair use and the CONTU guidelines on photocopying for interlibrary arrangements.

Alternatives

The growth of open access publishing, institutional repositories, author sharing and preprint servers has reduced dependence on mediated delivery for many users, while services such as Unpaywall and browser tools automatically locate legal free copies. Nonetheless, document delivery remains important for older literature, specialist materials, and fields where much publishing remains paywalled.
