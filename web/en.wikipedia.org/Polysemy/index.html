Polysemy

Polysemy (from Greek polysēmos, "having many signs") is the capacity for a sign — a word, phrase, or symbol — to have multiple related meanings. A word that has multiple meanings is called a polyseme. Polysemy differs from homonymy, in which the multiple meanings of a word are unrelated to one another; for example, "bank" (a financial institution) and "bank" (the side of a river) are generally treated as homonyms, while the senses of "head" (body part, leader, top of a bed) are treated as polysemous because they are historically and conceptually connected.

Characteristics

Polysemy is extremely common in natural language. Frequent, short words tend to accumulate senses over time through mechanisms such as metaphor, metonymy, and specialization or generalization of meaning. Dictionaries treat polysemous senses as sub-entries under a single headword, whereas homonyms usually receive separate entries. The boundary, however, is not always clear, and linguists disagree on tests for distinguishing the two; etymology is one criterion, since polysemous senses share a common historical origin.

Types

Scholars distinguish linear polysemy, in which senses form a chain of related extensions (for example by metonymy, as when "dish" refers first to a container and then to its contents), and non-linear or radial polysemy, in which several senses radiate from a central core sense, a view developed in cognitive linguistics by scholars such as George Lakoff and Dirk Geeraerts. Regular polysemy, described by Yuri Apresjan, refers to systematic alternations found across many words, such as animal/meat ("chicken", "lamb") or container/content patterns.

Applications

Polysemy is a central problem in lexicography, translation, and natural language processing, where word-sense disambiguation systems must determine which sense of a word is intended in a given context. It also features in philosophy of language and in legal interpretation, where contractual and statutory disputes may turn on which sense of a term applies.
