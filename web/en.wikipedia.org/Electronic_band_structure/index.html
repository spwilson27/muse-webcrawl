Electronic band structure

In solid-state physics, the electronic band structure (or simply band structure) of a solid describes the ranges of energy levels that electrons may occupy (energy bands) and the ranges they may not (band gaps). Band theory derives these structures from the quantum mechanics of electrons moving in the periodic potential of a crystal lattice, using Bloch's theorem.

Why bands form

In an isolated atom, electrons occupy discrete energy levels. When many atoms form a crystal, their atomic orbitals overlap and each level splits into a dense set of closely spaced states — an energy band. The bands occupied by the valence electrons (valence band) and the lowest empty or partly filled band (conduction band) determine the electrical behaviour of the material.

Conductors, semiconductors and insulators

In metals, the valence and conduction bands overlap or a band is partly filled, so electrons move freely. In insulators, the valence band is full and separated from the conduction band by a large gap (several electronvolts), preventing conduction. Semiconductors such as silicon have a small gap (~1 eV), allowing thermal excitation of carriers and controllable doping — the basis of all modern electronics. The position of the Fermi level within the band structure completes the description.

Calculation and measurement

Band structures are calculated with methods ranging from the nearly-free electron and tight-binding models to first-principles density functional theory, and are plotted along high-symmetry directions of the Brillouin zone. Experimentally they are probed by angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy (ARPES) and optical absorption.
