Aleut language

Aleut (Aleut: Unangam Tunuu) is a language of the Eskimo–Aleut language family. It is the language of the Aleut people (Unangax̂), spoken in the Aleutian Islands, the Pribilof Islands, and the Alaska Peninsula in Alaska, and on Bering Island in Russia. Aleut forms its own branch of the family, quite distinct from the Inuit and Yupik languages.

Dialects

Aleut has three main dialect groups: Eastern Aleut (Alaska Peninsula and eastern islands), Atkan (central islands around Atka), and Attuan (far western islands, now extinct as a first language, with a distinct mixed variety, Copper Island Aleut, surviving in Russia). The dialects form a chain of decreasing mutual intelligibility from east to west.

Grammar

The language is polysynthetic and agglutinative, with a distinctive anaphoric system for tracking referents in discourse, a small phoneme inventory including uvular consonants, and verb morphology capable of building long, sentence-like words with hundreds of derivational suffixes. Word order is typically verb-final.

Status

Aleut is severely endangered: fluent speakers number only in the low hundreds, most of them elderly. In Alaska it is written in a Latin-based orthography developed in the 1970s, while Russian Aleut has historically used Cyrillic. Revitalization efforts include school programs, dictionaries — notably the comprehensive work of Knut Bergsland — and community language classes.
