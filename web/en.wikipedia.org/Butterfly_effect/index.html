Butterfly effect

Butterfly effect
The Lorenz attractor, an icon of chaos theory
FieldChaos theory, dynamical systems
Named byEdward Norton Lorenz

The butterfly effect is the sensitive dependence on initial conditions in which a small change in one state of a deterministic nonlinear system can result in large differences in a later state. The term is closely associated with the work of mathematician and meteorologist Edward Norton Lorenz, who in the early 1960s found that rounding the input data of a weather model from six to three decimal places produced a completely different forecast.

Lorenz's discovery

In 1972 Lorenz gave a talk titled "Predictability: Does the Flap of a Butterfly's Wings in Brazil Set Off a Tornado in Texas?", giving the phenomenon its name. His simplified system of three coupled equations for atmospheric convection produced the Lorenz attractor, a strange attractor whose double-lobed shape coincidentally resembles butterfly wings. Trajectories starting arbitrarily close together diverge exponentially, quantified by the Lyapunov exponent.

Meaning and limits

The butterfly effect does not imply that any small event can cause any outcome; rather, in systems exhibiting deterministic chaos, long-term prediction is impossible in practice because measurement errors grow exponentially. This underlies the roughly two-week practical limit on weather forecasting despite ever-improving models.

In popular culture

The idea has spread widely through fiction — Ray Bradbury's 1952 story "A Sound of Thunder", time-travel narratives, and the 2004 film The Butterfly Effect — where it usually signifies that tiny actions can have vast unintended consequences.
