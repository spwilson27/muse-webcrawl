Ten Thousand

The Ten Thousand (Ancient Greek: οἱ Μύριοι, hoi Myrioi) were a force of mainly Greek mercenary hoplites who marched in 401 BCE into the heart of the Persian Achaemenid Empire in support of the pretender Cyrus the Younger against his brother, King Artaxerxes II. After Cyrus was killed at the Battle of Cunaxa and their generals were treacherously seized and executed, the soldiers fought their way north through hostile territory to the Black Sea, an epic retreat recorded by Xenophon, who served with the army, in his Anabasis.

The expedition

Recruited in 401 BCE, the Greek contingent numbered roughly 10,000 hoplites and 2,500 light troops, drawn from across the Greek world and commanded by the Spartan Clearchus. They marched from Sardis across Anatolia and down the Euphrates, defeating no army until they met Artaxerxes at Cunaxa, near Babylon. The Greeks routed the troops opposite them, but Cyrus was killed in the battle, leaving his cause — and his mercenaries — stranded some 2,400 km (1,500 mi) from the sea.

The retreat

When the Persian satrap Tissaphernes lured the Greek generals to a conference and had them killed, the soldiers elected new leaders, including Xenophon. The Ten Thousand fought north through the mountains of the Carduchians, endured a brutal Armenian winter with heavy losses to cold and hunger, and finally reached the Black Sea at Trapezus in 400 BCE. Xenophon's famous cry "Thálatta! Thálatta!" — "The sea! The sea!" — on Mount Theches became a byword for joyful deliverance. The survivors returned to Greek service and eventually home after further campaigning in Thrace and western Anatolia.

Significance

The march demonstrated the vulnerability of the Persian Empire to a disciplined Greek army operating deep in its territory, foreshadowing the conquests of Alexander the Great. The Anabasis remains one of the most widely read works of Greek literature and a foundational text of military history.
