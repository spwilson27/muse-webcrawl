Mirror symmetry

Mirror symmetry is a relationship between geometric objects called Calabi–Yau manifolds in string theory. Two such manifolds can have very different geometries yet give rise to identical physics when used as the extra dimensions for a string theory model; the pair are called mirror manifolds.

Background

The idea arose in the late 1980s from work on string dualities, and was dramatized by a 1991 result of Philip Candelas and collaborators, who used a mirror pair to compute the number of rational curves on a quintic threefold — a hard enumerative geometry problem — obtaining the numbers 2875 lines, 609,250 conics and so on, which mathematicians subsequently verified. The result launched a rich interplay between physics and algebraic geometry.

Mathematical theory

Mathematicians have developed mirror symmetry into precise conjectures and theorems, including the SYZ conjecture of Strominger, Yau and Zaslow, which explains the duality via special Lagrangian torus fibrations, and Maxim Kontsevich's homological mirror symmetry, which reformulates it as an equivalence between derived categories of coherent sheaves and Fukaya categories. Mirror symmetry trades complex-structure data on one manifold for symplectic (Kähler) data on its mirror.
