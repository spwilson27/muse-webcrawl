Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff limit

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Mass–radius diagram for compact stars, showing the TOV maximum mass]

The Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff limit (or TOV limit) is the maximum mass of a neutron star beyond which no known force can prevent gravitational collapse to a black hole. It is analogous to the Chandrasekhar limit for white dwarfs, but it is far harder to pin down because the equation of state of matter at nuclear densities is not precisely known.

History

In 1939, Richard Tolman derived the equations of hydrostatic equilibrium for a spherical body in general relativity, and J. Robert Oppenheimer and George Volkoff, in their paper "On Massive Neutron Cores", applied them to neutron matter and obtained a limit of roughly 0.7 solar masses. Their value was an underestimate because it treated the neutrons as an ideal Fermi gas, ignoring the strong nuclear repulsion that stiffens real neutron-star matter.

Modern value

Modern calculations with realistic equations of state, combined with observations — notably the existence of pulsars with masses of about 2.0–2.1 solar masses, such as PSR J0348+0432 and PSR J0740+6620, and the gravitational-wave signal of the 2017 neutron-star merger GW170817, whose remnant collapsed to a black hole — constrain the TOV limit to roughly 2.2–2.3 solar masses for a non-rotating star. Rapid rotation can support a somewhat larger mass temporarily (a "supramassive" or "hypermassive" neutron star) before angular momentum loss triggers collapse. The gap between the heaviest neutron stars and the lightest stellar black holes, once thought to be a clean 3–5 solar-mass "mass gap", has been complicated by gravitational-wave detections of objects within it.
