Weasel word

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A weasel word, or anonymous authority, is an informal term for words and phrases aimed at creating an impression that something specific and meaningful has been said, when in fact only a vague or ambiguous claim has been communicated. Examples include phrases such as "some people say", "most people think", "researchers believe", "experts agree" and unattributed superlatives like "up to" a stated figure. The use of weasel words gives the impression of authority or insight where the source is actually unspecified or dubious.

Origin

The expression derives from the weasel's reputation for sucking the contents out of eggs while leaving the shell apparently intact: just as an emptied egg looks whole, a sentence drained of meaning by weasel words can appear substantial. Stewart Chaplin's 1900 short story "Stained Glass Political Platform" is an early print use, and the term was popularised when Theodore Roosevelt used it in a 1916 speech attacking President Woodrow Wilson's rhetoric.

Forms

Common forms include vague attributions ("scientists say…"), the passive voice used to hide the agent ("mistakes were made"), euphemisms, false ranges ("up to 50% off"), and qualifying adverbs that quietly reverse a claim. In advertising, weasel words allow claims that appear strong but commit the advertiser to very little, such as "helps reduce the appearance of wrinkles".

On Wikipedia

Wikipedia's Manual of Style instructs editors to avoid weasel words, since unattributed formulations such as "some critics claim" violate the neutral point of view policy by presenting opinion without a source. The maintenance template {{who}} and the phrase "attribution needed" are used to flag them.
