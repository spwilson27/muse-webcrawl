Boxing in ancient Greece

Boxing (Greek: πυγμαχία, pygmachia) was a popular combat sport in ancient Greece, practiced since at least the archaic period and introduced into the Olympic Games in 688 BC. It features in Homer's Iliad, which describes a boxing match held during the funeral games of Patroclus, and in a rich record of vase paintings, sculpture and literature.

Rules and equipment

Greek boxers fought without weight classes, rounds or a ring; a bout continued until one man submitted by raising a finger or was unable to continue. There was no scoring system, and blows were aimed almost exclusively at the head. Boxers wrapped their hands with leather thongs (himantes), at first soft strips of ox hide, later harder and sharper straps, and eventually types of gloves; the Roman caestus developed from this tradition into a far more brutal weapon.

Training and competitors

Boxers trained in the palaestra under a paidotribe, practising on punching bags (korykos) and shadow-boxing. Famous victors included Diagoras of Rhodes and Theagenes of Thasos, credited with over a thousand victories across boxing and pankration. Victory at Olympia brought fame, statues and civic honours rather than prize money.

Legacy

Greek boxing influenced Roman gladiatorial combat and, through the survival of its imagery and texts, the modern revival of the sport, even though the modern game descends more directly from English bare-knuckle prizefighting.
