Symphony No. 3 (Copland)

The Symphony No. 3 by Aaron Copland was written between 1944 and 1946 and premiered on October 18, 1946, by the Boston Symphony Orchestra conducted by Serge Koussevitzky, who had commissioned the work through his music foundation. It is widely regarded as the essential American symphony, the culmination of Copland's "populist" style and his most ambitious orchestral statement.

Background

Copland worked on the symphony during the final years of the Second World War, a fact reflected in its tone of sober reflection giving way to triumph. Koussevitzky had long urged Copland to write a major symphony, remarking that whenever he performed a new American symphony he felt it could "tell us something about ourselves". Leonard Bernstein became one of the work's great champions, recording it and conducting it around the world.

Music

The symphony is scored for a large orchestra and lasts about 40 minutes. It is in four movements:

  1. Molto moderato – with simple expression: a slow movement built on spacious, open intervals that establish the work's tonal world.
  2. Allegro molto: a vigorous scherzo with driving rhythms and brilliant brass writing.
  3. Andantino quasi allegretto: a lyrical, contemplative movement growing from a solo flute melody.
  4. Molto deliberato – Allegro risoluto: the finale opens with the music that became Fanfare for the Common Man (1942), woven into a triumphant conclusion.

Copland did not set out to quote the fanfare; rather, the earlier brass-and-percussion piece became the seed of the symphony's peroration, unifying the work's themes and embodying its wartime optimism. The finale's broad, hymn-like climax made the symphony a defining document of mid-century American musical identity.

Reception

The premiere was a success; Koussevitzky called it "the greatest American symphony ever written". Bernstein later cut a passage of the finale in his performances (a cut Copland eventually sanctioned in the printed score). The Third Symphony remains Copland's largest purely orchestral work and a fixture of American orchestral repertoire.
