Henderson–Hasselbalch equation

In chemistry and biochemistry, the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation relates the pH of a chemical solution of a weak acid to the numerical value of the acid dissociation constant, Ka, of the acid and the ratio of the concentrations, [base]/[acid], of the acid and its conjugate base in an equilibrium.

Henderson–Hasselbalch equation

pH = pKa + log10([A−]/[HA])

Named after Lawrence Joseph Henderson and Karl Albert Hasselbalch

The equation can be used to estimate the pH of a buffer solution and to find the equilibrium pH in acid–base reactions. The Henderson–Hasselbalch equation is commonly used to calculate the isoelectric point of proteins.

History

Lawrence Joseph Henderson wrote an equation, in 1908, describing the use of carbonic acid as a buffer solution. Karl Albert Hasselbalch later re-expressed that formula in logarithmic terms, resulting in the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation.

Henderson was a biological chemist and physiologist at Harvard University, where he studied the acid–base balance of blood. Hasselbalch was a Danish chemist who had been studying the pH of blood using one of the first pH meters; he re-expressed Henderson's equation using S. P. L. Sørensen's pH notation, and his form is the one in general use today.

Derivation

The equation is derived from the definition of the acid dissociation constant. For the dissociation of a weak acid HA:

HA ⇌ H+ + A−

The equilibrium constant is:

Ka = [H+][A−] / [HA]

Taking the logarithm of both sides and rearranging gives:

pH = pKa + log10 ( [A−] / [HA] )

where pKa = −log10 Ka. An analogous equation applies to a weak base, giving pOH in terms of pKb.

Limitations

The Henderson–Hasselbalch equation assumes that the concentrations of acid and conjugate base at equilibrium equal their formal (prepared) concentrations. This approximation breaks down when the acid or base is relatively strong (pKa differing by more than a few units from the target pH), when concentrations are very dilute, or when other equilibria (such as the autoionisation of water) contribute significantly. It also ignores activity coefficients, so it is most accurate for dilute solutions.

Applications

The equation is widely used in analytical chemistry to design buffer solutions with a target pH, in biochemistry to predict the charge state of ionisable groups on amino acids and proteins, and in pharmacology, where the ionisation state of a drug at physiological pH affects its solubility and membrane permeability (the pH-partition hypothesis).
