Alchemy

Nature: Protoscience and philosophical tradition
Key goals: Chrysopoeia (gold-making), panacea, elixir of life
Practised: Antiquity to the 18th century

Alchemy (from Arabic al-kīmiyā) is an ancient branch of natural philosophy, a philosophical and protoscientific tradition that was practised throughout Europe, Africa, China, and Asia. Alchemists attempted to purify, mature, and perfect certain materials, pursuing the transmutation of base metals into gold, the discovery of a universal cure for disease, and the prolongation of life.

History

Western alchemy grew out of Hellenistic Egypt, where it fused Greek philosophy with Egyptian metallurgical crafts; figures such as Zosimos of Panopolis systematized its lore by around 300 CE. It flourished in the Islamic world, where scholars including Jābir ibn Hayyān (Geber) developed laboratory techniques such as distillation and crystallization. Transmitted to Latin Europe in the 12th century, alchemy was pursued by Albertus Magnus, Roger Bacon, and later Paracelsus, who redirected it toward medicine (iatrochemistry).

Concepts

Central alchemical aims included the philosopher's stone, a legendary substance capable of turning lead into gold and granting immortality; the elixir of life; and the alkahest, a universal solvent. Alchemical theory drew on the four elements and ideas of prima materia, and its texts used elaborate allegory and symbol — the ouroboros, the hermaphrodite, the union of opposites. Chinese alchemy, focused on longevity elixirs, led to discoveries including gunpowder; Indian rasayana pursued similar goals.

Legacy

By the 18th century, experimental work by figures such as Robert Boyle and Antoine Lavoisier transformed alchemy into modern chemistry. Though its central goals were unattainable, alchemy bequeathed chemistry much of its laboratory apparatus and technique, and it retained cultural influence through Jungian psychology and its rich symbolic art.
