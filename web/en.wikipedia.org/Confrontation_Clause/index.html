Confrontation Clause

The Confrontation Clause of the Sixth Amendment to the United States Constitution provides:

"In all criminal prosecutions, the accused shall enjoy the right… to be confronted with the witnesses against him…"

The clause is generally interpreted as giving a criminal defendant the right to be present at trial and to cross-examine witnesses who testify against them. It was made applicable to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment.

Purpose and history

The right of confrontation has roots in English common law's reaction against trials based on ex parte affidavits and depositions, such as the 1603 treason trial of Sir Walter Raleigh, who was convicted largely on an accomplice's out-of-court statement. The clause secures face-to-face encounter, oath, and cross-examination — which Wigmore called the greatest legal engine ever invented for the discovery of truth — and ensures testimony is given under circumstances of the accused's presence.

Testimonial statements: Crawford v. Washington

In Crawford v. Washington, 541 U.S. 36 (2004), the Supreme Court overhauled the doctrine, holding that out-of-court "testimonial" statements by a witness who does not appear at trial are barred by the Confrontation Clause unless the witness is unavailable and the defendant had a prior opportunity to cross-examine. The decision displaced the earlier reliability-based framework of Ohio v. Roberts (1980). Later cases — Davis v. Washington (2006), Melendez-Diaz v. Massachusetts (2009), Michigan v. Bryant (2011), and Ohio v. Clark (2015) — have refined the "primary purpose" test for distinguishing testimonial from non-testimonial statements.

Exceptions and related rights

The clause does not guarantee an absolute right to physically confront every witness: exceptions include forfeiture by wrongdoing (a defendant who procures a witness's absence), and limited accommodations such as one-way closed-circuit testimony by child witnesses where case-specific necessity is shown (Maryland v. Craig, 1990). The Compulsory Process Clause of the same amendment supplies the complementary right to obtain witnesses in one's favour.
