In astronomy, axial tilt, also known as obliquity, is the angle between an object's rotational axis and its orbital axis, or, equivalently, the angle between its equatorial plane and orbital plane. It differs from orbital inclination. At an obliquity of 0 degrees, the two axes point in the same direction; that is, the rotational axis is perpendicular to the orbital plane.

Earth's obliquity is currently about 23.44°, and this tilt — not Earth's varying distance from the Sun — is the principal cause of the seasons. Over a cycle of about 41,000 years, the tilt oscillates between 22.1° and 24.5°, one of the Milankovitch cycles that influence the timing and intensity of ice ages.

Seasons

As Earth orbits the Sun, its tilted axis keeps pointing in roughly the same direction in space (toward Polaris). For half the year the Northern Hemisphere leans toward the Sun and receives more direct sunlight and longer days, producing summer there and winter in the Southern Hemisphere; six months later the situation is reversed. The tropics of Cancer and Capricorn are defined by the axial tilt, marking the latitudes where the Sun can appear directly overhead, and the Arctic and Antarctic circles mark where the Sun can remain above or below the horizon for a full day.

Stability and variation

Earth's tilt is stabilized by the Moon, whose gravitational influence prevents the chaotic swings seen on Mars, whose obliquity has ranged from near 0° to over 60°. Earth's tilt is currently decreasing slowly, by about 0.47 arcseconds per year. Other planets have dramatic tilts: Uranus rotates on its side with an obliquity of about 98°, while Mercury's is nearly zero.

Origin

Axial tilts are thought to arise from the collisions and accretion events that built the planets. Earth's tilt may have been set or modified by the giant impact hypothesized to have formed the Moon.
