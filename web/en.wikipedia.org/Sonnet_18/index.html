Sonnet 18

Sonnet 18 in the 1609 Quarto

Sonnet 18 is one of the 154 sonnets written by William Shakespeare and published in the 1609 Quarto. It is one of the "procreation sonnets" (Sonnets 1–17 being addressed most urgently to that theme, Sonnet 18 turning instead to the promise that verse itself will immortalise the beloved) addressed to the "Fair Youth". Opening with the famous line "Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?", it is among the most celebrated poems in English.

Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?
Thou art more lovely and more temperate:
Rough winds do shake the darling buds of May,
And summer's lease hath all too short a date:
Sometime too hot the eye of heaven shines,
And often is his gold complexion dimm'd;
And every fair from fair sometime declines,
By chance or nature's changing course untrimm'd;
But thy eternal summer shall not fade
Nor lose possession of that fair thou owest;
Nor shall Death brag thou wander'st in his shade,
When in eternal lines to time thou growest:
So long as men can breathe or eyes can see,
So long lives this and this gives life to thee.

Analysis

The poem follows the Shakespearean sonnet form: three quatrains and a concluding couplet, rhyming abab cdcd efef gg, in iambic pentameter. The speaker rejects the comparison of the youth to a summer's day because summer is imperfect — too brief, too hot, subject to decline — whereas the youth's beauty, preserved "in eternal lines", will not fade. The couplet asserts the poem's own power: as long as people read it, the youth lives. The boast has proved self-fulfilling, as the sonnet remains one of the most widely quoted and anthologised short poems in the language.
