Second-order logic

Second-order logic permits quantification over sets and relations, not only individuals.

In logic and mathematics, second-order logic is an extension of first-order logic, which itself is an extension of propositional logic. Second-order logic is in turn extended by higher-order logics and type theory.

Quantification

First-order logic quantifies only over individual elements of the domain of discourse. Second-order logic also quantifies over subsets of the domain, relations between elements, and functions from the domain to itself. This extra expressive power allows, for example, a formulation of the induction principle for the natural numbers as a single axiom quantifying over all sets of numbers, rather than an infinite axiom schema.

Expressive power

With its standard ("full") semantics, second-order logic is far more expressive than first-order logic: it can characterise the natural numbers and the real numbers up to isomorphism (categoricity, shown by Dedekind), and can express properties such as finiteness and well-ordering that first-order logic cannot.

Metalogical results

This power comes at a price. Second-order logic with full semantics has no complete, effective proof system: the set of validities is not recursively enumerable, and both the compactness theorem and the Löwenheim–Skolem theorem fail. Under Henkin (general) semantics, by contrast, second-order logic collapses in deductive strength to many-sorted first-order logic and regains completeness. Gödel's incompleteness theorems also shape debate about whether second-order "logic" deserves the name; Quine famously dismissed it as "set theory in sheep's clothing".

Uses

Second-order logic is used in the foundations of mathematics, in Lindström's theorem characterising first-order logic, in descriptive complexity theory — where Fagin's theorem equates the class NP with existential second-order logic — and in formal semantics for natural language.
