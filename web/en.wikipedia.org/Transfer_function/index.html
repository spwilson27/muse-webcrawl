In engineering, a transfer function (also known as system function or network function) of a system, sub-system, or component is a mathematical function that theoretically models the system's output for each possible input. It is used in the mathematical modeling of dynamic systems, principally in control theory and signal processing, for systems that can be treated as linear and time-invariant.

Definition

For a single-input single-output LTI system described by a linear differential equation with constant coefficients, the transfer function H(s) is the ratio of the Laplace transform of the output Y(s) to the Laplace transform of the input X(s), assuming all initial conditions are zero: H(s) = Y(s)/X(s). Because the system is linear, H(s) is a rational function of the complex variable s — a ratio of polynomials. The roots of the numerator are the system's zeros; the roots of the denominator (the characteristic polynomial) are its poles.

Interpretation and use

Poles determine the system's natural modes and stability: for a continuous-time system, all poles must lie in the left half of the s-plane for bounded-input bounded-output stability. The frequency response is obtained by evaluating H(s) on the imaginary axis (s = jω), giving the gain and phase shift applied to a sinusoid of frequency ω; it is conventionally displayed as Bode plots or Nyquist plots. In the time domain, the transfer function corresponds via the inverse Laplace transform to the impulse response, and the output is the convolution of the input with that response. In block diagrams, systems in series multiply their transfer functions, systems in parallel add them, and feedback interconnections simplify algebraically — allowing engineers to design compensators (such as PID controllers) directly in the frequency domain. For discrete-time systems, the analogous tool is the z-transform transfer function H(z).

Limitations

Transfer functions apply strictly to linear time-invariant systems and hide internal state information: different state realizations can share a transfer function (through pole–zero cancellation), so controllability and observability cannot be judged from it alone. Nonlinear or time-varying systems require other methods, such as state-space models, describing functions, or Volterra series.
