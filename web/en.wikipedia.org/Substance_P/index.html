Substance P (SP) is an undecapeptide — a chain of 11 amino acid residues — belonging to the tachykinin family of neuropeptides. It is widely distributed in the central and peripheral nervous systems, where it acts as a neurotransmitter and neuromodulator, and it is best known for its roles in transmitting pain signals and in triggering the vomiting reflex.

Discovery and chemistry

Substance P was discovered in 1931 by Ulf von Euler and John Gaddum in extracts of horse intestine and brain that caused intestinal contraction and lowered blood pressure; the "P" stood for the powdered preparation. Its sequence was determined in 1971. It is encoded by the TAC1 gene, which also gives rise to other tachykinins, and it acts preferentially on the neurokinin-1 (NK1) receptor.

Functions

Released from the terminals of specific sensory nerves, substance P conveys nociceptive (pain) signals to the spinal cord and brain. In peripheral tissues it drives neurogenic inflammation: dilating blood vessels, increasing vascular permeability, and recruiting immune cells. In the brainstem it participates in the emetic reflex, and in the forebrain it modulates stress, anxiety, and mood pathways — which made NK1 antagonists candidates for antidepressant development. SP also stimulates smooth muscle contraction, salivation, and cell proliferation.

Clinical significance

The NK1 receptor antagonist aprepitant blocks substance P and is used clinically to prevent chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting. Altered substance P signaling has been investigated in chronic pain, inflammatory bowel disease, depression, and migraine, though many candidate drugs in these areas failed in trials.
