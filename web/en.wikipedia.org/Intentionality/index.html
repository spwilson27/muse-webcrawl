Intentionality

Intentionality is the power of minds and mental states to be about, to represent, or to stand for things, properties and states of affairs. To have intentionality is to be directed toward an object or state of affairs. It is a feature of many mental states, such as thinking about the Eiffel Tower, believing a proposition, desiring a slice of pizza, or perceiving an object.

History of the concept

The scholastic philosophers discussed intentionality in connection with the existence of objects in the mind. The term was revived by Franz Brentano in his Psychology from an Empirical Standpoint (1874), where he proposed it as the distinguishing mark of the mental: every mental phenomenon is characterized by reference to a content, direction toward an object — its "intentional inexistence". Brentano's student Edmund Husserl made intentionality central to phenomenology.

Contemporary philosophy of mind

Intentionality is central to debates about consciousness, mental representation, and naturalism. Naturalistic theories attempt to explain intentional content in causal, informational, or functional terms; John Searle distinguished intrinsic intentionality of minds from the derived intentionality of words, maps, and other artifacts. A persistent puzzle is how physical states of the brain can be about things at all, and how content is fixed when the object of a thought does not exist (as in thinking about unicorns).
