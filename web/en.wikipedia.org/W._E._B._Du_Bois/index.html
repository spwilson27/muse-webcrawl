William Edward Burghardt Du Bois (23 February 1868 – 27 August 1963) was an American sociologist, historian, civil rights activist, Pan-Africanist, author, and editor. In 1895 he became the first African American to earn a PhD from Harvard University. He was a co-founder of the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People (NAACP) in 1909 and the longtime editor of its magazine, The Crisis.

Scholarship

Du Bois's landmark book The Souls of Black Folk (1903) introduced the concepts of "double consciousness" and the "color line", and famously declared that "the problem of the twentieth century is the problem of the color-line". At Atlanta University he directed a pioneering programme of sociological research into African American life, including the Atlanta Studies and the data visualizations presented at the 1900 Paris Exposition. His history Black Reconstruction in America (1935) challenged the prevailing Dunning School interpretation of Reconstruction.

Activism

Du Bois famously disagreed with Booker T. Washington's "Atlanta Compromise", insisting instead on full civil and political rights and the cultivation of a "Talented Tenth" of educated black leaders. He helped organise the Niagara Movement in 1905 and a series of Pan-African conferences, beginning in London in 1900, that laid groundwork for African independence movements.

Later life

Increasingly drawn to socialism and embittered by American racism and Cold War persecution — he was indicted in 1951 as an unregistered foreign agent and acquitted, but denied a passport for much of the decade — Du Bois emigrated to Ghana at the invitation of Kwame Nkrumah in 1961, became a Ghanaian citizen, and died in Accra on the eve of the March on Washington in August 1963.
