Demerara

Demerara is a historical region in the Guianas, on the north coast of South America, now part of the country of Guyana. It was a Dutch colony until 1815 and a county of British Guiana from 1838 to 1966. It is located around the lower courses of the Demerara River, and its capital was Georgetown, on the river's mouth.

History

The Dutch established a colony on the Demerara River in the eighteenth century, organized around sugar plantations worked by enslaved Africans. Demerara, together with the neighboring colonies of Essequibo and Berbice, changed hands several times during the wars of the era before being formally ceded by the Netherlands to Britain in 1814–1815. In 1831 the three colonies were united as British Guiana. The Demerara rebellion of 1823, an uprising of enslaved people, was a significant event in the history of abolition in the British Empire.

Demerara sugar

The region gives its name to Demerara sugar, a type of minimally refined raw cane sugar with large, golden-brown crystals and a mild molasses flavor, originally produced in the colony. Most modern "Demerara-style" sugar is now produced elsewhere, notably in Mauritius, but retains the name and characteristic coarse crystal form. Demerara is also the name of a style of rum produced in Guyana, distilled in the region around the Demerara River, including from historic wooden pot and coffey stills.
