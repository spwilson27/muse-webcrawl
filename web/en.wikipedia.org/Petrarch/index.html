Francesco Petrarca (20 July 1304 – 18/19 July 1374), commonly anglicised as Petrarch, was an Italian scholar and poet of the early Renaissance and one of the earliest humanists. His rediscovery of Cicero's letters is often credited with initiating the 14th-century Renaissance. Petrarch is best known for his Italian lyric poetry, above all the Canzoniere, a sequence of 366 poems largely devoted to his idealised beloved, Laura.

Life

Petrarch was born in Arezzo, the son of an exiled Florentine notary, and grew up near Avignon, where the papal court then resided. He studied law at Montpellier and Bologna but abandoned it for literature after his father's death. In 1327, by tradition on Good Friday in the church of Sainte-Claire in Avignon, he first saw Laura, the woman celebrated in his verse for the rest of his life. He travelled widely in search of classical manuscripts, climbing Mont Ventoux in 1336 — an ascent often taken as a symbol of the new humanist spirit. In 1341 he was crowned poet laureate in Rome, the first such coronation since antiquity. He spent his final years at Arquà in the Euganean Hills.

Works and influence

Petrarch valued his Latin works — the epic Africa, the dialogue Secretum, and his collections of letters — above his Italian poems, yet it was the Canzoniere that shaped European lyric poetry for centuries: the Petrarchan sonnet form and its conventions of unrequited love were imitated across Europe, notably by Wyatt, Surrey and Shakespeare. His conception of the preceding centuries as a "dark age" and his programme of reviving classical learning profoundly influenced Renaissance education.
