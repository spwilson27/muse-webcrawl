United States presidential line of succession

The order in which officials succeed to the presidency is set by the Presidential Succession Act of 1947.

The United States presidential line of succession is the order in which officials of the United States federal government assume the powers and duties of the office of president of the United States if the incumbent president becomes incapacitated, dies, resigns, or is removed from office. The order of succession specifies that the office passes to the vice president; if the vice presidency is simultaneously vacant, or if the vice president is also incapacitated, the powers and duties pass to the speaker of the House of Representatives, president pro tempore of the Senate, and then Cabinet secretaries, depending on eligibility.

Presidential succession is referred to multiple times in the U.S. Constitution: Article II, Section 1, Clause 6, the Twentieth Amendment, and the Twenty-fifth Amendment. The vice president is designated as first in the presidential line of succession by the Article II succession clause, which also authorizes Congress to provide for a line of succession beyond the vice president. The current Presidential Succession Act was adopted in 1947 and last revised in 2006.

Constitutional basis

The Article II succession clause provides that "in Case of the Removal of the President from Office, or of his Death, Resignation, or Inability to discharge the Powers and Duties of the said Office, the Same shall devolve on the Vice President, and the Congress may by Law provide for the Case of Removal, Death, Resignation or Inability, both of the President and Vice President, declaring what Officer shall then act as President." The Twenty-fifth Amendment, ratified in 1967, clarified that the vice president becomes president (rather than merely acting president) when the president dies, resigns, or is removed, and established procedures for filling a vice-presidential vacancy and for declarations of presidential inability.

History of succession law

The first presidential succession act, passed in 1792, placed the president pro tempore of the Senate and then the speaker of the House in line after the vice president. The Presidential Succession Act of 1886 removed congressional officers from the line and placed Cabinet secretaries next, in the order their departments were created. President Harry S. Truman, believing elected officials should precede appointed ones, proposed restoring the speaker and president pro tempore to the front of the line; the 1947 act did so, placing the speaker ahead of the president pro tempore.

Eligibility

To be eligible to serve as acting president, a person must meet the constitutional requirements for the presidency: be a natural-born citizen of the United States, at least 35 years old, and a resident within the United States for at least 14 years. Cabinet officers who do not meet these requirements — for example, naturalized citizens — are skipped over in the line. Officers serving in an acting capacity are also ineligible under the statute.

Acting president

Officials in the statutory line other than the vice president serve only as acting president. On three occasions, vice presidents briefly became acting president under Section 3 of the Twenty-fifth Amendment while the president underwent medical procedures: George H. W. Bush for Ronald Reagan in 1985, Dick Cheney for George W. Bush in 2002 and 2007, and Kamala Harris for Joe Biden in 2021 — Harris being the first woman to hold presidential powers.
