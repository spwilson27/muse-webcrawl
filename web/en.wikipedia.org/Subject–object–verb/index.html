In linguistic typology, subject–object–verb (SOV) is a sentence structure in which the subject comes first, the object second, and the verb last: the equivalent of "She fruit eats." It is the most common word order among the languages of the world, found in roughly 45 percent of languages in large typological samples, and has been argued (following Joseph Greenberg) possibly to be the ancestral or default order from which others derive.

Examples

SOV is characteristic of Japanese, Korean, Mongolian, Turkic languages such as Turkish, Indo-Aryan languages such as Hindi and Bengali, Dravidian languages, Iranian languages such as Persian, and many languages of the Caucasus, Ethiopia, and indigenous America (e.g., Quechua). Ancient languages including Latin, Sanskrit, and Ancient Greek permitted SOV as their unmarked prose order, though with considerable flexibility due to rich case marking.

Properties

SOV correlates with a cluster of typological traits identified by Greenberg: postpositions instead of prepositions, genitives and adjectives before nouns, auxiliaries after main verbs, and subordinate clauses before the main clause — a broadly "head-final" profile. Because the verb comes last, listeners or readers must hold subject and object in memory until the predicate resolves the clause, a processing profile studied extensively in psycholinguistics. SOV languages typically mark the object with an accusative or dative case or particle — Japanese o, Korean eul/reul, Turkish accusative suffixes — though word order alone can serve where case is absent.

See also

Contrast with subject–verb–object (English, Chinese), verb–subject–object (Arabic, Irish), and the rarer orders such as object–subject–verb found in some Amazonian languages.
