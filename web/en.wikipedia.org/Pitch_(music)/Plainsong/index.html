Plainsong

Plainsong (also called plainchant) is a body of chants used in the liturgies of the Western Church: a single, unaccompanied melodic line sung in unison, with flexible rhythm following the words rather than a fixed meter. The best-known repertory is Gregorian chant, the chant of the Roman Rite, alongside related traditions such as Ambrosian, Mozarabic and Old Roman chant.

Characteristics

Plainsong melodies are built from the church modes (scales) rather than major and minor keys, and are classified by their liturgical function — antiphons, responsories, hymns, psalm tones, and the chants of the Mass. Notation developed from neumes written above the text, later placed on four-line staves; modern chant books, such as the Liber Usualis, use square notation. Chant is sung to Latin texts, syllabically or with many notes flowing over a single syllable (melisma).

History

The repertory coalesced in the early Middle Ages, blending Roman and Gallican practice under Carolingian patronage, and tradition attributed it to Pope Gregory I. Plainsong formed the foundation of Western art music: polyphony grew from chant in the medieval period, and chant melodies served as cantus firmi for centuries of composition. After a period of decline and corruption of editions, the monks of Solesmes Abbey led a nineteenth- and twentieth-century restoration of chant performance and scholarship.
