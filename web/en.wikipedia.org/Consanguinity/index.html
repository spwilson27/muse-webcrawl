Consanguinity

Consanguinity (from Latin consanguinitas, "blood relationship") is the characteristic of having a kinship relationship with another person through a common ancestor. It is a fundamental concept in law, genetics, anthropology and religion, where degrees of consanguinity determine rules about marriage, inheritance and succession.

Degrees of kinship

Legal systems compute kinship in degrees. In the civil-law (Roman) method, one counts generations up from one person to the common ancestor and back down to the other: parent and child are related in the first degree, siblings in the second, uncle/aunt and nephew/niece in the third, and first cousins in the fourth. The older canon-law method counted only the longer line, making first cousins related in the second degree.

Genetics

Relatives share a fraction of their genes identical by descent — on average 1/2 for parent–child and full siblings, 1/8 for first cousins. The coefficient of relationship and the inbreeding coefficient quantify this. Children of consanguineous unions have increased homozygosity and a modestly elevated risk of autosomal recessive disorders; for first-cousin offspring the additional risk of congenital anomalies is commonly estimated at roughly 1.7–2.8 percentage points above background.

Marriage law and custom

Marriage between close relatives is prohibited nearly everywhere (incest taboo); cousin marriage rules vary widely. It is legal in most of Europe, and in much of the Middle East, North Africa and South Asia cousin marriage is common and traditionally preferred — global estimates suggest around 10% of marriages are between second cousins or closer relatives. In the United States, first-cousin marriage is prohibited in about half the states.

Historical notes

The Catholic Church historically prohibited marriage within wide degrees of consanguinity (reduced to the fourth degree by the Fourth Lateran Council in 1215). Dynastic inbreeding, such as in the Spanish Habsburgs, is a famous illustration of its genetic consequences.
