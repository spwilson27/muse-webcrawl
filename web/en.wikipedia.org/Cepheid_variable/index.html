Cepheid variable

A Cepheid variable (or Cepheid) is a type of variable star that pulsates radially, varying in both diameter and temperature, which results in changes in brightness with a well-defined stable period and amplitude. Cepheids are important cosmic benchmarks for scaling galactic and extragalactic distances. A strong direct relationship between a Cepheid variable's luminosity and pulsation period, established by Henrietta Swan Leavitt in 1912, secures Cepheids as important standard candles for measuring distances to galaxies, enabling the determination of the Hubble constant and the size of the observable universe.

History

The prototype, Delta Cephei, was shown to be variable by John Goodricke in 1784. In 1908–1912, Henrietta Swan Leavitt, studying Cepheids in the Small Magellanic Cloud at Harvard College Observatory, discovered that their apparent brightness correlated with their pulsation period; because all the stars were at essentially the same distance, period must correlate with intrinsic luminosity. Calibration of this relation allowed Ejnar Hertzsprung, Harlow Shapley and later Edwin Hubble to measure cosmic distances: Hubble's 1924 identification of Cepheids in the Andromeda "nebula" proved it was a separate galaxy, vastly enlarging the known universe. Walter Baade's 1952 revision of the calibration, distinguishing classical Cepheids from the fainter Type II (W Virginis) stars, doubled the cosmic distance scale overnight.

Classes

Classical Cepheids (Type I) are young, massive (4–20 solar masses), Population I yellow supergiants with periods of about 1–100 days and luminosities up to ~100,000 times the Sun's; examples include Delta Cephei, Eta Aquilae and Polaris. Type II Cepheids are old, low-mass Population II stars, subdivided by period into BL Herculis, W Virginis and RV Tauri variables. Anomalous Cepheids occur in dwarf spheroidal galaxies and the Magellanic Clouds.

Mechanism

Pulsation is driven by the kappa mechanism: in a layer of partially ionised helium within the star, compression increases opacity, trapping heat and pushing the envelope outward; expansion then lets helium recombine, opacity falls, and the layer contracts again. Cepheids occupy the instability strip on the Hertzsprung–Russell diagram, where this valve-like behaviour sustains regular oscillation.

Role in the distance ladder

Because period can be measured without knowing distance, the period–luminosity relation gives absolute magnitude, and hence distance from apparent magnitude. Cepheids anchor the cosmic distance ladder: they calibrate Type Ia supernovae in the same galaxies, which extend measurements to cosmological distances. Hubble Space Telescope observations of Cepheids in dozens of galaxies underpin modern determinations of the Hubble constant near 70 km/s/Mpc, though an unresolved "Hubble tension" with early-universe measurements persists.
