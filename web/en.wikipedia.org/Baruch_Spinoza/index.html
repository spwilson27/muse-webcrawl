Baruch (de) Spinoza (24 November 1632 – 21 February 1677), also known under his Latinized pen name Benedictus de Spinoza, was a Dutch philosopher of Portuguese-Jewish origin. One of the foremost exponents of 17th-century Rationalism and one of the leading figures of the Dutch Golden Age, Spinoza laid the groundwork for the 18th-century Enlightenment and modern biblical criticism.

Spinoza, philosopher of the Dutch Golden Age.

Life

Spinoza was born in Amsterdam into a family of Sephardic Jews who had fled the Portuguese Inquisition. In 1656, aged 23, he was expelled (cherem) from the Amsterdam Jewish community for his heterodox views. He earned his living grinding lenses, declined a professorship at Heidelberg to preserve his intellectual freedom, and died in The Hague in 1677, probably of a lung illness aggravated by glass dust.

Philosophy

Spinoza's magnum opus, the Ethics (published posthumously in 1677), is written in the geometric manner, with definitions, axioms, and propositions modelled on Euclid. It argues that there is only one substance — God or Nature (Deus sive Natura) — of which everything else is a mode. Mind and body are the same thing conceived under the attributes of thought and extension. Human bondage arises from passive affects; freedom comes through understanding necessity and the intellectual love of God.

His Theological-Political Treatise (1670), published anonymously, defended freedom of thought and speech, subjected Scripture to historical criticism, and argued for a secular democratic state — views that led his work to be banned and placed on the Catholic Church's Index of Forbidden Books.
