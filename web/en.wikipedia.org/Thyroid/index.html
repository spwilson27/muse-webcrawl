The thyroid, or thyroid gland, is an endocrine gland in vertebrates. In humans it is in the neck and consists of two connected lobes. The lower two thirds of the lobes are joined by a thin band of tissue called the thyroid isthmus, giving the gland the shape of a butterfly or bow tie. It weighs about 25 grams in adults and is among the largest of the endocrine glands.

Structure and function

The gland is composed of spherical follicles lined with follicular cells and filled with colloid containing thyroglobulin. The thyroid secretes three hormones: thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3), which regulate the metabolic rate, growth, and development, and calcitonin, produced by the parafollicular (C) cells, which plays a role in calcium homeostasis. T4 and T3 synthesis requires iodine, absorbed from the diet and concentrated by the gland. Secretion is controlled by thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) from the anterior pituitary, itself regulated by thyrotropin-releasing hormone (TRH) from the hypothalamus — the hypothalamic–pituitary–thyroid axis — with negative feedback by circulating T4 and T3.

Clinical significance

Thyroid disorders are common. Hypothyroidism (underactivity) most often results from Hashimoto's thyroiditis or iodine deficiency — historically the leading cause of preventable intellectual disability worldwide — and causes fatigue, weight gain, and cold intolerance; it is treated with levothyroxine. Hyperthyroidism (overactivity) is most often caused by Graves' disease and causes weight loss, tremor, heat intolerance, and palpitations. Enlargement of the gland is called a goitre. Nodules are common and occasionally malignant: thyroid cancers include papillary, follicular, medullary, and anaplastic types, generally managed with surgery, radioactive iodine, and hormone suppression. Function is assessed with blood tests measuring TSH and free T4, supplemented by imaging and fine-needle biopsy.

History

Goitres were described in ancient China and treated with seaweed — an iodine source — centuries ago. The gland received its name (from Greek thyreos, "shield") in the 17th century. In the 19th century, Gull and Ord described myxoedema, Murray successfully treated it with thyroid extract (1891), and Kendall isolated thyroxine in 1914.
