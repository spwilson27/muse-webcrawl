Plantations of Ireland

The planted counties of Ireland, concentrated in Ulster and Munster.

The plantations of Ireland were the confiscations of land by the English crown and its colonisation with settlers from England and Scotland during the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. They followed earlier, less systematic settlement attempts in the Middle Ages and were a central instrument in the Tudor conquest of Ireland and the extension of English law, Protestant religion, and English-speaking culture across the island.

Background

By the early sixteenth century, direct English control in Ireland was largely confined to the Pale around Dublin. The Tudor monarchs, beginning with Henry VIII, sought to consolidate control through the policy of surrender and regrant, but rebellion and resistance led to a more coercive strategy: the seizure of rebel lands and their redistribution to loyal "undertakers" who undertook to settle them with English tenants.

The Munster Plantation

The first large-scale plantation followed the Desmond Rebellions (1569–73 and 1579–83) in Munster. After the defeat of the Earl of Desmond, some 300,000 acres of confiscated land were granted to English undertakers, including Sir Walter Raleigh, who received lands around Youghal, and the poet Edmund Spenser, who held Kilcolman Castle. The settlement was devastated during the Nine Years' War but was renewed after 1598.

The Ulster Plantation

The most consequential plantation began in 1609, following the Flight of the Earls in 1607, when the Gaelic lords Hugh O'Neill and Rory O'Donnell fled to continental Europe. Six counties — Armagh, Cavan, Coleraine (later Londonderry), Donegal, Fermanagh, and Tyrone — were confiscated and settled by English and especially Scottish migrants. The city of Derry was rebuilt by the livery companies of London and renamed Londonderry. The Plantation of Ulster created a durable Protestant, British-identifying population in the north-east whose descendants remain central to the politics of Northern Ireland.

Later plantations and consequences

Further confiscations followed the Irish Confederate Wars and the Cromwellian conquest (1649–53), under which Catholic landownership fell from about 60 per cent of Ireland in 1641 to roughly 8–9 per cent by the end of the century, reinforced by the Penal Laws. The plantations transformed Ireland's demography, landholding, language, and confessional balance, and their legacies — contested land, sectarian division, and partition in 1921 — shaped Irish history into the twenty-first century.
