Resulting trust

A resulting trust is an implied trust in English and Commonwealth trust law that arises where property is transferred in circumstances in which the recipient is not intended to enjoy the property beneficially. Unlike an express trust, it arises by operation of law, traditionally explained either by the presumed intention of the transferor or, on a modern analysis, by the absence of any intention to benefit the recipient — the equitable interest "results" back to the transferor.

Categories

Following the analysis of Lord Browne-Wilkinson in Westdeutsche Landesbank v Islington LBC (1996), resulting trusts are conventionally divided into two classes. Presumed resulting trusts arise where property is purchased in the name of another, or voluntarily transferred into another's name; equity presumes the provider did not intend a gift (subject to the countervailing presumption of advancement in some relationships). Automatic resulting trusts arise where an express trust fails to dispose of the whole beneficial interest, so that the surplus is held for the settlor or the settlor's estate.

Distinguishing other trusts

A resulting trust differs from a constructive trust, which is imposed by law to prevent unconscionable conduct regardless of intention, and from an express trust, which is deliberately created. Resulting trusts are frequently pleaded in disputes over jointly purchased homes and failed transfers, and they interact with claims in unjust enrichment.
