Teller–Ulam design

The Teller–Ulam design is the technical concept behind most modern thermonuclear weapons (hydrogen bombs). It is a staged radiation-implosion design in which a fission primary's X-ray energy is used to compress and ignite a physically separate secondary stage containing thermonuclear fuel. The design is named after physicists Edward Teller and Stanislaw Ulam, who developed the key ideas at Los Alamos in 1951. Equivalent designs were developed independently in the Soviet Union, the United Kingdom, France, and China.

Basic concept

In a Teller–Ulam weapon, the primary (a fission bomb) and the secondary (a capsule of fusion fuel, typically lithium-6 deuteride, surrounding a fissionable "spark plug") are placed inside a heavy radiation case, often called the hohlraum. When the primary detonates, most of its energy emerges briefly as X-rays, which fill the case and expose the secondary's surface uniformly. The outer layer of the secondary, the pusher/tamper, ablates violently, and the reaction drives an implosion that compresses the fusion fuel to extreme density and temperature. A fission spark plug at the centre of the secondary also ignites, heating the fuel from the inside and initiating fusion burn.

History

Early designs for a "Super" bomb, based on igniting a large volume of liquid deuterium by a fission explosion, proved impractical because the fuel lost energy by radiation as fast as it gained it. In early 1951 Ulam proposed staging: using one bomb to compress a second, physically separate mass of fuel. Teller soon suggested that X-rays from the primary, rather than material debris, could perform the compression — the insight known as radiation implosion. The first test of the principle, the Greenhouse George shot of May 1951, was followed by the full-scale Ivy Mike test of 1 November 1952, which yielded 10.4 megatons using liquid deuterium. The design was weaponised with solid lithium deuteride in the Castle Bravo test of 1954.

Further stages and variants

The concept can be extended to tertiary stages, each driven by the radiation of the previous one; the Soviet Tsar Bomba was a three-stage device. Modern warheads miniaturise the same principles. Closely related physics underlies inertial confinement fusion experiments, in which laser-driven hohlraums implode small fuel capsules.

Secrecy and independent invention

The details of the design remained highly classified for decades and were the subject of the 1979 United States v. Progressive, Inc. case concerning a magazine article by Howard Morland. Because radiation implosion is the only known route to compact high-yield thermonuclear weapons, all five declared nuclear-weapon states are understood to employ variants of it.
