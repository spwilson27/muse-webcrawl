The Battle of Mons was the first major action of the British Expeditionary Force (BEF) in the First World War. It was a subsidiary action of the Battle of the Frontiers, in which the Allies clashed with Germany on the French borders. At Mons, the British Army attempted to hold the line of the Mons–Condé Canal against the advancing German 1st Army.

Background

The BEF under Sir John French had deployed on the left of the French Fifth Army. On 23 August 1914, General Alexander von Kluck's 1st Army, advancing through Belgium in accordance with the Schlieffen Plan, collided with the two British corps along the canal.

The battle

British regular infantry, with rapid rifle fire so intense the Germans believed they faced machine guns, inflicted heavy casualties on the attacking Germans at the canal crossings. However, the retreat of the French on the British right exposed the position, and the BEF was ordered to fall back, beginning the long Great Retreat toward the Marne.

Legacy

The battle generated the legend of the Angels of Mons, a story that bowmen or angels had intervened to protect the British soldiers. British casualties were about 1,600; German losses were considerably higher, often estimated at 2,000–5,000.
