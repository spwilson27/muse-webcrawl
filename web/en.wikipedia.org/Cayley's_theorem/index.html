Cayley's theorem

In group theory, Cayley's theorem, named in honour of Arthur Cayley, states that every group G is isomorphic to a subgroup of a symmetric group. More specifically, G is isomorphic to a subgroup of the symmetric group whose elements are the permutations of the underlying set of G. It follows that any finite group of order n is a subgroup of the symmetric group Sn.

Proof

For each g in G, left multiplication by g defines a map Lg: G → G, x ↦ gx, which is a permutation of G. The assignment g ↦ Lg is an injective group homomorphism from G into the symmetric group on G, because Lgh = Lg ∘ Lh and Lg is the identity only when g is the identity element. Hence G is isomorphic to its image.

Significance

Cayley's theorem shows that studying abstract groups is equivalent to studying groups of permutations, justifying the concrete approach to group theory and establishing that the symmetric groups are universal objects containing copies of all finite groups. The theorem was first published by Cayley in 1854, in the paper in which he gave the first abstract definition of a group.

Generalizations

The analogous result for monoids says every monoid embeds in a transformation monoid, and the Yoneda lemma in category theory can be viewed as a far-reaching generalization of Cayley's theorem.
