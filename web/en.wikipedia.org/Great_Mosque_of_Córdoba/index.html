Great Mosque of Córdoba

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Great Mosque of Córdoba (Spanish: Mezquita de Córdoba), officially known by its ecclesiastical name, the Cathedral of Our Lady of the Assumption, is a mosque-cathedral in Córdoba, Andalusia, Spain. Begun in 785–786 under Abd al-Rahman I, founder of the Umayyad emirate of Córdoba, it became one of the largest mosques in the Islamic world and is regarded as a masterpiece of Moorish architecture. Since the Christian reconquest of Córdoba in 1236 it has served as a Roman Catholic cathedral, with a Renaissance cathedral nave inserted into its centre in the 16th century.

Architecture

The vast hypostyle prayer hall is famous for its forest of some 850 columns of jasper, onyx, marble, and granite — many reused from Roman and Visigothic buildings — supporting double-tiered horseshoe arches striped in red brick and white stone. Successive enlargements under Abd al-Rahman II, al-Hakam II, and the chamberlain al-Mansur extended the hall and courtyard. Al-Hakam II's expansion added the richly decorated maqsura with interlacing arches and a mihrab faced with Byzantine mosaic work, a gift of craftsmen and gold tesserae from the emperor in Constantinople.

Later history

After Ferdinand III took Córdoba, the mosque was consecrated as a cathedral. In 1523 the cathedral chapter, against the city council's wishes, began building a cruciform Renaissance church in the middle of the prayer hall; Charles V, who had approved the project, is said to have lamented that they had destroyed something unique to build something commonplace. The site is the centrepiece of the UNESCO World Heritage listing for the historic centre of Córdoba (1984, extended 1994). Its religious status remains debated in Spain, as the Catholic Church controls worship there while Muslim groups have periodically requested permission to pray in the building.
