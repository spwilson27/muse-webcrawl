A supernova (plural: supernovae or supernovas) is a powerful and luminous explosion of a star. It is the largest explosion that takes place in space, briefly outshining an entire galaxy and radiating more energy than the Sun will emit over its whole life. The event marks the end of a star's life cycle and seeds the interstellar medium with heavy elements forged in the star and in the explosion itself.

Classification and causes

Supernovae fall into two broad groups by mechanism. Type Ia supernovae occur when a white dwarf in a binary system accretes matter (or merges with another white dwarf) until it approaches the Chandrasekhar limit of about 1.44 solar masses and undergoes runaway carbon fusion that destroys the star completely. Because their peak luminosities are nearly uniform, Type Ia supernovae serve as standard candles for measuring cosmic distances — the basis of the 1998 discovery of the accelerating expansion of the universe.

Core-collapse supernovae (Types Ib, Ic, and II) occur in massive stars of at least eight solar masses. When the core's iron ash can no longer support fusion, it collapses in a fraction of a second into a neutron star or black hole, and the rebounding shock wave — revived by an immense burst of neutrinos — expels the star's outer layers at speeds up to a tenth of the speed of light.

Historical supernovae

Guest stars recorded by historical astronomers include SN 1054, which produced the Crab Nebula; SN 1572, observed by Tycho Brahe; SN 1604, Kepler's supernova, the most recent observed in the Milky Way; and SN 1987A in the Large Magellanic Cloud, the closest supernova of the modern era and the first from which neutrinos were detected.
