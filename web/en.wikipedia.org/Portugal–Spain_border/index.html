Portugal–Spain border

The border, known in Portuguese as A Raia and in Spanish as La Raya.

The Portugal–Spain border, known as "The Stripe" (Portuguese: A Raia; Spanish: La Raya), is 1,214 kilometres (754 mi) long and is one of the oldest borders in the world, largely unchanged since the Treaty of Alcañices in 1297. It separates the Portuguese Republic from the Kingdom of Spain along the western edge of the Iberian Peninsula.

Course

The border runs from the mouth of the Minho River on the Atlantic coast in the north to the mouth of the Guadiana River on the Gulf of Cádiz in the south. Long stretches follow rivers, including the Minho, Douro (Duero), Tagus (Tejo), and Guadiana, while other sections run overland through the Meseta and the cork-oak landscapes of the Alentejo and Extremadura.

History

The frontier was first defined by the Treaty of Zamora in 1143, which recognised the County of Portugal as a kingdom. The Treaty of Alcañices (1297) between Ferdinand IV of Castile and Denis of Portugal fixed most of the present line. The most notable disputed area is the municipality of Olivenza (Olivença), administered by Spain since 1801 but claimed by Portugal, which does not recognise Spanish sovereignty over it, though it does not actively press the claim.

Since 1986 both countries have been members of the European Union and, since the 1990s, of the Schengen Area, so routine border controls have been abolished; the border is crossed by numerous roads, railways, and bridges, including the Guadiana International Bridge.
