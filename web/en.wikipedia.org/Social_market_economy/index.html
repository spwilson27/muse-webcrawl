Social market economy

The social market economy underpinned West Germany's post-war "economic miracle"

The social market economy (German: Soziale Marktwirtschaft), also called Rhine capitalism, is a socioeconomic model combining a free-market capitalist economic system with social policies and regulation that ensure fair competition and a welfare state. It was the economic model of West Germany after the Second World War and remains the guiding framework of the modern German economy.

Origins

The concept grew out of ordoliberal thought developed at the University of Freiburg in the 1930s and 1940s by economists such as Walter Eucken and Franz Böhm. The term itself is associated with Alfred Müller-Armack, who in 1946 described a system aimed at reconciling "the principle of freedom in the market with that of social balance". Economics minister Ludwig Erhard implemented the model from 1948, beginning with currency reform and the abolition of price controls, helping to launch the Wirtschaftswunder (economic miracle).

Principles

Core elements include private property and free markets with strong competition policy (including an independent cartel authority), a stable currency guaranteed by an independent central bank, social insurance systems for health, unemployment, and old age, co-determination of workers in large firms, and a social safety net intended to correct market outcomes without displacing the market itself. The model is often contrasted with both laissez-faire capitalism and centrally planned economies, and with the more individualistic Anglo-Saxon model.

Influence

The social market economy influenced the economic constitution of the European Union; the Treaty of Lisbon describes the EU's aim as a "highly competitive social market economy". Variants of the model are also associated with Austria and the Nordic countries, though these differ in detail.
