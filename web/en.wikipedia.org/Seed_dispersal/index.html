Seed dispersal

Seed dispersal is the movement or transport of seeds away from the parent plant. Dispersal reduces competition between parent and offspring, allows plants to colonise new habitats, and helps populations escape seed predators and pathogens that concentrate near the parent. Plants, being immobile, rely on vectors — wind, water, animals — or on mechanical ejection to move their seeds.

Modes of dispersal

Wind dispersal (anemochory) uses light seeds, wings or plumes: the samaras of maples autorotate as they fall, while dandelions and milkweeds bear feathery pappi that catch the air. Water dispersal (hydrochory) carries buoyant seeds and fruits, famously the coconut, which can survive long ocean voyages. Animal dispersal (zoochory) takes two main forms: epizoochory, in which hooked or sticky seeds attach to fur or feathers, and endozoochory, in which animals eat fleshy fruits and deposit the seeds in their droppings, often after gut passage has scarified the seed coat. Ants disperse the seeds of many forest herbs (myrmecochory), attracted by fatty appendages called elaiosomes. Some plants dispense with vectors entirely: in ballochory, fruits such as those of witch hazel and touch-me-not explosively eject their seeds, while gravity alone (barochory) drops heavy nuts beneath the parent.

Ecological significance

Dispersal shapes plant distributions, forest regeneration and the spread of invasive species. The Janzen–Connell hypothesis proposes that distance- and density-dependent mortality near parents favours dispersed seeds. Many animal dispersers are mutualists on which plants depend, so the loss of frugivores through hunting or habitat fragmentation can impair forest regeneration. Long-distance dispersal events, though rare, explain disjunct distributions and the colonisation of oceanic islands.
