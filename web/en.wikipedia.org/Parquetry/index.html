Parquetry

Parquetry is a geometric mosaic of wood pieces used for decorative effect in flooring and furniture. Unlike marquetry, which forms pictorial designs, parquetry assembles pieces into repeating geometric patterns — most famously the herringbone, chevron, and basketweave patterns of wooden block floors, and the cube and lozenge patterns of seventeenth-century French furniture. The word derives from the Old French parchet, meaning a small enclosed area or compartment.

Herringbone parquet flooring, the classic application of parquetry.

History

Large-scale decorative wood flooring in geometric patterns emerged in France in the seventeenth century, when patterned parquet was installed at the Palace of Versailles to replace marble floors that required constant washing and damaged the joists below; the parquet de Versailles — large square panels of interlocking diagonal squares — dates from 1684. Parquet became the standard floor of grand European interiors, and block parquetry spread in the nineteenth century as industrial cutting made the small blocks cheap to produce. In furniture-making, parquetry veneers decorated cabinets and commodes by makers such as André-Charles Boulle.

Materials and laying

Parquet blocks are traditionally solid hardwood — oak is the most common, with walnut, teak, mahogany, and maple also used, sometimes in contrasting combinations. Individual blocks or engineered tiles are bonded to a level subfloor with adhesive, then sanded and finished with oil, wax, or lacquer. The contrast between pieces arises from using woods of different colours and from varying the direction of the grain, which catches the light differently across the pattern. Worn parquet can usually be re-sanded and refinished several times, giving it a service life of many decades.
