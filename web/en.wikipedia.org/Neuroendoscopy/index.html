Neuroendoscopy

A rigid endoscope of the type used in intraventricular neurosurgery.

Neuroendoscopy is a minimally invasive surgical procedure in which a neurosurgeon uses an endoscope — passed through small openings such as a burr hole in the skull, the mouth, or the nose — to access regions of the brain and central nervous system that would otherwise require open surgery. Modern neuroendoscopes combine high-definition cameras, fibre-optic illumination, irrigation channels, and working channels for instruments.

History

Endoscopic inspection of the ventricles was attempted as early as 1910 by Victor Lespinasse and developed by William Mixter, who performed an early endoscopic third ventriculostomy in 1923. The technique was long limited by poor optics and was largely displaced by shunting for hydrocephalus, but advances in rod-lens optics and imaging in the late 20th century led to a resurgence, championed by surgeons such as Michael Apuzzo and Per Grunert.

Procedures

The most common neuroendoscopic operation is endoscopic third ventriculostomy (ETV), in which an opening is made in the floor of the third ventricle to treat obstructive hydrocephalus without a shunt. Other applications include biopsy and removal of intraventricular tumours such as colloid cysts, fenestration of arachnoid cysts, evacuation of intracerebral haemorrhage, endonasal transsphenoidal removal of pituitary adenomas, and assisted microvascular decompression.

Advantages and limitations

Compared with open craniotomy, neuroendoscopy generally offers smaller incisions, less brain retraction, shorter hospital stay, and faster recovery. Limitations include a restricted field of view, two-dimensional imaging in many systems, a learning curve for hand–eye coordination, and difficulty controlling major bleeding through narrow working channels.
