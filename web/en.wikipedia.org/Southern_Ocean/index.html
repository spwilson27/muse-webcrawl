The Southern Ocean, also known as the Antarctic Ocean, comprises the southernmost waters of the world ocean, encircling Antarctica. Unlike the other oceans, which are defined by the continents around them, it is distinguished by a current: the Antarctic Circumpolar Current, which flows eastward around the continent and separates cold subantarctic waters along the Antarctic Convergence. It is generally taken to extend from the coast of Antarctica north to about 60°S latitude.

Recognition and history

Although Cook's voyages of the 1770s proved that a navigable ocean surrounds the southern continent, the Southern Ocean's status was long disputed. The International Hydrographic Organization recognised it in 1937, dropped it in 1953, and its draft 2000 limits were never universally ratified; nevertheless the term is standard in science, and in 2021 National Geographic formally recognised it as the fifth ocean. It is the second-smallest ocean after the Arctic.

Oceanography and climate role

The Antarctic Circumpolar Current, the world's largest, transports roughly 130–150 million cubic metres of water per second past every meridian south of Tasmania and the Cape. Powerful westerly winds (the "roaring forties", "furious fifties", and "screaming sixties") drive upwelling that makes the Southern Ocean a crucial sink for anthropogenic carbon dioxide and heat, and its deep-water formation ventilates the global ocean.

Ecosystem

Cold, nutrient-rich waters support immense krill populations that underpin food webs of whales, seals, penguins, and seabirds. Fisheries target Antarctic krill and Patagonian toothfish under the CCAMLR convention system. Sea ice extent around Antarctica varies dramatically between summer and winter and, after years of slight growth, fell to record lows in the late 2010s and 2020s.
