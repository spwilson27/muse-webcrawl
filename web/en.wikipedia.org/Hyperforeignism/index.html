Hyperforeignism

Speakers often "correct" loanwords toward an imagined foreign pattern.

A hyperforeignism is a special case of hypercorrection resulting from speakers misidentifying the distribution of a pattern found in loanwords and applying it to words that do not display that pattern, to make them sound more foreign. This can lead to the production of pronunciations and spellings that are inconsistent with the norms of either the language of origin or the language of adoption.

Examples

In English, a common example is the pronunciation of habanero as if it were spelled "habañero", by analogy with Spanish words such as jalapeño—even though habanero contains no ñ. Some speakers pronounce the Italian word bruschetta with a [ʃ] ("broo-shet-ta") instead of the Italian [sk], overapplying the German-style "sch". Similarly, the pronunciation of Beijing with a French-like "zh" sound ([beɪˈʒɪŋ]) results from misapplied French phonetics; Mandarin has no such sound in the name. The spelling "coup de tat" for coup d'état reflects a hyperforeign spelling.

Mechanism

Hyperforeignisms arise because speakers learn limited rules about a foreign language's orthography or phonology—such as "Spanish j sounds like English h" or "French words drop final consonants"—and then apply those rules beyond their actual scope. The phenomenon shows that speakers possess implicit, if imperfect, models of foreign sound systems, and that prestige or perceived authenticity motivates the "foreignising" correction.
