Old Turkic (also Old Turkish) is the earliest attested stage of the Turkic language family, recorded in Central Asia and Mongolia between roughly the 7th and 13th centuries CE. It is known from royal inscriptions of the Second Turkic Khaganate, from manuscripts of the Uyghur Khaganate and its successor states, and from scattered documents in several scripts. Old Turkic is not the direct ancestor of modern Turkish alone but stands near the root of the whole Common Turkic branch.

The Orkhon inscriptions

The most famous Old Turkic texts are the stone monuments erected in the Orkhon Valley of Mongolia in the 720s–730s in honour of the prince Kül Tigin, his brother the emperor Bilge Khagan, and the chancellor Tonyukuk. Written in the angular Old Turkic runiform script, they recount the rise, fall and restoration of the Turkic empire with remarkable literary power. The script was deciphered in 1893 by the Danish philologist Vilhelm Thomsen, unlocking the earliest history of the Turkic peoples in their own words.

Language features

Old Turkic is an agglutinative language with vowel harmony, no grammatical gender, and a rich set of derivational suffixes; its syntax is predominantly subject–object–verb. Texts display dialects and chronological layers sometimes distinguished as Old Turkic proper (Orkhon Turkic) and Old Uyghur, the latter heavily represented in Buddhist, Manichaean and civil documents from Turfan, written in the cursive Old Uyghur alphabet derived from Sogdian.

Legacy

Old Turkic vocabulary and grammar underlie all later Turkic languages, and the Orkhon inscriptions are celebrated in Turkey, Mongolia and the Central Asian republics as foundational monuments of Turkic civilisation. The modern languages most closely related to it are argued to be the Siberian Turkic languages such as Khakas and Shor.
