Colonialism

Colonialism is the policy and practice of a power extending control over foreign territory, generally with the aim of economic dominance. In colonialism, colonizers impose their religion, economics, and other cultural practices on indigenous peoples. The term also denotes the period and ideology of European expansion from the 15th century onward, closely related to but distinguishable from imperialism.

History

European colonialism began with Portuguese and Spanish voyages of the late 1400s — Columbus reached the Americas in 1492 — and came to encompass empires held by Britain, France, the Netherlands, Belgium, Germany, Italy, and others in the Americas, Africa, Asia, and Oceania. Colonies served as sources of raw materials and captive markets, and settlement colonies displaced indigenous populations, as in the Americas and Australia. The late 19th-century "Scramble for Africa" formalized European partition of that continent at the Berlin Conference of 1884–85.

Decolonization and legacy

After the Second World War, independence movements and shifting geopolitics brought rapid decolonization across Asia and Africa, and the United Nations declared in 1960 that colonialism should be ended swiftly. Colonialism's legacies — debated by historians — include arbitrary borders, extractive institutions, the global spread of languages and religions, massive population transfers including the transatlantic slave trade, and enduring economic and political inequalities analyzed in postcolonial studies. Landscapes shaped by colonial rule include former colonial capitals preserved as heritage sites, such as Colonial Williamsburg; territories such as Colombia emerged from Spanish colonial rule into independence in the 19th century.
