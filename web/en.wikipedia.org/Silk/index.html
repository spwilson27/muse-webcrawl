Silk

Silk is a natural protein fibre, some forms of which can be woven into textiles. The best-known silk is obtained from the cocoons of the larvae of the mulberry silkworm Bombyx mori, reared in captivity in the practice called sericulture. Silk is composed mainly of fibroin, coated with sericin, and is prized for its softness, lustre and strength. The shimmering appearance of silk is due to the triangular prism-like structure of the fibre, which refracts light at different angles.

History

Silk production originated in Neolithic China; the earliest surviving examples date to around 3630 BC. Legend credits the empress Leizu (Xi Ling-shi) with discovering sericulture. For millennia China guarded the secret of its production, and silk became a luxury traded along the Silk Road to the West, where it was worth its weight in gold. According to tradition, silkworm eggs were smuggled to the Byzantine Empire in the 6th century AD, and production later spread through the Islamic world to Italy, France and beyond.

Production

Silkworms are fed on mulberry leaves and, when ready to pupate, spin cocoons of a single filament that may be 300–900 m long. The cocoons are softened in hot water and the filaments reeled together into raw silk thread; roughly 2,000–3,000 cocoons are needed for a pound of silk. Most silk is harvested by killing the pupae inside the cocoon; "peace silk" or ahimsa silk allows the moth to emerge, at the cost of shorter fibres. China and India together produce the overwhelming majority of world output.

Properties and uses

Silk is one of the strongest natural fibres, absorbent, breathable, and comfortable in both warm and cool weather. It is used for garments, upholstery, parachutes, sutures and luxury goods. Because production is labour-intensive and controversial with animal-welfare advocates (PETA campaigns against it), silk remains a premium fibre today.
