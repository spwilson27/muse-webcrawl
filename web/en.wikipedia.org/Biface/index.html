Biface

In archaeology, a biface is a two-sided stone tool and is used as a multi-purpose knife, manufactured through a process of lithic reduction, that displays flake removals on two sides of the tool. The best-known bifaces are the hand axes of the Acheulean industry of the Lower Palaeolithic, found across Africa, Europe and western Asia from about 1.7 million years ago.

A flint biface (hand axe), worked on both faces.

Description

Bifaces are shaped by striking flakes from a core or large flake using hard hammerstones and, in later stages, softer hammers of antler, bone or wood. Working alternately on both faces thins the piece and produces a continuous cutting edge around much of its perimeter. Common forms include oval, triangular and cordiform (heart-shaped) hand axes, as well as cleavers with a straight transverse edge.

History of use

Bifacial technology first appears in the Oldowan-to-Acheulean transition in East Africa. Acheulean hand axes persisted for over a million years with remarkable standardization, and bifacial thinning reached its refinement in the Solutrean laurel-leaf points of Upper Palaeolithic Europe and the large bifaces of the Americas. Bifaces also served as cores from which usable flakes could be struck on demand.

Function

Experimental archaeology and use-wear analysis suggest hand axes were general-purpose tools used for butchering animals, chopping wood, digging, and processing plant material. Their symmetry has also prompted debated hypotheses about display or social signalling.
