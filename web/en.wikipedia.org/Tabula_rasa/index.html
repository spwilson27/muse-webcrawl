Tabula rasa

Tabula rasa (Latin for "blank slate") is the theory that individuals are born without built-in mental content, and that all knowledge comes from experience or perception. Proponents of the tabula rasa thesis favour the "nurture" side of the nature versus nurture debate, arguing that human behaviour and knowledge are largely the products of learning and socialisation rather than innate ideas or instincts.

The metaphor derives from the erasable wax writing tablet of antiquity.

History

The metaphor of the mind as a writing surface goes back to Aristotle's De Anima, which compares the intellect to a tablet on which nothing is yet written, and was developed by the Stoics and by the Persian philosopher Ibn Sina (Avicenna). In the 13th century Thomas Aquinas held that the intellect begins as a tabula rasa while rejecting some conclusions later drawn from the idea.

The thesis is most famously associated with John Locke, whose Essay Concerning Human Understanding (1689) attacked the doctrine of innate ideas and asked readers to suppose "the mind to be, as we say, white paper, void of all characters, without any ideas", furnished only by experience through sensation and reflection.

Modern debate

Twentieth-century behaviourism adopted a strongly empiricist stance, but research in linguistics (notably Noam Chomsky's arguments for an innate language faculty), developmental psychology, and behavioural genetics has supported significant innate structure. Contemporary consensus in cognitive science generally holds that the mind is neither a pure blank slate nor fully pre-formed: domain-specific predispositions are shaped and filled in by experience. Steven Pinker's The Blank Slate (2002) is a prominent modern critique of the doctrine.
