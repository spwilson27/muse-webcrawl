Soto Zen

Sōtō Zen (曹洞宗, Sōtōshū) is one of the three traditional sects of Zen Buddhism in Japan, alongside Rinzai and Ōbaku. It is the largest of the three and traces its lineage to the Chinese Caodong school founded by Dongshan Liangjie and Caoshan Benji during the Tang dynasty.

History

The school was brought to Japan by Dōgen Zenji (1200–1253), who travelled to China, studied under the Caodong master Rujing, and returned to found the monastery of Eihei-ji in Fukui Prefecture. Dōgen's writings, collected in the Shōbōgenzō, are foundational texts of the school. A second patriarchal centre, Sōji-ji, was developed by Keizan Jōkin, who helped popularise Soto Zen among laypeople and spread it across Japan.

Practice

Soto Zen is characterised by shikantaza — "just sitting" — a form of zazen meditation without a koan or other object of concentration, in which the practitioner sits in alert, objectless awareness. Dōgen taught the unity of practice and enlightenment: zazen is not a means to awakening but its expression. Soto monks typically face a wall while sitting, in contrast to the inward-facing arrangement of Rinzai.

Today

Soto Zen counts roughly 14,000 temples in Japan and has spread internationally, with major centres in North America and Europe, influenced in the West by teachers such as Shunryu Suzuki, founder of the San Francisco Zen Center.
