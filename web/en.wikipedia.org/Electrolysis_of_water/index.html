Electrolysis of water


The Hofmann voltameter, used to demonstrate the electrolysis of water

Electrolysis of water, also known as electrochemical water splitting, is the process of using electricity to decompose water into oxygen (O2) and hydrogen (H2) gas by electrolysis. Hydrogen gas released in this way can be used as hydrogen fuel, but must be kept environmentally sealed as it is flammable and potentially explosive.

History

Water was first electrolysed shortly after the invention of the voltaic pile in 1800; William Nicholson and Anthony Carlisle decomposed water using a voltaic pile the same year. Michael Faraday formulated the laws of electrolysis in the 1830s, and the Hofmann voltameter (1866) became the classic demonstration apparatus, collecting twice as much hydrogen as oxygen by volume.

Reactions

The overall reaction is:

2 H2O(l) → 2 H2(g) + O2(g)

At the cathode, water is reduced to hydrogen and hydroxide ions; at the anode, hydroxide ions (or water) are oxidised to oxygen. In practice a voltage greater than the thermodynamic minimum of 1.23 volts is required because of overpotentials at the electrodes; commercial electrolysers typically operate at 1.8–2.0 volts per cell.

Techniques

  • Alkaline water electrolysis – uses a liquid alkaline electrolyte such as potassium hydroxide; the oldest and most mature industrial technology.
  • Proton-exchange membrane (PEM) electrolysis – uses a solid polymer electrolyte, allowing compact high-pressure operation and rapid response to variable renewable power.
  • Solid oxide electrolysis – operates at high temperature (700–850 °C) with ceramic electrolytes, achieving higher efficiency where waste heat is available.
  • Anion-exchange membrane electrolysis – a newer approach combining features of alkaline and PEM systems.

Applications

Hydrogen produced by electrolysis ("green hydrogen" when powered by renewable electricity) is used in refining, ammonia production, steelmaking trials, energy storage and fuel-cell vehicles. The main barriers are cost and efficiency relative to hydrogen from steam methane reforming.
