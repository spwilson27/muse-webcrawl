Synthetic biology

Synthetic biology (SynBio) is a multidisciplinary field of science and engineering that designs and constructs new biological parts, devices, and systems, or redesigns existing natural biological systems for useful purposes. It applies engineering principles — standardisation, modularity, and abstraction — to biology, combining knowledge from genetic engineering, molecular biology, systems biology, biochemistry, and computer science.

Engineered microorganisms are a central platform of synthetic biology.

History

The term was used as early as 1910 by Stéphane Leduc, but the modern field coalesced around 2000, when researchers demonstrated artificial genetic circuits such as the toggle switch and the repressilator in Escherichia coli. The iGEM competition, founded in 2003 at MIT, popularised standardised DNA parts ("BioBricks"). In 2010 the J. Craig Venter Institute created a bacterial cell controlled by a chemically synthesised genome; in 2016 it reported the minimal cell JCVI-syn3.0 with only 473 genes.

Approaches

Synthetic biologists work "top-down", re-engineering existing organisms, and "bottom-up", assembling life-like systems from non-living components. Major subfields include genetic circuit design, metabolic engineering for the production of chemicals and fuels, genome-scale engineering, cell-free protein synthesis, and xenobiology, which explores alternative nucleic acids (XNA) and expanded genetic codes. Genome editing tools such as CRISPR–Cas9 have greatly accelerated strain construction.

Applications

Engineered yeast producing the antimalarial precursor artemisinic acid was an early commercial success. Other applications include engineered immune cells (CAR-T therapy), biosensors for pollutants and disease markers, nitrogen-fixing microbes for agriculture, bio-based materials such as spider silk proteins, and DNA data storage.

Ethics and safety

The field raises concerns about biosafety, biosecurity, and the governance of "dual-use" research, including the synthesis of pathogen genomes. Community norms, screening of commercial DNA synthesis orders, and international guidelines aim to manage these risks.
