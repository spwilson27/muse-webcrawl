Dinitrogen tetroxide, commonly known as nitrogen tetroxide (NTO), is the chemical compound N2O4. It is a useful reagent in chemical synthesis and a major component of rocket propellants. It is in equilibrium with nitrogen dioxide: as temperature rises, the colourless N2O4 dissociates into the brown, paramagnetic gas NO2.

Structure and properties

The molecule is planar with an unusually long N–N bond of about 1.78 Å, which accounts for its ready dissociation. It is a powerful oxidizer and is highly toxic and corrosive; contact with skin causes severe burns, and inhalation can cause pulmonary edema. Dinitrogen tetroxide is hygroscopic and reacts with water to form nitric and nitrous acids.

Use as rocket propellant

NTO is one of the most common storable liquid rocket oxidizers. Combined with hydrazine-family fuels (such as UDMH or MMH, forming propellant combinations like Aerozine 50), it is hypergolic — the propellants ignite on contact — making engines simple and reliable to restart. It was used in the Titan rockets, the Apollo Lunar Module, the Space Shuttle's orbital maneuvering system, and many satellites and interplanetary probes. Its disadvantages are toxicity and the need to keep it from freezing, since its melting point is only −11.2 °C.

Production and other uses

Industrially, dinitrogen tetroxide is obtained by the catalytic oxidation of ammonia, the first step of the Ostwald process for nitric acid. It is also used as a nitrating agent and as an intermediate in the manufacture of fertilizers and explosives.
