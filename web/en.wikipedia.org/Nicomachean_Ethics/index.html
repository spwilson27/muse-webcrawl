The Nicomachean Ethics (Ancient Greek: Ἠθικὰ Νικομάχεια) is the best-known work on ethics by the Greek philosopher Aristotle. Written in the 4th century BC, probably as lecture notes, it consists of ten books and takes its name from Aristotle's son Nicomachus, to whom it may have been dedicated or who may have edited it. Together with the Eudemian Ethics and the Politics, it constitutes the core of Aristotle's practical philosophy and is one of the most influential works in the history of Western ethics.

The Nicomachean Ethics, Aristotle's treatise on the good life

Overview

The work begins with the famous observation that every art, inquiry, action, and pursuit aims at some good, and that the highest human good is eudaimonia — usually translated as "happiness," "flourishing," or "living well." Aristotle argues that eudaimonia is an activity of the soul in accordance with virtue over a complete life. Identifying the function (ergon) of a human being as rational activity, he concludes that human excellence consists in performing that rational activity well, in accordance with the virtues.

Virtue and the golden mean

Aristotle distinguishes intellectual virtues, acquired by teaching, from moral virtues, acquired by habit. Moral virtue is described as a state of character concerned with choice, lying in a mean between excess and deficiency relative to us — the doctrine of the golden mean. Courage, for example, is the mean between cowardice and rashness; temperance the mean between insensibility and self-indulgence. Practical wisdom (phronesis) enables a person to determine the mean in particular situations.

Justice, friendship, and pleasure

Book V treats justice, both universal and particular. Books VIII and IX contain an extended analysis of friendship (philia), distinguishing friendships of utility, pleasure, and virtue, the last being the highest form. Books VII and X discuss pleasure and akrasia (weakness of will), concluding that the contemplative life of the philosopher is the happiest, though the life of moral and political virtue is happy in a secondary way.

Influence

The Nicomachean Ethics profoundly shaped medieval scholasticism, especially through the commentaries and synthesis of Thomas Aquinas, and Islamic and Jewish philosophy through thinkers such as Al-Farabi, Averroes, and Maimonides. In the 20th century it became a foundational text for the revival of virtue ethics associated with philosophers such as G. E. M. Anscombe and Alasdair MacIntyre.
