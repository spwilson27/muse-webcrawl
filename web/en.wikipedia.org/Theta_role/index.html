Theta role

In generative grammar, theta roles describe the semantic relationship between a predicate and its arguments.

In generative linguistics, a theta role (also called a thematic role, though the two terms are distinguished in some theories) is the formal device for representing syntactic argument structure—the number and type of noun phrase arguments required by a particular verb—in the lexicon. For example, the verb put requires three arguments: an agent, a theme, and a location.

The assignment of theta roles is governed by the theta criterion, proposed by Noam Chomsky: each argument bears one and only one theta role, and each theta role is assigned to one and only one argument. The set of roles a predicate assigns is its theta grid.

Common theta roles

  • Agent: the volitional causer of an event (Mary opened the door).
  • Theme: the entity moved or affected by an event (Mary opened the door).
  • Patient: the entity that undergoes a change of state.
  • Experiencer: the entity that perceives or feels an event.
  • Stimulus: the entity that causes a perception or feeling.
  • Goal: the destination of a movement or transfer.
  • Source: the origin of a movement or transfer.
  • Location: where an event takes place.
  • Instrument: the means by which an event is carried out.
  • Beneficiary: the entity for whose benefit an event occurs.

Theta roles versus grammatical relations

Theta roles are not the same as grammatical relations such as subject and object. In the passive sentence "The door was opened by Mary," the theme the door is the subject, while the agent remains an agent. Theta theory thus helps explain how the same semantic structure can be realised by different syntactic arrangements, and underlies analyses of phenomena such as the unaccusative–unergative distinction and argument-structure alternations.
