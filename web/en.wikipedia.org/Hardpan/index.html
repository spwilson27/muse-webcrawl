Hardpan

TypeSoil horizon
FormationCompaction; cementation by silica, iron oxides, or calcium carbonate
EffectsImpeded drainage and root penetration

In soil science, a hardpan is a dense, hardened layer of soil, usually found below the upper topsoil, that is largely impervious to water and resists penetration by roots and digging tools. Hardpans form either by compaction — from heavy machinery or repeated ploughing at a constant depth (a "plough pan") — or by natural cementation, in which dissolved minerals such as silica, iron oxides, or calcium carbonate precipitate and bind soil particles together.

Types

Named varieties include caliche, cemented by calcium carbonate in arid regions; duripan, cemented by silica; and fragipan, a dense but brittle layer common in some forest and loess soils. Claypans, dense clay-rich horizons, behave similarly by restricting water movement.

Agricultural significance

Hardpans restrict root growth, cause water to perch above the layer after rain, and can leave crops alternately waterlogged and drought-stressed. Farmers break them up by deep ripping or subsoiling, by growing deep-rooted cover crops, or by avoiding traffic on wet soil. In construction, hardpan affects foundations and excavation costs.
