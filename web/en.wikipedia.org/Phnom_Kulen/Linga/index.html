Linga

A linga (Sanskrit: लिङ्ग, "sign" or "mark"; also spelled lingam) is the aniconic symbol of the Hindu god Shiva, representing his creative power and presence in worship. On Phnom Kulen, the sacred mountain of the early Angkorian kings in Cambodia, lingas are among the most distinctive surviving monuments.

The river of a thousand lingas

At Kbal Spean, a tributary of the Siem Reap River on the Kulen plateau, the sandstone riverbed was carved over long stretches with hundreds of lingas set in neat grids, alongside images of Vishnu, Shiva and other gods, largely dated to the eleventh and twelfth centuries. Water flowing over the carvings was ritually sanctified before descending to the reservoirs and temples of Angkor below — a hydraulic blessing that made the capital's irrigation water holy. Similar carved lingas and yonis appear at other points along the mountain's streams.

Significance

Phnom Kulen is where Jayavarman II, founder of the Angkorian line after the Chenla period, was consecrated in 802; the devaraja cult centred on the royal linga, the portable embodiment of kingship housed in the state temple-mountain. The mountain's linga carvings therefore express the ideology of the empire at its source: Shiva's power, the king's legitimacy and the fertility of the waters on which Angkor's rice economy depended. The site remains a place of pilgrimage and is protected within Phnom Kulen National Park.
