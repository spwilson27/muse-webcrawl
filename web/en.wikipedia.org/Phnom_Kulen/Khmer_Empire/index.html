The Khmer Empire (Khmer: ចក្រភពខ្មែរ) was a Hindu-Buddhist empire in Southeast Asia, centred in what is now Cambodia. Growing out of the former kingdoms of Funan and Chenla, it lasted from 802 to 1431 CE and at its height controlled much of present-day Cambodia, Thailand, Laos, and southern Vietnam. Its capital, Angkor, hosted monumental architecture including Angkor Wat, the largest religious structure ever built.

History

The empire is conventionally founded in 802, when Jayavarman II proclaimed himself universal monarch on Phnom Kulen. Yasovarman I moved the capital to Angkor around 900. The 11th and 12th centuries saw expansion under Suryavarman I and Suryavarman II, builder of Angkor Wat. After a Cham sack of Angkor in 1177, Jayavarman VII rebuilt the capital, embraced Mahayana Buddhism, and constructed Angkor Thom and the Bayon.

Hydraulic civilization

Angkorian prosperity rested on an elaborate water management network of reservoirs (barays), canals, and embankments that stored monsoon water for dry-season rice irrigation. Scholars have described Angkor as a "hydraulic city"; recent research links the system's increasing rigidity and vulnerability to droughts and floods — recorded in tree rings — to the empire's decline in the 14th and 15th centuries.

Decline

Repeated wars with the rising Siamese kingdom of Ayutthaya culminated in the sack of Angkor in 1431, after which the court moved south toward Phnom Penh. Theravada Buddhism, which had spread among the population, replaced the state cults of the Angkorian kings. Angkor Wat itself was never entirely abandoned.
