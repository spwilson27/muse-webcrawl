Chenla

Chenla (Khmer: ចេនឡា) is the name given in Chinese historical records to the polity or polities that succeeded the kingdom of Funan in the interior of mainland Southeast Asia, roughly from the late sixth to the early ninth century. Its history forms the background against which the plateau of Phnom Kulen — the later sacred mountain of the Angkorian kings — rose to prominence.

From Funan to Chenla

Chinese sources describe Chenla first as a Funanese vassal centred up the Mekong, in the region of modern Champasak in southern Laos, which conquered its overlord around the sixth century. Seventh-century kings such as Bhavavarman I and Mahendravarman, and above all Ishanavarman I, who founded the capital of Ishanapura (modern Sambor Prei Kuk), extended Chenla's power across what is now Cambodia. Later embassies distinguished a "Land Chenla" in the interior from a "Water Chenla" in the delta, suggesting fragmentation into rival principalities during the eighth century.

Towards Angkor

In 802, according to later inscriptions, Jayavarman II was consecrated on Phnom Kulen as a universal monarch, founding the line that would build Angkor and ritually uniting the Chenla successor states. The mountain's sanctity — its riverbed carved with lingas — thus links the Chenla age to the empire that followed. Historians caution that "Chenla" was a Chinese label rather than a single unified kingdom, and modern scholarship increasingly treats it as a shifting constellation of Khmer principalities sharing art, religion and kingship.
