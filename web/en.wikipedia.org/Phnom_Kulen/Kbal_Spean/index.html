Kbal Spean (Khmer: ក្បាលស្ពាន, "bridge head") is an Angkorian-era archaeological site on the southwest slopes of the Kulen Hills, northeast of Angkor in Cambodia. It is commonly known as the "River of a Thousand Lingas" because of the extensive carvings — lingas, yonis, and images of Hindu deities — cut into the bed and banks of the Stung Kbal Spean river.

Carvings

The carvings, which date mainly to the reigns of Suryavarman I and Udayadityavarman II in the 11th century with additions into the 13th century, were intended to sanctify the river water flowing over them, so that it would arrive blessed at the royal city and its reservoirs downstream. Rows of lingas cover stretches of the sandstone riverbed, interspersed with reliefs of Vishnu reclining on the serpent Ananta, Brahma on a lotus, and Shiva with Uma riding the bull Nandi.

Setting and access

The site lies within Phnom Kulen National Park and is reached by a roughly 1.5 km uphill forest trail from the road. Near the carvings, a natural stone bridge — the "spean" of the name — crosses the stream, and a small waterfall lies downstream. The site was brought to wider attention in 1968 by ethnologist Jean Boulbet, though the area was off-limits for much of the later 20th century owing to Cambodia's conflicts.
