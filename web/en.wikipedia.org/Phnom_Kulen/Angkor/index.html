Angkor (Khmer: អង្គរ, "capital city") was the capital city of the Khmer Empire, which flourished from approximately the 9th to 15th centuries AD. The city and empire produced numerous architectural masterpieces, such as Angkor Wat and the Bayon, and established sophisticated hydraulic systems for managing water resources. Angkor is one of the most important archaeological sites in Southeast Asia and among the largest pre-industrial urban complexes in the world.

History

The Angkorian period is conventionally dated from 802 CE, when Jayavarman II proclaimed himself universal monarch (chakravartin) on Phnom Kulen, to 1431, when Ayutthaya forces sacked the capital. Successive kings built vast temple-mountains and reservoirs (barays) that supported intensive rice agriculture and a population that may have approached a million people.

Architecture

Angkor Wat, built by Suryavarman II in the early 12th century, is the world's largest religious monument and appears on Cambodia's flag. Later, Jayavarman VII built the walled city of Angkor Thom, whose Bayon temple is famed for its towers carved with serene faces. Temples were built of sandstone, laterite, and brick, decorated with extensive bas-reliefs depicting Hindu epics and historical scenes.

Decline and rediscovery

Theories for Angkor's decline include warfare with Siam, religious upheaval, and the failure of its water management system under climatic extremes revealed by tree-ring studies. The site was never fully abandoned — Theravada Buddhist monks maintained Angkor Wat — but it was brought to wide Western attention after French naturalist Henri Mouhot published accounts of his 1860 visit.
