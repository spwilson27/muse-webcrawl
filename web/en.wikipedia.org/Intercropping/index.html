Intercropping


Alternating rows of two crops in an intercropping system.

Intercropping is a multiple-cropping practice in agriculture that involves growing two or more crops simultaneously in the same field. The component crops are chosen to complement one another — drawing on different soil depths or nutrient pools, maturing at different times, or providing services such as shade, support, nitrogen fixation or pest deterrence. One of the best-known traditional systems is the "Three Sisters" of Indigenous North American agriculture, in which maize provides a trellis for climbing beans, the beans fix nitrogen, and squash shades the soil, conserving moisture and suppressing weeds. Intercropping is entirely distinct from the interbreeding of archaic and modern humans; the words merely share a prefix.

Types

Common arrangements include mixed intercropping (crops sown together without a distinct row pattern), row intercropping, strip intercropping (strips wide enough for separate cultivation but close enough to interact), and relay intercropping, in which a second crop is sown into a standing first crop before harvest. Agroforestry systems intercrop annuals with trees, as in alley cropping.

Advantages and drawbacks

Well-designed intercrops often outyield sole crops per unit land — measured by the land equivalent ratio — improve resource-use efficiency, diversify farm income and food supply, reduce pest and disease pressure, and stabilise yields under variable weather. Drawbacks include competition between crops if poorly matched, and difficulty mechanising planting and harvest, one reason intercropping declined in industrialised monoculture systems even as it remains widespread among smallholders in Africa, Asia and Latin America.
