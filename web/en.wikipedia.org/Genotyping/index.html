Genotyping

Genotyping is the process of determining differences in the genetic make-up (genotype) of an individual by examining the individual's DNA sequence using biological assays and comparing it to another individual's sequence or a reference sequence. It reveals the alleles an individual has inherited from their parents. Traditionally genotyping is the use of DNA sequences to define biological populations by the use of molecular tools. It does not usually involve defining the genes of an individual.

Techniques

Current methods of genotyping include restriction fragment length polymorphism (RFLP) analysis, polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-based assays, DNA microarrays (gene chips), and DNA sequencing. In a typical PCR-based assay, short DNA primers are designed to flank a variable region; differences in fragment length or fluorescence reveal which alleles are present. SNP genotyping arrays can assay hundreds of thousands of single-nucleotide polymorphisms simultaneously from a small DNA sample.

Modern high-throughput approaches include whole-genome sequencing, which determines nearly an individual's entire genotype, and reduced-representation methods such as genotyping-by-sequencing used in plant and animal breeding. Direct-to-consumer genetic testing services genotype customers at hundreds of thousands of markers to estimate ancestry and health risks.

Applications

Genotyping is used in a wide range of fields:

  • Medicine — diagnosing inherited disorders, carrier screening, predicting drug response (pharmacogenomics), and identifying disease-associated variants in genome-wide association studies.
  • Forensics — DNA profiling of short tandem repeats to identify individuals from biological samples, a technique pioneered by Alec Jeffreys in 1984.
  • Parentage and identity testing — confirming biological relationships in humans, livestock, and pets.
  • Agriculture — marker-assisted selection to breed crops and animals with desirable traits.
  • Ecology and conservation — identifying populations, tracking individuals, and measuring genetic diversity without capturing animals, using DNA from hair, feathers, or feces.

History

Before molecular methods, genotypes were inferred indirectly from phenotypes — for example, from blood groups or from the outcomes of genetic crosses analyzed with Punnett squares. The discovery of abundant DNA polymorphisms in the 1970s and 1980s, and the invention of PCR by Kary Mullis in 1983, made direct genotyping routine. The cost per genotype has since fallen by many orders of magnitude.
