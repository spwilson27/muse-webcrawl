Cayley's theorem

Cayley's theorem, named for the British mathematician Arthur Cayley, states that every group G is isomorphic to a subgroup of a symmetric group — specifically, to a subgroup of the group of permutations of the underlying set of G itself. Consequently, the study of abstract groups can be reduced, in principle, to the study of permutation groups.

Proof idea

For each element g of G, define the map Tg that sends each x in G to gx (left multiplication). Each Tg is a permutation of the set G, and the assignment g ↦ Tg is an injective group homomorphism into the symmetric group on G, since TgTh = Tgh and distinct group elements act differently. Thus G is isomorphic to its image.

Significance

The theorem justified the abstract definition of a group: no generality is lost by thinking of groups as groups of permutations, even though for a finite group of order n the embedding may land in the much larger symmetric group Sn of order n!.

Related results

Analogous representation theorems include Yoneda's lemma in category theory, of which Cayley's theorem can be seen as a special case, and Cayley's theorem for semigroups.
