Harvard Computers

The women computers of Harvard College Observatory

Periodc. 1877–1920s
InstitutionHarvard College Observatory
DirectorEdward Charles Pickering
Notable membersWilliamina Fleming, Antonia Maury, Annie Jump Cannon, Henrietta Swan Leavitt, Cecilia Payne-Gaposchkin

The Harvard Computers were a team of women who processed astronomical data at the Harvard College Observatory from the late 1870s through the early 20th century, under its director, Edward Charles Pickering. Hired as "computers" — the era's job title for people who performed calculations and reduced data — they classified hundreds of thousands of stars from photographic plates, discovered nebulae, novae and variable stars, and laid the foundations of modern stellar astronomy, all for wages of about 25 to 30 cents an hour.

Background

Pickering, appointed director in 1877, faced a flood of photographic plates from new sky surveys and, short of funds, hired women graduates of the new women's colleges — initially, legend has it, after threatening that his Scottish maid Williamina Fleming could do better than his male assistant. At its height the group numbered around 80 women over the decades; contemporaries sometimes called them, condescendingly, "Pickering's Harem", while their meticulous plate work underpinned the observatory's monumental publications.

Major achievements

Fleming devised the first workable system of stellar spectral classification and catalogued over 10,000 stars, also discovering the Horsehead Nebula. Antonia Maury refined the scheme using spectral line widths, anticipating the discovery of giant and dwarf stars. Annie Jump Cannon synthesised their work into the Harvard Classification Scheme — the sequence O, B, A, F, G, K, M — single-handedly classifying some 350,000 stars for the Henry Draper Catalogue (1918–1924), still the basis of stellar taxonomy. Henrietta Swan Leavitt, studying variable stars in the Magellanic Clouds, discovered the period–luminosity relation for Cepheid variables (announced 1908, published 1912), which became a cornerstone of the cosmic distance ladder and enabled the measurement of the size of the universe. Cecilia Payne-Gaposchkin's 1925 doctoral thesis, applying the new physics of ionisation, showed that stars consist overwhelmingly of hydrogen and helium — later called perhaps the most brilliant PhD thesis ever written in astronomy.

Legacy

The Computers exemplified both the indispensable, under-recognised labour of women in early modern science and the transformative shift from visual to photographic astronomy. Their half-million glass plates survive as the Harvard Plate Collection, now being digitised by the DASCH project, and their stories have been popularised in works such as Dava Sobel's The Glass Universe (2016).
