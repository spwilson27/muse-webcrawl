Electron capture detector

An electron capture detector (ECD) is a device for detecting atoms and molecules in a gas by the attachment of electrons via electron capture ionisation. It is used in gas chromatography to detect trace quantities of chemical compounds, and is especially sensitive to halogenated compounds such as pesticides and to other electronegative species. The detector was invented by the scientist James Lovelock in 1957.

How it works

A radioactive beta emitter, typically nickel-63, inside the detector cell emits electrons that ionise the carrier gas (nitrogen or argon–methane), producing a steady standing current between electrodes. When sample molecules with high electron affinity enter the cell, they capture electrons to form negative ions, which recombine rapidly; the resulting decrease in current — measured, in modern designs, by pulsing the voltage to keep the current constant — is proportional to the concentration of the analyte.

Sensitivity and uses

The ECD is among the most sensitive gas-chromatographic detectors, capable of measuring compounds at parts-per-trillion levels. Because of this sensitivity it was instrumental in detecting the widespread distribution of chlorinated pesticides such as DDT and of chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) in the atmosphere — Lovelock's measurements with the ECD provided early evidence that led to awareness of ozone depletion. It remains in routine use for environmental monitoring of pesticides, polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs) and other halogenated pollutants.
