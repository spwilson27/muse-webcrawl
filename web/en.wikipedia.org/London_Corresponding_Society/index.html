The London Corresponding Society (LCS) was a federation of local reading and debating clubs founded in 1792 by the shoemaker Thomas Hardy. It campaigned for parliamentary reform, in particular universal male suffrage and annual parliaments, and was notable for recruiting working-class members at a penny-a-week subscription.

History

Inspired by the ideals of the French Revolution and by Thomas Paine's Rights of Man, the society grew rapidly and established corresponding links with reform societies across Britain. The government of William Pitt the Younger regarded it as seditious. In 1794 Hardy and other leaders were tried for high treason but acquitted, in trials that became a landmark in British legal history.

Suppression

Repressive legislation followed, including the Treasonable Practices Act and Seditious Meetings Act of 1795. In 1799 the society was named in legislation banning it outright, and it effectively ceased to exist, though its methods — mass membership, cheap publications and national conventions — influenced later movements such as Chartism.
