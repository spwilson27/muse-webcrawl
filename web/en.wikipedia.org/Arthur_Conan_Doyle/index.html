Arthur Conan Doyle

Arthur Conan Doyle

Born: 22 May 1859, Edinburgh, Scotland

Died: 7 July 1930 (aged 71), Crowborough, England

Occupation: Writer, physician

Notable works: Sherlock Holmes stories; The Lost World

Sir Arthur Ignatius Conan Doyle (22 May 1859 – 7 July 1930) was a British writer and physician. He created the character Sherlock Holmes in 1887 for A Study in Scarlet, the first of four novels and fifty-six short stories about Holmes and Dr Watson. The Sherlock Holmes stories are milestones in the field of crime fiction.

Early life and medical career

Doyle was born in Edinburgh into an Irish Catholic family. He studied medicine at the University of Edinburgh, where he met Joseph Bell, whose powers of observation became a model for Holmes. He served as a ship's surgeon on voyages to West Africa and the Arctic before setting up a medical practice, and later worked as a volunteer physician in the Boer War.

Writing career

While waiting for patients, Doyle wrote fiction. After the success of A Study in Scarlet (1887) and The Sign of the Four (1890), the short stories published in The Strand Magazine from 1891 made Holmes a sensation. Doyle grew ambivalent about his creation and killed Holmes off at the Reichenbach Falls in "The Final Problem" (1893), but public pressure eventually brought the detective back in The Hound of the Baskervilles (1901–02) and "The Adventure of the Empty House" (1903).

Doyle also wrote historical novels such as The White Company, the Professor Challenger science fiction series beginning with The Lost World (1912), and plays, poems, and non-fiction.

Spiritualism and later life

After the deaths of family members, including his son Kingsley in the First World War, Doyle devoted himself to spiritualism, writing and lecturing extensively in its defence. He was knighted in 1902, largely for a pamphlet defending British conduct in the Boer War. He died of a heart attack at his home, Windlesham, in Crowborough, Sussex.
