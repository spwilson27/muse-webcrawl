ROM cartridge

A ROM cartridge, often just called a cartridge or cart, is a replaceable part designed to be connected to a consumer electronics device such as a home computer, video game console, or, to a lesser extent, electronic musical instruments. It consists of a printed circuit board containing read-only memory (ROM) chips, housed in a protective plastic shell with a connector that plugs into the host device.

History

Cartridges were used to distribute software for early home computers in the 1970s and became the standard medium for video game consoles from the Fairchild Channel F (1976), the first console with interchangeable game cartridges, through the Atari 2600, Nintendo Entertainment System, Sega Genesis, and Nintendo 64. Advantages included near-instant loading, durability, and the ability to include extra hardware on the board, such as memory mappers, co-processors, and battery-backed save RAM. Disadvantages were high manufacturing cost and limited storage compared with optical discs, which displaced cartridges in most home consoles by the late 1990s.

Legacy

Handheld systems continued to use cartridges and card formats for decades, and the Nintendo Switch (2017) returned to game cards for a home console. Cartridge images ("ROMs") are widely used with emulators, and flash cartridges allow homebrew software to run on original hardware.
