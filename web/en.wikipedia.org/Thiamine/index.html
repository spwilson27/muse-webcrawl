Thiamine

Other namesVitamin B1, aneurine
FormulaC12H17N4OS+
FunctionCoenzyme in carbohydrate and amino-acid metabolism
DeficiencyBeriberi, Wernicke–Korsakoff syndrome

Thiamine, also known as vitamin B1, is an essential micronutrient that the human body cannot produce and must obtain from the diet. It is found in whole grains, legumes, some meats and fish, and is added to many fortified foods. In the body it is converted to thiamine pyrophosphate, a coenzyme required by enzymes involved in energy metabolism, including pyruvate dehydrogenase and alpha-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase.

Deficiency

Thiamine deficiency causes beriberi, which can affect the cardiovascular system ("wet" beriberi) or the nervous system ("dry" beriberi), and, particularly in people with alcohol dependence, the Wernicke–Korsakoff syndrome, a serious neurological disorder. Deficiency became a major public-health problem in populations dependent on polished white rice, from which the thiamine-rich husk had been removed.

History

The Dutch physician Christiaan Eijkman showed in the 1890s that unpolished rice cured beriberi-like symptoms in chickens; Casimir Funk isolated the "anti-beriberi factor" from rice polishings in 1912 and coined the term "vitamine". Thiamine was isolated in pure form in 1926 by Barend Jansen and Willem Donath, and its structure was determined and synthesis achieved by Robert R. Williams in the 1930s.
