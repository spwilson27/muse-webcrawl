Micropropagation

Micropropagation or tissue culture is the practice of rapidly multiplying plant stock material to produce many progeny plants, using modern plant tissue culture methods. Micropropagation is used to multiply a wide variety of plants, such as those that have been genetically modified or bred through conventional plant breeding methods. It is also used to provide a sufficient number of plantlets for planting from seedless plants (e.g. sweet potatoes and bananas) or plants that do not respond well to vegetative reproduction.

The American botanist Frederick Campion Steward discovered and pioneered micropropagation and plant tissue culture in the late 1950s and early 1960s, and the French botanist Georges Morel developed the technique for orchids. Tissue culture is used in the conservation of rare plant species through techniques such as cryopreservation.

Stages

Micropropagation typically proceeds through four stages: establishment of a sterile culture from an explant, multiplication of shoots on a nutrient medium containing plant growth regulators, rooting of the shoots, and acclimatisation (hardening off) of the plantlets to greenhouse and field conditions. The culture medium, commonly Murashige and Skoog medium, supplies macronutrients, micronutrients, vitamins, amino acids, and a carbon source such as sucrose.

Applications

Commercial micropropagation is used extensively in horticulture and forestry to produce disease-free, genetically uniform plants at scale, including orchids, bananas, strawberries, potatoes, and many ornamental and timber species. Because plantlets can be produced year-round in a laboratory, the technique also supports germplasm storage and international exchange of planting material.
