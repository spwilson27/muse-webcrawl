Ruse de guerre

A ruse de guerre (French for "ruse of war") is an act of military deception intended to mislead an adversary about one's capabilities, intentions or dispositions, which is expressly permitted under the laws of war. The concept is distinguished from perfidy, which is prohibited: perfidy feigns protected status — such as surrender, ceasefire, civilian status or the emblems of the Red Cross — to kill, injure or capture an adversary who relies on that protection.

Legal basis

The Hague Regulations of 1907 (Article 24) state that "ruses of war and the employment of measures necessary for obtaining information about the enemy and the country are considered permissible". Additional Protocol I to the Geneva Conventions (1977), Article 37(2), gives examples: the use of camouflage, decoys, mock operations and misinformation. The same Protocol prohibits perfidy and regulates the use of enemy flags and uniforms — traditionally allowed while approaching or manoeuvring, but not while actually attacking.

Examples

Historical ruses range from camouflage and dummy equipment (such as the inflatable "Ghost Army" of 1944–45) to false radio traffic, double agents and entire phantom formations, as in Operation Fortitude, which convinced German commanders that the 1944 Normandy landings would come at Calais. At sea, Q-ships and merchant raiders disguised their identity until engaging, and warships customarily flew false colours before hoisting their true ensign to fire. Feigned retreats — famously used by steppe armies and by the Normans at Hastings (in some accounts) — lure pursuers into disorder. Modern military deception doctrine treats such measures as a legitimate, protected part of operations so long as they do not abuse protected signs or statuses.
