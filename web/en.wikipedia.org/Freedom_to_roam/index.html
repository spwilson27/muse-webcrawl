Freedom to roam

The freedom to roam, or everyman's right, is the general public's right to access certain public or privately owned land, lakes, and rivers for recreation and exercise. The right is most fully developed in the Nordic countries — in Finland (jokamiehenoikeus), Sweden (allemansrätten), and Norway (allemannsretten) — where people may walk, ski, camp briefly, and forage for berries and mushrooms on uncultivated land regardless of ownership, provided they do no harm and respect privacy around homes.

Around the world

In Norway, the Outdoor Recreation Act of 1957 codifies the right of access to utmark (outlying land). Sweden's allemansrätten is protected in the constitution though defined mainly by custom and statute law on specific activities. Scotland's Land Reform (Scotland) Act 2003 established a statutory right of responsible access to most land and inland water. England and Wales offer a more limited "right to roam" over designated access land (mountain, moor, heath, down, and registered common land) under the Countryside and Rights of Way Act 2000. Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania, Iceland, Switzerland, and Austria recognize comparable traditions or laws to varying degrees.

Scope and responsibilities

The right typically covers walking, cycling, riding, swimming, and short-term camping, plus picking wild berries, mushrooms, and flowers. It does not extend to hunting or fishing (which are separately regulated), motorized vehicles, cultivated fields, gardens, or the immediate vicinity of dwellings. The governing principle is often summarized as "do not disturb, do not destroy," and roamers bear responsibility for fires, litter, and damage.
