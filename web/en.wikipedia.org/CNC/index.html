CNC

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Computer numerical control (CNC) is the automated control of machine tools and other manufacturing equipment by means of a computer executing pre-programmed sequences of commands. CNC is used to operate mills, lathes, routers, grinders, laser and plasma cutters, and 3D printers, enabling precise, repeatable production of complex parts.

How it works

A part design created in CAD software is converted by CAM software into toolpaths, typically expressed in G-code. The machine's controller reads the instructions and drives servo or stepper motors to position the tool and workpiece along multiple axes, controlling spindle speed, feed rate, tool changes and coolant. Modern machines commonly work in three to five axes simultaneously.

History

Numerical control originated in the late 1940s and 1950s, when John T. Parsons and the MIT Servomechanisms Laboratory developed machines programmed by punched tape for machining helicopter rotor blades and aircraft parts. As computers became cheaper, tape-based NC gave way to CNC in the 1970s, and machining centres with automatic tool changers became standard in industry.

Advantages and applications

CNC machining offers high accuracy and repeatability, reduced operator error, and the ability to produce geometries impractical by hand. It is central to aerospace, automotive, medical device, electronics and toolmaking industries, and desktop CNC machines have brought the technology to small workshops and hobbyists.
