Szemerédi regularity lemma

The Szemerédi regularity lemma is a fundamental result in graph theory, proved by Endre Szemerédi in the 1970s as a tool for his proof of Szemerédi's theorem on arithmetic progressions. It states, informally, that every sufficiently large graph can be approximated by a bounded-complexity “random-like” structure: its vertices can be partitioned into a bounded number of equal parts so that the edges between almost all pairs of parts are distributed as evenly as in a random graph.

Statement

A pair of vertex sets (A, B) is called ε-regular if all sufficiently large subsets A′ ⊆ A and B′ ⊆ B have edge densities that differ from the density of (A, B) by at most ε. The lemma says that for every ε > 0 and every minimum number of parts m, there is a bound M(ε, m) such that every graph can be partitioned into between m and M parts, all but an ε-fraction of the pairs being ε-regular. The bound produced by the proof is of tower type, and Timothy Gowers showed that such enormous bounds are in general unavoidable.

Applications

Replacing a huge graph by its constant-sized “reduced graph” makes many counting and embedding problems tractable. Consequences include the triangle counting lemma, the Ruzsa–Szemerédi theorem, a proof of Szemerédi's theorem itself via the Furstenberg–Katznelson correspondence, removal lemmas, and foundations for property testing of graphs in theoretical computer science.
