New Urbanism is an urban design movement that promotes environmentally friendly habits by creating walkable neighbourhoods containing a wide range of housing and job types. It arose in the United States in the early 1980s as a reaction against suburban sprawl, single-use zoning, and automobile dependence, and it draws on the patterns of pre-war towns and cities built before the rise of the car.

The Congress for the New Urbanism (CNU), founded in 1993, adopted the Charter of the New Urbanism in 1996, setting out principles for the region, the neighbourhood, and the block, street, and building.

[ Image: A walkable main street in a New Urbanist town, with porches and mixed-use buildings ]

Principles

Core principles include walkability (most daily needs within a ten-minute walk), connected street networks, mixed-use and mixed-income development, a range of housing types, quality architecture emphasising the public realm, traditional neighbourhood structure with a defined centre and edge, increased density, green transportation, and sustainability. The "transect" organises design along a gradient from rural to urban intensity.

History and examples

The resort town of Seaside, Florida (begun 1981), designed by Andrés Duany and Elizabeth Plater-Zyberk, was the movement's first full-scale demonstration. Later projects include Kentlands in Maryland, Poundbury in England (championed by the then Prince Charles, with master planner Leon Krier), and Celebration, Florida, developed by Disney. New Urbanist ideas have influenced form-based zoning codes, transit-oriented development, and post-disaster rebuilding plans.

Criticism

Critics argue that many New Urbanist projects are expensive, aesthetically nostalgic, and still car-dependent because they are built on greenfield suburban sites; that they can accelerate gentrification; and that their social goals outrun market realities. Supporters respond that the principles work best as infill and retrofit of existing communities.
