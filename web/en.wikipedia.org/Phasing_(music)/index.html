Phasing (music)

Phasing is a compositional technique in which the same part (a repeated pattern) is played on two musical instruments in steady but not identical tempo. Over time the two patterns gradually move out of sync, creating echo effects, then gradually return to unison — a complete phase cycle. The technique is a hallmark of minimal music.

Development

The American composer Steve Reich discovered the effect accidentally in 1965 while working with two tape recorders playing loops of a preacher's voice, which slowly drifted out of synchronization. He exploited the phenomenon in the tape pieces It's Gonna Rain (1965) and Come Out (1966), then transferred it to live performers in Piano Phase (1967), in which one pianist holds a steady tempo while the other gradually accelerates through a twelve-note pattern until the parts realign. Violin Phase (1967) and Clapping Music (1972) — the latter using abrupt pattern shifts rather than gradual tempo change — extended the idea. Terry Riley's In C (1964) achieves related effects by free alignment of repeated modules.

Related effects

In audio production, phasing or flanging also names an electronic effect in which a signal is mixed with a slightly delayed copy of itself, producing a sweeping comb-filter sound; the two usages share the underlying wave-phase principle but are distinct practices.
