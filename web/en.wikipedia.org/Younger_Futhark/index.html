The Younger Futhark, also called Scandinavian runes, is a runic alphabet of 16 characters used during the Viking Age for writing Old Norse. It developed from the 24-rune Elder Futhark during the 7th–8th centuries CE, paradoxically shrinking even as the language's sound system grew more complex, so that several runes served multiple sounds.

Development and variants

Two main variants existed: the Danish "long-branch" runes and the Swedish-Norwegian "short-twig" runes with simplified shapes, possibly developed for speed. A staveless (Hälsinge) variant omitted vertical strokes almost entirely. Around 1000 CE, "dotted" runes began distinguishing sounds such as e and g, prefiguring the expanded medieval runic alphabet.

Use

Younger Futhark appears on thousands of Viking Age runestones, especially in Sweden's Uppland region, commemorating the dead, claiming inheritance and marking Christian faith alongside older formulas. Shorter inscriptions record ownership, love notes, graffiti — including runes carved by Varangians in Hagia Sophia — and everyday messages on wooden sticks from Bergen and other towns. Runic writing persisted in parts of Scandinavia, notably Dalarna, into the modern era.
