Monochord

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A monochord is an ancient musical and scientific instrument consisting of a single string stretched over a resonating box, typically with a movable bridge. It was used from antiquity through the Renaissance to investigate the mathematical relationships between string length and pitch, and thus stands at the origin of both music theory and experimental acoustics.

Pythagoras and the ratios of consonance

Tradition credits Pythagoras (6th century BCE) with discovering on the monochord that the principal consonances correspond to simple whole-number ratios of string length: stopping the string at half its length gives the octave (2:1), at two-thirds the perfect fifth (3:2), and at three-quarters the perfect fourth (4:3). These experiments — later transmitted by writers such as Boethius, whose De institutione musica dominated medieval music teaching — became the canonical demonstration that number underlies harmony, and the monochord served as the standard tool for teaching tuning and intervals (the division of the monochord).

Later use

Medieval and Renaissance theorists used monochords to construct scales and temperaments; multi-string descendants used for actual music-making include the tromba marina, a large single-string bowed instrument, while in East and Southeast Asia single-string zithers such as the Vietnamese đàn bầu developed independently as performance instruments. Scientific use of the monochord persisted into the early modern period in demonstrations of vibration and harmonics.
