Zombie film

A scene from Night of the Living Dead (1968), the foundational modern zombie film

A zombie film is a film genre that depicts zombies — fictional undead beings, usually created through the reanimation of human corpses. Historically, zombies in film were often depicted as mindless, reanimated corpses under the control of a master, following the Haitian folklore of the zonbi. Beginning with George A. Romero's Night of the Living Dead (1968), the genre shifted toward flesh-eating ghouls arising from an unexplained apocalyptic event, and zombies became a staple of horror cinema.

History

The first zombie films, such as White Zombie (1932) and I Walked with a Zombie (1943), drew on Haitian Vodou traditions. Romero's Night of the Living Dead reimagined the creatures as cannibalistic undead whose bite spreads their condition, and his sequel Dawn of the Dead (1978) added social satire. The genre flourished in Italian cinema in the late 1970s and in low-budget productions worldwide during the 1980s.

A resurgence began in the early 2000s with 28 Days Later (2002), which popularised fast-moving infected, and the Resident Evil series, adapted from video games. Edgar Wright's Shaun of the Dead (2004) blended the genre with comedy, while the South Korean film Train to Busan (2016) achieved international success. Zombie stories also spread to television, most prominently in The Walking Dead.

Characteristics

Modern zombie films typically feature small groups of survivors contending with hordes of the undead and with social breakdown; destruction of the brain or head is usually the only way to kill a zombie. The genre is frequently read as allegory for consumerism, pandemics, and societal collapse.
