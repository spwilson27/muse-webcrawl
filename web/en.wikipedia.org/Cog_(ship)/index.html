Cog (ship)

A cog is a type of single-masted sailing cargo ship that was widely used in northern European waters from about the 10th to the 14th centuries. Cogs were the workhorse vessels of the Hanseatic League and dominated trade in the North Sea and the Baltic.

Characteristics

Cogs were clinker-built of oak, with a flat bottom that allowed them to beach at low tide, straight stem and stern posts, and a single square sail. Later examples added sterncastle and forecastle structures and a sternpost-mounted rudder, replacing the side steering oar. Typical cogs were around 15–25 metres long and could carry 100–200 tons of cargo — wine, grain, timber, wool and herring among the commonest.

Archaeology

The best-preserved example is the Bremen cog, discovered in the Weser River in 1962 and dated to about 1380; it is displayed at the German Maritime Museum in Bremerhaven. Other wrecks, such as the Kamper cog in the Netherlands, have informed full-scale sailing replicas.
