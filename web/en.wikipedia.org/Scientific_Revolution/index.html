Scientific Revolution

The Scientific Revolution was a series of events that marked the emergence of modern science during the early modern period, when developments in mathematics, physics, astronomy, biology, and chemistry transformed the views of society about nature. Conventionally dated from the publication of Nicolaus Copernicus's De revolutionibus orbium coelestium (On the Revolutions of the Heavenly Spheres) in 1543 to Isaac Newton's Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica in 1687, it replaced the Aristotelian–Ptolemaic worldview with heliocentrism, the mechanical philosophy, and a new commitment to observation, experiment, and mathematical description.

Astronomy and physics

Copernicus proposed a heliocentric model retaining circular orbits; Tycho Brahe's unprecedentedly accurate observations enabled Johannes Kepler to formulate his three laws of planetary motion (1609, 1619), including elliptical orbits. Galileo Galilei, wielding the new telescope from 1609, observed the moons of Jupiter, the phases of Venus, sunspots, and lunar mountains — undermining Aristotelian cosmology — and founded the science of motion with his analysis of falling bodies and projectiles. Descartes supplied an influential mechanical philosophy and analytic geometry, while Newton unified Kepler's astronomy and Galileo's mechanics under universal gravitation and his three laws of motion, inventing calculus (independently developed by Leibniz) to do so.

Method and institutions

Francis Bacon articulated an inductive experimental method (Novum Organum, 1620), and experimental practice was embodied by the air-pump experiments of Robert Boyle and Robert Hooke and William Harvey's demonstration of the circulation of the blood (1628). New institutions sustained the enterprise: the Royal Society of London (1660) and the Paris Académie des Sciences (1666), along with the first scientific journals (1665). The printing press, improved instruments (telescope, microscope, barometer, pendulum clock), and patronage networks linked scholars across Europe.

Other sciences

In medicine, Andreas Vesalius's De humani corporis fabrica (1543) re-founded anatomy on dissection. In chemistry, Boyle's The Sceptical Chymist (1661) attacked Aristotelian elements and laid groundwork later completed by Lavoisier's chemical revolution in the 18th century. Microscopists such as Leeuwenhoek revealed a microbial world, and naturalists began systems of classification culminating in Linnaeus (18th century).

Historiography

The very concept of a "Scientific Revolution" was popularised by Herbert Butterfield (1949) and Alexandre Koyré, who famously called it the most important transformation since the rise of Christianity's worldview. Later historians have stressed continuities with medieval scholastic natural philosophy, the contributions of craftsmen and non-European knowledge traditions, and the diversity of early modern "sciences," sometimes preferring the plural term "early modern science." Thomas Kuhn's The Structure of Scientific Revolutions (1962) drew its central examples — paradigm shifts — from this period.
