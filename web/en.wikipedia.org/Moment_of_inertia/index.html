Moment of inertia


Image placeholder: a flywheel — high moment of inertia by design

The moment of inertia, denoted I and sometimes called the mass moment of inertia or rotational inertia, is a property of a rigid body that determines the torque needed for a desired angular acceleration about an axis of rotation: it is the rotational analogue of mass in linear motion. It depends not only on the body's mass but on how that mass is distributed relative to the axis.

I = Σ mi ri²  =  ∫ r² dm

For a point mass the moment of inertia is mr². Standard results include a solid sphere, (2/5)MR²; a hoop or thin cylindrical shell about its central axis, MR²; a solid cylinder or disc, (1/2)MR²; and a thin rod about its centre, (1/12)ML². The parallel axis theorem gives the moment about any axis from the moment about a parallel axis through the centre of mass: I = Icm + Md².

Dynamics

With moment of inertia, Newton's second law for rotation reads τ = Iα, and rotational kinetic energy is ½Iω². Angular momentum is L = Iω. Because bodies spinning with the same angular velocity but different mass distributions carry different energies, changes in mass distribution change rotation speed when angular momentum is conserved — the principle behind a figure skater's spin accelerating as the arms are drawn in. In three dimensions the moment of inertia is a tensor, with three principal moments about principal axes.

Applications

Engineers use flywheels with large moments of inertia to smooth power delivery; designers of vehicles and spacecraft compute inertia tensors for stability and control; and the measured moment of inertia of a planet (from its gravity field) constrains its internal density distribution.
