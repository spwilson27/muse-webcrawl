Decadent movement


Art associated with the decadent movement

The Decadent movement was a late nineteenth-century artistic and literary movement, centered in Western Europe, that followed an aesthetic ideology of excess and artificiality. "Decadent" was initially used as a term of criticism by journalists and academics, but was defiantly adopted by a generation of French writers and artists.

Associated with symbolism and aestheticism, the movement flourished in France and spread to Britain, Germany, Russia, and elsewhere. Its central figures include Charles Baudelaire, Joris-Karl Huysmans, whose novel À rebours (1884) is often called the "bible of decadence," and in England, Oscar Wilde and Aubrey Beardsley.

Characteristics

Decadent works characteristically embrace artifice over nature, perversity, refined and morbid subject matter, and the doctrine of "art for art's sake." The movement reflected a sense of cultural exhaustion and fascination with decay at the fin de siècle. The journal The Yellow Book became closely associated with English decadence in the 1890s.

Legacy

Although short-lived as a self-conscious movement, decadence influenced modernism and later countercultures, and writers such as Wilde left a lasting mark on English literature.
