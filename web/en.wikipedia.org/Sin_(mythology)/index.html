Sin (mythology)

Sin (Akkadian: Sîn) or Nanna (Sumerian: Nanna) was the god of the moon in the religion of ancient Mesopotamia. As Nanna he was worshipped by the Sumerians from at least the third millennium BCE; as Sin he was venerated throughout Akkad, Assyria and Babylonia. His chief sanctuary was the temple Ekišnugal at Ur in southern Mesopotamia, and he was also the patron deity of the city of Harran in the north.

Character and family

Sin was the son of Enlil and Ninlil and the father of the sun god Shamash (Utu) and of Inanna/Ishtar, goddess of Venus. His symbol was the crescent moon, and he was commonly depicted as an old man with a flowing beard of lapis lazuli, riding a winged bull or travelling across the night sky in a boat. As the moon, he governed the calendar, measured time, and was believed to oversee fertility, cattle herding and the menstrual cycle.

Cult centres

The great ziggurat of Ur stood within Sin's temple precinct; the kings of the Third Dynasty of Ur lavishly endowed his cult, and the king's daughter traditionally served as his high priestess (en-priestess), most famously Enheduanna in the Akkadian period. In Harran, his temple Ekhulkhul was rebuilt by the Assyrian and Babylonian kings; the last Babylonian king, Nabonidus (r. 556–539 BCE), whose mother was a devotee of Sin of Harran, elevated the moon god's cult and restored his temples, a devotion some sources present as a cause of friction with the priesthood of Marduk in Babylon.

Later influence

The cult of Sin at Harran survived into the Islamic period, where Harranian "Sabians" continued lunar worship for centuries. The name Mount Sinai has occasionally been speculatively linked to Sin, though this etymology is not generally accepted.
