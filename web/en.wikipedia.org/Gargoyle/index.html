Gargoyle

In architecture, a gargoyle is a carved or formed grotesque figure with a spout designed to convey water from a roof and away from the side of a building, thereby preventing rainwater from eroding the mortar between stone blocks. Decorative figures without a spout are properly called grotesques or chimeras, though "gargoyle" is often used for both.

Etymology and legend

The word derives from Old French gargouille (throat or gullet), related to the verb "to gargle". A French legend tells of St Romanus of Rouen subduing a dragon called La Gargouille whose head was mounted on the cathedral, inspiring waterspout carvings.

History

Waterspouts in animal form appear in ancient Egyptian, Greek and Roman architecture — lion-headed spouts survive on the Acropolis and at Pompeii. Gargoyles flourished in medieval Gothic architecture from the 13th century, notably on French cathedrals including Notre-Dame de Paris and Chartres, where they also served apotropaic and didactic purposes. The Gothic Revival of the 19th century restored many weathered examples; architect Viollet-le-Duc famously added chimeras to Notre-Dame.

Modern use

Gargoyles continue to appear on Gothic Revival and Art Deco buildings — including the Chrysler Building's eagle ornaments — and remain popular decorative motifs, garden ornaments and figures in fantasy literature and games.
