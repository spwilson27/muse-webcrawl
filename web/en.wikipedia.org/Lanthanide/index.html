Lanthanide

The lanthanide (/ˈlænθənaɪd/) or lanthanoid (/ˈlænθənɔɪd/) series of chemical elements comprises at least the 14 metallic chemical elements with atomic numbers 58 through 71, from cerium through lutetium. All lanthanides are f-block elements, corresponding to the filling of the 4f electron shell. Lanthanum — or, occasionally, lutetium — is often included as well, giving 15 elements in total. Together with the chemically similar scandium and yttrium, the lanthanides are commonly grouped as the rare-earth elements.

Properties

The lanthanides are silvery, relatively soft metals with high melting and boiling points. They are quite reactive, tarnishing in air and reacting slowly with cold water and rapidly with hot water. Because the 4f electrons are poorly shielded, ionic radii shrink steadily across the series — the lanthanide contraction — a phenomenon with significant consequences for the chemistry of the elements that follow, such as hafnium being nearly identical in size to zirconium.

Chemistry

The dominant oxidation state is +3, with trivalent cations Ln3+ formed by loss of the two 6s electrons and one 4f or 5d electron. Exceptions include Ce(IV), Tb(IV), and Eu(II) and Yb(II), reflecting the stability of empty, half-filled and filled 4f subshells. Lanthanide compounds are mostly ionic, and their magnetism and colors arise from the unpaired 4f electrons; sharp f–f transitions make many of their ions useful as phosphors and laser media.

Occurrence and production

Despite the name "rare earth", the lanthanides are not especially rare: cerium is about as abundant in the Earth's crust as copper. The principal ore minerals are monazite, bastnäsite and xenotime. Because the elements are chemically very similar, their separation historically required laborious fractional crystallization; modern production uses solvent extraction and ion-exchange chromatography. Promethium is the exception — it is essentially absent from nature because all its isotopes are radioactive.

Applications

Lanthanides underpin many modern technologies: neodymium and samarium permanent magnets, europium and terbium phosphors in displays and lamps, lanthanum in camera lenses and nickel–metal hydride batteries, cerium in catalytic converters and lighter flints (misch metal), gadolinium in MRI contrast agents, and erbium in fiber-optic amplifiers.

Nomenclature

The name derives from lanthanum, itself from the Greek lanthanein ("to lie hidden"), because lanthanum was "hidden" in cerium oxide. The IUPAC recommends "lanthanoid" over "lanthanide" on the grounds that the suffix "-ide" usually denotes a negative ion, though "lanthanide" remains in wide common use.
