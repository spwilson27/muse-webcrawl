Lactic acid fermentation

Lactic acid fermentation is a metabolic process by which glucose or other six-carbon sugars are converted into cellular energy and the metabolite lactate. It is an anaerobic fermentation reaction that occurs in some bacteria and animal cells, such as muscle cells, when oxygen is scarce. In homolactic fermentation, one molecule of glucose yields two molecules of lactic acid; heterolactic fermentation also produces carbon dioxide and ethanol or acetic acid.

Biochemistry

Glycolysis splits glucose into two molecules of pyruvate, generating a net of two ATP and two NADH. To regenerate NAD+ and keep glycolysis running without oxygen, the enzyme lactate dehydrogenase reduces pyruvate to lactate, consuming the NADH. The process is far less efficient than aerobic respiration, which extracts up to about 30 ATP per glucose.

Applications

Lactobacillus and related lactic acid bacteria drive the production of yoghurt, cheese, sauerkraut, kimchi, pickles, sourdough bread, kefir and many fermented sausages and beverages; the acid they produce preserves food and creates characteristic flavours. In exercising muscle, lactate produced during intense effort is shuttled to the liver and reconverted to glucose in the Cori cycle; contrary to popular belief, lactate itself is not the direct cause of delayed-onset muscle soreness.
