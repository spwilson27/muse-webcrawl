Phosphorus Cycle

The phosphorus cycle: rock to soil to life to sea and, over geological time, back to rock.

The phosphorus cycle is the biogeochemical cycle describing the movement of phosphorus through the lithosphere, hydrosphere, and biosphere. Unlike carbon and nitrogen, phosphorus has no significant gaseous phase, so its cycle is slow and sedimentary: it moves from rocks to soils to organisms to ocean sediments, and back to land only through geological uplift over tens of millions of years.

Pathway

Weathering releases phosphate from apatite-bearing rock. Plants absorb the dissolved phosphate ions and build them into DNA, RNA, ATP, and membranes; animals obtain phosphorus by eating plants or other animals, also stocking it in bones and teeth as calcium phosphate. Decomposers return phosphate from dead tissue to soil, but runoff continuously bleeds phosphorus to rivers and the sea, where it settles into sediments.

Human impact

Mining of phosphate rock for fertilizer — largely via phosphoric acid (see the phosphoric-acid perspective on this cycle) — plus deforestation and erosion have greatly increased phosphorus flows. Excess nutrients fuel algal blooms and oxygen-depleted dead zones in lakes and coastal seas. Because economically mineable phosphate rock is finite and concentrated in few countries, notably Morocco, phosphorus security and recycling from manure and wastewater are active policy concerns.
