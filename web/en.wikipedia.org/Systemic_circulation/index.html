Systemic circulation

The systemic circulation is the part of the cardiovascular system which transports oxygenated blood away from the heart through the aorta to the rest of the body, and returns oxygen-depleted blood back to the heart. The systemic circulation is longer and higher-pressure than the pulmonary circulation, which carries blood between the heart and the lungs.

Course

Oxygenated blood leaves the left ventricle of the heart through the aortic valve into the aorta, the largest artery in the body. The aorta branches into progressively smaller arteries and arterioles, which deliver blood to capillary beds in every tissue. After exchanging oxygen, nutrients and waste products with the tissues, the blood returns through venules and veins, ultimately reaching the right atrium via the superior and inferior venae cavae.

Function

The systemic circulation supplies oxygen and nutrients to all the tissues of the body other than the lungs themselves, removes carbon dioxide and metabolic waste, transports hormones, and helps regulate body temperature and pH. Blood flow through the systemic circuit is governed by arterial blood pressure and local vasodilation and vasoconstriction.

Clinical significance

Diseases of the systemic circulation include hypertension, atherosclerosis and peripheral artery disease. Failure of the left side of the heart leads to congestion of the systemic circulation, producing symptoms such as peripheral edema.
