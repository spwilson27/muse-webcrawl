Insulator (electricity)

Insulator (electricity)
FunctionElectrical isolation and mechanical support
MaterialsPorcelain, glass, silicone rubber, polymers
ApplicationsOverhead power lines, substations, wiring

An electrical insulator is a material whose internal electric charges do not flow freely; very little electric current will flow through it under the influence of an electric field. Insulators are used in electrical equipment to support and separate electrical conductors without allowing current through themselves.

Properties

Insulating materials have high resistivity and high dielectric strength — the maximum field they can withstand before breakdown. Air, glass, porcelain, many plastics, and dry wood are common insulators. Unlike conductors, insulators have few mobile charge carriers; their electrons are tightly bound to atoms.

Power line insulators

Overhead transmission lines are supported by strings of disc-shaped insulators made of porcelain or toughened glass, or by long-rod composite insulators of silicone rubber over a fibreglass core. Their stacked, shedded shape lengthens the surface leakage path and sheds rain, preventing flashover. The number of discs increases with line voltage. Vintage glass insulators from telegraph and power lines are widely collected.

Other uses

Insulators coat wires and cables, form the bodies of plugs, switches, and circuit breakers, and support busbars in substations. In electronics, insulating substrates and dielectrics are fundamental to capacitors and printed circuit boards.
