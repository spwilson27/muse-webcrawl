Animal slaughter

Animal slaughter is the killing of animals, usually referring to the killing of domestic livestock. It is estimated that each year, more than 80 billion land animals are slaughtered for food around the world. Slaughter is typically performed by regulated methods designed to stun the animal unconscious before death, in accordance with animal welfare legislation that varies by jurisdiction.

Overview

In most countries, animals are slaughtered in specialised facilities called slaughterhouses or abattoirs. Common livestock species include cattle, sheep, pigs, chickens, goats and ducks. Slaughter practices in many countries are regulated by law concerning humane treatment, hygiene and meat inspection.

Methods

Stunning

Stunning renders an animal insensitive to pain before exsanguination (bleeding). Common methods include captive-bolt stunning for cattle, electrical stunning for poultry and pigs, and controlled-atmosphere (gas) stunning. Some religious slaughter traditions, such as halal and shechita, have their own prescribed methods and are exempt from stunning requirements in a number of jurisdictions.

Exsanguination

Following stunning, the animal's major blood vessels are cut so that it dies from blood loss. The carcass is then dressed, eviscerated and inspected before being chilled and processed.

Animal welfare

Animal slaughter is the subject of ongoing ethical debate. Welfare concerns include transport conditions, handling, lairage, stunning efficacy and the slaughter of conscious animals. Organisations and legislators have responded with auditing standards, CCTV requirements in some jurisdictions, and the development of cultivated and plant-based alternatives.

Religious slaughter

Jewish shechita and Islamic dhabihah prescribe specific rules for slaughter, including a swift cut by a trained slaughterer and invocations before killing. The permissibility and labelling of meat from unstunned slaughter remain politically contested in several European countries.
