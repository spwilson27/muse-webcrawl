Hypostyle

Type: Architectural element
Examples: Karnak, Persepolis, Great Mosque of Córdoba

In architecture, a hypostyle hall is a large room whose flat roof is supported by rows of columns, the name deriving from the Greek hypóstȳlos, "under columns". The form allowed builders to create vast interior spaces before the development of long-span beams and arches.

Ancient Egypt

The most famous example is the Great Hypostyle Hall at Karnak in the Temple of Amun-Re, built chiefly under Seti I and Ramesses II. Its 134 massive sandstone columns cover about 5,000 square metres; the twelve central columns, nearly 21 metres tall, raised a clerestory that admitted light and air above the lower side roofs.

Persia and the Islamic world

The Apadana at Persepolis used slender columns to support a timber roof over audiences of thousands. Hypostyle plans became characteristic of early mosques, notably the Great Mosque of Córdoba, whose forest of double-tiered arches on reused columns expanded in stages to hold huge congregations, and the mosques of Kairouan and Damascus.

Characteristics

Because stone lintels span only short distances, hypostyle halls have closely spaced columns and a dim, forest-like interior. Raised central naves with clerestory lighting, as at Karnak, are a recurring refinement.
