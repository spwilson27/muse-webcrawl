Lunar phase

The lunar phases as they appear in the Northern Hemisphere

Synodic month: ~29.53 days
Primary phases: new, first quarter, full, last quarter

A lunar phase or Moon phase is the apparent shape of the directly sunlit portion of the Moon as viewed from Earth. The lunar phases gradually change over a synodic month (about 29.53 days) as the Moon's orbital positions around Earth and Earth around the Sun shift. The visible side of the Moon is variously sunlit, depending on the position of the Moon in its orbit, so the fraction of the Moon's disk that is illuminated varies from 0% (at new moon) to nearly 100% (at full moon).

The eight phases

The lunar cycle is conventionally divided into eight phases. The four primary phases are the new moon, first quarter, full moon, and last (or third) quarter. Between them lie four intermediate phases: waxing crescent, waxing gibbous, waning gibbous, and waning crescent. "Waxing" means the illuminated portion is growing, while "waning" means it is shrinking. A crescent Moon is less than half illuminated; a gibbous Moon is more than half illuminated.

Cause

The phases are not caused by Earth's shadow — that is a lunar eclipse, which can occur only at full moon. Instead, the phase depends on the relative positions of the Sun, Earth, and Moon. At new moon, the Moon lies between Earth and the Sun, so the sunlit half faces away from Earth. At full moon, Earth lies between the Sun and Moon, and the whole near side is illuminated.

Hemisphere differences

Observers in the Northern and Southern Hemispheres see the phases mirrored: a waxing crescent lit on its right side in the north appears lit on its left side in the south. Near the equator, the crescent can appear as a "wet moon" or Cheshire moon, with its points facing upward.

Cultural significance

Lunar phases underpin many lunisolar calendars and religious observances. The Islamic calendar begins each month at the sighting of the crescent moon, and the dates of Easter and Passover are computed from the full moon following the spring equinox.
