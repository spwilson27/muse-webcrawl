Public policy

Public policy is made and implemented through government institutions.

Public policy is the set of principles, laws, regulations, funding decisions, and courses of action adopted by a government in response to perceived public problems. Political scientist Thomas R. Dye's classic definition describes it as "whatever governments choose to do or not to do". Public policy spans fields such as health, education, welfare, taxation, transport, environment, housing, and foreign affairs, and is studied both as a political process and as a professional discipline taught in schools of public policy and public administration.

The policy process

Textbook models describe a cycle of stages: agenda-setting (problems reaching government attention), policy formulation, adoption or legitimation, implementation, and evaluation, with feedback into new agendas. Scholars such as John Kingdon showed that agendas open when problem, policy, and political "streams" converge in a window of opportunity, while the advocacy-coalition and punctuated-equilibrium frameworks explain long periods of stability broken by rapid change. Implementation research emphasises that outcomes depend on street-level bureaucrats, intergovernmental relations, and target-group behaviour, not merely on legislative intent.

Instruments and analysis

Governments act through regulation, taxation, spending and subsidies, public provision of services, information campaigns, and "nudges" drawn from behavioural science. Policy analysis applies economics, statistics, and evaluation methods — cost–benefit analysis, randomised trials, and programme evaluation — to compare options, while think tanks, interest groups, and the media shape debate. Modern challenges include evidence-based policy making, policy transfer between countries, and governing long-horizon problems such as climate change and pandemics.
