Differential geometry of curves

The Frenet frame (tangent, normal, binormal) moves along a space curve.

The differential geometry of curves is the branch of differential geometry that studies smooth curves in the plane and in three-dimensional space using the methods of differential and integral calculus. It describes a curve's local shape by quantities — curvature and torsion — that are invariant under rigid motions.

Plane curves

A plane curve can be reparametrised by arc length s. Its signed curvature κ(s) measures the rate at which the tangent direction turns; a circle of radius R has constant curvature 1/R, and a straight line has curvature zero. Osculating circles approximate the curve to second order. Global results include the theorem of turning tangents (the total turning of a simple closed convex curve is 2π) and the four-vertex theorem.

Space curves and the Frenet–Serret formulas

For a curve in space, at each point one defines the unit tangent T, principal normal N, and binormal B = T × N, forming the moving Frenet frame. The Frenet–Serret formulas express the derivatives of these vectors in terms of the curvature κ, which measures bending, and the torsion τ, which measures twisting out of the osculating plane; a curve is planar exactly when its torsion vanishes.

Fundamental theorem

The fundamental theorem of the local theory of curves states that, given smooth functions κ(s) > 0 and τ(s), there exists a space curve with that curvature and torsion, and it is unique up to rigid motion. Thus curvature and torsion completely determine a curve's shape — the starting point for the corresponding theory of surfaces and, ultimately, for Riemannian geometry and general relativity.
