The axolotl (Ambystoma mexicanum) is a paedomorphic salamander endemic to the lake complex near Mexico City, principally Lake Xochimilco. Unlike most amphibians it reaches adulthood without undergoing metamorphosis, retaining larval features — feathery external gills and a finned tail — throughout its life, a phenomenon called neoteny.

Biology

Axolotls are carnivorous, feeding on small fish, worms, insect larvae and crustaceans, which they ingest by suction. They are renowned for their regenerative abilities: axolotls can regrow entire limbs, parts of the spinal cord, heart tissue and portions of the brain without scarring, making them a major model organism in regenerative biology. Their genome, at about 32 billion base pairs, is the largest animal genome yet sequenced.

Conservation

The wild population has collapsed because of urbanisation, water pollution and introduced fish such as tilapia and carp; the species is listed as critically endangered, and surveys in Xochimilco found densities falling from about 6,000 per square kilometre in 1998 to fewer than 100 within a decade. Paradoxically, the axolotl is common in captivity, kept worldwide as a pet and laboratory animal — most captive animals are leucistic (pink-white) morphs.

In culture

The name derives from the Nahuatl āxōlōtl, linked to the Aztec god Xolotl, who in myth transformed himself into an axolotl to escape sacrifice. The animal became a Mexican cultural icon and appears on the 50-peso banknote issued in 2021.
