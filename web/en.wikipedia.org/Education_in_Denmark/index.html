Education in Denmark

Education in Denmark is compulsory for children between the ages of about 6 and 16 and is predominantly publicly financed and free of charge, including at the university level. The system is supervised by the Ministry of Children and Education and the Ministry of Higher Education and Science. Denmark is noted for its folk high schools, its strong vocational sector, and a generous student grant system (SU) that pays monthly stipends to students in higher education.

Primary and lower secondary education

The Folkeskole is the municipal primary and lower secondary school, comprising an optional preschool class and grades 1–9 (with an optional tenth grade). Education is compulsory, though attendance at the Folkeskole itself is not: a substantial minority of children attend state-subsidized private schools (friskoler and private schools), and home schooling is permitted. There are no formal examinations until the final years, and pupils are taught by the same class teacher for many subjects over several years.

Upper secondary education

After the Folkeskole, most young people enter either general upper secondary programmes — the STX (gymnasium), the business-oriented HHX, or the technical HTX, typically lasting three years — or vocational education and training (EUD), which combines school-based learning with apprenticeships in firms. The higher preparatory examination (HF) offers a shorter two-year route.

Higher education

Denmark has eight universities, including the University of Copenhagen (founded 1479) and Aarhus University, alongside university colleges offering professional bachelor's degrees and business academies. Tuition is free for students from the EU/EEA, and the state educational grant (Statens Uddannelsesstøtte, SU) provides monthly support. Degrees follow the Bologna structure of bachelor's, master's, and PhD.

Folk high schools and adult education

The folk high school movement, inspired by N. F. S. Grundtvig in the nineteenth century, offers non-formal residential adult education without examinations; around seventy folk high schools still operate. Adult and continuing education is extensive, including the AMU labour-market training system.
