Thrombocytosis

SpecialtyHematology
SymptomsOften none; headache, dizziness, chest pain, fainting, numbness of hands and feet
CausesReactive (secondary) or clonal (essential thrombocythemia)
Diagnostic methodComplete blood count; platelet count above 450 × 109/L
TreatmentTreat underlying cause; low-dose aspirin; cytoreductive therapy in high-risk cases

Thrombocytosis is the presence of high platelet counts in the blood. Normally, the platelet count ranges from 150 to 450 × 109/L; counts persistently above this range indicate thrombocytosis. Although often discovered incidentally on a routine blood test, marked elevations can be associated with abnormal clotting (thrombosis) or, paradoxically, bleeding.

Classification

Thrombocytosis is divided into primary (clonal) and secondary (reactive) forms. Primary thrombocytosis occurs in myeloproliferative neoplasms such as essential thrombocythemia, polycythemia vera, and primary myelofibrosis, and is frequently driven by mutations in the JAK2, CALR, or MPL genes. Reactive thrombocytosis is far more common and arises as a physiological response to another process.

Causes

Reactive thrombocytosis may follow infection, chronic inflammation (such as rheumatoid arthritis or inflammatory bowel disease), iron deficiency, blood loss, surgery, trauma, malignancy, or removal of the spleen (splenectomy). Certain drugs, including epinephrine and vincristine, can also raise the platelet count transiently.

Signs and symptoms

Most people with reactive thrombocytosis have no symptoms attributable to the platelet count itself. In clonal disease, symptoms may include headache, visual disturbances, dizziness, burning pain in the hands and feet (erythromelalgia), and abnormal bleeding or clotting, including stroke, myocardial infarction, and deep vein thrombosis.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis rests on a complete blood count showing a persistently elevated platelet count. Further evaluation seeks an underlying cause: blood smear examination, iron studies, inflammatory markers, and molecular testing for JAK2, CALR, and MPL mutations. Bone marrow biopsy may be performed when a myeloproliferative neoplasm is suspected.

Treatment

Reactive thrombocytosis generally requires no platelet-lowering treatment; management is directed at the underlying condition. In essential thrombocythemia, low-dose aspirin is commonly used, and patients at high risk of thrombosis may receive cytoreductive agents such as hydroxyurea or anagrelide.
