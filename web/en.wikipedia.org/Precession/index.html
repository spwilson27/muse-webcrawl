Precession

Precession is a change in the orientation of the rotational axis of a rotating body. When a torque is applied to a spinning object — as gravity acts on a tilted spinning top — the axis does not simply tip over; instead it sweeps out a cone around the vertical, a motion described by the equations of rigid-body dynamics and the conservation of angular momentum.

Physics

For a spinning top or gyroscope with angular momentum L, an external torque τ changes the angular momentum at the rate dL/dt = τ. Because gravity's torque on a tilted top is perpendicular to L, it changes the direction of L rather than its magnitude, causing the axis to rotate about the vertical. The precession rate is inversely proportional to the spin rate: as friction slows the top, precession quickens and the top eventually falls. The same mathematics governs gyroscopes used in navigation, the stabilizing spin of rifled projectiles, and Larmor precession of magnetic moments in nuclear magnetic resonance.

Torque-free precession

A rotating body with unequal principal moments of inertia precesses even without external torque, because its angular velocity vector moves around its angular momentum vector. Earth undergoes a small torque-free wobble (the Chandler wobble, with a period of about 433 days) in addition to its forced precession.

Astronomical precession

The most famous example is the precession of the equinoxes: gravitational torques from the Sun and Moon on Earth's equatorial bulge cause Earth's axis to trace a cone over a cycle of about 25,772 years. Hipparchus discovered the effect in the 2nd century BC by comparing star positions across generations. Consequences include the slow shift of the celestial poles (Polaris has not always been, and will not always be, the pole star), the drift of the equinoxes through the zodiac, and — combined with changes in orbital eccentricity and obliquity — Milankovitch cycles that pace the ice ages. Planets' orbits also precess; the anomalous precession of Mercury's perihelion, 43 arcseconds per century beyond Newtonian prediction, was explained by general relativity and became one of its first triumphs.

Other examples

Precession appears in bicycle-wheel gyroscopes, the wobble of a tossed coin (Euler disk), relativistic frame-dragging around rotating masses (the Lense–Thirring effect, measured by Gravity Probe B), and the precession of accretion disks and jets around black holes and young stars.
