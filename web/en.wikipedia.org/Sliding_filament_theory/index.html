Sliding filament theory

The sliding filament theory explains how muscles contract: the thin filaments (actin) of the sarcomere slide past the thick filaments (myosin), shortening the sarcomere without the filaments themselves changing length.

History

The theory was proposed independently in 1954 in two papers in Nature: by Andrew Huxley and Rolf Niedergerke, and by Hugh Huxley and Jean Hanson. Using interference and electron microscopy, they observed that the A band (the length of the myosin filaments) stays constant during contraction while the I band and H zone shorten, implying sliding rather than filament shortening or folding.

The cross-bridge cycle

Contraction is driven by myosin heads that bind actin, pivot in a “power stroke” fuelled by ATP hydrolysis, and detach when fresh ATP binds, cycling as long as calcium ions keep the binding sites exposed. Calcium, released from the sarcoplasmic reticulum after a nerve impulse, binds troponin, shifting tropomyosin off the actin binding sites. When calcium is pumped back, the muscle relaxes. The theory underlies modern understanding of skeletal, cardiac, and (in modified form) smooth muscle.
