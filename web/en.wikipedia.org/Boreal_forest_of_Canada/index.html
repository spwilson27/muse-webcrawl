Boreal forest of Canada

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Canada's boreal forest is a vast region comprising about one third to one half of the country's land area — roughly 3.7 to 5.5 million square kilometres depending on how the zone is defined — and about three quarters of Canada's total forest land. Part of the circumpolar boreal forest (taiga), it stretches from Newfoundland and Labrador in the east to the Yukon and the Mackenzie Delta in the northwest, forming one of the largest intact forest ecosystems on Earth.

Ecology

The region is a mosaic of coniferous forest — black spruce, white spruce, jack pine, balsam fir, and tamarack — interspersed with enormous numbers of lakes, rivers, bogs, fens, and other wetlands. Broadleaf species such as trembling aspen, balsam poplar, and paper birch are common, especially after fire. The boreal zone supports breeding populations of billions of songbirds, vast herds of woodland caribou, moose, wolves, lynx, martens, and grizzly and black bears, and its wetlands are of global significance for waterfowl.

People and economy

Few people live in the boreal region, but it is the homeland of many Indigenous peoples whose territories cover most of it. Forestry, mining, oil and gas extraction (including the Athabasca oil sands of Alberta), and hydroelectric development are major industries. Approximately 90 percent of the forest is publicly owned, and forestry is concentrated in the southern portion nearest markets.

Conservation

Because of its size, intactness, and role in storing carbon — Canada's boreal forest and peatlands hold an estimated 300 billion tonnes or more of carbon — the region is a focus of international conservation campaigns. The Canadian Boreal Forest Agreement (2010) brought logging companies and environmental groups together to plan conservation and sustainable forestry, and Indigenous-led protected areas have expanded across the region.
