Moral panic

A moral panic is the feeling of fear spread among a large number of people that some evil threatens the well-being of society. A definition given by the sociologist Stanley Cohen is "a condition, episode, person or group of persons emerges to become defined as a threat to societal values and interests". The persons who supposedly threaten society are called "folk devils" by Cohen.

Cohen's model

In his 1972 book Folk Devils and Moral Panics, Cohen studied the media reaction to the Mods and Rockers youth subcultures in 1960s Britain. He proposed stages of a moral panic: a threat is identified; it is depicted in simple, recognisable terms by the media; public concern builds; authorities and opinion-makers respond; and the panic recedes or results in social change such as new laws or enforcement.

Characteristics

Sociologists Erich Goode and Nachman Ben-Yehuda identified five criteria of a moral panic: concern, hostility, consensus, disproportionality, and volatility. The media are widely seen as central in amplifying panics through sensational reporting and "deviancy amplification".

Examples

Commonly cited examples include the Satanic ritual abuse panic of the 1980s, panics over comic books in the 1950s, video game violence, the "video nasties" controversy in the United Kingdom, and various drug panics.
