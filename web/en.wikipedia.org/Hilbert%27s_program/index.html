Hilbert's program

Hilbert's program was a proposed solution to the foundational crisis of mathematics, suggested by the German mathematician David Hilbert in the early 1920s. Hilbert proposed that all of mathematics be formalized completely, and that the consistency of each formal system be proved using only secure, finitary methods — methods that deal with concrete, finite objects and are free of suspect infinitary reasoning.

Aims

The program had two main goals: completeness, that every true mathematical statement could be derived within the formal system; and consistency, that the system could never produce a contradiction. Hilbert's optimism was famously expressed in his declaration "Wir müssen wissen. Wir werden wissen." ("We must know. We shall know.")

Gödel's incompleteness theorems

In 1931, Kurt Gödel published his incompleteness theorems, which showed that any consistent formal system strong enough to contain basic arithmetic cannot prove its own consistency, and cannot be complete. These results are generally regarded as demonstrating that Hilbert's program, in its original form, cannot be carried out. Nevertheless, the program profoundly influenced mathematical logic, proof theory, and the development of computer science.
