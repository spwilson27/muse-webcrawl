Competitive exclusion principle

Complete competitors cannot coexist indefinitely

In ecology, the competitive exclusion principle, sometimes called Gause's law, states that two species competing for the same limiting resources cannot coexist at constant population values. When one species has even the slightest advantage over another, the one with the advantage will dominate in the long term; the weaker competitor must then adapt to a different ecological niche, migrate, switch resources, or face extinction.

History

The principle is named after Soviet ecologist Georgy Gause, who proposed it on the basis of laboratory experiments with Paramecium species in the 1930s, published in his 1934 book The Struggle for Existence. Earlier statements of the idea appeared in the work of Joseph Grinnell (1904) and in observations by Charles Darwin on the struggle for existence.

Experimental evidence

Gause cultured Paramecium aurelia and Paramecium caudatum together; although each thrived alone, P. aurelia consistently outcompeted and eliminated P. caudatum when they shared a limited food supply. Later experiments with flour beetles (Tribolium) by Thomas Park confirmed that competitive outcomes depend on environmental conditions such as temperature and humidity. In nature, apparent exceptions are usually resolved by character displacement, resource partitioning, or fluctuating conditions that prevent either species from reaching equilibrium dominance.

Relation to niche theory

The principle underlies modern niche theory: stable coexistence requires that species differ in their use of resources (their realized niches) enough that intraspecific competition exceeds interspecific competition. The "paradox of the plankton" — the coexistence of many phytoplankton species on few resources — is a well-known challenge to the principle, explained in part by non-equilibrium dynamics and spatial heterogeneity.
