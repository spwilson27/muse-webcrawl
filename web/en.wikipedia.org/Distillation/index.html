Distillation

Distillation is the process of separating the components of a liquid mixture by selective boiling and condensation, exploiting differences in the volatility of the components. It may produce a nearly complete separation (pure components) or a partial separation that enriches the mixture in one or more components. Distillation is one of the oldest and most widely used unit operations in chemical engineering.

Principles

When a liquid mixture is heated, the vapour above it is richer in the more volatile component than the liquid. Condensing that vapour yields a liquid enriched in the volatile component — the distillate — while the less volatile residue remains behind. In fractional distillation, repeated vaporisation–condensation cycles inside a fractionating column achieve much sharper separations; mixtures whose components have constant boiling composition are called azeotropes and resist simple distillation.

Applications

Distillation is used to refine crude oil into fuels and petrochemical feedstocks, to produce distilled spirits such as whisky and vodka, to desalinate seawater (especially in multi-stage flash plants), to concentrate essential oils and perfumes, and to purify laboratory chemicals. Variants include vacuum distillation for heat-sensitive materials, steam distillation for plant extracts, and extractive or azeotropic distillation using an added entrainer.

History

Evidence of distillation appears in Mesopotamia around 3000 BC, and Greek alchemists of Alexandria developed the alembic still. Medieval Arab scholars refined the technique and applied it to alcohol and perfumes; by the industrial era, continuous column stills transformed petroleum and chemical manufacture.
