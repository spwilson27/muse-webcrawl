Spanish missions in Mexico

The Spanish missions in Mexico were a series of religious outposts established by Spanish Catholic Franciscans, Jesuits, Dominicans and Augustinians in New Spain to spread the Christian faith among the indigenous peoples and to consolidate Spanish colonial rule. Beginning soon after the conquest of the Aztec Empire in 1521, missions were founded across central Mexico and later throughout the northern frontier regions that are now northern Mexico and the southwestern United States.

The mendicant missions of central Mexico

Twelve Franciscans arrived in 1524, followed by Dominicans (1526) and Augustinians (1533). The friars built hundreds of imposing fortress-like monasteries, many now UNESCO World Heritage sites, and conducted mass conversions, studied indigenous languages, and compiled grammars and histories — most famously Bernardino de Sahagún's Florentine Codex on Nahua culture. Missions concentrated indigenous populations into settlements (congregaciones), where residents were taught European agriculture, crafts and Catholic practice; catastrophic epidemics and harsh labour regimes accompanied the system.

The northern frontier

From the late sixteenth century, missions extended into the north: Jesuits worked in Sinaloa, Sonora and Baja California (where Eusebio Kino founded a chain of missions in the Pimería Alta), and Franciscans founded missions in New Mexico, Texas, Tamaulipas, Coahuila and later Alta California. The five Franciscan missions of the Sierra Gorda of Querétaro, built in the 1750s–60s under Junípero Serra, are celebrated for their richly carved mestizo-baroque facades. After Mexico's independence, the missions were secularised in the 1830s and their lands distributed.

Legacy

The missions profoundly shaped Mexico's religious culture, settlement patterns, architecture and place names. Their history is controversial: celebrated as centres of education and cultural exchange, they are also criticised as instruments of coercion and demographic collapse.
