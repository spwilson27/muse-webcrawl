Tyrannicide

Harmodius and Aristogeiton, celebrated tyrannicides of Athens
ConceptKilling of a tyrant
Classical exemplarsHarmodius and Aristogeiton; Brutus

Tyrannicide is the killing or assassination of a tyrant. The term originates in classical antiquity: in ancient Greece and Rome, tyrannicides were often celebrated as heroes, most famously Harmodius and Aristogeiton, who killed the Athenian tyrant Hipparchus in 514 BC and were honoured with statues in the Agora after the establishment of democracy.

The ethics and legitimacy of tyrannicide have been debated by philosophers and theologians for two millennia. Classical authors such as Cicero praised the assassination of Julius Caesar by Brutus and Cassius; medieval writers including John of Salisbury defended tyrannicide in limited circumstances, while others, and much of later political theory, treated it as murder or a dangerous precedent. The concept overlaps with, but is distinguished from, regicide — the killing of a monarch regardless of character — and from assassination generally.

Notable examples

Historical acts described as tyrannicide include the assassinations of Julius Caesar (44 BC), the Spanish Inquisitor Pedro de Arbués (1485), and Rafael Trujillo (1961), as well as the 1944 plot against Adolf Hitler led by Claus von Stauffenberg. Justifications offered have ranged from natural law and resistance to oppression to constitutional duty.
