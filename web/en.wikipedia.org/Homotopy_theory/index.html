Homotopy theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Homotopy theory is a branch of algebraic topology concerned with the properties of spaces and maps that are preserved under continuous deformation. Two maps are homotopic if one can be continuously deformed into the other; a homotopy equivalence between spaces is a pair of maps, inverse up to homotopy, and spaces related by such an equivalence are considered the same from the homotopy-theoretic point of view. The fundamental objects of study include homotopy groups, which record the equivalence classes of maps from spheres into a space, and cohomology theories, which provide computable invariants.

Basic concepts

The fundamental group π1, introduced by Poincaré, detects loops that cannot be contracted to a point; the higher homotopy groups πn generalise it to maps of n-dimensional spheres. Unlike homology groups, homotopy groups are usually difficult to compute — even the homotopy groups of spheres, πn(Sk), remain only partially known despite a century of work involving spectral sequences, fibrations, and obstruction theory. Tools such as the long exact sequence of a fibration, the Hurewicz theorem (relating the first nontrivial homotopy and homology groups), the Blakers–Massey theorem, and Whitehead's theorem organise the subject's computations.

Modern developments

In the later twentieth century homotopy theory abstracted into the study of model categories, following Daniel Quillen, and of ∞-categories, in which homotopy-coherent structures are built into the foundations. Stable homotopy theory studies spectra — sequences of spaces representing generalised cohomology theories — and has produced deep results connecting topology with number theory through objects such as the image of the J-homomorphism and chromatic homotopy theory's periodic families. Homotopy type theory, developed in the 2000s, reinterprets these ideas in logic and computer science, treating types as spaces and equalities as paths.
