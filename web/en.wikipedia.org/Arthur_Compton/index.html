Arthur Compton

Arthur Holly Compton (September 10, 1892 – March 15, 1962) was an American physicist who won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1927 for his discovery of the Compton effect, which demonstrated the particle nature of electromagnetic radiation. He later directed the Metallurgical Laboratory of the Manhattan Project, which produced the first controlled nuclear chain reaction.

Early life and the Compton effect

Compton was born in Wooster, Ohio, and educated at the College of Wooster and Princeton University, where he earned his PhD in 1916. While at Washington University in St. Louis, he discovered in 1922–1923 that X-rays scattered from electrons in graphite increased in wavelength by an amount depending only on the scattering angle — an effect explicable only if X-rays behaved as particles (photons) transferring energy and momentum to electrons. The result, also demonstrated independently by Peter Debye, provided decisive evidence for Einstein's light-quantum hypothesis. Compton shared the 1927 Nobel Prize with C. T. R. Wilson.

Cosmic rays and the Manhattan Project

In the 1930s Compton organized a worldwide survey of cosmic-ray intensity, establishing that cosmic rays consist mainly of charged particles. During the Second World War he headed the Manhattan Project's Metallurgical Laboratory in Chicago, overseeing Enrico Fermi's construction of Chicago Pile-1, the first nuclear reactor (December 1942), and the plutonium-production reactors at Hanford. After the war he served as chancellor of Washington University (1945–1953).
