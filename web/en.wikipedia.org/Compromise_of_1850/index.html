Compromise of 1850

The Compromise of 1850 was a package of five separate bills passed by the United States Congress in September 1850 that defused a political confrontation between slave and free states over the status of territories acquired in the Mexican–American War. It temporarily avoided threats of Southern secession and postponed the sectional crisis for a decade.

Provisions

  • California was admitted as a free state.
  • The remaining Mexican Cession was organised into the territories of Utah and New Mexico, where the question of slavery would be decided by popular sovereignty.
  • Texas relinquished claims to parts of New Mexico in exchange for federal assumption of its public debt.
  • The slave trade (though not slavery itself) was abolished in the District of Columbia.
  • A much stricter Fugitive Slave Act was enacted, requiring citizens and officials in free states to assist in the return of escaped slaves and denying alleged fugitives a jury trial.

Passage and consequences

The measures were shepherded by Whig Senator Henry Clay and Democrat Stephen A. Douglas, with the support of Senator Daniel Webster; after the death of President Zachary Taylor, President Millard Fillmore signed them. The Fugitive Slave Act proved deeply unpopular in the North, galvanising abolitionist sentiment—it helped inspire Harriet Beecher Stowe's Uncle Tom's Cabin (1852)—while the settlement's collapse came with the Kansas–Nebraska Act of 1854. Historians debate whether the compromise averted civil war in 1850 or merely delayed it.
