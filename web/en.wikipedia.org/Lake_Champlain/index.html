Lake Champlain (French: Lac Champlain) is a large freshwater lake in North America, lying mostly within the borders of the United States (states of Vermont and New York) but also extending north into the Canadian province of Quebec. It is one of the largest lakes in the United States by surface area, and is connected to the St. Lawrence River by the Richelieu River to the north, and at its southern end to the Hudson River via the Champlain Canal.

Geography

The lake is long and narrow, stretching about 125 miles (201 km) from Whitehall, New York, in the south to the Richelieu River in Quebec in the north. The Vermont side is generally low and marshy in the south, while the New York shore is dominated by the Adirondack Mountains. Major islands include Grand Isle, North Hero, and Isle La Motte. The largest city on the lake is Burlington, Vermont.

History

The lake is named for the French explorer Samuel de Champlain, who encountered it in July 1609; a battle with Iroquois near Ticonderoga followed. The valley was a major military corridor in the French and Indian War and the American Revolutionary War; Fort Ticonderoga and Crown Point guarded its southern reaches. In September 1814, during the War of 1812, an American naval squadron under Thomas Macdonough decisively defeated the British at the Battle of Plattsburgh.

For a brief period in 1998, Lake Champlain was legislatively designated one of the Great Lakes, a title that was quickly rescinded after public controversy.

Ecology and folklore

The lake supports fisheries for lake trout, landlocked Atlantic salmon, and bass, and battles invasive species such as zebra mussels and Eurasian watermilfoil. It is also famous for "Champ", a lake monster said to inhabit its waters, in the tradition of the Loch Ness Monster.
