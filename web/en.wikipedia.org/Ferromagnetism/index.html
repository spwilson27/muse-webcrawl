Ferromagnetism

Ferromagnetism
MaterialsIron, nickel, cobalt, gadolinium, many alloys
MechanismExchange interaction aligning magnetic moments
Key conceptsMagnetic domains, hysteresis, Curie temperature

Ferromagnetism is the mechanism by which materials such as iron form permanent magnets or are strongly attracted to magnets. In ferromagnetic materials, the magnetic moments of atoms align parallel to one another within regions called domains, owing to the quantum-mechanical exchange interaction; above the Curie temperature, thermal agitation destroys this order and the material becomes merely paramagnetic.

Explanation

Unpaired electrons in transition metals have spins that, through the exchange interaction, preferentially align in the same direction. In an unmagnetised sample the domains point in random directions and cancel out; an external field rotates and grows favourably oriented domains, producing a net magnetisation that can persist when the field is removed (remanence).

Hysteresis

The relation between applied field and magnetisation traces a hysteresis loop: energy is dissipated as domains rearrange. Materials with wide loops ("hard" magnets) retain strong magnetisation and are used for permanent magnets; "soft" materials with narrow loops are used in transformer cores and electromagnets.

Curie temperature

Each ferromagnet has a characteristic Curie temperature above which ferromagnetism disappears — about 770 °C for iron, 358 °C for nickel, and 1,115 °C for cobalt. The phenomenon was systematised by Pierre Weiss's molecular field theory and explained microscopically by Heisenberg's exchange model.

Applications

Ferromagnetism underlies permanent magnets, electric motors and generators, transformers, magnetic recording media, MRI machines, and compass needles — the lodestone known since antiquity is magnetite, a ferrimagnetic iron oxide.
