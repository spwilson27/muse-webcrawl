Chicago Pile-1 (CP-1) was the world's first artificial nuclear reactor. Built by the Manhattan Project's Metallurgical Laboratory at the University of Chicago, it achieved the first human-made, self-sustaining nuclear chain reaction on 2 December 1942, under physicist Enrico Fermi.

Construction

The reactor was assembled in a squash court beneath the west stands of Stagg Field. It was a "pile" of about 385 tonnes of graphite blocks — the neutron moderator — embedding roughly 45 tonnes of uranium oxide and 5.4 tonnes of uranium metal, supported in a wooden framework. Cadmium control rods could be inserted to absorb neutrons and shut the reaction down; the pile had no radiation shielding or cooling system, as it was intended to run only at very low power.

First criticality

On the morning of 2 December 1942, Fermi's team progressively withdrew the control rods while monitoring neutron counts. That afternoon the reaction became self-sustaining and ran for about 28 minutes at roughly half a watt before Fermi ordered it shut down. Arthur Compton famously reported success to Washington with the coded message about the "Italian navigator" landing in the New World.

Aftermath

CP-1 was dismantled in February 1943 and rebuilt with shielding at Argonne as Chicago Pile-2. The experiment proved that a controlled chain reaction was possible — a foundational step for both nuclear weapons and nuclear power. The site is now a National Historic Landmark, marked by Henry Moore's sculpture Nuclear Energy.
