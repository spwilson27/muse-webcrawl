Intuitionism

Intuitionism is a philosophy of mathematics, most fully developed by the Dutch mathematician L. E. J. Brouwer in the early twentieth century, holding that mathematical objects are mental constructions of the mathematician and that a mathematical statement is meaningful only insofar as it corresponds to a construction that can, in principle, be carried out. On this view, mathematics is not a description of a mind-independent realm of abstract objects, as Platonism claims, but an activity of the mind.

Background

Intuitionism grew out of dissatisfaction with the set-theoretic foundations of nineteenth-century analysis and with paradoxes such as Russell's paradox. Brouwer argued that the paradoxes arose from applying the logic of finite situations, especially the law of excluded middle, to infinite totalities. Intuitionism should be distinguished from, though it influenced, the formalism of David Hilbert and the logicism of Frege and Russell.

Core ideas

The central claims of intuitionism include:

  • Existence means constructibility. To say that a mathematical object exists is to say that a method exists for constructing it. Pure existence proofs that show a contradiction from assuming non-existence, without giving a construction, are rejected.
  • Rejection of the law of excluded middle for infinite domains. For a statement P about infinitely many numbers, one may not assert P or not-P until one of them has been proved by a construction.
  • The continuum is not a completed totality. Real numbers are given by choice sequences that may remain forever unfinished, so the continuum is a medium of becoming rather than a finished set of points.

Intuitionistic logic

Arend Heyting formalized intuitionistic logic in 1930, codifying the logic implicit in intuitionist practice. In this logic, double-negation elimination and proof by contradiction in its classical form are not generally valid. Later, the Brouwer–Heyting–Kolmogorov interpretation explained the intuitionistic connectives in terms of proofs: a proof of A → B is a construction converting any proof of A into a proof of B.

Influence

Although intuitionism never displaced classical mathematics, it profoundly influenced the study of constructive mathematics, type theory, and computer science. The Curry–Howard correspondence connects intuitionistic proofs with computer programs, so intuitionistic ideas are built into proof assistants such as Coq and Agda.
