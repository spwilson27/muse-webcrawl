Flinders Petrie

Sir William Matthew Flinders Petrie (3 June 1853 – 28 July 1942), commonly known as Flinders Petrie, was an English Egyptologist and a pioneer of systematic methodology in archaeology and the preservation of artefacts. He held the first chair of Egyptology in the United Kingdom, at University College London, and excavated many of the most important archaeological sites in Egypt in conjunction with his wife, Hilda Petrie.

Career

Petrie first visited Egypt in 1880 to survey the Great Pyramid of Giza; his meticulous measurements of the pyramid and of Stonehenge established his reputation for exact fieldwork. He went on to excavate at Tanis, Naukratis, Amarna, Abydos, and dozens of other sites, and in the 1890s in Palestine. He developed the technique of seriation — sequence dating of pottery — to order Egyptian predynastic burials chronologically, a foundational contribution to archaeological method.

Legacy

Petrie founded the Egyptian Research Account (later the British School of Archaeology in Egypt) and trained a generation of archaeologists. Much of his collection is now in the Petrie Museum of Egyptian Archaeology at UCL. His views on race and his advocacy of eugenics have drawn criticism from later scholars.
