Behavioural economics

Behavioural economics (spelled behavioral economics in American English) combines elements of economics and psychology to understand how and why people behave the way they do in the real world. It differs from neoclassical economics, which assumes that most people have well-defined preferences and make well-informed, self-interested decisions based on those preferences — the model of homo economicus. Behavioural economics finds that actual human decisions routinely depart from this ideal.

Foundations

The field rests on three broad themes identified by Matthew Rabin: people experience bounded rationality, making systematic errors in judgment; bounded willpower, choosing in the present at the expense of their long-term interests; and bounded self-interest, caring about fairness and others' payoffs. Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky's heuristics-and-biases programme and their prospect theory (1979) provided the psychological foundations, while Richard Thaler demonstrated pervasive anomalies in economic behaviour such as the endowment effect, sunk cost fallacy, and mental accounting.

Development

Early classical economists such as Adam Smith wrote extensively about psychology, but psychological explanation fell out of favour as economics was formalised. It returned through the work of Herbert Simon on bounded rationality, the experimental economics of Vernon Smith, and the psychology of judgment. In the United Kingdom, the Behavioural Insights Team (established 2010, often called the "Nudge Unit") applied behavioural science to public policy, influencing government practice worldwide.

Concepts

Important concepts include loss aversion, status quo bias, default effects, hyperbolic discounting, social norms, reciprocity, framing, and choice overload. Behavioural economics also underpins behavioural finance, which studies cognitive limits and arbitrage constraints as explanations for financial market anomalies.

Criticism and debate

Some economists argue that market forces correct individual irrationality, and that the field's long list of biases lacks parsimony. Others question the replicability of classic experiments. Nonetheless, behavioural findings have been institutionalised in policy units in dozens of countries and recognised by several Nobel Prizes.
