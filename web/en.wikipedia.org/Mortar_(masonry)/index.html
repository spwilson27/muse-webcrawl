Mortar (masonry)

[Image: A bricklayer applying mortar with a trowel]

Mortar is a workable paste used to bind building blocks such as stones, bricks and concrete masonry units, to fill and seal the irregular gaps between them, and sometimes to add decorative colours or patterns to masonry structures. It becomes hard when it sets and solidifies the structure. In its broadest sense, mortar includes pitch, asphalt and soft mud or clay, as used between mud bricks, and is contrasted with concrete, which it resembles but lacks coarse aggregate.

Composition

Modern mortars are typically made from a mixture of sand, a binder such as cement or lime, and water. Early mortars included gypsum (used in ancient Egypt), lime (used throughout the ancient and medieval worlds) and mud. The invention of Portland cement in the nineteenth century produced stronger, faster-setting mortars, but pure cement mortars are too hard for some historic and soft masonry; lime and blended cement-lime mortars remain important, including in repointing work on old buildings. Mortar types are standardized by strength (e.g. ASTM types M, S, N, O and K).

Properties and uses

Mortar should generally be weaker than the units it binds, so that stresses and moisture movement are accommodated and any deterioration occurs in the sacrificial joints rather than in the bricks or stones. Functions beyond adhesion include accommodating slight movements, compensating for irregularities in units, resisting weather penetration, and providing fire resistance. As the mortar sets it undergoes hydration (in cement mortars) or carbonation (in lime mortars).
