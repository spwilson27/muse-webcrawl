Crown of thorns

The crown of thorns was a woven crown of thorns forced onto Jesus's head by Roman soldiers during the Passion, according to the Gospels of Matthew, Mark, and John. Intended to mock Jesus's claim to kingship — together with a reed "sceptre" and a purple robe — it became one of the instruments of the Passion (Arma Christi) and a major subject of Christian devotion and art.

Biblical account

The Gospels recount that during Jesus's trial before Pontius Pilate, soldiers twisted a crown of thorns and placed it on his head, struck him, and saluted him with "Hail, King of the Jews!" The crown appears again in the scene at Golgotha in John's Gospel.

The Paris relic

A relic venerated as the Crown of Thorns was kept in Constantinople and in 1238 was pawned to Venetian bankers by the Latin Emperor Baldwin II; King Louis IX of France redeemed it and brought it to Paris in 1239, building the Sainte-Chapelle to house it. From it, individual thorns were distributed as prized relics across Europe. The crown itself — a circlet of rushes, its thorns long since removed — survived the French Revolution and the 2019 Notre-Dame fire, from which it was rescued, and is kept by the Cathedral of Notre-Dame de Paris.

Symbolism

In Christian theology the crown of thorns signifies Christ's suffering and kingship through humiliation, and echoes the curse of thorns in Genesis. It is among the most frequently depicted objects of the Passion, and "crown of thorns" has passed into common usage as a metaphor for a painful or burdensome honour.
