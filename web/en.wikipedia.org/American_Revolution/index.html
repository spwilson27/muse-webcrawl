American Revolution

The American Revolution (1765–1783) was a political and military struggle in which the Thirteen Colonies of British America rejected British parliamentary rule and founded the United States of America. Rooted in Enlightenment ideas about natural rights and government by consent, the Revolution grew out of disputes over taxation without representation, including the Stamp Act (1765), the Townshend Acts and the Tea Act, which provoked the Boston Tea Party in 1773.

Origins

After the Seven Years' War, Britain sought revenue from its colonies and tightened imperial control. Colonial resistance formed around the slogan "no taxation without representation". The First Continental Congress met in 1774; fighting began at Lexington and Concord in April 1775. On 4 July 1776 the Second Continental Congress adopted the Declaration of Independence, drafted principally by Thomas Jefferson.

War of Independence

The Revolutionary War (1775–1783) saw George Washington command the Continental Army through defeats around New York, winter hardship at Valley Forge, and victories at Saratoga (1777) — which brought France into the war as an ally — and Yorktown (1781), where Cornwallis surrendered. The Treaty of Paris (1783) recognised American independence and extended US territory to the Mississippi River.

Political settlement

The weak Articles of Confederation were replaced by the Constitution of 1787, creating a federal republic with separated powers; the Bill of Rights followed in 1791. The Revolution spread democratic and republican ideals, inspired later movements in France, Haiti and Latin America, and reshaped the Atlantic world, though its promises of liberty coexisted with the continuation of slavery and the dispossession of Native Americans.
