The tanpura (also tambura) is a long-necked plucked string instrument from India that provides a continuous harmonic drone in Indian classical music. It does not play melody or rhythm; instead, its cyclical plucking of four (or more) strings tuned to the tonic and fifth of the raga creates the tonal foundation against which the soloist performs.

Construction

The tanpura has a large resonator traditionally carved from a single piece of wood or made from a hollowed gourd, joined to a long, hollow neck without frets. Its strings pass over a wide, slightly curved bridge; fine threads (jivari) inserted under the strings at the bridge produce the instrument's characteristic rich, buzzing overtone spectrum. Male and female tanpuras differ in size and pitch range; the smaller instrumental tanpuri accompanies instrumentalists.

Playing and tuning

The player plucks the strings in a steady, unbroken cycle with the middle and index fingers, resting the instrument on the floor or lap. A typical four-string tuning is Pa–Sa–Sa–Sa (fifth, tonic, tonic, lower tonic), adjusted to the raga's requirements. In modern concerts the acoustic tanpura is often replaced or supplemented by an electronic tanpura or a shruti box, and by smartphone apps.

Significance

More than an accompaniment, the tanpura defines the pitch universe of the performance: singers and instrumentalists tune to it, and its shimmering drone is considered essential to the atmosphere (rasa) of the raga.
