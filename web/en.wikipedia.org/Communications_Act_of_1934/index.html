Communications Act of 1934

Communications Act of 1934
Long title: An Act to provide for the regulation of interstate and foreign communication by wire or radio, and for other purposes.
Enacted: June 19, 1934
Public Law 73-416
Repealed in part by the Telecommunications Act of 1996

The Communications Act of 1934 is a United States federal law signed by President Franklin D. Roosevelt on June 19, 1934. The Act created the Federal Communications Commission (FCC), replacing the Federal Radio Commission, and gave the new agency authority over both interstate wireline telephone and radio broadcasting.

Background

The Act consolidated federal oversight of electronic communications. Radio had previously been governed by the Radio Act of 1927, while interstate telephone service was overseen by the Interstate Commerce Commission under the Mann-Elkins Act of 1910. The Communications Act brought both under a single independent commission.

Structure

The Act is organized into seven titles. Title I contains general provisions and defines the FCC's jurisdiction. Title II governs common carriers, including telephone companies. Title III covers radio broadcasting licensing, requiring broadcasters to operate in the "public interest, convenience, and necessity." Title IV addresses procedural and judicial review matters, Title V covers penal provisions and forfeitures, Title VI covers miscellaneous provisions including cable communications (added in 1984), and Title VII covers miscellaneous subjects such as emergency communications.

Amendments and legacy

The Act has been amended many times, most significantly by the Cable Communications Policy Act of 1984 and the Telecommunications Act of 1996, which deregulated substantial parts of the communications industry while leaving the 1934 framework largely intact. The Act's Section 230, added in 1996, provides liability protections for online platforms hosting third-party content.
