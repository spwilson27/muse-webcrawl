Charles Robert Darwin (12 February 1809 – 19 April 1882) was an English naturalist, geologist, and biologist, widely known for his contributions to evolutionary biology. His proposition that all species of life have descended from a common ancestor is now generally accepted and considered a fundamental scientific concept. In a joint presentation with Alfred Russel Wallace, he proposed that this branching pattern of evolution resulted from a process he called natural selection, in which the struggle for existence has a similar effect to the artificial selection involved in selective breeding.

Early life and the Beagle voyage

Darwin was born in Shrewsbury, the fifth child of Robert Darwin, a wealthy doctor, and Susannah Wedgwood. He studied medicine at Edinburgh and then theology at Cambridge, where his passion for natural history flourished under mentors such as John Stevens Henslow. From 1831 to 1836 he served as ship's naturalist aboard HMS Beagle on its survey voyage around the world. His observations — of South American fossils, of the geology of South America, and especially of the mockingbirds and finches of the Galápagos Islands differing from island to island — laid the foundations of his theory.

Development of the theory

Back in London, Darwin worked up his findings, publishing his journal of the voyage (1839) and works on coral reefs, volcanic islands, and geology. Reading Malthus in 1838 crystallised his idea of natural selection: organisms better adapted to their environment tend to survive and produce more offspring. He drafted a sketch of the theory in 1842 and a longer essay in 1844, but delayed publication for nearly two decades while he gathered evidence, including eight years of work on barnacles. In 1858, Alfred Russel Wallace sent Darwin an essay independently describing the same mechanism; their papers were presented jointly to the Linnean Society, and Darwin rushed his "abstract" into print as On the Origin of Species in November 1859.

Later work

Darwin thereafter applied the theory to humans in The Descent of Man, and Selection in Relation to Sex (1871), and wrote books on the fertilisation of orchids, climbing plants, insectivorous plants, the expression of the emotions, and the formation of vegetable mould by earthworms (1881), his final book. From 1842 he lived at Down House in Kent with his wife Emma (née Wedgwood), his cousin; they had ten children, three of whom died young. His chronic ill health — long debated, with hypotheses including Chagas disease and Crohn's disease — limited his public life.

Reception and legacy

By the 1870s the scientific community largely accepted evolution as fact, though natural selection's primacy was debated until the modern synthesis of the 1930s–40s united it with Mendelian genetics. Darwin was honoured with burial in Westminster Abbey, near Newton. His ideas transformed biology and left a lasting mark on philosophy, anthropology, and public culture.
