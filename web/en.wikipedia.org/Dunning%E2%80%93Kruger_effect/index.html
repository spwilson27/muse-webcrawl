Dunning–Kruger effect

FieldPsychology
Named afterDavid Dunning and Justin Kruger
Original study1999, Cornell University

The Dunning–Kruger effect is a cognitive bias in which people with limited competence in a particular domain overestimate their abilities. It was first described by social psychologists David Dunning and Justin Kruger in a 1999 paper, "Unskilled and Unaware of It", based on studies of undergraduate students' performance in humour, grammar and logic tests.

Findings

In the original studies, participants in the lowest quartile of performance estimated their scores far above their actual results, while top performers slightly underestimated their relative standing. The proposed explanation is metacognitive: the skills needed to perform well in a domain are the same skills needed to recognise poor performance, so the least skilled lack the ability to detect their own errors.

Criticism and debate

Later research has debated how much of the pattern reflects a genuine psychological bias versus statistical artefacts such as regression to the mean combined with a general "better-than-average" effect. The effect has nonetheless become a widely cited concept in popular discussions of expertise and overconfidence.

Cultural impact

The effect is often invoked in commentary about politics, management and online discourse, and inspired an Ig Nobel Prize awarded to Dunning and Kruger in 2000.
