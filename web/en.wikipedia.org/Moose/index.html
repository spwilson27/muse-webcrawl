The moose (Alces alces), known as the elk in Europe, is the largest and heaviest extant species in the deer family (Cervidae). It is distinguished by the broad, palmate antlers of the males, its long legs, a pendulous muzzle, and a flap of skin called a bell hanging beneath the throat. Moose inhabit the boreal forests and temperate broadleaf woodlands of the Northern Hemisphere, in North America, Europe and Asia.

Description

Adult moose stand 1.4 to 2.1 metres at the shoulder and can weigh over 700 kg in the largest Alaskan subspecies. Their long legs allow them to wade through deep snow and shallow water. The muzzle is large and flexible, adapted for stripping leaves and for feeding on submerged aquatic plants. Only males grow antlers, which are shed each winter.

Habitat and diet

Moose favour habitats with shrubs and young trees for browsing, along with lakes and wetlands whose aquatic vegetation provides both food and refuge from predators and biting insects. They are herbivores, consuming large quantities of leaves, twigs, bark and aquatic plants; an adult may eat 30 kg of vegetation a day.

Behaviour

Moose are generally solitary, the strongest social bond being between a cow and her calves. The mating season, or rut, occurs in autumn, when bulls compete with sparring matches. Despite their size, moose are strong swimmers and can run at speeds up to 56 km/h.

Predators and conservation

Wolves, bears and orcas are natural predators; calves are especially vulnerable. Moose populations are large across much of their range, though they face pressure from habitat change, parasites such as winter ticks and brainworm, and collisions with vehicles.
