Miracle on the Han River

The Miracle on the Han River (Hanguk-ui ganguk, "Han River miracle") refers to the period of rapid economic growth in South Korea following the Korean War, during which the country transformed from one of the poorest in the world into a major industrial and technological power within a few decades. The phrase, modelled on the German "Miracle on the Rhine" (Wirtschaftswunder), evokes the Han River, which flows through Seoul.

In the early 1960s South Korea's per-capita income was comparable to that of poor countries in Africa and Asia. Under a series of five-year economic plans beginning in 1962, the government pursued export-led industrialisation: first light manufactures such as textiles and footwear, then heavy industry — steel, shipbuilding, automobiles, petrochemicals — and later electronics and semiconductors. Family conglomerates known as chaebol, including Samsung, Hyundai and LG, grew into global firms with state support.

Consequences

Growth averaged around 8–9 percent a year for three decades, accompanied by dramatic rises in life expectancy, literacy and urbanisation; Seoul hosted the 1988 Summer Olympics as a symbol of arrival. The transformation also carried costs: authoritarian labour repression, environmental damage, financial fragility exposed in the 1997 Asian financial crisis, and wide conglomerate influence. The term remains shorthand for South Korea's development model and is studied worldwide as a case of state-guided catch-up growth.

Not to be confused with the Miracle on Ice.
