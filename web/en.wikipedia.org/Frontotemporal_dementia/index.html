Frontotemporal dementia (FTD), also called frontotemporal lobar degeneration, is a group of brain disorders caused by progressive nerve cell loss in the brain's frontal and temporal lobes. Unlike Alzheimer's disease, FTD typically begins earlier — most often between ages 45 and 65 — and presents first with changes in personality, behaviour, or language rather than memory.

Subtypes and symptoms

The behavioural variant (bvFTD) causes disinhibition, apathy, loss of empathy, compulsive behaviours, and changes in eating habits. The language variants, grouped as primary progressive aphasia, include the semantic variant, with loss of word meaning, and the nonfluent/agrammatic variant, with effortful, grammatically broken speech. FTD overlaps clinically and genetically with motor neuron disease (ALS).

Causes and pathology

Underlying pathologies most commonly involve abnormal accumulations of TDP-43 or tau protein. A substantial minority of cases are inherited, most often through mutations in the C9orf72, MAPT, or GRN genes. Brain imaging shows atrophy of the frontal and/or temporal lobes.

Treatment

There is no cure and no approved disease-modifying treatment; care focuses on managing symptoms, safety, and supporting carers. Life expectancy after symptom onset is typically around 7 to 13 years depending on subtype.
