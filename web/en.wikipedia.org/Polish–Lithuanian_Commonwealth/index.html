Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth

The Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth, formally the Kingdom of Poland and the Grand Duchy of Lithuania, was a bi-confederal state established by the Union of Lublin in 1569 and lasting until the Partitions of Poland in 1795. One of the largest and most populous states in 16th–17th century Europe, it operated an elective monarchy and a distinctive noble democracy.

Origins

The union developed from the Union of Krewo (1385) through personal union under the Jagiellonians to the real union of 1569 under Sigismund II Augustus, last Jagiellonian king.

Institutions

From 1573 kings were freely elected by the szlachta and bound by the Henrician Articles and pacta conventa. The Warsaw Confederation (1573) guaranteed religious tolerance unusual in its age. The liberum veto later paralysed the Sejm.

Wars, Deluge and decline

The Commonwealth fought Sweden, Muscovy/Russia, the Ottomans and Cossacks. The Khmelnytsky Uprising (1648) and the Swedish Deluge (1655–1660) devastated it. John III Sobieski's victory at the Battle of Vienna (1683) was a late triumph. Reform came with the Constitution of 3 May 1791, but Russia, Prussia and Austria partitioned the state out of existence by 1795.
