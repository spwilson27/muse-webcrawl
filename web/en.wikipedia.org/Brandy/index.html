Brandy

Brandy is a spirit produced by distilling wine or fermented fruit mash. The name comes from the Dutch brandewijn, "burnt wine," referring to the application of heat in distillation. Most brandies are made from grapes and are aged in wooden casks, typically oak, which imparts colour and flavour; the term is also used for spirits distilled from other fruits, such as applejack, kirsch (cherry) and slivovitz (plum).

Famous types

The best-known grape brandies come from France: Cognac, from the delimited region around the town of Cognac, and Armagnac in Gascony, both protected appellations with strict production rules and age classifications (V.S., V.S.O.P., X.O. and beyond). Other notable styles include Spanish Brandy de Jerez, aged in sherry casks by the solera system; Italian brandy and grappa (a pomace brandy); pisco from Peru and Chile; and metaxa from Greece.

Production

Wine — usually made from white grape varieties such as Ugni blanc — is distilled, traditionally twice in copper pot stills for Cognac or once in a column still for Armagnac, and the spirit is matured in oak before blending and bottling at around 40% alcohol by volume. Fruit brandies (eaux-de-vie) are often unaged and bottled clear.

Serving and uses

Brandy is traditionally served neat in a snifter as an after-dinner digestif, and is an ingredient in classic cocktails such as the Sidecar, Brandy Alexander and Vieux Carré. It is also used in cooking — flambéing, sauces and desserts — and historically was prized for medicinal purposes and as a safe ration at sea.
