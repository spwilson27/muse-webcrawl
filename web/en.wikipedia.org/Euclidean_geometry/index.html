Euclidean geometry is a mathematical system attributed to the Alexandrian Greek mathematician Euclid, which he described in his textbook on geometry, the Elements. Euclid's method consists in assuming a small set of intuitively appealing axioms (postulates) and deducing many other propositions (theorems) from them. It is the geometry of flat space: in it, the angles of a triangle sum to 180 degrees, and through a point not on a given line exactly one line can be drawn parallel to the given line.

The postulates

Euclid's five postulates state that: a straight line can be drawn between any two points; a finite line can be extended indefinitely; a circle can be drawn with any centre and radius; all right angles are equal; and — the famous fifth or parallel postulate — if a line crossing two straight lines makes the interior angles on one side sum to less than two right angles, the two lines, extended far enough, meet on that side. Generations of mathematicians tried to derive the fifth postulate from the other four. In the nineteenth century, Bolyai, Lobachevsky, and Gauss showed that denying it produces the consistent hyperbolic geometry, and Riemann's elliptic geometry showed another alternative; these non-Euclidean geometries later became essential to Einstein's general theory of relativity.

Modern treatment

Modern presentations recast Euclidean geometry as the study of Euclidean spaces: flat affine spaces equipped with an inner product, in which distance is given by the Pythagorean formula. In the twentieth century, David Hilbert's Foundations of Geometry (1899) supplied a complete, rigorous axiom system repairing gaps in Euclid's reasoning. Euclidean geometry remains the first deductive system most students meet, and it describes physical space accurately at everyday scales.
