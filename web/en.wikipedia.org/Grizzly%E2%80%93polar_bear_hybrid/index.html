A grizzly–polar bear hybrid is the offspring of a grizzly bear and a polar bear. Portmanteau names combine the parents in either order: pizzly (polar bear father) and grolar bear (grizzly father). Although the two species are close relatives that split several hundred thousand years ago and hybridisation had been observed in zoos, wild hybrids were only confirmed in the 21st century.

Wild occurrences

In 2006 a hunter on Banks Island in the Canadian Arctic shot a bear with creamy-white fur patched with brown, later confirmed by DNA analysis to be a first-generation hybrid. Subsequent specimens around the western Hudson Bay and Victoria Island regions have included second-generation hybrids, showing the offspring are fertile. Researchers link increasing overlap of the species' ranges — grizzlies moving north as the Arctic warms and polar bears spending more time ashore — to the encounters, as climate change shrinks sea-ice habitat.

Characteristics

Hybrids show intermediate features: off-white fur with brown patches, a slightly humped back, longer claws than a polar bear but a narrower skull profile than a grizzly. Their behaviour and diet also appear intermediate, which some biologists suggest may disadvantage them in both parents' niches.

Conservation debate

Hybrids raise policy questions because legal protections (for example under endangered-species law) generally do not cover hybrid animals, and gene flow from grizzlies could affect the genetic integrity of threatened polar bear populations.
