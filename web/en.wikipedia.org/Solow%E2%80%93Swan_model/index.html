Solow–Swan model

The Solow–Swan model, or neoclassical growth model, is an economic model of long-run economic growth developed independently by Robert Solow and Trevor Swan in 1956. It explains growth through capital accumulation, labour force growth, and (exogenous) technological progress.

The model

Output is produced by a neoclassical production function (often Cobb–Douglas) with diminishing returns to capital. Saving a fixed fraction of output adds to the capital stock; depreciation and population growth dilute it. The economy converges to a steady state where capital per effective worker is constant, and living standards grow only at the rate of technological progress.

Implications and influence

The model predicts conditional convergence: poorer economies should grow faster than richer ones, other things equal. Solow's 1957 growth-accounting work attributed most US growth to the "Solow residual" of technology. The model remains the starting point of growth economics and earned Solow the 1987 Nobel Memorial Prize; endogenous growth theory later sought to explain technology within the model.
