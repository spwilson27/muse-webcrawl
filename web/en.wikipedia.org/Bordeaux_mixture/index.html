Bordeaux mixture

Bordeaux mixture (French: bouillie bordelaise) is a fungicide and bactericide made by mixing copper sulfate (CuSO4) and slaked lime (calcium hydroxide, Ca(OH)2) in water. Invented in the Bordeaux region of France in the 1880s, it was the first truly effective fungicide and is still used worldwide, including in organic agriculture, to control fungal diseases of vines, fruit trees and potatoes.

Invention

Downy mildew (Plasmopara viticola), accidentally imported from America, was devastating French vineyards in the late 1870s. The botany professor Pierre-Marie-Alexis Millardet of the University of Bordeaux noticed that vines along roadsides, which had been daubed with a poisonous-looking paste of copper sulfate and lime to discourage grape thieves, had kept their leaves. After trials in 1883–1885, he published the recipe in 1885; the mixture saved the French wine industry and was rapidly adopted against potato blight and other diseases.

Action and preparation

A typical recipe uses copper sulfate and lime in proportions such as 1:1:100 with water. The lime neutralises the acidic copper sulfate, so the spray does not burn foliage, and produces a gelatinous precipitate that sticks to plant surfaces. Copper ions released slowly by weathering denature fungal enzymes and prevent spores from germinating; the mixture is protective rather than curative, so it must be applied before infection, and reapplied after rain.

Uses and concerns

Bordeaux mixture controls downy mildews, potato and tomato blight, apple scab, peach leaf curl and many bacterial diseases. However, copper accumulates in soils, where high levels can harm earthworms and soil microbes, so the European Union limits copper applications in agriculture (currently to an average of 4 kg of copper per hectare per year). Overuse can also cause russeting of fruit and damage to foliage. Despite these concerns, more than 130 years after its invention, it remains a mainstay of vineyard and orchard protection.
