In Buddhism, a bodhisattva (Sanskrit: बोधिसत्त्व, romanized: bodhisattva; Pali: bodhisatta) is a person who is on the path towards Buddhahood. In Mahayana Buddhism, a bodhisattva is any person who has made a vow, known as a bodhisattva vow, to attain full Buddhahood and to liberate all sentient beings from samsara, the cycle of birth, death, and rebirth. The bodhisattva ideal is central to Mahayana thought, in which compassion (karuna) is cultivated alongside wisdom (prajna), and the attainment of enlightenment is postponed or redirected so that all beings may be freed.

Etymology

The Sanskrit term bodhisattva is composed of bodhi ("awakening," "enlightenment") and sattva ("being," "essence," or "one whose aim is"). Hence it can be translated as "enlightenment-being" or "one whose essence is enlightenment." In early Buddhist texts, the term referred principally to the Buddha in his previous lives, as recounted in the Jataka tales, during which he practiced the perfections (paramitas) over countless lifetimes.

In early Buddhism and Theravada

In the Pali Canon, bodhisatta is used almost exclusively for the historical Buddha before his awakening, and for the future Buddha Maitreya. The path of the bodhisattva was described in later Theravada literature, such as Buddhaghosa's Visuddhimagga, as an exceedingly rare and long path requiring many eons of practice. Most Theravada practitioners historically aimed at arahantship, though the bodhisattva path remained a respected option, and modern Theravada teachers such as Walpola Rahula have noted its continuing relevance.

In Mahayana Buddhism

The Mahayana tradition greatly expanded the concept. Mahayana sutras, including the Lotus Sutra and the Prajnaparamita literature, present the bodhisattva path as superior to and ultimately inclusive of the paths of the sravaka (disciple) and pratyekabuddha. Central practices include the six perfections (paramitas): generosity (dana), morality (sila), patience (ksanti), diligence (virya), meditation (dhyana), and wisdom (prajna). The career of the bodhisattva is mapped over ten stages (bhumis), beginning with the generation of bodhicitta, the mind of awakening.

Celestial bodhisattvas

Highly advanced bodhisattvas came to be venerated as powerful celestial beings residing in Buddha-fields. Among the most widely worshipped are Avalokiteshvara (the bodhisattva of compassion, known in China as Guanyin and in Tibet as Chenrezig), Manjushri (wisdom), Samantabhadra (practice and vows), Ksitigarbha (vow to save beings in the hell realms), and Maitreya, the future Buddha currently residing in Tusita heaven. In East Asia, devotion to such figures became a defining feature of popular Buddhism.

Bodhisattva vows

The bodhisattva vows formalize the commitment to the path. A common formulation, the four great vows, reads: "Sentient beings are numberless; I vow to liberate them. Delusions are inexhaustible; I vow to end them. Dharma gates are boundless; I vow to enter them. The Buddha way is unsurpassable; I vow to attain it." In Tibetan Buddhism, the vows are conferred in ceremonies based on the works of Shantideva and Asanga, whose treatises remain the principal manuals of bodhisattva training.
