Mercury Theatre

The Mercury Theatre was an independent repertory theatre company founded in New York City in 1937 by Orson Welles and John Houseman. It became famous for its innovative stage productions — notably a modern-dress Julius Caesar — and for its radio series The Mercury Theatre on the Air, whose 1938 adaptation of The War of the Worlds caused a celebrated public sensation.

Stage productions

The Mercury grew out of Welles and Houseman's work with the Federal Theatre Project, including the 1936 "Voodoo" Macbeth in Harlem and the suppressed pro-union musical The Cradle Will Rock (1937), which the company famously performed without scenery after being locked out of its theatre. Its first independent production, Caesar (1937), staged Shakespeare's play in modern dress as an anti-fascist allegory to acclaim. Other productions included The Shoemaker's Holiday, Heartbreak House and Native Son (1941).

Radio

In 1938 CBS gave the company a weekly hour, first as First Person Singular and then as The Mercury Theatre on the Air. Its Halloween broadcast of H. G. Wells's The War of the Worlds on 30 October 1938, presented as fake news bulletins, convinced some listeners that a Martian invasion was under way and made Welles nationally famous. Renamed the Campbell Playhouse under sponsorship, the series ran until 1940.

Film and legacy

The Mercury's success brought Welles a contract with RKO Pictures, and many company actors followed him to Hollywood to appear in Citizen Kane (1941) and The Magnificent Ambersons (1942). The company effectively dissolved as Welles's film career took over, but it remains a landmark in American theatre and broadcasting history.
