Iñupiat

The Iñupiat (singular Iñupiaq, dual Iñupiak) are an Indigenous people of Alaska, native to the Arctic and subarctic coasts from Norton Sound and the Seward Peninsula north to the Canadian border. They speak Iñupiaq, an Inuit language of the Eskimo–Aleut family, and are culturally and linguistically related to the Inuit of Canada and Greenland. Their name means "the real people" (iñu, person; -piaq, real or genuine).

Life and culture

Traditionally the Iñupiat lived in semi-permanent coastal and riverine communities, hunting bowhead whales, seals, walrus and beluga at sea, and caribou, fish and waterfowl inland. The spring bowhead hunt, conducted from skin boats (umiat) by crews led by an umialik (boat owner-captain), remains the centrepiece of community life in whaling villages such as Utqiaġvik (Barrow), culminating in the Nalukataq whaling festival. Sod houses, dog teams, and elaborate sharing networks structured survival in the Arctic environment; regional groups included the Bering Strait, Kotzebue Sound, North Slope and inland Nunamiut peoples.

Modern era

Contact with Yankee whalers, traders, missionaries and schools from the nineteenth century brought trade goods, disease epidemics and pressure on language and subsistence. The Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act of 1971 conveyed land and capital to regional corporations such as the Arctic Slope Regional Corporation and NANA Regional Corporation; Iñupiat communities are also organised in the North Slope Borough and Northwest Arctic Borough. Language revitalisation, subsistence rights and the impacts of Arctic climate change are major present-day concerns.
