Atman (Hinduism)

Ātman (Sanskrit: आत्मन्) is a Sanskrit word meaning the true or eternal Self or the self-existent essence of each individual, which persists across multiple bodies and lifetimes. In Hinduism, ātman is a central concept of the Upanishads and of schools of Hindu philosophy.

Etymology and origins

The earliest use of the word is found in the Rigveda, where it can mean breath or vital essence. In the Upanishads, ātman becomes the inner self, distinct from the body, senses, and mind, and the object of self-realization.

Relation to Brahman

The great sayings (mahāvākya) of the Upanishads, such as "tat tvam asi" ("thou art that") and "aham brahmāsmi" ("I am Brahman"), express the relation between ātman and Brahman, the ultimate reality. In Advaita Vedanta, ātman and Brahman are identical; realization of this identity is liberation (moksha). Dualist schools such as Dvaita hold that the individual self remains eternally distinct from God.

Rebirth and liberation

Ātman transmigrates through rebirth (samsara) according to karma until it attains liberation. Hindu traditions prescribe paths of knowledge, devotion, and action toward this realization. Buddhism, by contrast, is characterized by the doctrine of anattā (no-self), denying a permanent ātman.
