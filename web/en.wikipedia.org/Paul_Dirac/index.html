Paul Adrien Maurice Dirac (8 August 1902 – 20 October 1984) was an English theoretical physicist who is regarded as one of the founders of both quantum mechanics and quantum electrodynamics. His relentless demand for mathematical beauty and logical consistency produced some of the deepest results in modern physics — above all the Dirac equation of 1928, which unified quantum mechanics with special relativity and unexpectedly predicted the existence of antimatter. Famously taciturn, he was known among colleagues for answering questions with utter economy; his textbook The Principles of Quantum Mechanics (1930) remains a classic.

The Dirac equation and antimatter

Seeking a wave equation for the electron consistent with both quantum theory and relativity, Dirac in 1928 derived a first-order equation built from four-component spinors and matrices:

(iħγμ∂μ − mc)ψ = 0

The equation naturally explained electron spin and the fine structure of hydrogen, but it also admitted negative-energy solutions. Interpreting these, Dirac predicted in 1931 a positively charged counterpart of the electron — the positron — discovered in cosmic rays by Carl Anderson the following year. For this work he shared the 1933 Nobel Prize in Physics with Erwin Schrödinger, becoming at 31 one of the youngest laureates in the subject.

Other contributions

Dirac's fingerprints are everywhere in quantum theory: the transformation theory of 1926, Fermi–Dirac statistics, the delta function, bra–ket (Dirac) notation, the relativistic quantum theory of radiation (1927), magnetic monopoles (1931), and foundational work on quantum field theory and constrained dynamics. He held the Lucasian chair at Cambridge — Newton's chair — for 37 years, then moved to Florida State University in his retirement. He is buried in Westminster Abbey near Newton, and the Dirac Medal and the Paul Dirac Medal of the Institute of Physics commemorate him.
