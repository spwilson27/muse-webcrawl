Commerce raiding

Commerce raiding is a form of naval warfare that targets an enemy's merchant shipping rather than its battle fleet. Known in French as the guerre de course, the strategy aims to strangle an opponent's trade, raise its insurance costs, and force it to divert warships to convoy escort. Raiders may be regular warships, converted merchantmen, submarines, or — historically — privateers sailing under letters of marque.

History

Privateering flourished in the age of sail; France's Jeune École theorists of the 1880s revived the guerre de course as a strategy for a weaker naval power against Britain. During the American Civil War, Confederate raiders such as CSS Alabama sank or captured dozens of Union merchant ships. In both World Wars, Germany employed surface raiders and, most destructively, U-boats against Allied commerce, while Allied navies answered with convoys.

Law

The Declaration of Paris of 1856 abolished privateering among its signatories. Cruiser rules required warships to stop and search merchant vessels and provide for the safety of crews before sinking them — rules eroded by unrestricted submarine warfare in the 20th century.

Effectiveness

Commerce raiding rarely wins wars alone, but it can impose severe economic strain: the U-boat campaigns twice brought Britain close to crisis before convoy, air cover, and codebreaking restored the balance.
