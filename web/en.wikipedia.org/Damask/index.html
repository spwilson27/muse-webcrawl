Damask

Damask is a reversible patterned fabric of silk, wool, linen, cotton, or synthetic fibres, formed by weaving. Damasks are woven with one warp yarn and one weft yarn, usually with the pattern in warp-faced satin weave and the ground in weft-faced or sateen weave. The pattern appears as a contrast between light and shadow rather than colour, and reverses on the back of the cloth.

History

The name comes from the city of Damascus in Syria, a major trading post on the Silk Road famed in the Middle Ages for such fabrics. Damask weaving was practised in China as early as the Tang dynasty and spread through the Islamic world to Europe. By the early modern period, linen damask for table linen was a speciality of Flanders and later of Ireland, while silk damasks were produced in Italy and France.

Uses

Damask is traditionally used for tablecloths and napkins, upholstery, curtains and formal clothing. Modern damasks are woven on jacquard looms, which allow the intricate floral and geometric patterns characteristic of the fabric.
