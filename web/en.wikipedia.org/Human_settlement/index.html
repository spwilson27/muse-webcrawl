Human settlement

Summary

Types: Hamlet, village, town, city, metropolis

Patterns: Nucleated, dispersed, linear

Studied in: Settlement geography, urban planning

A human settlement is a place where people live and carry out their daily activities. Settlements range in size from a single dwelling or small hamlet to a vast metropolitan area, and they may be permanent or temporary. In geography, settlements are studied in terms of their site, situation, form, function, and hierarchy.

History

Permanent settlements emerged with the Neolithic Revolution around 10,000 BCE, when agriculture allowed communities to remain in one place. Early farming villages such as Jericho and Çatalhöyük grew near reliable water and fertile land. Over time, some settlements developed into towns and cities as trade, craft specialisation, and administration expanded, notably in Mesopotamia, the Indus Valley, Egypt, and China.

Site and situation

The site of a settlement is the physical character of the land it occupies, including water supply, relief, soil, and building materials. Its situation is its position relative to other places — trade routes, ports, river crossings, and defensive positions have all shaped where settlements thrive.

Patterns and hierarchy

Settlements are often described as nucleated (clustered around a central point), dispersed (scattered dwellings, common in farming regions), or linear (strung along a road, river, or valley). Central place theory, developed by Walter Christaller, describes how settlements form a hierarchy in which larger centres provide more specialised goods and services to surrounding smaller ones. In many countries today, most people live in urban settlements, following centuries of urbanisation accelerated by industrialisation.
