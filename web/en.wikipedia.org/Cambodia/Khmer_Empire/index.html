The Khmer Empire (Khmer: អាណាចក្រខ្មែរ), or the Angkorian Empire, was a Hindu-Buddhist empire in Southeast Asia, centred in what is now Cambodia. The empire, which grew out of the former polities of Funan and Chenla, at times ruled over and/or vassalised parts of present-day Laos, Thailand, Vietnam, Myanmar and Malaysia. Its greatest legacy is Angkor, the site of its capital, whose monumental temples — including Angkor Wat and the Bayon — are a source of fierce national pride.

History

The empire is conventionally dated from 802 CE, when Jayavarman II declared himself universal monarch (chakravartin) on Phnom Kulen. Successors consolidated the state at Angkor, where Yasovarman I founded the capital of Yasodharapura around 900. Under Suryavarman II (r. 1113–c. 1150), who built Angkor Wat, the empire reached toward its greatest extent. After a sack by the Chams in 1177, Jayavarman VII (r. 1181–c. 1218) expelled the invaders, reunified the empire, embraced Mahayana Buddhism and undertook a vast building programme: Angkor Thom, the Bayon, Ta Prohm, Preah Khan, hospitals and rest houses across the realm.

From the 13th century the empire weakened under pressure from the rising Thai kingdoms, religious transformation toward Theravada Buddhism, and ecological stresses on Angkor's hydraulic network. In 1431 Ayutthayan forces sacked Angkor; the court relocated south, ending the Angkorian era.

Society and economy

Khmer society was organised around the god-king (devaraja) cult and a temple-based bureaucracy that recorded donations and labour on stone inscriptions in Sanskrit and Old Khmer. Wet-rice agriculture, supported by great reservoirs such as the West Baray, sustained an urban population possibly near one million. The empire traded with China — whose envoy Zhou Daguan left a famous account of Angkor in 1296–97 — and its art, architecture and statecraft influenced the whole region.
