Angkor (Khmer: អង្គរ, "capital city") is a region of Cambodia that served as the capital of the Khmer Empire from the 9th to the 15th century CE. Its vast complex of stone temples, reservoirs (barays) and canals — spread over more than 400 square kilometres near the modern city of Siem Reap — constitutes one of the most important archaeological sites in Southeast Asia. At its height Angkor was among the largest pre-industrial cities in the world, with a low-density urban sprawl possibly covering 1,000 square kilometres and supporting up to a million inhabitants.

History

Successive capitals were built at Angkor by kings from Yasovarman I (who founded Yasodharapura around 900) onward. Its supreme monuments include Angkor Wat, built by Suryavarman II in the early 12th century as a Hindu temple-mountain dedicated to Vishnu — the largest religious monument in the world — and the walled city of Angkor Thom, founded by Jayavarman VII in the late 12th century, whose Bayon temple bears giant carved faces. An immense hydraulic system of reservoirs and canals underpinned rice agriculture and the city's growth.

Decline and rediscovery

Angkor's decline in the 14th–15th centuries involved Ayutthayan invasions, religious change from Hinduism and Mahayana Buddhism to Theravada Buddhism, ecological strain and possible climate-driven failures of the water system; the court moved south toward Phnom Penh. Angkor Wat itself was never abandoned, remaining a Buddhist shrine. The site was brought to Western attention by the French naturalist Henri Mouhot, whose travel accounts were published in the 1860s. Angkor was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1992 and is Cambodia's premier tourist destination; the apsara and Angkor Wat appear on the national flag.
