The Khmer Rouge (French for "Red Khmer") was the popular name of the Communist Party of Kampuchea and its followers, who ruled Cambodia as Democratic Kampuchea from 1975 to 1979 under the leadership of Pol Pot. Their radical programme of agrarian socialism caused the deaths of an estimated 1.5 to 2 million people — roughly a quarter of Cambodia's population — in what became known as the Cambodian genocide.

Rise to power

The Cambodian communist movement grew out of the Indochinese Communist Party and gained strength during the civil war of 1970–1975 against the US-backed Khmer Republic of Lon Nol, aided by the devastation of American bombing and by the patronage of Prince Norodom Sihanouk, who had been deposed in 1970. On 17 April 1975 Khmer Rouge forces captured Phnom Penh and immediately ordered the complete evacuation of the cities.

Rule

The regime abolished money, markets, private property, religion and formal education, and drove the population into rural cooperatives where they worked under brutal conditions. The secret police (Santebal) ran prisons such as S-21 (Tuol Sleng), where some 14,000–20,000 people were tortured and executed. Ethnic Vietnamese, Cham Muslims, monks, and former officials were targeted for extermination, and mass purges repeatedly struck the party itself.

Fall and aftermath

Border clashes with Vietnam led to a Vietnamese invasion in December 1978; Phnom Penh fell on 7 January 1979 and the Khmer Rouge retreated to the Thai border, where they waged a guerrilla war for two more decades, retaining Cambodia's seat at the United Nations with international backing. The movement finally collapsed after Pol Pot's death in 1998. Senior leaders Nuon Chea and Khieu Samphan were convicted of crimes against humanity and genocide by the UN-backed Extraordinary Chambers in the Courts of Cambodia.
