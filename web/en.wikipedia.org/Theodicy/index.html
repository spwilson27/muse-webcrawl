Theodicy

A theodicy is an attempt to reconcile the existence of evil and suffering in the world with the belief in an omnipotent, omnibenevolent, and omniscient God. The term was coined by Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz in his 1710 work Théodicée.

The problem of evil

The logical problem of evil, traced to Epicurus, asks how God can permit suffering. Defences respond that evil is a privation of good (Augustine), that free will makes moral evil possible, or that suffering is necessary for soul-making (the Irenaean tradition, developed by John Hick).

Leibniz

Leibniz argued that God, choosing among possible worlds, necessarily created the best of all possible worlds — a view Voltaire satirised in Candide after the 1755 Lisbon earthquake shook European confidence in providence.

Modern discussion

Philosophers distinguish a theodicy, which purports to give God's actual reasons, from a mere defence showing that God's existence is logically compatible with evil. The scale of suffering in events such as the Holocaust has led some theologians toward protest theology or anti-theodicy.
