Afrotheria

CladePlacentalia
SuperorderAfrotheria
Included ordersProboscidea, Sirenia, Hyracoidea, Tubulidentata, Macroscelidea, Afrosoricida

Afrotheria ("African animals") is a superorder of placental mammals that evolved on the African continent when it was isolated as an island during the Cretaceous and early Cenozoic. It unites groups that look strikingly different — elephants, manatees, hyraxes, aardvarks, elephant shrews, tenrecs, and golden moles — whose common ancestry was revealed by molecular phylogenetics in the late 1990s rather than by anatomy.

Evolutionary history

Africa separated from the other southern continents of Gondwana roughly 105–115 million years ago, and its mammal fauna evolved in isolation for tens of millions of years. Afrotheres diversified into ecological roles filled elsewhere by other lineages: aardvarks resemble anteaters, golden moles resemble true moles, and tenrecs resemble hedgehogs and shrews — classic convergent evolution that long hid their relationships.

Classification

Afrotheria is usually divided into two main branches. Afroinsectiphilia includes the aardvark (Tubulidentata), elephant shrews (Macroscelidea), and the tenrecs and golden moles (Afrosoricida). Paenungulata unites elephants (Proboscidea), sea cows (Sirenia), and hyraxes (Hyracoidea) — a grouping already suspected from anatomy — together with extinct groups such as the embrithopods and desmostylians.

Characteristics

Shared anatomical features are subtle, and the clade rests mainly on molecular evidence: DNA sequence analyses and the presence of distinctive retroposon insertions. Many afrotheres have a relatively low and variable body temperature and, in several groups, abdominal testes and elongated snouts. The discovery of Afrotheria, along with Xenarthra, Euarchontoglires, and Laurasiatheria, established the modern four-superorder classification of placental mammals.
