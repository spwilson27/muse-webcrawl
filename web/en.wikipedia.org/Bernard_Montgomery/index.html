Bernard Montgomery

Field Marshal Bernard Law Montgomery, 1st Viscount Montgomery of Alamein (17 November 1887 – 24 March 1976), nicknamed "Monty", was a senior British Army officer who served in both World Wars. He came to fame commanding the British Eighth Army in North Africa, defeating Rommel's Afrika Korps at the Second Battle of El Alamein (1942) — a turning point of the war. He later commanded the 21st Army Group in north-west Europe from D-Day through the end of the war, accepting the surrender of German forces in northern Europe at Lüneburg Heath in May 1945. Meticulous, egotistical, and famously careful with his soldiers' lives, Montgomery remains one of the most debated Allied commanders.

Career

Early service

The son of an Anglican bishop, Montgomery was educated at St Paul's and Sandhurst. Badly wounded in the First World War at Meteren in 1914, he returned as a staff officer and developed the rigorous training and planning methods that defined his later generalship. Between the wars he commanded in Palestine, India, and Britain, and in 1939–40 led the 3rd Division in France, escaping via Dunkirk.

North Africa and Italy

Appointed to the Eighth Army in August 1942 after the death of General Gott, Montgomery halted Rommel at Alam el Halfa, then won decisively at El Alamein (23 October – 11 November 1942), pursuing the Axis across Libya into Tunisia. In 1943 he led the Eighth Army in the invasions of Sicily and mainland Italy.

North-west Europe

Montgomery commanded all Allied ground forces in the Normandy landings of June 1944 and thereafter the British-Canadian 21st Army Group. His record includes the costly battles around Caen, the failed Operation Market Garden at Arnhem (September 1944), the defence against the Ardennes offensive's northern shoulder, the Rhine crossing (Operation Plunder, March 1945), and the advance to the Baltic. On 4 May 1945 German forces in the Netherlands, north-west Germany, and Denmark surrendered to him at Lüneburg Heath.

Later life and assessment

Knighted and raised to the peerage as Viscount Montgomery of Alamein, he served as Chief of the Imperial General Staff and then as NATO's Deputy Supreme Allied Commander in Europe until 1958. His 1958 memoirs inflamed old rivalries, particularly with American commanders; historians credit his gifts for organisation, morale, and set-piece battle while criticising his caution, slowness of exploitation, and difficult personality in coalition warfare.
