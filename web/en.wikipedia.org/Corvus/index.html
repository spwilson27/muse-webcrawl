Corvus

Corvus is a genus of medium to large-sized birds in the family Corvidae, including the crows, ravens and rooks. The genus, first described by Carl Linnaeus in 1758, contains roughly 45–50 extant species found on all temperate continents except South America (and also New Zealand and many islands, in some cases by human introduction). Members of the genus are characterised by black plumage, stout bills and remarkable intelligence.

Species

Well-known species include the common raven (Corvus corax), the largest passerine bird; the carrion crow (C. corone); the hooded crow (C. cornix); the rook (C. frugilegus); the American crow (C. brachyrhynchos); the jackdaws, sometimes placed in their own genus; the New Caledonian crow (C. moneduloides); and the Hawaiian crow or ʻalalā (C. hawaiiensis), extinct in the wild and the subject of reintroduction efforts.

Intelligence and behaviour

Corvus species rank among the most intelligent birds, showing tool manufacture (famously the New Caledonian crow's hooked tools), causal reasoning, play, social learning, food caching with pilfering deterrence, and recognition of individual human faces. Their brain-to-body mass ratios are comparable to those of great apes, achieved with a differently organised pallium.

In culture

Crows and ravens figure prominently in mythology and folklore worldwide — as tricksters, omens, and divine messengers such as Odin's ravens Huginn and Muninn — and in literature from Aesop to Edgar Allan Poe's "The Raven".
