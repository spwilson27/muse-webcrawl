Totopo

A totopo is a flat, round, or triangular corn product originating in Mexico, made from nixtamalized corn masa, tortillas, or corn flour, and typically baked or fried until crisp. Totopos are a staple of Mexican cuisine and are the basis of dishes such as chilaquiles and nachos. In the United States and elsewhere they are widely known as tortilla chips.

History

Totopos have pre-Hispanic roots in the corn-based foodways of Mesoamerica. The name derives from the Nahuatl word totopochtli, meaning a toasted or crisp tortilla. In the Isthmus of Tehuantepec region of Oaxaca, particularly around Juchitán, totopos are traditionally baked in clay ovens called comixcales and are distinguished by small holes pierced in the dough before baking, which let steam escape and produce an especially dry, crisp texture.

Preparation

Traditional totopos are made from corn dough prepared by nixtamalization — cooking dried corn in an alkaline solution of slaked lime — which improves flavor, nutrition, and texture. The dough is pressed into discs, sometimes pricked with holes, and baked or toasted. Modern commercial versions are usually made by cutting corn tortillas into wedges and deep-frying them, then salting them while hot.

Uses

Totopos are served with salsa, guacamole, refried beans, and ceviche. Broken or whole, they form the base of chilaquiles, in which they are simmered briefly in green or red salsa, and of nachos, in which they are layered with melted cheese and toppings. Stale tortillas are traditionally repurposed as totopos, making the dish an economical way to avoid waste.
