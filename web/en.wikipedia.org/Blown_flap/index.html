Blown flap

Blown flaps, or jet flaps, are a type of high-lift device used on the wings of some aircraft to reduce the stalling speed at which the aircraft can safely fly. Air or exhaust gas, taken from the engines, is blown over the trailing edge flaps, energising the boundary layer and delaying flow separation, which substantially increases lift at low speeds.

Mechanism

A slot in the wing or flap ducts high-pressure air — commonly compressor bleed air from a jet engine — so that it blows tangentially over the upper surface of the lowered flap. By the Coandă effect, the jet stays attached to the curved flap surface and is deflected downward; because the airflow remains attached at flap deflections that would normally cause separation, the wing produces far more lift, enabling shorter take-offs and slower approaches. Simpler variants blow air over the wing itself rather than the flap.

Applications

Blown flaps were studied extensively in the 1950s and 1960s and used operationally on aircraft such as the Lockheed F-104 Starfighter, whose thin wing otherwise produced dangerously high landing speeds, and on the Blackburn Buccaneer. The complexity of the ducting and dependence on engine power limited their wider adoption; modern designs generally prefer large conventional flaps and slats.
