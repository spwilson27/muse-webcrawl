Value investing

Value investing is an investment strategy that involves picking stocks that appear to be trading for less than their intrinsic or book value. Value investors actively seek stocks they believe the market has undervalued, expecting the price to rise as the market recognises the company's true worth.

History

The strategy was developed by Benjamin Graham and David Dodd at Columbia Business School in the 1920s and 1930s and set out in their 1934 book Security Analysis. Graham's 1949 book The Intelligent Investor popularised the approach, including the concepts of intrinsic value and the "margin of safety" — buying at a significant discount to intrinsic value to limit downside risk.

Notable practitioners

Warren Buffett and Charlie Munger of Berkshire Hathaway are the best-known modern value investors, though Buffett has described their approach as evolving toward buying "wonderful companies at fair prices" rather than "fair companies at wonderful prices." Other prominent practitioners include Seth Klarman, Joel Greenblatt, and Walter Schloss.

Measures

Value investors commonly examine metrics such as the price-to-earnings ratio, price-to-book ratio, dividend yield, and free cash flow, alongside qualitative assessment of management and competitive position. The approach contrasts with growth investing, which favours companies expected to grow earnings rapidly even at high valuations.
