MIDI

MIDI (Musical Instrument Digital Interface) is a technical standard that describes a communication protocol, digital interface, and electrical connectors that connect a wide variety of electronic musical instruments, computers, and related audio devices for playing, editing, and recording music.

History

MIDI was developed in the early 1980s through cooperation between rival synthesizer manufacturers, principally Dave Smith of Sequential Circuits and Ikutaro Kakehashi of Roland, who recognised that an industry standard would let instruments from different makers interoperate. The specification was unveiled in 1983, when a Sequential Prophet-600 was connected to a Roland Jupiter-6. MIDI 1.0 became the durable backbone of electronic music production; the long-awaited MIDI 2.0 specification was adopted in 2020, adding bidirectional communication, higher-resolution controllers, and per-note articulation.

How it works

MIDI carries event messages — note on, note off, velocity, pitch bend, control change, program change — rather than audio. A standard MIDI file (.mid) can record an entire performance in a few kilobytes. The original hardware interface uses 5-pin DIN connectors at 31.25 kbit/s with 16 channels per cable; MIDI is now also carried over USB, Bluetooth, and network connections. General MIDI standardised instrument patch maps so files would sound similar on different devices.

Applications

Beyond music performance, MIDI is used in stage lighting control, theatre cues, and show control. Its longevity and universality have made it one of the most successful standards in the history of electronic instruments.
