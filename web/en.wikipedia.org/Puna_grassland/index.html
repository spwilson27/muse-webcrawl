Puna grassland

The puna is a Neotropical montane grassland ecoregion of the central Andes, lying at elevations between roughly 3,200 and 5,000 metres above sea level in Peru, Bolivia, northern Chile, and northwestern Argentina. It forms part of the Andean high plateau, or Altiplano, and is one of the highest and most climatically extreme grassland regions in the world.

Climate

The puna has a cold, dry climate with strong daily temperature swings: intense solar radiation by day and frequent freezing at night, in a pattern sometimes described as "summer every day, winter every night." Rainfall, concentrated in the austral summer, ranges from under 250 mm in the dry puna of the southwest to about 1,000 mm in the humid puna of the northeast near Lake Titicaca.

Flora and fauna

Vegetation is dominated by tussock grasses of the genera Festuca, Stipa (ichu grass), and Calamagrostis, together with cushion plants such as Azorella (yareta) adapted to frost and wind. Scattered stands of Polylepis trees form some of the highest forests on Earth. Characteristic animals include the vicuña and guanaco, the domesticated llama and alpaca, the Andean fox, the puma, the Andean goose, giant coot, and three species of flamingo that feed in the region's salt lakes and wetlands (bofedales). The vizcacha, a chinchilla relative, inhabits rocky outcrops.

Human use

Indigenous peoples have grazed llamas and alpacas and cultivated frost-resistant crops — potatoes, quinoa, and kañiwa — on the puna for thousands of years. The region contains important mining districts, and overgrazing by introduced livestock and climate change are its principal conservation threats.
