The Green Revolution, or the Third Agricultural Revolution, was a period of technology transfer initiatives that saw greatly increased crop yields in the developing world, beginning in the mid-20th century. It led to the adoption of new technologies, including high-yielding varieties (HYVs) of cereals, especially dwarf wheat and rice, chemical fertilisers, agrochemicals, controlled irrigation, and mechanisation.

Development

The programme grew out of work in Mexico from 1944, sponsored by the Rockefeller Foundation, where agronomist Norman Borlaug bred semi-dwarf, disease-resistant wheat varieties; Mexico became self-sufficient in wheat by 1963. The International Rice Research Institute (IRRI) in the Philippines released the high-yielding rice IR8 in 1966. The package spread through India (under M. S. Swaminathan), Pakistan, and much of Asia and Latin America; Borlaug received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1970. The term "Green Revolution" was popularised by USAID administrator William Gaud in 1968.

Effects

Cereal production more than doubled in many developing countries between 1961 and 1985, averting predicted famines and enabling population growth. Critics note environmental costs — heavy water use, fertiliser runoff, pesticide dependence, soil degradation and loss of crop genetic diversity — and social effects, including benefits skewed toward larger landowners and regions with irrigation. Africa saw less impact. Its legacy continues in debates over food security and a possible "second green revolution" based on biotechnology and sustainable intensification.
