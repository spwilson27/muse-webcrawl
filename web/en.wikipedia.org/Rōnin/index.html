Rōnin

A rōnin (Japanese: 浪人, "drifter" or "wanderer") was a samurai without a lord or master during the feudal period (1185–1868) of Japan. A samurai became masterless upon the death of his master or after the loss of his master's favor or privilege.

During the Edo period, with the shogunate's rigid class system and laws, the number of rōnin greatly increased. Confiscation of fiefs during the rule of the third Tokugawa shōgun Iemitsu resulted in an especially large increase of rōnin. During previous ages, samurai were able to move between masters and even occupations and marry between classes. However, during the Edo period, samurai were restricted, and were above all forbidden to become employed by another master without their previous master's permission.

Etymology and status

The word rōnin literally means "wave man", meaning "wanderer". The term originated in the Nara and Heian periods, when it referred to serfs who had fled or deserted their master's land. It came to be used for a samurai who had no master (hence "wave man" illustrating one who is socially adrift).

Because a rōnin had no lord to serve, he could become a mercenary, a bandit, or a bodyguard for wealthy merchants. Some rōnin joined the ranks of peasants and farmers, while others sought employment as teachers of swordsmanship or opened schools of martial arts.

The Forty-seven Rōnin

The most famous rōnin are the Forty-seven Rōnin, whose story is told in the Chūshingura. After their lord Asano Naganori was compelled to commit seppuku for assaulting the court official Kira Yoshinaka, his retainers, led by Ōishi Yoshio, became rōnin. They waited nearly two years before avenging their lord by killing Kira in 1703, after which they were themselves ordered to commit seppuku. Their story became a celebrated example of the samurai code of honor, bushidō.

In popular culture

Rōnin feature prominently in Japanese and Western popular culture, including Akira Kurosawa's films Seven Samurai (1954) and Yojimbo (1961), the manga Lone Wolf and Cub, and numerous video games. The figure of the masterless swordsman has become an enduring archetype of independence and moral ambiguity.
