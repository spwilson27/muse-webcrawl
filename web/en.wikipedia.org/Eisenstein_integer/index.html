In mathematics, an Eisenstein integer (named after Gotthold Eisenstein), also called an Eulerian integer, is a complex number of the form z = a + bω, where a and b are integers and ω = (−1 + √−3)/2 = e2πi/3 is a primitive (non-real) cube root of unity.

Properties

The Eisenstein integers form a commutative ring of algebraic integers in the algebraic number field Q(√−3), and they form a triangular lattice in the complex plane, in contrast to the Gaussian integers, which form a square lattice. The norm of an Eisenstein integer is N(a + bω) = a² − ab + b², its squared absolute value.

Euclidean domain

The Eisenstein integers form a Euclidean domain with respect to the norm: for any two elements, division with remainder leaves a remainder of smaller norm. Consequently the ring is a principal ideal domain and a unique factorization domain. Its six units are ±1, ±ω and ±ω², the sixth roots of unity.

Eisenstein primes

An Eisenstein integer is an Eisenstein prime if it cannot be written as a product of two non-unit elements. Rational primes congruent to 2 modulo 3 remain prime in the ring, while the prime 3 ramifies (it is, up to a unit, the square of 1 − ω), and primes congruent to 1 modulo 3 split into factors of equal norm. The arithmetic of the ring can be used to prove results such as the characterization of primes expressible as p = x² − xy + y² and cases of Fermat's Last Theorem for exponent 3.
