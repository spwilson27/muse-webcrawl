Gauss sum

In algebraic number theory, a Gauss sum or Gaussian sum is a finite exponential sum formed by combining a multiplicative character and an additive character of a finite field or a ring of integers modulo n. Gauss sums were studied by Carl Friedrich Gauss in connection with quadratic reciprocity and the construction of regular polygons, and they remain fundamental objects in analytic number theory.

Quadratic Gauss sums

The classical quadratic Gauss sum is formed with the Legendre symbol modulo an odd prime p: it is the sum over residues a of the Legendre symbol (a/p) times the exponential e2πia/p. Gauss determined that this sum equals √p if p ≡ 1 (mod 4) and i√p if p ≡ 3 (mod 4) — evaluating even the sign of the sum took him several years and was, he wrote, a problem that "tormented" him. The absolute value of a Gauss sum is generally the square root of the modulus, a fact connected with the size of periods of cyclotomic fields.

General theory and applications

General Gauss sums are attached to Dirichlet characters and appear in the functional equations of Dirichlet L-functions. Closely related Jacobi sums are used in counting points on curves over finite fields and in primality testing (the Adleman–Pomerance–Rumely test). Gauss sums also arise in the theory of cyclotomy, in the evaluation of quadratic residues, and in physics in the study of quantum chaos and number-theoretic phases.
