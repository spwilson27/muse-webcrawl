The Oslo I Accord, formally known as the Declaration of Principles on Interim Self-Government Arrangements (DOP), is the first of the Oslo Accords, signed between Israel and the Palestine Liberation Organization (PLO) in Washington, D.C. on 13 September 1993. It was the product of secret negotiations held in Oslo, Norway, beginning in early 1993, outside the official Madrid peace process.

Background

The official Israeli–Palestinian talks in Washington had deadlocked, partly because Israel refused to negotiate with the PLO directly. A back channel in Norway, initiated by researchers of the Fafo institute and facilitated by Norwegian foreign ministry officials, allowed Israeli academics and then officials to meet PLO representatives. Yossi Beilin and Yair Hirschfeld opened the channel; Uri Savir and Joel Singer later formalized it for Israel under Prime Minister Yitzhak Rabin and Foreign Minister Shimon Peres, facing PLO negotiators led by Ahmed Qurei (Abu Ala).

Terms

The Declaration provided for: mutual recognition (exchanged in letters of 9–10 September 1993, in which the PLO renounced terrorism and recognized Israel, and Israel recognized the PLO as the Palestinians' representative); establishment of a Palestinian Interim Self-Government Authority (the Palestinian Authority) for a transitional period not exceeding five years; Israeli withdrawal from the Gaza Strip and the Jericho area (implemented by the 1994 Cairo Agreement); elections to a Palestinian Council; and permanent-status negotiations, to begin by the third year, covering Jerusalem, refugees, settlements, security arrangements, and borders.

Significance

The signing ceremony on the White House lawn, hosted by President Bill Clinton, produced the iconic image of Rabin shaking hands with PLO chairman Yasser Arafat. The accord earned Rabin, Peres, and Arafat the 1994 Nobel Peace Prize. It was followed by the more detailed Oslo II Accord of 1995, but final-status talks never produced an agreement.
