Constitutions of Clarendon

The Constitutions of Clarendon were a set of legislative procedures passed by Henry II of England in 1164. The Constitutions were composed of 16 articles and represented an attempt to restrict ecclesiastical privileges and curb the power of the Church and its courts, and to extend the reach of the king's government into legal matters that had been the preserve of the Church. They were named after Clarendon Palace, Wiltshire, where they were promulgated.

Contents

Among the most contentious provisions were those requiring that clergy convicted of serious crimes in church courts ("criminous clerks") be handed over to royal courts for punishment; that appeals to Rome require the king's consent; that no tenant-in-chief or royal officer be excommunicated without the king's permission; and that disputes over advowsons and church property be settled in the king's court.

The Becket controversy

Thomas Becket, Archbishop of Canterbury, initially assented under pressure but then repudiated his consent, becoming the focus of resistance to the Constitutions. The ensuing quarrel led to Becket's exile in France, his dramatic return, and his murder by four knights in Canterbury Cathedral on 29 December 1170. Public outrage and Becket's swift canonization forced Henry to perform public penance and, in the Compromise of Avranches (1172), to abandon the most objectionable customs, effectively ending the attempt to implement the Constitutions in full.

Significance

The Constitutions are a landmark in the medieval struggle between regnum and sacerdotium — royal and ecclesiastical power — and in the development of English common law, whose expansion under Henry II they accompanied.
