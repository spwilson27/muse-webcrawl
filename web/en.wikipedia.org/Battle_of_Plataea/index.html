The Battle of Plataea was the final land battle of the second Persian invasion of Greece, fought in August 479 BC near the city of Plataea in Boeotia. The largest Greek army ever assembled to that date — some 40,000 hoplites with tens of thousands of light troops, commanded by the Spartan regent Pausanias — faced the Persian army left in Greece by Xerxes under Mardonius. After days of manoeuvre, cavalry harassment and a confused Greek withdrawal, the Spartans and Tegeans, cut off from the rest of the army, defeated the Persian infantry in close combat; Mardonius was killed, and the Persian camp was stormed. Together with the near-simultaneous victory at Mycale, Plataea ended the Persian attempt to conquer mainland Greece.

Background

After the naval victory at Salamis in 480 BC, Xerxes withdrew to Asia, leaving Mardonius with a picked army to complete the conquest the following spring. Mardonius wintered in Thessaly, tried unsuccessfully to detach Athens by diplomacy, briefly reoccupied Athens, and then withdrew into Boeotia, whose leading city, Thebes, was pro-Persian. On the plain of the Asopus river, suited to his superior cavalry, he built a fortified camp. The Greek allies, having finally mustered in full strength at the Isthmus, advanced into Boeotia under Pausanias.

The battle

For over a week the armies faced each other across the Asopus. Persian cavalry under Masistius harassed the Greeks, cut their supply lines through the Cithaeron passes and fouled their water supply, forcing Pausanias to plan a night withdrawal to a better position near Plataea. The withdrawal, begun late and executed unevenly, left the Spartans and Tegeans on the right exposed at dawn, the Athenians on the left separated by rising ground, and the centre far behind. Mardonius, believing the Greeks in full flight, launched a general attack. The Spartans, pressed hard by Persian archery, delayed their counter-charge until the sacrifices proved favourable, then advanced and broke the Persian guard in hand-to-hand fighting; Mardonius, conspicuous on a white horse, was killed by a Spartan named Aeimnestus. The Persian army dissolved; the Greeks stormed the camp, while the Athenians overcame the Thebans on the other wing. Artabazus, commanding the Persian reserve, withdrew his 40,000 men northwards to Asia without engaging.

Aftermath

The victory was commemorated with lavish dedications, including the Serpent Column at Delphi (now in Istanbul) listing the allied cities, and a tithe of the spoils. Plataea established the Plataeans' honoured status among the allies and inaugurated annual rites for the fallen. Strategically, the initiative passed to the Greeks: the Delian League's offensives in the Aegean and Asia Minor followed, and Persia never again invaded the Greek mainland.
