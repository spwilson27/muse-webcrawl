Translucency

Translucency is a physical property of materials that allows light to pass through but does not permit objects on the other side to be seen clearly. It lies between transparency, in which objects are seen sharply, and opacity, in which light is blocked entirely. Translucent materials scatter light as it passes through them, so that the transmitted light is diffuse rather than directional.

Optical behaviour

When light strikes a translucent material, some of it is absorbed, some reflected, and some transmitted. The transmitted portion undergoes scattering at internal boundaries, inclusions, or surface roughness, which destroys the image-forming quality of the light. Examples include frosted glass, wax paper, human skin, and thin slices of marble. The degree of translucency can be described by optical properties such as turbidity and the scattering coefficient.

Applications

Translucent materials are widely used for privacy glazing, lampshades, packaging, cosmetics, and architectural cladding. In computer graphics, subsurface scattering models simulate translucency in materials such as skin, milk, and jade, greatly improving the realism of rendered images.
