Orthogonal frequency-division multiplexing

OFDM divides a channel into many narrow, overlapping but mutually orthogonal subcarriers.

Orthogonal frequency-division multiplexing (OFDM) is a type of digital transmission used in telecommunications that consists of many relatively low data-rate carriers used in parallel instead of one high data-rate carrier. Each subcarrier is modulated with a conventional scheme such as QAM or PSK at a low symbol rate, and the subcarriers are spaced so that their spectra overlap without interfering — the orthogonality condition.

Principles

By dividing the data stream among many narrowband subchannels, OFDM makes each subchannel experience approximately flat fading, greatly simplifying equalisation and improving robustness against multipath propagation and narrowband interference. A cyclic prefix (guard interval) inserted before each symbol absorbs inter-symbol interference. Transmitters and receivers are efficiently implemented with the inverse and forward FFT.

Applications

OFDM underlies a large share of modern broadband systems: Wi-Fi (IEEE 802.11a/g/n/ac/ax), 4G LTE and 5G NR mobile networks, DSL (in its DMT form), digital television standards DVB-T and ATSC 3.0, and digital radio such as DAB.

Drawbacks

The main disadvantages are a high peak-to-average power ratio, which stresses transmitter amplifiers, and sensitivity to carrier frequency offset and phase noise, which destroy orthogonality if not corrected.
