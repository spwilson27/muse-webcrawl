Arsenopyrite (IMA symbol: Apy) is an iron arsenic sulphide mineral with the formula FeAsS. It is the most common ore of arsenic and a frequent companion of gold deposits. The name comes from its composition and its resemblance to pyrite; the older mining term for it was mispickel.

Properties and identification

Arsenopyrite forms steel-grey to silver-white prismatic crystals, often striated and twinned, with a black streak. It is hard (5.5–6 on Mohs scale), dense, and brittle. When struck or heated it gives off a characteristic garlic odour from released arsenic vapour. Its structure is derived from the marcasite type, with each iron atom coordinated by three sulphur and three arsenic atoms.

Occurrence

Arsenopyrite is a high-temperature hydrothermal mineral found in pegmatites, quartz veins, and metamorphic rocks, commonly associated with gold, cassiterite (tin), wolframite, galena and pyrite. Notable localities include Freiberg (Germany), Cornwall (England), Bolivia, China and the Giant Mine at Yellowknife, Canada. In many gold ores the gold is "refractory", locked within arsenopyrite crystals, requiring oxidation before extraction.

Environmental significance

Weathering of arsenopyrite releases arsenic into soils and waters, contributing to natural arsenic contamination. Roasting of gold ores at Yellowknife produced large quantities of arsenic trioxide dust, stored underground since the mid-20th century — a significant long-term environmental hazard.
