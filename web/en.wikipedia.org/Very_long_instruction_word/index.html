Very long instruction word

Very long instruction word (VLIW) refers to instruction set architectures designed to exploit instruction-level parallelism by specifying multiple independent operations in a single, wide instruction. In a VLIW processor, the compiler — rather than complex hardware scheduling logic — is responsible for packing operations that can execute simultaneously into one instruction word.

Design

VLIW machines typically have multiple functional units of different types (integer ALUs, floating-point units, load/store units, branch units). Because scheduling is static, the hardware can be simpler than in superscalar designs, but the burden of finding parallelism falls entirely on the compiler, and code compiled for one VLIW implementation is generally not binary-compatible with others of different widths.

History and examples

The concept was pioneered by Josh Fisher at Yale University in the early 1980s and commercialised through Multiflow Computer. Later examples include the Texas Instruments TMS320C6000 family of digital signal processors and the Itanium architecture developed by HP and Intel, which used a related approach called EPIC.
