Seat belt

A typical three-point seat belt.

A seat belt, also known as a safety belt, is a vehicle safety device designed to secure the driver or a passenger of a vehicle against harmful movement that may result during a collision or a sudden stop. A seat belt reduces the likelihood of death or serious injury in a traffic collision by reducing the force of secondary impacts with interior strike hazards, by keeping occupants positioned correctly for maximum effectiveness of the airbag (if equipped), and by preventing occupants from being ejected from the vehicle in a crash or if the vehicle rolls over.

History

Seat belts were first offered in aircraft and racing cars in the early 20th century. The modern three-point belt was developed by Swedish engineer Nils Bohlin for Volvo, which introduced it in 1959 and released the patent freely to other manufacturers. Nash and Ford had offered belts as options in the mid-1950s, and Saab fitted them as standard in 1958.

Types

The most common design is the three-point belt, combining a lap strap with a diagonal shoulder strap retracted from a reel. Older vehicles used two-point lap belts, which are associated with "seat belt syndrome" abdominal injuries. Modern belts incorporate pretensioners that pull the belt tight at the moment of a crash and load limiters that feed out webbing to reduce chest forces.

Effectiveness

Studies consistently show seat belts reduce the risk of death for front-seat occupants by roughly 40–50 per cent and of serious injury by a similar margin. In the United States, usage rose from about 14 per cent in the early 1980s to around 90 per cent in recent years, credited with saving hundreds of thousands of lives.

Legislation

Victoria, Australia, enacted the world's first compulsory seat belt wearing law in 1970. The United Kingdom made front-belt wearing compulsory in 1983. Most countries now mandate belts for all occupants, enforced by fines and, in many jurisdictions, seat belt reminders.
