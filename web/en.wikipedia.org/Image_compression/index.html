Image compression

Image compression is a type of data compression applied to digital images, to reduce their cost for storage or transmission. Algorithms may take advantage of visual perception and the statistical properties of image data to provide superior results compared with generic data compression methods.

Lossy and lossless compression

Image compression may be lossy or lossless. Lossless compression is preferred for archival purposes and often for medical imaging, technical drawings, and clip art. Lossy compression methods, especially when used at low bit rates, introduce compression artifacts. Lossy methods are especially suitable for natural images such as photographs in applications where a minor, sometimes imperceptible, loss of fidelity is acceptable to achieve a substantial reduction in bit rate.

Methods

Lossless methods include run-length encoding, entropy encoding such as Huffman and arithmetic coding, and dictionary-based schemes such as the Lempel–Ziv–Welch algorithm used in GIF and PNG. Lossy methods typically transform the image into a domain where coefficients can be quantized and many discarded: the discrete cosine transform (DCT) underlies JPEG, while wavelet transforms underlie JPEG 2000. Modern formats such as WebP, AVIF, and HEIF combine intra-frame prediction with transform coding.

Applications

Image compression is essential to digital photography, web publishing, video streaming (where intra-frame coding compresses each frame as an image), medical imaging archives, and satellite and space imagery, where bandwidth is severely limited. Typical photographic compression ratios range from 10:1 to 20:1 with little visible degradation.
