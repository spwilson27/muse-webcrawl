Whip (politics)

A whip is an official of a political party appointed to ensure party discipline in a legislature. Whips seek to ensure that legislators attend voting sessions and vote according to the party's official policy, and they act as a channel of communication between the parliamentary leadership and backbench members.

Origin of the term

The term derives from the "whipper-in" in fox hunting, who keeps the hounds from straying from the pack. It was applied to parliamentary party managers in the British House of Commons by the late 18th century; Edmund Burke is credited with an early use in 1769.

United Kingdom

In the Westminster system, each party has a Chief Whip and a number of junior whips. Government whips hold offices in the Royal Household or Treasury. The importance of a vote is communicated by underlining items on the weekly notice (also called "the whip") once, twice, or three times — a "three-line whip" denotes a vote on which attendance and support are obligatory, and defying it can lead to withdrawal of the whip, meaning temporary expulsion from the parliamentary party. Whips also manage pairing arrangements and, by convention, do not speak in debates.

Other systems

Similar offices exist in other Westminster-derived parliaments (Canada, Australia, India, Ireland, New Zealand) and in the United States Congress, where the majority and minority whips of the House and Senate count votes and persuade members, ranking second in their party's chamber leadership. In the US the role is persuading rather than disciplining, since congressional parties cannot expel members.
