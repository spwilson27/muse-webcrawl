Governor of New York


The State Capitol in Albany.

The governor of New York is the head of government of the U.S. state of New York, the chief executive of the state government and the commander-in-chief of the state's military forces. The governor is elected by the people for a four-year term with no term limit, and is responsible for implementing state laws, preparing the executive budget, appointing agency heads, and signing or vetoing legislation passed by the New York State Legislature.

Powers

Often described as among the most powerful governorships in the United States, the office controls an executive budget process in which the governor's proposed appropriations can only be reduced, not increased, by the legislature. The governor may issue executive orders, grant pardons and reprieves, convene special sessions of the legislature, and exercise a line-item veto over appropriation bills. The official residence is the New York State Executive Mansion in Albany.

History

The office was created by the state constitution of 1777, and its first holder was George Clinton, who served seven terms. Four governors of New York have become president of the United States: Martin Van Buren, Grover Cleveland, Theodore Roosevelt and Franklin D. Roosevelt. Kathy Hochul became the state's first female governor in August 2021, succeeding Andrew Cuomo upon his resignation, and was elected to a full term in 2022.
