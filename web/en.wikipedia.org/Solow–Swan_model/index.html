Solow–Swan model

FieldMacroeconomics, growth theory
Developed byRobert Solow and Trevor Swan (1956)
Key conceptSteady state; exogenous technological progress
The Solow diagram: investment, depreciation, and the steady state

The Solow–Swan model, or exogenous growth model, is a neoclassical model of long-run economic growth. It explains growth in output through capital accumulation, labour (or population) growth, and increases in productivity, known as total factor productivity or technological progress, which is treated as exogenous — arising from outside the model.

The model

Published independently by Robert Solow and Trevor Swan in 1956, the model uses a production function, commonly of Cobb–Douglas form Y = Kα(AL)1−α, combining capital K, effective labour AL, and technology A. Because capital exhibits diminishing returns, an economy converges to a steady state in which capital per effective worker is constant and output per worker grows only at the rate of technological progress. Savings raise the level of income but not its long-run growth rate.

Implications and influence

The model predicts conditional convergence: poorer economies should grow faster than richer ones, other things equal, a prediction supported by some empirical studies. Solow's 1957 growth-accounting application attributed most of the growth in U.S. output per worker to the unexplained "residual" of technological progress, prompting the development of endogenous growth theory in the 1980s, which sought to explain technology within the model. Solow received the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in 1987 in part for this work.
