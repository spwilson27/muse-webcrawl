Cinéma vérité (French for "truthful cinema") is a style of documentary filmmaking developed by French filmmaker and anthropologist Jean Rouch, inspired by Soviet director Dziga Vertov's Kino-Pravda ("film truth") newsreels. The style combines improvisation with the use of the camera to unveil truth or highlight subjects hidden behind crude reality.

Characteristics

Cinéma vérité practitioners used newly available lightweight cameras and synchronized sound equipment to film everyday life with minimal artifice. Unlike the American Direct Cinema movement, which prized the filmmaker as an unobtrusive fly-on-the-wall observer, vérité filmmakers acknowledged—and often deliberately used—the camera's presence as a catalyst that provokes subjects to reveal themselves.

History

Rouch's Moi, un noir (1958) and the Rouch–Edgar Morin film Chronique d'un été (Chronicle of a Summer, 1961) are landmark works of the movement. The approach influenced the French New Wave and generations of documentary filmmakers, and its techniques passed into fiction filmmaking, television journalism, and reality television.
