Extreme value theory

Extreme value theory (EVT) is a branch of statistics dealing with the extreme deviations from the median of probability distributions. It seeks to assess, from a given ordered sample of a random variable, the probability of events that are more extreme than any previously observed — floods beyond the historical record, record stock-market crashes, or exceptional wind speeds.

Foundations

The Fisher–Tippett–Gnedenko theorem states that the maxima of blocks of independent, identically distributed observations, suitably normalized, can converge only to one of three types, unified in the generalized extreme value (GEV) distribution: the Gumbel (light-tailed), Fréchet (heavy-tailed), and Weibull (bounded-tail) families. A complementary approach, peaks over threshold, models exceedances above a high threshold with the generalized Pareto distribution, justified by the Pickands–Balkema–de Haan theorem. Key figures in the theory's development include Maurice Fréchet, Emil Gumbel, whose 1958 Statistics of Extremes founded modern practice, and Boris Gnedenko.

Applications

Engineers use EVT to set design loads — the "hundred-year flood" and extreme wind and wave levels for dams, dikes, and offshore structures; the Dutch Delta Works are a famous application. In finance and insurance, EVT underpins estimates of value at risk and reinsurance pricing for catastrophic claims, where the heavy-tailed Fréchet regime is common. It is also applied to climatology (heatwaves and record rainfall), materials strength, and telecommunications traffic.
