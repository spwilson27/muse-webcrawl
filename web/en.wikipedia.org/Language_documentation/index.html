Language documentation

Language documentation (also called documentary linguistics) is a subfield of linguistics concerned with creating a lasting, multipurpose record of a language. It involves recording spoken and signed language use — narratives, conversation, elicitation — and producing annotated transcriptions, translations, lexicons, and grammatical descriptions, together with audio and video archives that can serve both scholarly and community purposes.

Goals and methods

Unlike purely descriptive or theoretical work, documentation prioritises the primary data themselves: the recordings and their annotation. Fieldworkers typically build corpora of transcribed and time-aligned texts, compile dictionaries, and deposit materials in digital archives such as endangered-language repositories, using open formats so that records remain accessible to future generations and to speaker communities.

Language endangerment

Documentation has become urgent because of rapid language loss: of the roughly 7,000 languages spoken today, a large fraction are endangered as speakers shift to dominant languages. Documenting an endangered language may be the only way to preserve knowledge of it for descendants and researchers after the last fluent speakers die. Documentation is often paired with language revitalization efforts led by communities themselves.

History

The modern field took shape in the 1990s, with Nikolaus Himmelmann's programmatic writings distinguishing documentation from description, and with funding initiatives such as the DoBeS programme and the Endangered Languages Documentation Programme supporting projects worldwide.
