Nucleophile

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A nucleophile is a chemical species that forms bonds by donating an electron pair to an electron-deficient centre called an electrophile. The term combines Greek nucleus and philos ("nucleus-loving"), reflecting the attraction of electron-rich species for positively charged or electron-poor nuclei. All molecules and ions with a free pair of electrons, or at least one pi bond, can act as nucleophiles; typical examples include the hydroxide ion (OH⁻), ammonia (NH₃), halide ions, water, alcohols, amines, and carbanions.

Nucleophilicity

The strength of a nucleophile, called nucleophilicity, is a kinetic property describing how fast it reacts with electrophiles. It should not be confused with basicity, which is a thermodynamic measure of proton affinity. Nucleophilicity increases with electron density and polarisability, and it is strongly affected by solvent: in polar protic solvents, small, heavily solvated ions such as fluoride are weak nucleophiles, while in polar aprotic solvents they become much stronger. Within a row of the periodic table, nucleophilicity increases to the left; down a group, larger and more polarisable atoms (e.g. iodide vs. fluoride) are better nucleophiles in protic solvents.

The concept was developed in the 1930s by Christopher Ingold, and quantitative scales of nucleophilicity have since been proposed, such as the Swain–Scott equation and Mayr's nucleophilicity parameters.

Reactions

Nucleophilic attack is the central step of many fundamental reactions in organic chemistry, including nucleophilic substitution (SN1 and SN2), nucleophilic addition to carbonyl compounds, conjugate (Michael) addition, and acyl substitution. Ambident nucleophiles, such as cyanide or thiocyanate, can attack through either of two atoms. Some nucleophiles display unusually high reactivity attributed to the alpha effect, in which an adjacent atom bearing a lone pair enhances nucleophilicity, as in hydrazine and hydroxylamine.
