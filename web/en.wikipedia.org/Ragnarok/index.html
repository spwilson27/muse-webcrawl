Ragnarök

Ragnarök is a series of events in Norse mythology, including a great battle foretold to result in the deaths of many major figures of the Norse pantheon — including Odin, Thor, Freyr, Heimdall and Loki — together with natural disasters and the submersion of the world in water. After these events, the world is said to resurface anew and fertile, the surviving and returning gods will meet, and the world will be repopulated by two human survivors, Líf and Lífþrasir.

Etymology

The Old Norse word ragnarök is a compound of regin ("gods, powers") and rök ("fate, doom, end"), giving "final destiny of the gods". A variant form, ragnarøkkr, "twilight of the gods", was popularised by Richard Wagner's opera cycle Der Ring des Nibelungen, whose final part is Götterdämmerung.

Attestations

Ragnarök is attested in the Poetic Edda, compiled in the 13th century from earlier traditional material, and in the Prose Edda, written in the 13th century by Snorri Sturluson. The poem Völuspá gives the fullest account: a seeress (völva) recites to Odin the history and future of the world.

The events

Portents precede the end: the Fimbulvetr, a winter lasting three seasons with no summer between; moral chaos and war among kin; the wolf Fenrir breaks free of his fetters; the serpent Jörmungandr rises from the ocean; and the ship Naglfar, made from the nails of the dead, sets sail carrying the hosts of Hel. The rooster Gullinkambi crows to wake the gods, and Heimdall blows the Gjallarhorn.

On the plain of Vígríðr the gods meet their enemies. Odin is swallowed by Fenrir, who is then killed by Odin's son Víðarr. Thor kills Jörmungandr but takes nine steps and dies from its venom. Freyr falls to the fire giant Surtr, Loki and Heimdall kill each other, and Týr and the hound Garm likewise perish together. Surtr then sets the world ablaze, and the earth sinks into the sea.

Aftermath

The earth rises again from the waters, green and beautiful. Baldr and Höðr return from Hel, and the surviving gods gather on Iðavöllr. The human survivors Líf and Lífþrasir, hidden in the wood Hoddmímis holt, emerge to repopulate the world. A new sun — the daughter of the old — rides her mother's path across the sky.

Interpretation

Scholars have compared Ragnarök with other Indo-European eschatologies and debated influences from Christianity and from natural events such as volcanic eruptions in Iceland. The theme pervades modern adaptations of Norse myth in literature, comics, film and games.
