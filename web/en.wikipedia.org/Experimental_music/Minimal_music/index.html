Minimal music

Minimal music (also called minimalism) is a form of art music or other compositional practice that employs limited or minimal musical materials. Prominent features of minimalist music include repetitive patterns or pulses, steady drones, consonant harmony, and gradual transformation. It began in the 1960s in New York City's Downtown scene as a reaction against the complexity of serialism and aleatoric music.

Origins

The movement's founding figures include La Monte Young, whose sustained-tone works such as the Trio for Strings (1958) are early landmarks; Terry Riley, whose In C (1964), built from 53 short repeated phrases, became a manifesto of the style; Steve Reich, who developed phasing techniques in It's Gonna Rain (1965) and Piano Phase (1967) and ensemble works like Music for 18 Musicians (1976); and Philip Glass, whose additive-process works and operas, beginning with Einstein on the Beach (1976, with Robert Wilson), brought minimalism to wide audiences. The term was applied to music by critic Michael Nyman in 1968.

Characteristics

Minimalist works often use diatonic scales, steady pulse, and slow, audible processes of change. Reich described his approach in the 1968 essay "Music as a Gradual Process". Influences include West African drumming, Indonesian gamelan, Indian classical music, and jazz.

Influence

Minimalism profoundly influenced later concert music (John Adams, Arvo Pärt's tintinnabuli, and the "postminimalist" generation), film scoring, and popular music from ambient and electronic dance genres to the work of Brian Eno and krautrock bands.
