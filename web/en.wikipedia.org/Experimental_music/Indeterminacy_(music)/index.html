Indeterminacy (music)

Graphic scores leave aspects of performance undetermined.

Indeterminacy is a composing approach in which some aspects of a musical work are left unspecified or subject to chance. The concept was developed most prominently by the American composer John Cage, who distinguished indeterminacy in composition—where chance operations produce a fixed score—from indeterminacy in performance, where the notation itself does not determine a unique result.

History

Cage's Music of Changes (1951) used I Ching coin tosses to fix every parameter of the score, yet it is fully notated. Works such as Imaginary Landscape No. 4 (1951), for twelve radios, and 4′33″ (1952), in which the performer makes no deliberate sounds, pushed indeterminacy further, allowing ambient sound and performer decisions to shape each performance differently.

Related practices

In Europe, composers associated with aleatoric techniques—including Pierre Boulez and Karlheinz Stockhausen—allowed performers limited choices of order or detail while retaining tighter compositional control than Cage advocateds. Graphic scores by Earle Brown, Cornelius Cardew, and Morton Feldman used visual notation whose interpretation is inherently indeterminate.

Reception

Indeterminacy challenged traditional notions of authorship, the musical work, and the boundary between music and noise. It influenced free improvisation, live electronics, and later experimental practices across genres.
