Video art

Video art is an art form which relies on using video technology as a visual and audio medium. It emerged during the late 1960s and early 1970s as new consumer video technology — above all Sony's Portapak portable recorder, released in 1967 — became available outside corporate television. Video art can take many forms: recordings broadcast or shown in galleries, installations combining monitors and sculpture, and live or recorded performance works.

History

The Korean-American artist Nam June Paik, associated with the Fluxus movement, is widely credited as the founder of video art; his 1963 exhibition of prepared televisions in Wuppertal and his 1965 Portapak recordings of Pope Paul VI's visit to New York are landmark moments. Wolf Vostell likewise incorporated televisions into installations and happenings from the late 1950s. In the 1970s, artists such as Joan Jonas, Bruce Nauman, Vito Acconci, and VALIE EXPORT used the camera's immediacy for performance and body art, while others manipulated the electronic signal itself with synthesizers and magnets.

Relation to experimental film

Video art developed in dialogue with underground film and structural film, but artists often stressed video's differences: instant playback, real-time feedback, closed-circuit installation, and the possibility of live transmission. As technology shifted from analogue tape to digital files and projection, single-channel tapes gave way to immersive multi-screen installations by artists such as Bill Viola.

Preservation

Because early video formats degrade and playback equipment becomes obsolete, the preservation and migration of video artworks is a major concern for museums and archives.
