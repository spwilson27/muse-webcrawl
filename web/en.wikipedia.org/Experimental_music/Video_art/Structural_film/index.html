Structural film

Structural strategies migrated from film into video art, where loops and screen-based rephotography became central devices.

Structural film describes a body of experimental cinema — fixed camera, flicker, loops, rephotography — whose formal strategies were later taken up and transformed by video art. When artists began working with portable video in the late 1960s and 1970s, many brought a structuralist attention to the apparatus: the scan lines, feedback, monitor-as-object and real-time duration of the electronic medium replaced the grain and sprocket holes of film.

From film to video

Artists such as Peter Campus, Joan Jonas and Steina and Woody Vasulka explored signal processing, feedback loops and closed-circuit installation with a rigour comparable to that of structural filmmakers like Michael Snow — who himself moved between film, video and gallery installation. In gallery practice, the loop, a signature structural-film printing technique, became the standard mode for presenting moving-image work, and rephotography found its analogue in re-scanned and re-recorded video.

Assessment

Scholars treat structural film as a bridge between modernist cinema and contemporary artists' moving image: its insistence that the work's shape be legible in its structure survives in durational video, single-take installation and algorithmic gallery pieces.
