Structural film

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Structural film is a movement in experimental cinema of the 1960s and 1970s in which the film is organized by a predetermined formal system — a fixed camera, a zoom, a loop, a flicker rhythm — so that structure itself becomes the content. The term was introduced by P. Adams Sitney in an essay of 1969. As moving-image practice moved into the gallery in the era of video art, structural film became a key reference: its insistence on duration, repetition, and the apparatus of projection anticipated the looped installations of gallery video.

Characteristics and legacy

Sitney's four devices — fixed camera position, flicker effect, loop printing, and rephotography — define works such as Michael Snow's Wavelength (1967) and La Région Centrale (1971), Hollis Frampton's Zorns Lemma (1970), and the flicker films of Paul Sharits and Tony Conrad. The British structural/materialist school around the London Film-Makers' Co-op went further, treating the film strip and its chemical and optical processes as the work itself.

Later moving-image artists working in video and digital media, from gallery installation makers to contemporary "durational" filmmakers, have repeatedly drawn on structural film's strategies of loops, real-time duration, and systematic form.
