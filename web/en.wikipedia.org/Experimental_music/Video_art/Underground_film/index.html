Underground film

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Underground film is a term for the independent, avant-garde cinema that flourished outside the commercial film industry, principally in the United States in the 1950s and 1960s. The label was popularized by critic Jonas Mekas and the New American Cinema movement, whose filmmakers made low-budget 16 mm works for film societies, lofts, and cooperatives, often in defiance of censorship and of Hollywood form. When video art emerged in the late 1960s, many of its practitioners came from, or defined themselves against, this underground film milieu.

History

Building on the earlier avant-gardes of Maya Deren and Kenneth Anger, the underground embraced personal, poetic, and transgressive filmmaking: Anger's Scorpio Rising (1963), Jack Smith's Flaming Creatures (1963) — repeatedly seized by police — the films of Stan Brakhage, Andy Warhol's Factory productions, and the work of Jonas and Adolfas Mekas. Distribution ran through the Film-Makers' Cooperative (founded 1962) and Anthology Film Archives (1970), while Mekas's "Movie Journal" column in The Village Voice served as the movement's critical voice.

By the 1970s the term gave way to "experimental film" and "avant-garde film," and many underground filmmakers moved toward structural film or into video and gallery installation. The underground's do-it-yourself ethos — cheap cameras, cooperative distribution, disregard for the market — became a lasting template for independent media art.
