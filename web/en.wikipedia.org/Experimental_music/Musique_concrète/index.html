Musique concrète

Musique concrète (French for "concrete music") is a type of music composition that utilizes recorded sounds as raw material. Sounds are often modified through the application of audio effects and tape manipulation techniques, and may be assembled into montage form. It exploits the acousmatic listening experience — sounds heard without seeing their source.

History

The technique was developed around 1948 by Pierre Schaeffer, a French composer and radio engineer working at Radiodiffusion Française in Paris. His first works, the Cinq études de bruits (Five Noise Studies), made from recordings of trains, spinning tops, and percussion, culminated in the Étude aux chemins de fer. In 1949 Pierre Henry joined him, and together they composed Symphonie pour un homme seul (1950). Schaeffer founded the Groupe de Recherche de Musique Concrète (GRMC) in 1951, attracting composers such as Karlheinz Stockhausen, Iannis Xenakis, and Edgard Varèse, whose Poème électronique (1958) grew out of this milieu.

Techniques

Early techniques included cutting and splicing tape, playing recordings at altered speeds, reversing them, looping them into sillons fermés (locked grooves), and filtering. Schaeffer proposed the objet sonore (sound object) as the basic unit of composition and "reduced listening" — attending to a sound's intrinsic qualities apart from its source.

Legacy

Musique concrète laid the groundwork for electronic music studios worldwide, sampling, sound collage, and modern sound design. Its rivalry with the Cologne school of purely synthesized elektronische Musik shaped the early history of electroacoustic music.
