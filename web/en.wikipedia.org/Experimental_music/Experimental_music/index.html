Experimental music

This page is the article on experimental music, reached via a subpage path.

Experimental music is a general label for any music or music genre that pushes existing boundaries and genre definitions. Experimental compositional practice is defined in part by a tendency to incorporate unconventional, or newly invented, instrumental timbres; unusual instruments or forms; and elements of improvisation, chance, and indeterminacy. A common description, following composer Michael Nyman's 1974 book Experimental Music: Cage and Beyond, contrasts experimental music — in which the outcome is not fully determined by the score — with the European avant-garde, in which complex notation fixes a difficult but determinate result.

Experimental performance, exploring new sounds and procedures.

History

Early twentieth-century precursors include Luigi Russolo's Futurist noise intoners (The Art of Noises, 1913), Erik Satie's "furniture music", Edgard Varèse's works for percussion and electronic sound, and Henry Cowell's tone clusters and string piano. In the 1950s, John Cage's chance operations and the prepared piano made indeterminacy central, while in Paris Pierre Schaeffer developed musique concrète from recorded sound, and in Cologne Karlheinz Stockhausen and others pursued purely electronic music.

Practices and influence

The Downtown scene in New York, minimalism (La Monte Young, Terry Riley, Steve Reich, Philip Glass), free improvisation, and experimental rock and electronic scenes (from the Velvet Underground and Captain Beefheart to industrial, noise, and glitch) all intersect with the experimental tradition. Cornelius Cardew's Scratch Orchestra and graphic scores exemplified participatory, open works. The term remains contested: Cage disliked the implication of mere trial without intent, preferring to speak of music whose outcome is unforeseen.
