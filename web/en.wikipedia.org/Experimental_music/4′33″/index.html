ComposerJohn Cage
Composed1952
Premiere29 August 1952, Maverick Concert Hall, Woodstock, New York

MovementsThree (30″, 2′23″, 1′40″)

4′33″ (pronounced "four minutes, thirty-three seconds" or just "four thirty-three") is a three-movement composition by American experimental composer John Cage, written in 1952 for any instrument or combination of instruments. The score instructs the performers not to play their instruments during the entire duration of the piece; instead, the music consists of the sounds of the environment — rain on the roof, audience restlessness, wind in the trees — that the listeners hear while it is performed. It has become one of the most famous and controversial compositions of the twentieth century, and a defining emblem of experimental music.

Premiere and structure

Pianist David Tudor premiered the work on 29 August 1952 at the Maverick Concert Hall in Woodstock, New York, marking the movements by closing and opening the piano lid without striking a single key. The movements last 30 seconds, 2 minutes 23 seconds, and 1 minute 40 seconds; Cage determined the durations using chance operations, and later produced versions with different lengths, including a successor piece, 0′00″ (1962). The original audience, expecting a conventional recital, grew audibly restive — a reaction Cage welcomed as part of the music.

Ideas behind the work

Cage was influenced by his 1951 visit to an anechoic chamber at Harvard, where, in supposedly total silence, he heard two sounds — his nervous system and his blood circulation — concluding that "there is no such thing as silence." Robert Rauschenberg's White Paintings, which Cage called "airports for lights, shadows and particles", provided a visual parallel. Cage also drew on Zen Buddhism: 4′33″ frames listening itself as the content of music, and refuses the distinction between intentional and unintentional sound.

Reception and legacy

Initially dismissed by many as a prank, the piece has been taken increasingly seriously as a philosophical statement about music, attention, and authorship. It has been performed by orchestras and rock musicians, recorded many times, and in 2004 was voted one of the landmark works in BBC and academic polls of twentieth-century music. A 2010 campaign to make a recording of 4′33″ the UK Christmas number one (as "Cage Against the Machine") testified to its continuing cultural afterlife; disputes over royalties for "silent" tracks by other artists have even tested its status in copyright law.
