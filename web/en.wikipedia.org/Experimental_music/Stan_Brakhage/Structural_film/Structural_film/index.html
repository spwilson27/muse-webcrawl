Structural film

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Structural film is a term coined by the critic P. Adams Sitney in 1969 for a mode of experimental filmmaking that emerged in the United States in the 1960s, in which the shape or formal structure of the film as a whole is predetermined and simplified, and that structure becomes the film's primary content. Sitney identified four characteristic devices: a fixed camera position, the flicker effect, loop printing, and rephotography off a screen.

Context

Stan Brakhage, whose lyrical, camera-in-hand work defined an earlier generation of American avant-garde film, represented in many ways the opposite pole from structural film: where Brakhage pursued a personal, visionary cinema of great formal complexity and spontaneity, the structural filmmakers emphasized impersonal systems, duration, and the material properties of the medium itself. Filmmakers associated with the tendency include Michael Snow (Wavelength, 1967), Hollis Frampton (Zorns Lemma, 1970), Ernie Gehr, George Landow, and Paul Sharits.

In Britain the related movement of structural/materialist film, associated with the London Film-Makers' Co-op and theorists such as Peter Gidal, placed still greater emphasis on film's material base and on resisting illusionism and narrative identification.
