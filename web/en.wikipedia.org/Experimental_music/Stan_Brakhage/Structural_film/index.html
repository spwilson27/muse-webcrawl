Structural film

Structural film was a movement in experimental film in the United States in the 1960s and to a lesser extent in Europe and Japan in the 1970s. The term was coined by the critic P. Adams Sitney in 1969 to describe films whose primary quality is their overall shape: the film is structured in advance, often around a single formal device, and its content is minimal and subsidiary to that structure.

Characteristics

Sitney identified four characteristics, rarely all present at once: fixed camera position (or the appearance of it), the flicker effect, loop printing, and rephotography off a screen. Structural films eschew narrative and representation in favour of foregrounding the materiality of the film medium itself.

Key works and filmmakers

Central works include Tony Conrad's The Flicker (1966), composed of alternating black and clear frames; Michael Snow's Wavelength (1967), a 45-minute slow zoom across a loft; Hollis Frampton's Zorns Lemma (1970), organized by alphabetical and cyclical structures; Ernie Gehr's Serene Velocity (1970); and George Landow's (Owen Land's) films. In Britain, the London Film-Makers' Co-op developed a related "structural/materialist" film practice associated with Peter Gidal and Malcolm Le Grice.

Legacy

Structural film is a defining chapter of avant-garde cinema, influencing minimalism in other arts and later moving-image practices. Its films remain staples of courses on experimental film and are preserved by Anthology Film Archives in New York.
