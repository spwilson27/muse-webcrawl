Musique concrète

Tape machines in a Paris studio, where recorded sounds were cut, looped and transformed into compositions.

Musique concrète (French for "concrete music") is a type of electroacoustic music made from recorded sounds — natural environmental sounds, voices, instruments or noises — rather than from abstract musical notation. It was developed in France in the 1940s by composer and engineer Pierre Schaeffer, working at the Studio d'Essai of French national radio in Paris.

Techniques and history

Schaeffer's first études of 1948, such as the Étude aux chemins de fer built from train recordings, exploited the new possibilities of disc and then magnetic-tape recording: sounds could be cut, spliced, looped, reversed, re-pitched and layered. With Pierre Henry and colleagues in the Groupe de Recherche de Musique Concrète, Schaeffer developed the theory of the objet sonore (sound object) and "reduced listening". Works such as the Symphonie pour un homme seul (1950) established the school, whose techniques later influenced electronic music, sampling and hip-hop production.
