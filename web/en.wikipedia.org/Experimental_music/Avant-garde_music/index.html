Avant-garde music

Stylistic originsModernism, Futurism, Dada
Cultural originsEarly 20th century, Europe and the United States
Key figuresEdgard Varèse, John Cage, Karlheinz Stockhausen, Pierre Boulez, Iannis Xenakis, La Monte Young
Related genresExperimental music, musique concrète, minimalism, free improvisation

Avant-garde music is music that pushes against or beyond the boundaries of the established conventions of its time, introducing new sounds, structures, technologies, or conceptions of what music is. The term borrows the military metaphor of the "advance guard" and was applied to radical art in the early twentieth century. It overlaps with, but is usually distinguished from, experimental music: where the avant-garde posits historically forward-looking innovation, experimental music (in Michael Nyman's influential account) emphasises open processes whose outcomes are unforeseeable.

History

Early landmarks include Luigi Russolo's Futurist manifesto The Art of Noises (1913) and his mechanical intonarumori; Erik Satie's furniture music and Dada sound experiments; and Edgard Varèse's scores for percussion and sirens, which pursued the "liberation of sound". After the Second World War, the Darmstadt summer courses became a hub for integral serialism (Boulez, Stockhausen), while in New York the circle around John Cage developed chance and indeterminate music, and Pierre Schaeffer's Paris studio pioneered musique concrète from recorded sound. The 1960s added Fluxus event scores, minimalism, live electronics, and free improvisation, and later decades saw avant-garde approaches migrate into rock, jazz, and electronic dance music.

Characteristics and debates

Common features include noise and extended instrumental techniques, novel notations, new instruments and electronic media, altered tunings and forms, and challenges to the roles of composer, performer, and audience. Critics have charged the avant-garde with hermeticism and institutional self-perpetuation; defenders reply that it keeps the art form's possibilities open. By now much former avant-garde practice — atonality, electronics, prepared piano — has been absorbed into mainstream concert, film, and popular music, raising the question whether a single "advance guard" can still be said to exist.
