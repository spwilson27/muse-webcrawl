Experimental film

Experimental film, experimental cinema, or avant-garde film is a mode of filmmaking that rigorously re-evaluates cinematic conventions and explores non-narrative forms or alternatives to traditional narratives and methods of working. Many experimental films, particularly early ones, relate to arts in other disciplines: painting, dance, literature and poetry, or arise from research and development of new technical resources.

History

The European avant-gardes of the 1920s produced landmarks such as Fernand Léger's Ballet Mécanique (1924), Man Ray's films, Dziga Vertov's Man with a Movie Camera (1929) and Buñuel and Dalí's Un Chien Andalou (1929). In America, Maya Deren's Meshes of the Afternoon (1943) inaugurated a postwar movement carried forward by Kenneth Anger, Stan Brakhage (a leading figure, with works like Dog Star Man and Mothlight), Jonas Mekas, Hollis Frampton and Michael Snow, whose Wavelength (1967) epitomised structural film.

Characteristics

Common traits include non-linear or absent narrative, abstraction, hand-processed or scratched film, non-diegetic sound or silence, loops, found footage, and an emphasis on the materiality of film itself. Experimental works are typically low-budget, self-financed, and distributed through cooperatives such as Canyon Cinema and the Film-Makers' Cooperative rather than commercial channels.

Relation to other arts

Experimental film overlaps with video art, expanded cinema, and installation practice, and has influenced mainstream music videos, title sequences and advertising.
