Aleatoric music

Aleatoric music (also aleatory music or chance music; from the Latin word alea, meaning "dice") is music in which some element of the composition is left to chance, and/or some primary element of a composed work's realisation is left to the determination of its performer(s). The term is most often associated with procedures in which the chance element involves a relatively limited number of possibilities.

Types

Musicologist and composer histories often distinguish three forms: (1) the use of chance procedures to produce a determinate, fixed score — as in John Cage's Music of Changes (1951), composed using the I Ching; (2) "mobile form", where fixed elements can be reordered by the performer, as in works by Earle Brown and Stockhausen's Klavierstück XI; and (3) indeterminate notation (including graphic scores), where the score underdetermines the performance, as in Cornelius Cardew's Treatise.

History

Precursors include the 18th-century Musikalisches Würfelspiel (dice games for composing minuets). In the 1950s Pierre Boulez popularised the term aléatoire while seeking a controlled form of chance, distinguishing his approach from Cage's more radical indeterminacy. Aleatoric devices were widely taken up by Lutosławski (who used controlled aleatorism in orchestral works), Penderecki, and others.
