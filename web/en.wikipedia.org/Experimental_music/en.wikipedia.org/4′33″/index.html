4′33″ (pronounced "four minutes, thirty-three seconds" or just "four thirty-three") is a three-movement composition by American experimental composer John Cage. Composed in 1952, for any instrument or combination of instruments, the score instructs performers not to play their instruments for the entire duration of the piece. The title refers to the total length of the first performance. The content of the composition is not intended to be "four minutes and thirty-three seconds of silence" — as is sometimes assumed — but rather the sounds of the environment heard by the audience during the performance.

Premiere

The work premiered on 29 August 1952 at Maverick Concert Hall in Woodstock, New York, with pianist David Tudor, who indicated the beginning and end of each movement by closing and opening the keyboard lid and timed the piece with a stopwatch. The audience, hearing wind, rain and their own restless murmurs, was largely bewildered.

Background and meaning

Cage was influenced by his experience in an anechoic chamber in 1951 — where he heard his own nervous system and blood circulation, concluding that true silence does not exist — by Robert Rauschenberg's White Paintings, and by Zen Buddhism. The piece became the most famous and controversial of his works and a landmark of experimental music, reframing ambient noise as music.

Legacy

4′33″ has been performed by orchestras and rock musicians alike, was the subject of a 2004 copyright dispute when Mike Batt settled with Cage's publisher after releasing a silent track, and reached the charts in a 2010 UK campaign ("Cage Against the Machine").
