Avant-garde

The avant-garde pushes the boundaries of accepted art.

The avant-garde (from French, "advance guard" or "vanguard") comprises works, artists, and movements that are experimental, radical, or unorthodox with respect to art, culture, politics, or society. It is frequently characterized by aesthetic innovation and initial unacceptability. In music, the term denotes composers and performers who move beyond established practice toward new sounds, structures, and modes of presentation.

Origins of the term

The military metaphor of an advance guard was applied to art in 19th-century France, notably by the utopian socialist Henri de Saint-Simon, who cast artists as the vanguard of social progress. By the early 20th century the term attached itself to movements such as Futurism, Dada, and Surrealism.

Avant-garde music

In music, avant-garde practice has included the atonality and serialism of the Second Viennese School, the noise experiments of Luigi Russolo's The Art of Noises (1913), musique concrète, electronic music, chance and indeterminate procedures, and extended instrumental techniques. Post-war centres such as Darmstadt and the studios of Paris and Cologne institutionalized the search for new musical languages, while later generations—minimalists, spectralists, and free improvisers—defined themselves partly against that orthodoxy.

Criticism

Theorists such as Peter Bürger argued that the historical avant-garde sought to abolish the autonomy of art and reintegrate it into everyday life, an aim he judged to have failed. Others note that yesterday's avant-garde routinely becomes today's establishment, raising the question of whether genuine vanguard practice remains possible within institutions that quickly absorb it.
