Structural film

Structural film was an experimental film movement prominent in the United States in the 1960s and early 1970s. The term was coined by the critic and theorist P. Adams Sitney in a 1969 essay in Film Culture. Unlike the lyrical underground film associated with filmmakers such as Stan Brakhage, structural film insisted on the material properties of the medium itself.

Characteristics

Sitney identified four formal devices, usually present in combination rather than all at once: a fixed camera position (or the appearance of one), the flicker effect, loop printing, and rephotography off a screen. The overall shape of a structural film is predetermined and simplified, and that shape — rather than narrative or expressive content — becomes the primary impression of the work. Works are often long, static, and deliberately anti-illusionistic, foregrounding grain, frame edges, and the mechanics of projection.

Key filmmakers and works

Michael Snow's Wavelength (1967), a 45-minute gradual zoom across a loft, is the movement's canonical work; his Back and Forth (1969) and La Région Centrale (1971) extended its logic. Other central figures include Hollis Frampton (Zorns Lemma, 1970), Tony Conrad (The Flicker, 1966), Paul Sharits, Ernie Gehr (Serene Velocity, 1970), and George Landow (later Owen Land). In Britain, a related "structural/materialist" tendency was articulated by Peter Gidal and the London Film-Makers' Co-op.

Legacy

Structural film reshaped film theory, encouraging analysis of cinema as a temporal and material art, and it influenced later gallery-based moving-image practice and video art.
