Structural film

Structural film foregrounds the medium's formal properties.

Structural film was a movement in experimental cinema prominent in the United States in the 1960s and 1970s. The term was coined by the critic P. Adams Sitney in 1969 to describe films whose overall shape—their structure—takes precedence over narrative or representational content.

Characteristics

Sitney identified four recurring devices, rarely all present in a single work: a fixed camera position, the flicker effect, loop printing, and rephotography off a screen. Structural films emphasize the material properties of cinema—grain, frame, duration, and projection—often demanding sustained attention to minimal change.

Key works

Michael Snow's Wavelength (1967), a 45-minute slow zoom across a New York loft, is the movement's canonical work; his Back and Forth (1969) explored pan movement. Hollis Frampton's Zorns Lemma (1970) organized images through an alphabetic structure, and his (nostalgia) (1971) paired photographs with belated narration. Other practitioners included Ernie Gehr, George Landow (Owen Land), Paul Sharits, and Joyce Wieland.

Structural/materialist film

In Britain, filmmakers around the London Film-Makers' Co-operative, including Peter Gidal and Malcolm Le Grice, developed a related "structural/materialist" practice with a more explicitly political theory of representation. The movement's legacy persists in gallery-based moving-image art and durational cinema.
