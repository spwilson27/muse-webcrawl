Structural film

Structural filmmakers foregrounded the material properties of film itself — grain, flicker, loops and fixed framing.

Structural film was a movement in experimental filmmaking in the United States in the 1960s, named by critic P. Adams Sitney in 1969. Structural films insist on their own formal shape over their content: Sitney identified four characteristics — fixed camera position, the flicker effect, loop printing, and rephotography off a screen — of which a given film typically uses some combination rather than all.

Artists and relation to Stan Brakhage

Key structural filmmakers include Michael Snow (Wavelength, 1967), Hollis Frampton, Tony Conrad (The Flicker, 1966), Paul Sharits and George Landow. The movement is often contrasted with the lyrical, visionary avant-garde cinema of Stan Brakhage, whose hand-painted and densely edited subjective films represented the preceding generation; structural filmmakers such as Snow and Frampton reacted toward a cooler, more conceptual and materialist cinema in which the apparatus itself became the subject.

Structural/materialist film

In Britain, Peter Gidal and the London Film-Makers' Co-op extended the movement as "structural/materialist film", adding an explicitly anti-illusionist political programme.
