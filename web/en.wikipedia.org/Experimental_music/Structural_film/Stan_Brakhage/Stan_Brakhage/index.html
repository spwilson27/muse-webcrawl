Stan Brakhage

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Stanley Brakhage (January 14, 1933 – March 9, 2003), better known as Stan Brakhage, was an American experimental filmmaker. Over a career of more than five decades he made nearly 400 films, ranging from a few seconds to several hours in length, and he is regarded as one of the most important figures in twentieth-century avant-garde cinema. His work is characterized by handheld camerawork, rapid montage, multiple exposures, painting and scratching directly on film stock, and an almost complete rejection of synchronized sound and conventional narrative.

Work and ideas

Brakhage sought to liberate the eye from the conventions of ordinary perception and of commercial cinema, arguing in essays such as "Metaphors on Vision" (1963) for an art of pure visual experience, like the untutored vision of a child. Major works include Window Water Baby Moving (1959), which filmed the birth of his first child; Dog Star Man (1961–64); The Art of Vision (1965); Scenes From Under Childhood (1967–70); and The Text of Light (1974), filmed entirely through a crystal ashtray. In later decades he produced many hand-painted films, including collaborations with composer James Tenney's music in mind, though most of his films are silent.

Although sometimes contrasted with the structural film movement — whose predetermined forms rejected Brakhage's improvisational lyricism — his films were shown in the same underground venues and journals, and his tireless lecturing and writing did much to build the institutional world of American experimental film. He taught at the University of Colorado at Boulder from 1981.
