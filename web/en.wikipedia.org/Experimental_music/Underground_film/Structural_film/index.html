Structural film

Structural film took shape within the underground film scene of 1960s New York.

Structural film was an experimental film movement prominent in North America in the 1960s and 1970s, emerging from the underground film culture of New York screenings, co-ops and artist-run distribution. Critic P. Adams Sitney coined the term in 1969 for films whose overall shape — a fixed frame, a flicker pattern, a loop, or rephotography — is predetermined and takes precedence over narrative content.

Context: the underground film scene

Underground film venues such as the Film-Makers' Cooperative (founded 1962) and Jonas Mekas's screenings provided the infrastructure in which structural filmmakers including Michael Snow, Hollis Frampton, Tony Conrad, Paul Sharits, Ernie Gehr and Joyce Wieland showed their work. Where the earlier underground had celebrated the personal, diaristic cinema of Stan Brakhage and Mekas, structural film pursued an impersonal, materialist investigation of the medium's basic conditions: grain, duration, projection and the flat screen.

Legacy

The movement influenced gallery-based moving-image art and the British structural/materialist school, and its devices — loops, flicker, durational fixed shots — remain staples of artists' film and video.
