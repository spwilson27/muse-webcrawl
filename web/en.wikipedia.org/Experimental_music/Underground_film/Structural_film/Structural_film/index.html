Structural film

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Structural film was a tendency within underground and experimental cinema, named by P. Adams Sitney in 1969, in which a film's overall shape is fixed in advance by a simple system — a single slow zoom, a loop, a flicker pattern, a rephotographed image — and that shape, rather than any story or expressive content, is what the viewer watches. Structural films were a product of the same underground screening culture of cooperatives and lofts that had nurtured the earlier avant-garde, but they reacted against the personal lyricism of filmmakers such as Stan Brakhage, favouring duration, repetition, and the physical facts of film: grain, frame, projector light.

Key works

Michael Snow's Wavelength (1967), a 45-minute zoom across a loft, is the canonical example; his Back and Forth (1969) and La Région Centrale (1971) extended the method. Hollis Frampton's Zorns Lemma (1970) organizes images by an alphabetic system, while Ernie Gehr's Serene Velocity (1970) and Paul Sharits's flicker films explore fixed-camera and frame-rate structures. Tony Conrad's The Flicker (1966) consists almost entirely of alternating black and clear frames.

In Britain, the London Film-Makers' Co-op developed a structural/materialist variant, theorized by Peter Gidal, that insisted on the film strip, chemicals, and projection as the work's real subject.
