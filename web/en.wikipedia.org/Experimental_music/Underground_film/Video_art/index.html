Video art

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Video art is an art form that uses video technology as its medium. It emerged in the 1960s and 1970s when portable video equipment first became available to artists, and it developed partly out of the underground film scene: cheaper and more immediate than film, video allowed real-time feedback, long durations, and manipulation of the electronic signal itself, and it promised a democratic alternative to broadcast television.

Origins

Nam June Paik, who began exhibiting manipulated television sets in 1963 and acquired one of the first portable video recorders in 1965, is widely regarded as the founder of video art; his work with cellist Charlotte Moorman and his later video sculptures and satellite broadcasts made television itself the material of art. Wolf Vostell and the Fluxus circle also used televisions in installations and happenings in the early 1960s. In the United States, artists such as Joan Jonas, Vito Acconci, Bruce Nauman, and Martha Rosler used the new medium for performance documentation, conceptual pieces, and critiques of mass media.

Forms

Video art takes many forms: single-channel tapes shown on a monitor, multi-screen installations, closed-circuit works that incorporate the viewer's live image, and projected gallery pieces. Unlike underground film, which was screened in cinemas and cooperatives, video art was oriented from the start toward galleries, public-access television, and the art market, and from the 1990s large-scale projection made figures such as Bill Viola central to contemporary museum programming.
