Underground film

Underground film is a term for a mode of experimental and independent filmmaking that flourished in the United States in the late 1950s and 1960s, made and shown outside the commercial film industry. The phrase was popularized by the critic Manny Farber and embraced by the New American Cinema movement, whose manifesto of 1960 demanded personal, low-budget filmmaking free of Hollywood formulas.

History

The underground grew out of postwar avant-garde film and the Beat culture of New York and San Francisco. Jonas Mekas, through his "Movie Journal" column in The Village Voice and the journal Film Culture, championed filmmakers including Stan Brakhage, Kenneth Anger, Shirley Clarke, and Andy Warhol. The Film-Makers' Cooperative (founded 1962) and the New York Film Festival's early programs gave the work an institutional base, while screenings in lofts and galleries often courted scandal — films such as Jack Smith's Flaming Creatures (1963) and Jean Genet's Un Chant d'Amour were seized by police, making the underground a flashpoint in censorship battles.

Style

Underground films embraced handheld camerawork, direct address, improvisation, erotic and camp subject matter, and the visible texture of cheap 16mm stock. Warhol's durational works such as Sleep (1963) and Empire (1964) pushed the mode toward the fixed-frame minimalism that critics would soon reclassify as structural film.

Legacy

The underground established the infrastructure — cooperatives, festivals, journals — on which later independent cinema and video art were built, and its do-it-yourself ethos influenced punk and No Wave filmmaking in the 1970s and 1980s.
