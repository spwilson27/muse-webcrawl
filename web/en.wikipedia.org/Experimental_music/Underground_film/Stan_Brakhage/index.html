James Stanley Brakhage (January 14, 1933 – March 9, 2003) was an American filmmaker, regarded as one of the most important figures in twentieth-century experimental film. Working largely within the underground film milieu and almost entirely outside commercial cinema, he made nearly 350 films over five decades, most of them silent and screened without conventional narrative.

Work

Brakhage explored birth, mortality, sexuality and childhood in films such as Window Water Baby Moving (1959), which documented the birth of his first child, and the five-part Dog Star Man (1961–64). His techniques included handheld camerawork, fast cutting, multiple exposures, scratching and painting directly on film stock, and collaging objects onto the film strip, as in Mothlight (1963), made from moth wings and plant matter pressed between tape. His writings, beginning with Metaphors on Vision (1963), argued for an "untutored eye" free of the conventions of Renaissance perspective.

Influence

Brakhage taught for many years at the University of Colorado Boulder, and his work decisively shaped the American avant-garde, even as younger structural filmmakers defined their cooler, more systematic practice partly in reaction against his romantic lyricism.
