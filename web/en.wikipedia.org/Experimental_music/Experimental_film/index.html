Experimental film

Other namesAvant-garde film, underground film
Key figuresMan Ray, Maya Deren, Stan Brakhage, Jonas Mekas, Kenneth Anger
CharacteristicsNon-narrative, abstract, handmade, low-budget

Experimental film, or avant-garde film, is a mode of filmmaking that rigorously re-evaluates cinematic conventions and explores non-narrative forms or alternatives to traditional narratives and methods of working. Many experimental films, particularly early ones, relate to arts in other disciplines: painting, dance, literature and theatre, or arise from research and development of new technical resources.

History

The European avant-garde of the 1920s produced foundational works by artists such as Man Ray, Fernand Léger (Ballet Mécanique, 1924), Hans Richter and Viking Eggeling, who treated film as animated abstract painting. Luis Buñuel and Salvador Dalí's Un Chien Andalou (1929) brought surrealism to the screen. In the United States, Maya Deren's Meshes of the Afternoon (1943) inaugurated a personal, poetic cinema made outside the studio system.

Post-war developments

Stan Brakhage became the most prolific figure of American experimental film, painting and scratching directly on film stock and exploring childbirth, nature and perception in works such as Dog Star Man (1961–64). Jonas Mekas championed the "New American Cinema" and diaristic filmmaking, while the structural film movement of the 1960s–70s (Michael Snow, Hollis Frampton, Peter Gidal) foregrounded the material properties of the medium — grain, flicker, loops and fixed camera setups.

Techniques and exhibition

Experimental filmmakers frequently shoot on 16 mm or Super 8, hand-process footage, use found footage and collage, and reject synchronized sound or conventional editing. Films are often distributed through cooperatives such as Canyon Cinema and the Film-Makers' Cooperative, and screened in galleries, festivals and cinematheques rather than commercial cinemas.
