Aleatoric music

Chance operations, such as dice throws, shape aleatoric works.

Aleatoric music (also aleatory music or chance music; from Latin alea, "dice") is music in which some element of the composition is left to chance, or some primary element of a composed work's realization is left to the determination of its performer(s). The term is most often associated with procedures in which the chance element involves a relatively limited number of possibilities.

History

Early precedents include the Musikalisches Würfelspiel (musical dice games) of the 18th century, attributed to composers including Haydn and Mozart, in which dice selected pre-composed measures. In the 20th century, Charles Ives experimented with elements left to performers, and Henry Cowell coined related usages. The term was popularized in the 1950s by the composer and acoustician Werner Meyer-Eppler, and taken up by Pierre Boulez in his 1957 lecture "Aléa".

Types

John Cage distinguished several approaches: chance-generated music, in which chance operations fix the score (as in his Music of Changes, composed using the I Ching); indeterminate music, in which the score leaves aspects unspecified for the performer; and forms in which pre-composed sections are reordered or combined freely, sometimes called "mobile form", as in Boulez's Third Piano Sonata or Karlheinz Stockhausen's Klavierstück XI.

Open form

Closely related is open form, in which sections of music may be performed in an order chosen by the performer or conductor, a technique explored by Earle Brown and Morton Feldman in graphic and mobile scores.
