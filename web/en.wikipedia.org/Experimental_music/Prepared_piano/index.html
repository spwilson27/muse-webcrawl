Prepared piano

A prepared piano is a piano that has had its sounds temporarily altered by placing bolts, screws, mutes, rubber erasers, or other objects on or between its strings. The result is a percussive, timbrally varied instrument that can evoke a whole percussion ensemble.

History

The technique was pioneered by John Cage. Cage first prepared a piano in 1940 for Bacchanale, written for a dance by Syvilla Fort when the performance space was too small for a percussion ensemble. Earlier experiments included Henry Cowell's "string piano" techniques (playing directly on the strings) and Erik Satie's insertion of paper in Le Piège de Méduse (1913). Cage's best-known work for the instrument is the Sonatas and Interludes (1946–48), a cycle of 16 sonatas and 4 interludes requiring elaborate preparation specified in a detailed chart.

Technique

Objects are chosen and positioned according to the composer's preparation chart, which specifies the string, the material, and the distance from the damper. Different materials produce distinct effects: screws give a metallic, gamelan-like tone; rubber erasers mute and soften; bolts add a rattling resonance. Because the strings' harmonic series is disrupted, the pitch produced by a key may bear little relation to its normal tuning.

Later use

Composers including Lou Harrison, and later performers such as Margaret Leng Tan, have written for prepared piano. The concept influenced extended techniques across instruments and prepared-guitar practices in rock and experimental music.
