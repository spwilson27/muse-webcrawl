John Milton Cage Jr. (September 5, 1912 – August 12, 1992) was an American composer and music theorist. A pioneer of indeterminacy in music, electroacoustic music, and non-standard use of musical instruments, Cage was one of the leading figures of the post-war avant-garde. Critics have lauded him as one of the most influential composers of the 20th century. He was also instrumental, through his long association with choreographer Merce Cunningham, in the development of modern dance.

Chance and the prepared piano

Cage studied with Henry Cowell and briefly with Arnold Schoenberg, who said he had "no feeling for harmony" but was "an inventor — of genius". In 1938 he invented the prepared piano, placing screws, bolts, and rubber between the strings to create a percussive orchestra under one player's hands, heard in his Sonatas and Interludes (1946–48). From 1951, consulting the I Ching to make compositional decisions, he produced works such as Music of Changes and Imaginary Landscape No. 4 for twelve radios, embracing chance so that sounds could "be themselves".

4′33″ and later work

His most famous and controversial work is 4′33″ (1952), in which performers do not play their instruments for four minutes and thirty-three seconds; the "music" is the ambient sound of the environment. Inspired by Zen Buddhism and an anechoic chamber experience, Cage argued there is no such thing as silence. His writings, beginning with Silence (1961), his teaching at Black Mountain College and the New School, and collaborations with Cunningham, Robert Rauschenberg, and David Tudor shaped experimental music, happenings, and Fluxus. Later works used "time brackets", mesostics, and large-scale multimedia events such as HPSCHD (1969).
