Garam masala

Image placeholder: whole "warming" spices — cardamom, cloves, cinnamon, and black pepper

Garam masala (from Hindustani garam, "hot/warm", and masālā, "mixture of spices") is a blend of ground spices originating from the Indian subcontinent. It is common in Indian, Pakistani, Nepalese, Bangladeshi, and Sri Lankan cuisines, and is used alone or with other seasonings. The word garam refers not to chilli heat but to the spices' intensity and, in Ayurvedic tradition, their warming effect on the body.

Ingredients

There is no single recipe; blends vary by region, household, and dish. A typical North Indian garam masala contains black and green cardamom, black peppercorns, cloves, cinnamon or cassia bark, cumin, coriander, mace, and bay leaves. Some versions include star anise, fennel, nutmeg, or dried rose petals; South Indian blends may differ substantially. The whole spices are usually dry-roasted to release their aromas and then ground.

Use

Garam masala is often added towards the end of cooking, or sprinkled over a finished dish, so that its volatile aromas are not lost. It seasons curries, lentil dishes (dal), rice dishes such as biryani and pilaf, kebabs, and snacks, and is an ingredient in dishes popularised worldwide, including chicken tikka masala. Commercial blends are widely sold, though freshly ground homemade mixtures are generally considered superior in fragrance.

Related blends

Related South Asian spice mixtures include chaat masala, tandoori masala, panch phoron, and the Tamil sambar powder; garam masala is also comparable in spirit — though not ingredients — to Chinese five-spice powder and Japanese shichimi.
