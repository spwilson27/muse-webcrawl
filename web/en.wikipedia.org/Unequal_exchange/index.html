Unequal exchange

Unequal exchange is a concept in Marxist economics and dependency theory holding that international trade between developed and developing countries systematically transfers value from the poorer "periphery" to the richer "core", even when trade is formally free and prices are set by competitive markets. The term is associated above all with the Greek-French economist Arghiri Emmanuel, whose book Unequal Exchange: A Study of the Imperialism of Trade appeared in French in 1969 and in English in 1972.

The argument

Emmanuel argued that wages are the independent variable in the world economy: because labour is internationally immobile while capital is mobile, profit rates tend to equalize across countries but wage rates do not. When goods produced with low-wage labour are exchanged for goods produced with high-wage labour at prices of production, more labour time is embodied in the exports of the poor country than it receives back in imports. The exchange is therefore unequal in labour-value terms even though both sides gain in use-value terms compared with not trading at all.

Antecedents and related ideas

The idea has antecedents in the Prebisch–Singer hypothesis about declining terms of trade for primary commodities, and it was taken up by dependency theorists such as Samir Amin, who treated unequal exchange as a mechanism of underdevelopment and accumulation on a world scale. Paul Baran and Paul Sweezy's work on monopoly capital addressed related questions about surplus transfer.

Criticism

Critics object that the theory depends on the labour theory of value, that observed price differences largely reflect productivity differences, and that trade has historically allowed poor countries to industrialize — the experience of East Asian export-led growth is frequently cited. Emmanuel replied that rising exports do not by themselves refute a transfer of value embedded in the terms of trade. The concept continues to be debated in discussions of global value chains and ecologically unequal exchange, which extends the argument to transfers of energy and materials.
