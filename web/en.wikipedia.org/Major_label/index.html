Major label

A major label is one of the dominant record labels that together control the majority of the global recorded-music market, in contrast to independent ("indie") labels. Since 2012 the industry has been dominated by three majors — the "Big Three": Universal Music Group, Sony Music Entertainment, and Warner Music Group — which together account for roughly two-thirds or more of worldwide recorded-music revenues.

History

Through the late 20th century the market was shared among six majors (EMI, CBS/Sony, BMG, PolyGram, Warner, and MCA/Universal). A wave of mergers — PolyGram into Universal (1998), BMG into Sony (2004–2008), and EMI's recorded-music arm into Universal (2012) — consolidated the industry to three. Each major owns a portfolio of subsidiary labels (such as Interscope, Republic, Columbia, RCA, Atlantic, and Parlophone) and operates its own distribution and publishing arms.

Role and criticism

Majors offer artists large advances, global marketing, playlist and radio promotion, and worldwide distribution, historically in exchange for broad rights in recordings and a large share of revenue. The rise of digital distribution and streaming has lowered barriers for independent artists, and majors have responded by acquiring distributors and offering "label services" deals. Critics argue the majors' market power shapes popular culture conservatively and disadvantages artists; defenders point to the scale of investment and risk involved in developing global acts.
