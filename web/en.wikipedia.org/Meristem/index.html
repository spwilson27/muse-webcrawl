Meristem

A meristem is a region of plant tissue consisting of undifferentiated, actively dividing cells (meristematic cells) capable of producing new organs throughout the plant's life. Meristems give rise to all the differentiated tissues of the plant and allow the indeterminate growth characteristic of plants, in contrast to the largely fixed body plan of animals. Their activity is controlled by plant hormones — auxin, cytokinin, gibberellins — and by gene networks involving transcription factors such as WUSCHEL and CLAVATA.

Types

Apical meristems

Found at the tips of roots and shoots, apical meristems drive primary growth — the lengthening of the plant body. The shoot apical meristem produces leaves, stems and eventually flowers, organised into layered tunica and corpus zones; the root apical meristem lies behind a protective root cap and includes a quiescent centre of rarely dividing cells surrounded by initials. Apical cells in mosses, ferns and related plants take the form of a single tetrahedral dividing cell.

Lateral meristems

The vascular cambium and cork cambium are cylinders of dividing cells that produce secondary growth — secondary xylem (wood) inward and secondary phloem outward, and protective bark — thickening stems and roots in woody plants. Grass stems also contain intercalary meristems at the bases of internodes and leaf blades, allowing regrowth after grazing or mowing.

Development and totipotency

Meristem cells are small, thin-walled and densely cytoplasmic. When floral signals arrive, the shoot apical meristem converts to an inflorescence and then floral meristems, whose identity is specified by ABC-model genes. Because meristematic cells are totipotent, they are widely exploited in plant tissue culture and micropropagation: a fragment of meristem can regenerate a whole plant, and meristem culture is used to eliminate viruses from valuable cultivars of potato, orchids and fruit trees.
