The Kazakh Khanate (Kazakh: Qazaq handyğy) was a Kazakh state in Central Asia, successor to the Golden Horde, that existed from 1465 to 1847. At its height under Kasym Khan in the early 16th century it controlled much of the steppe from the Caspian Sea to the Altai Mountains and was home to perhaps a million people; it is considered the foundation of modern Kazakh statehood.

Origins

In the 1460s the sultans Kerei and Janibek, descendants of Jochi, broke away from the Uzbek khan Abu'l-Khayr and led their followers east into Moghulistan, where the local khan granted them lands in the Chu valley. After Abu'l-Khayr's death in 1468, many of his subjects joined them, and the name Qazaq — "free wanderer" or "adventurer" — attached to the new polity. Under Burunduk Khan and then Kasym Khan (r. 1511–1518), the khanate expanded across the steppe and took the Syr Darya towns.

Expansion and division

Khaks under Khak-Nazar (1538–1580) and Tauekel (1582–1598) renewed expansion, briefly taking Tashkent and fighting Bukhara. In the early 18th century the khanate fragmented into three zhuzes (hordes): the Senior, Middle, and Junior Zhuzes, each with its own khans. From the 1720s, invasions by the Dzungar Khanate — the years of disaster remembered as Aktaban Shubyryndy — drove the Kazakhs westward and pushed the zhuzes to seek Russian protection.

Absorption into Russia

Abul Khair of the Junior Zhuz took an oath of allegiance to Russia in 1731, and through the 18th and 19th centuries the Russian Empire progressively abolished the khans' authority, imposing administrative statutes (1822–1824) on the Middle and Junior Zhuzes. The last major resistance, the revolt of Kenesary Kasymov (1837–1847), ended with his death, closing the history of the khanate.

Society and legacy

Khanate society was nomadic and clan-based, governed by khans advised by biys (judges) applying customary law, codified in the Zheti Zhargy code associated with Tauke Khan. Modern Kazakhstan celebrated the 550th anniversary of the khanate in 2015 as a symbol of national statehood.
