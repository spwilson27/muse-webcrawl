Reliquary

A reliquary (also referred to as a shrine, by the French term châsse, and the Latin term feretrum when used for a portable shrine) is a container for relics. They may be the physical remains of saints, such as bones, pieces of clothing, or some object associated with them. Reliquaries were common in Christianity, especially during the Middle Ages, and also appear in Buddhism, Hinduism, and other traditions.

A medieval châsse reliquary, typically decorated with Limoges enamel.

Christian reliquaries

In Christianity, relics have usually been considered important devotional objects since the early centuries of the faith. Early reliquaries took the form of caskets, often modelled on sarcophagi, while later examples included monstrance-like vessels in which the relic could be displayed behind glass or rock crystal. A particularly distinctive medieval form was the "speaking reliquary," shaped like the body part it contained — a head, arm, or foot — usually fashioned from precious metal over a wooden core.

Among the most famous reliquaries are the Shrine of the Three Kings in Cologne Cathedral, said to contain the relics of the Biblical Magi; the Sainte-Chapelle in Paris, built by Louis IX to house the Crown of Thorns; and the châsses of Limoges enamel made in southwestern France. During the Protestant Reformation, many reliquaries in northern Europe were destroyed or melted down, and the veneration of relics was criticized by reformers such as John Calvin.

Buddhist reliquaries

In Buddhism, reliquaries called stupas enshrine the cremated remains of the Buddha or of revered monks. Relics of the Buddha were, according to tradition, divided into eight portions after his death and distributed among stupas across northern India. Smaller portable reliquaries, often of crystal or gold, are found throughout the Buddhist world.
