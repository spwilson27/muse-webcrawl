Distributism

Distributism is an economic theory asserting that the world's productive assets should be widely owned rather than concentrated. It was developed in the early twentieth century in Europe by Catholic thinkers, principally Hilaire Belloc and G. K. Chesterton, drawing on the principles of Catholic social teaching, especially Pope Leo XIII's encyclical Rerum novarum (1891) and Pope Pius XI's Quadragesimo anno (1931).

Overview

Distributists hold that widespread private ownership of the means of production — land, tools, and capital — is essential to human freedom and dignity. They contrast this both with capitalism, which they say tends to concentrate ownership in a few hands and reduce most people to wage labour, and with socialism, which concentrates ownership and control in the state. Belloc set out the argument in The Servile State (1912) and An Essay on the Restoration of Property (1936); Chesterton popularized it in works such as What's Wrong with the World (1910) and The Outline of Sanity (1926).

Key principles

  • Subsidiarity — matters ought to be handled by the smallest, lowest or least centralized competent authority.
  • Wide ownership — families should own enough productive property to support themselves independently.
  • Guilds and cooperatives — small producers should organize in guilds or cooperatives rather than be absorbed into large corporations.
  • Preference for the local and small-scale, including small farms, workshops and shops, encouraged through taxation and anti-monopoly policy.

Influence

Distributism influenced the Catholic social tradition and, through E. F. Schumacher's Small Is Beautiful (1973), later movements for localism and appropriate technology. The Distributist League flourished in Britain between the wars, and the ideas saw revivals in the twentieth and twenty-first centuries among agrarians, communitarians and critics of both corporate and state concentration of economic power. Worker cooperatives such as those of the Mondragon Corporation are sometimes cited as real-world approximations of distributist practice.

Criticism

Critics argue that distributism lacks a workable programme for an industrial economy, romanticises pre-industrial life, and does not explain how widespread small ownership could be restored or maintained against economies of scale without heavy state intervention of the kind the theory itself distrusts.
