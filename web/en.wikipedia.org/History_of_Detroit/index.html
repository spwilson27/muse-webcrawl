History of Detroit

The history of Detroit spans more than three centuries, from a French colonial fur-trading post to the centre of the American automobile industry and, later, to the largest municipal bankruptcy in United States history and a period of attempted revival.

Founding and early history

Detroit was founded in 1701 by Antoine de la Mothe Cadillac, who established Fort Pontchartrain du Détroit on the river strait (détroit) between lakes Erie and St. Clair. The settlement passed to Britain after the French and Indian War and to the United States after the American Revolution. A fire in 1805 destroyed nearly the whole town, prompting the motto still on the city's flag: Speramus Meliora; Resurget Cineribus — "We hope for better things; it will rise from the ashes."

The Motor City

In the early 20th century Detroit became the capital of the automobile age. Henry Ford's moving assembly line at Highland Park (1913) and the five-dollar day made mass car ownership possible, and the "Big Three" — Ford, General Motors, and Chrysler — drew hundreds of thousands of workers, including African Americans in the Great Migration. The population peaked at about 1.85 million in 1950, then declined with suburbanisation, racial conflict (notably the 1967 uprising), and the restructuring of the auto industry. In 2013 Detroit filed for Chapter 9 bankruptcy, the largest such filing by a U.S. city, emerging in 2014 amid programmes of blight removal and downtown reinvestment.
