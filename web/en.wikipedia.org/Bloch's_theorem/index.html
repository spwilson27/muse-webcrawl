Bloch's theorem

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In condensed matter physics, Bloch's theorem states that solutions to the Schrödinger equation in a periodic potential can be written as a plane wave modulated by a function with the same periodicity as the potential. It is named after the physicist Felix Bloch, who proved it in 1929.

Statement

For a potential U(r) periodic over a Bravais lattice, the eigenfunctions of the Hamiltonian can be chosen in the form

ψnk(r) = eik·r unk(r),

where unk(r) has the periodicity of the lattice: u(r + R) = u(r) for every lattice vector R. Such solutions are called Bloch functions or Bloch states; k is the crystal momentum and n the band index.

Consequences

Bloch's theorem is the foundation of the electronic band theory of solids. It implies that electrons in a perfect crystal propagate without scattering from the periodic lattice itself, and it leads directly to the concepts of energy bands and band gaps that distinguish metals, semiconductors, and insulators.

Related results

The theorem is a special case of Floquet theory for spatially periodic systems. Bloch's theorem for waves in photonic and phononic crystals follows the same mathematics and underpins modern metamaterials.
