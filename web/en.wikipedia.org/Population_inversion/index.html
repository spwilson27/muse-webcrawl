Population inversion

A population inversion is a non-equilibrium distribution of atoms, ions, or molecules in which more particles occupy a higher energy state than a lower one. It reverses the normal Boltzmann distribution, in which lower states are always more populated at thermal equilibrium. Population inversion is the essential precondition for stimulated emission to dominate over absorption, and therefore for the operation of lasers and masers.

How inversion is achieved

Because a two-level system illuminated by radiation can at best reach equal populations in its two states, practical lasers use three-level or four-level schemes. Energy is supplied by "pumping" — optical pumping with flashlamps or other lasers, electrical discharge, chemical reactions, or current injection in semiconductor devices. In a three-level system such as the original ruby laser, atoms are pumped to a high level, decay rapidly to a metastable upper laser level, and accumulate there until they outnumber the ground state. Four-level systems, such as Nd:YAG, are more efficient because the lower laser level empties quickly, so only a small excited population is needed for inversion.

Consequences and applications

In an inverted medium, a passing photon of the right energy is more likely to trigger stimulated emission — producing a second, identical photon — than to be absorbed. The medium thus provides optical gain, and placed inside a resonator it sustains laser oscillation. Quantitatively, inversion and gain are described by the Einstein coefficients and, in semiconductor lasers, by the Bernard–Duraffourg condition relating quasi-Fermi levels to the band gap.
