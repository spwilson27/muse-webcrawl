Ode

An ode (from Ancient Greek: ᾠδή, romanized: ōidḗ, lit. 'song') is a type of lyric poetry. Odes are elaborately structured poems praising or glorifying an event or individual, describing nature intellectually as well as emotionally. A classic ode is structured in three sections: the strophe, the antistrophe, and the epode.

History

The form originates in ancient Greece. The choral odes of Pindar celebrated victors in the Panhellenic games and were performed with music and dance, while the monodic odes of Sappho and Alcaeus were sung by a single voice. The Roman poet Horace adapted the ode into Latin in comparatively regular stanzas, a model that dominated later European practice.

Forms in English poetry

English odes are commonly divided into the Pindaric (or regular) ode, the Horatian ode, and the irregular ode, in which the poet disregards fixed stanza patterns. Famous examples include John Dryden's Alexander's Feast, Wordsworth's "Ode: Intimations of Immortality", and John Keats's great odes of 1819, including "Ode to a Nightingale" and "Ode on a Grecian Urn", which use the form for concentrated meditation on art, nature and mortality.

Characteristics

Odes typically address their subject directly in an elevated tone, combine praise with reflection, and favour elaborate diction and imagery. Although the form declined in popularity in the twentieth century, poets continue to write odes, including ironic and everyday variations on the classical model.
