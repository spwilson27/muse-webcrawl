Mountain railway

A mountain railway is a railway that operates in mountainous terrain, climbing steep gradients by adhesion, by rack-and-pinion drive, or by cable haulage. Such lines were built to open alpine valleys, reach mines and resorts, and carry trunk routes across great ranges, and they are remarkable engineering works of tunnels, spirals, switchbacks and viaducts.

Technologies

Ordinary adhesion railways can manage gradients of roughly 2.5–4 percent, extended by spirals and zig zags, as on the Semmering (1854, the first true mountain main line), the Gotthard and India's Darjeeling and Nilgiri lines. For steeper ascents the rack railway — pioneered on the Mount Washington Cog Railway (1869) and the Rigi (1871) — uses a toothed rail engaged by cog wheels, climbing grades beyond 20 percent. Funiculars haul cars by cable on the steepest urban and summit inclines. Three mountain railways of India and several Alpine lines are UNESCO World Heritage Sites.

Notable lines

  • The Bernina and Albula lines of the Rhaetian Railway in Switzerland, famed for the Landwasser Viaduct and Brusio spiral viaduct
  • The Flåm Railway in Norway, descending 866 metres in 20 kilometres
  • The Qinghai–Tibet railway, the world's highest line, crossing the Tanggula Pass at 5,072 metres on permafrost
  • The Pikes Peak and Mount Washington cog railways in the United States
  • The Jungfrau Railway, mostly in tunnel inside the Eiger and Mönch, reaching Europe's highest station at 3,454 metres
