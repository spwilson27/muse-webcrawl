The Factory was the name given to a series of studios in New York City used by the American artist Andy Warhol between 1962 and 1984. Part art studio, part social scene, the Factory became the hub of Warhol's production of silkscreen paintings and experimental films, and a famous gathering place for artists, musicians, and the entourage known as the Warhol Superstars.

History

The original Factory occupied the fifth floor of 231 East 47th Street in Midtown Manhattan from 1963 to 1967. Decorated by Billy Name with silver paint, tin foil, and mirrors, it became known as the Silver Factory. There Warhol and his assistants mass-produced silkscreened images of celebrities and consumer products, embracing an industrial model of art-making reflected in the studio's name.

In 1968 the Factory moved to the sixth floor of the Decker Building at 33 Union Square West. That June, Warhol was shot and seriously wounded there by Valerie Solanas, a radical feminist writer who had appeared in one of his films. After the shooting, access to the Factory became tightly controlled and its atmosphere more businesslike. Later locations included 860 Broadway and, finally, 22 East 33rd Street.

Films and people

The Factory produced hundreds of Warhol's underground films, including Sleep (1963), Empire (1964), Chelsea Girls (1966), and the films later directed by Paul Morrissey. Its regulars included Edie Sedgwick, Nico, Lou Reed and the Velvet Underground, whose debut album Warhol produced and whose performances were staged in his Exploding Plastic Inevitable shows.

Legacy

The Factory blurred the boundaries between studio, salon, film set, and nightclub, and it remains a symbol of 1960s New York bohemia and of Warhol's redefinition of the artist as impresario and brand.
