Lajos Kossuth de Udvard et Kossuthfalva (19 September 1802 – 20 March 1894) was a Hungarian nobleman, lawyer, journalist, politician and statesman who was Governor-President of the Kingdom of Hungary during the revolution and war of independence of 1848–49. A spellbinding orator, he became the symbol of Hungarian national aspirations and one of the most famous European revolutionaries of the nineteenth century.

Early career

Born into a minor noble family of Slovak origin in Monok, Kossuth studied law and entered politics as a deputy to the Diet. His handwritten parliamentary reports — which evaded press censorship by circulating as letters — made his reputation and also brought imprisonment in 1837–40. After his release he edited the Pesti Hírlap, advocating reform, the abolition of serfdom and Hungarian economic independence from Austria.

Revolution of 1848

As finance minister in the first responsible Hungarian government of Lajos Batthyány, Kossuth organised the national defence after the Habsburg counter-revolution began. His recruiting speeches raised a Honvéd army almost from nothing. Following the dethronement of the Habsburgs in the Hungarian Declaration of Independence of 14 April 1849, Kossuth became head of state as Governor-President. Despite victories in the spring campaign of 1849, Hungary was crushed when Tsar Nicholas I sent a large Russian army to Austria's aid; Kossuth resigned in August 1849 and fled into exile.

Exile and legacy

Kossuth was interned by the Ottoman Empire, then released following British and American pressure. His tour of the United States in 1851–52 drew enormous crowds — he addressed Congress and remains one of only a handful of foreign statesmen honoured with a statue in the U.S. Capitol. He spent his final decades in Turin, refusing amnesty because he would not accept the Austro-Hungarian Compromise of 1867. When his body was returned to Budapest in 1894, his funeral became a vast national demonstration. Kossuth is remembered as a founding figure of modern Hungary; the national anthem's companion song, the Kossuth Lajos azt üzente, and Budapest's Kossuth Square keep his memory central to Hungarian identity.
