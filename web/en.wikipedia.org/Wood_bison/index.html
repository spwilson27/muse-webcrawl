Wood bison

The wood bison (Bison bison athabascae) is a subspecies of the American bison native to the boreal forests of northern Canada and Alaska. It is the largest land animal in North America: bulls can weigh over 900 kilograms (2,000 lb), noticeably heavier and darker than the plains bison, with a more squared, towering hump and reduced beard and cape.

Scientific nameBison bison athabascae
RangeNorthern Alberta, British Columbia, Northwest Territories, Yukon; reintroduced in Alaska
MassBulls up to about 900–1,000 kg
StatusNear Threatened (recovered from a few hundred animals)

Characteristics and habitat

Wood bison inhabit meadows, sedge sloughs, and open boreal forest in the Mackenzie and Peace–Athabasca regions. Compared with plains bison, they have a darker, woolier coat, horns that rise above the hair, and the highest point of the hump set farther forward over the front legs.

Decline and recovery

Once numbering perhaps 168,000, wood bison were reduced by hide hunting to a remnant of roughly 250 animals in the remote country that became Wood Buffalo National Park (established 1922). Controversially, thousands of plains bison were shipped north in the 1920s, bringing diseases (tuberculosis and brucellosis) and hybridization. Later conservation herds were founded from disease-free stock, including the Mackenzie Bison Sanctuary herd, and wood bison were reintroduced to Alaska beginning in 2015. Several thousand now live in free-ranging herds, and the subspecies is listed as threatened in Canada but has improved to Near Threatened globally.
