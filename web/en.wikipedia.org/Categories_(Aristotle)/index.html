Categories (Aristotle)

The Categories (Greek: Κατηγορίαι, Katēgoriai) is a short treatise by Aristotle that opens the Organon, the collection of his logical works. It classifies everything that can be said or thought into ten categories — the highest kinds or genera of being — and investigates the relations between terms, laying groundwork for Aristotle's logic and metaphysics.

The ten categories

Aristotle lists ten ways in which things can be predicated: substance (e.g. man, horse), quantity (four cubits long), quality (white, grammatical), relation (double, greater), place (in the marketplace), time (yesterday), position (sitting, lying), possession (shod, armed), action (cutting, burning) and passion (being cut, being burned). Substance is primary: all other categories inhere in or are said of substances.

Content

The treatise opens with definitions of homonyms, synonyms and paronyms, then presents the categories and the rules for how terms combine. Its final chapters, the so-called postpredicaments, discuss opposition, priority, simultaneity and kinds of motion. Distinctions drawn here — between what is "said of" a subject and what is "present in" a subject — structure Aristotle's theory of primary and secondary substance in both the Categories and the Metaphysics.

Influence

Through Porphyry's Isagoge and Boethius's Latin translations and commentaries, the Categories became the starting point of logic teaching in late antiquity and the medieval schools, and provoked the long-running debate over universals. Its scheme of categories was a principal target and model for Kant's later table of categories.
