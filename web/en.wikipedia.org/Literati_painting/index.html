Literati painting

Literati painting (Chinese: 文人畫, wénrénhuà) is a style of Chinese painting practiced by scholar-officials and literati rather than professional court painters. Rather than aiming at polished realism, literati artists valued personal expression, calligraphic brushwork, and intellectual cultivation, treating painting as an extension of poetry and calligraphy — the "Three Perfections."

Origins

The ideal was articulated in the Song dynasty by Su Shi (1037–1101), who held that a true painter grasps the inner spirit of things rather than their outward likeness, and that painting and poetry are fundamentally one. Literati painting matured in the Yuan dynasty, when scholar-artists such as Zhao Mengfu and the Four Masters of the Yuan — Huang Gongwang, Wu Zhen, Ni Zan, and Wang Meng — withdrew from Mongol rule and developed deliberately austere, archaic styles.

Characteristics

  • Primacy of brushwork: calligraphic strokes reveal the artist's character and cultivation.
  • Limited palette: ink monochrome (sometimes with light color) over the mineral colors of professional painting.
  • Integration of poem, inscription, and seals directly onto the picture surface.
  • Subjects of personal resonance: bamboo, plum, orchid, old pines, and empty landscapes.
  • Deliberate "clumsiness" or amateurism as a mark of sincerity, in contrast to academic finish.

Ming dynasty and the Wu School

In the Ming dynasty, Dong Qichang (1555–1636) codified the theory of the Northern and Southern Schools, exalting the literati (Southern) lineage. The Wu School, centered on Suzhou and including Shen Zhou and Wen Zhengming, dominated literati taste. Critics later faulted Dong's canon-making for narrowing the tradition, but his framework shaped Chinese art historiography for centuries.

Influence

The literati ideal spread to Korea (as muninhwa) and Japan (as bunjinga or Nanga), where it flourished in the Edo period. Its emphasis on the artist's personality anticipated aspects of modern expressive art, and scholar-painters such as Shitao and Bada Shanren pushed its expressive possibilities to extremes in the early Qing dynasty.
