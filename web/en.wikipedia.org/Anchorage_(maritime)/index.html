Anchorage (maritime)

An anchorage is an area at sea, in a harbour approaches zone, or in a river where vessels may lie at anchor in relative safety, usually while waiting for a berth, for orders, for favourable weather or tide, or to carry out transfers of cargo and stores. Anchorages are shown on nautical charts, often marked with an anchor symbol, and may be assigned or regulated by a port authority or vessel traffic service.

Characteristics of a good anchorage

A suitable anchorage offers adequate depth for the vessel's draft at all states of the tide, shelter from prevailing wind and swell, and a seabed that provides good holding ground — mud and clay hold an anchor well, while rock and coral give poor holding. Sufficient swinging room must exist so that vessels riding to wind and tide do not foul one another.

Regulation and practice

Large ports designate numbered anchorage areas for different classes of traffic: deep-draft anchorages, quarantine anchorages where vessels await health clearance, and explosives anchorages set apart for dangerous cargo. Vessels at anchor must display the prescribed lights and shapes under the COLREGs and keep an anchor watch. Famous natural anchorages include Spithead off Portsmouth, the Downs off Kent, and Scapa Flow in Orkney.

Anchoring versus mooring

Lying at anchor relies on the vessel's own ground tackle, whereas a mooring uses permanent equipment fixed to the seabed. Anchorages allow flexibility but demand vigilance against dragging in heavy weather, when a vessel may need to weigh anchor and put to sea.
