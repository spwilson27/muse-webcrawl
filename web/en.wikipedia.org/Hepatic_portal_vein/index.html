Hepatic portal vein

SourceSplenic vein, superior mesenteric vein, inferior mesenteric vein, pancreatic vein
Drains toLiver sinusoids
LengthAbout 8 cm (adult)

The hepatic portal vein is a blood vessel that carries blood from the gastrointestinal tract, gallbladder, pancreas, and spleen to the liver. This blood contains nutrients and toxins extracted from digested contents, which the liver processes before the blood returns to the heart via the hepatic veins and inferior vena cava.

Structure

In adults the portal vein is about 8 cm long. It forms behind the neck of the pancreas by the union of the superior mesenteric vein and the splenic vein, and ascends behind the first part of the duodenum to the porta hepatis, where it divides into right and left branches supplying the two lobes of the liver. The inferior mesenteric vein usually joins the splenic vein or the junction of the two main tributaries.

Function

The portal vein supplies roughly 75 percent of the liver's blood flow and about half of its oxygen. Because blood from the intestines passes through the liver first — the "first pass" — hepatocytes can store nutrients, detoxify harmful substances, and process drugs before they reach the systemic circulation. The portal system is a classic example of a venous portal system, in which blood passes through two capillary beds in series.

Clinical significance

Obstruction or thrombosis of the portal vein, often a consequence of cirrhosis, raises pressure in the portal system (portal hypertension). This can produce splenomegaly, ascites, and dangerous portosystemic collateral vessels such as esophageal varices. Portal vein thrombosis may also complicate pancreatitis, abdominal infection, and hypercoagulable states.
