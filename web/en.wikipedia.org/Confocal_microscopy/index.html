Confocal microscopy

Confocal microscopy, most frequently confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM), is an optical imaging technique for increasing optical resolution and contrast of a micrograph by means of a spatial pinhole to block out-of-focus light in image formation. By scanning a focused point of light across a specimen and collecting only the in-focus fluorescence through the pinhole, the instrument builds an image point by point, and can assemble stacks of optical sections into three-dimensional reconstructions.

History

The principle was patented by Marvin Minsky in 1957, but practical instruments awaited the laser and the computer. In the 1980s, commercial laser scanning confocal microscopes made the technique a standard tool of cell biology, typically combined with fluorescent labelling of specific structures.

Variants and applications

Variants include spinning-disk (Nipkow disk) confocal microscopy, which uses many pinholes for faster, gentler live-cell imaging, and two-photon excitation microscopy, which achieves similar optical sectioning using infrared light. Confocal microscopy is used in neurobiology, developmental biology, materials science, and ophthalmology (confocal scanning laser ophthalmoscopy).
