Multiverse

The multiverse is the hypothetical set of all universes, including the one in which we live. Together, these universes comprise everything that exists: the entirety of space, time, matter, energy, information, and the physical laws and constants that describe them. The different universes within the multiverse are called "parallel universes," "flat universes," "other universes," "alternate universes," "many universes," "planiverse," "parent universes," "child universes," "bubble universes," or "bubble universes," depending on the model.

Scientific models

Several theories in physics suggest multiverse structures. Eternal inflation in cosmology produces an endless mosaic of bubble universes with possibly different constants; the string theory landscape suggests some 10500 possible vacuum states. Hugh Everett's many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics implies branching universes for every quantum outcome. Max Tegmark proposed a taxonomy of four levels of multiverse, from regions beyond our cosmic horizon to entirely different mathematical structures.

Debate

Multiverse proposals are controversial because they are difficult or impossible to test directly. Proponents argue they can explain the apparent fine-tuning of physical constants via selection effects; critics, including some prominent cosmologists, argue that untestable universes fall outside science. The term "multiverse" was coined by philosopher William James in 1895 and popularized in fiction by Michael Moorcock and later writers, and fictional parallel worlds long predate the physics usage (see alternate history).
