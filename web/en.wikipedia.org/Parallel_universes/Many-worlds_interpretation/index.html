Many-worlds interpretation

The many-worlds interpretation (MWI) is an interpretation of quantum mechanics that asserts that the universal wavefunction is objectively real and that there is no wavefunction collapse. In this view, all possible outcomes of quantum measurements are realized in a vast, continually branching ensemble of universes — a multiverse — in which quantum effects spawn decohered branches that do not interact.

Origin

The interpretation was formulated by Hugh Everett III in his 1957 Princeton doctoral thesis, supervised by John Archibald Wheeler, as the "relative state" formulation. Everett proposed that measurement is simply an interaction between quantum systems that correlates them, with observers themselves described by the wavefunction. Bryce DeWitt popularized the theory in the 1960s and 1970s and coined the name "many-worlds."

Key ideas

Decoherence — the irreversible spread of quantum correlations into the environment — explains why branches appear classical and why observers in different branches cannot detect one another. Because every outcome occurs, probability must be recovered from decision-theoretic or symmetry arguments, a task pursued by David Deutsch, David Wallace, and others. Critics question the ontology's extravagance and the derivation of the Born rule; supporters value the interpretation's determinism and its removal of any special role for measurement or observers.

Relation to other interpretations

Unlike the Copenhagen interpretation or objective-collapse theories (such as GRW), many-worlds adds no new equations and modifies none. Polls of physicists show it has a substantial minority of adherents, particularly among quantum cosmologists.
