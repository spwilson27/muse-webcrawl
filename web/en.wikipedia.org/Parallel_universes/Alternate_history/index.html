Alternate history

Alternate history (also called alternative history, allohistory, or uchronia) is a genre of speculative fiction in which one or more historical events occur differently from how they unfolded in reality. Most works in the genre posit a "point of divergence" — a moment at which history branches from the real timeline — and explore the consequences for individuals and societies.

History of the genre

Early examples include Louis-Napoléon Geoffroy's 1836 novel Napoléon et la conquête du monde, imagining Napoleon's victory, and the 1895 British collection When William Came is another invasion-scare precursor. The genre became a distinct field within science fiction in the twentieth century with works such as L. Sprague de Camp's Lest Darkness Fall (1939) and Ward Moore's Bring the Jubilee (1953). Philip K. Dick's The Man in the High Castle (1962), which imagines an Axis victory in the Second World War, won the Hugo Award and remains one of the best-known examples.

Themes and notable works

Popular points of divergence include the American Civil War, the two World Wars, and major assassinations or accidents. Harry Turtledove became the genre's most prolific modern author, writing series such as the Southern Victory sequence. Alternate history overlaps with counterfactual history as practiced by historians, though academic counterfactuals aim at analyzing real causation rather than storytelling. The genre frequently uses the conceit of parallel universes, sometimes linking its timelines through travel or communication between worlds.
