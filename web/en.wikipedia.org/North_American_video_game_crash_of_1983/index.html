North American video game crash of 1983

The video game crash of 1983 was a large-scale recession in the video game industry that occurred from 1983 to 1985, primarily in the United States and Canada. The crash, which bankrupted several companies that produced home computers and video game consoles, is attributed to market saturation, a flood of low-quality games, and growing competition from home computers.

Causes

By 1982 the market was flooded with consoles and with games from new third-party publishers. Atari, which had lost a court battle to prevent third-party development, could not control quality or quantity. Retailers over-ordered during the boom, and when demand slowed they returned unsold stock. Two high-profile failures damaged confidence: the hastily produced Pac-Man port for the Atari 2600 and the E.T. the Extra-Terrestrial game, millions of copies of which went unsold; Atari famously buried truckloads of unsold cartridges in a New Mexico landfill, a fact confirmed by a 2014 excavation.

Effects

Industry revenues fell from a peak of about $3.2 billion in 1982 to around $100 million by 1985, a decline of about 97 percent. Magnavox exited the console business, and numerous third-party developers folded. Retailers became wary of devoting shelf space to video games.

Recovery

The North American console market was revived in 1985 by the launch of the Nintendo Entertainment System, which Nintendo marketed cautiously as an "entertainment system" with a quality-control seal and a licensing regime designed to prevent a repeat of the glut. The industry subsequently shifted its center of gravity toward Japanese manufacturers.
