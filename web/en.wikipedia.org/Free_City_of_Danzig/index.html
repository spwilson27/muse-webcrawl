Free City of Danzig

The Free City of Danzig (German: Freie Stadt Danzig; Polish: Wolne Miasto Gdańsk) was a semi-autonomous city-state that existed between 1920 and 1939, established under the Treaty of Versailles following the end of World War I. It consisted of the Baltic Sea port of Danzig (now Gdańsk, Poland) and nearly 200 surrounding villages and towns, covering about 1,966 square kilometres (759 square miles). The Free City was placed under the protection of the League of Nations, while Poland retained control of its foreign relations, customs union, railway, and postal services.

History

The Free City was proclaimed on November 15, 1920. Its population of roughly 400,000 was overwhelmingly German-speaking, though it had a significant Polish minority and a small Jewish community. Its constitution, guaranteed by the League of Nations, provided for an elected Volkstag (parliament) and a Senate government, with a League-appointed High Commissioner to arbitrate disputes with Poland.

Separated from Germany by the Polish Corridor, Danzig became a persistent source of German–Polish tension. In the early 1930s the Nazi Party gained power in the Free City: it won a majority in the 1933 Volkstag elections and progressively Nazified the city's institutions, enacting anti-Semitic measures modeled on the Nuremberg Laws. On September 1, 1939, Germany invaded Poland, beginning World War II with attacks on the Polish post office in Danzig and the Westerplatte peninsula; the Free City was annexed to Nazi Germany. After the war, the city became part of Poland as Gdańsk, and its German population was expelled.
