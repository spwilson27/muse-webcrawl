Metamorphism

Metamorphism is the solid-state alteration of pre-existing rocks (protoliths) in response to changes in temperature, pressure, and chemically active fluids, without complete melting. The resulting metamorphic rocks — such as marble from limestone, slate from shale, and gneiss from granite — record the tectonic history of the crust.

Types

Regional metamorphism affects large volumes of crust during mountain building, producing foliated rocks with grade increasing toward orogenic cores. Contact metamorphism occurs around igneous intrusions, baking country rock into hornfels. Dynamic metamorphism is localized along fault zones (mylonites), while burial, hydrothermal, and shock (impact) metamorphism reflect other settings.

Grade and facies

Metamorphic grade describes the intensity of metamorphism: low-grade (slate, phyllite), medium-grade (schist), and high-grade (gneiss, migmatite). Assemblages of minerals stable under particular pressure–temperature conditions define metamorphic facies — greenschist, amphibolite, granulite, blueschist, and eclogite — whose distribution maps subduction and collision zones. Index minerals (chlorite, biotite, garnet, staurolite, kyanite, sillimanite) mark Barrow's zones of increasing grade.

Textures

Directed pressure produces foliation — slaty cleavage, schistosity, and gneissic banding — while non-foliated rocks such as marble and quartzite recrystallize without preferred orientation.
