Double agent

A double agent serves two intelligence services, nominally or in reality.

A double agent is a person who works as a spy for two intelligence or security services, one of which is usually the agent's real employer and the other the target organisation — or who pretends to spy for one service while actually spying for another. Double agents are principal agents of the counterintelligence services, allowing a state to feed deception material to an adversary and to learn what the adversary is interested in by studying the questions it tasks the agent with answering.

Types

A re-doubled agent is a double agent who is discovered by the target and "turned" again — in fact feeding deception back to the original service. A triple agent pretends to be a double agent for one side while secretly loyal to the other. Agents caught and turned under threat of exposure or death are said to be operating under duress; their material is treated with suspicion.

Historical examples

The most famous system of double agents was Britain's Double Cross System of the Second World War, run by MI5's Twenty Committee, which turned essentially every German agent in Britain. Agents such as GARBO (Juan Pujol García) and ZIGZAG (Eddie Chapman) fed elaborate deception, culminating in Operation Fortitude, which convinced Germany that the Normandy landings were a diversion. During the Cold War, Kim Philby and the Cambridge Five exemplified penetration agents whose apparent loyalty concealed service to the Soviet Union.

Handling

Running a double agent requires elaborate verification ("bona fides"), chicken feed — genuine but low-value information passed to build the agent's credibility — and constant counterintelligence oversight, since the agent's true allegiance may be ambiguous even to the agent.
