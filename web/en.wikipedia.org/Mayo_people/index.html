The Mayo or Yoreme are an Indigenous people of northwestern Mexico, living in the Mayo River valley and coastal plain of southern Sonora and northern Sinaloa. They are closely related to the Yaqui, with whom they share the Cahitan branch of the Uto-Aztecan language family.

History

At Spanish contact in the sixteenth century the Mayo lived in farming villages along the river, growing maize, beans and squash and fishing the Gulf of California coast. Jesuit missionaries entered the region in the early seventeenth century, organizing mission towns and introducing new crops and livestock; the Mayo, unlike the Yaqui, mounted comparatively little armed resistance, though they joined regional uprisings in 1740 and later conflicts over land.

Culture

Mayo ceremonial life blends Catholic Holy Week observances with pre-Christian ritual. The deer dance (maso yi'ira) and the matachín dances, performed with elaborate headdresses and accompanied by harp, violin and drum, are central to fiestas in villages such as Júpare and Etchojoa. Traditional crafts include woven blankets, masks and pottery.

Present day

Today tens of thousands of people identify as Yoreme; the Mayo language is still spoken but is under pressure from Spanish. Many Mayo work in the commercial agriculture of the Mayo valley, and community organizations pursue recognition of Indigenous land and cultural rights.
