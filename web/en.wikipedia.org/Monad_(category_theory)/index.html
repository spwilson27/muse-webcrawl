Monad (category theory)


Structure: Endofunctor with unit and multiplication
Also called: Triple (historical)
Generalizes: Monoid

In category theory, a branch of mathematics, a monad is a triple (T, η, μ) consisting of an endofunctor T on a category together with two natural transformations: a unit η : Id → T and a multiplication μ : T2 → T, satisfying associativity and unit laws analogous to those of a monoid. Monads are sometimes described as "monoids in the category of endofunctors".

Definition and laws

The multiplication must satisfy an associativity law (applying μ before or after applying T to μ gives the same result from T3), and the unit must satisfy left and right identity laws (composing η with μ in either way gives the identity on T). These coherence conditions mirror the axioms of a monoid, with functor composition in place of the monoid operation.

Examples and origins

Monads arise naturally from adjunctions: every adjunction between categories gives rise to a monad, and, following work by Eilenberg and Moore and by Kleisli, every monad can be recovered from canonical adjunctions. Examples include the free-monoid (list) monad, the powerset monad, and monads arising from algebraic theories such as groups and rings. The concept was introduced in the 1950s and 1960s, first under names such as "standard construction" and "triple"; the term "monad" was popularized by Jean Bénabou.

Applications

Monads provide a unifying account of algebraic structures (through their categories of algebras), of notions of computation in functional programming, and of constructions in algebraic topology and geometry.
