Rear mid-engine, rear-wheel-drive layout

In automotive design, a rear mid-engine, rear-wheel-drive layout (RMR) is a configuration in which the engine is located in the middle of the vehicle — between the axles or just ahead of the rear axle — and drives the rear wheels. It is the classic layout of modern supercars and remains the standard in Formula One and most top-level sports prototype racing.

Advantages

Placing the engine's mass near the centre of the car lowers the polar moment of inertia, making the car change direction quickly, and concentrates weight over the driven wheels for traction. The layout also frees the front of the car for aerodynamics and crash structure.

Disadvantages

RMR cars can be prone to sudden oversteer ("snap oversteer") at the limit, since the low polar moment that aids agility also makes spins develop quickly. The layout also sacrifices cabin and luggage space and complicates cooling.

Examples

Notable RMR production cars include the Lamborghini Miura — a pioneer of the layout in 1966 — the Ferrari Berlinetta Boxer and Testarossa, the McLaren F1, the Porsche Carrera GT, and the Toyota MR2. The DeLorean DMC-12, by contrast, is rear-engined (engine behind the rear axle) rather than mid-engined.
