Proto-Slavic (abbreviated PS or PSl) is the unattested, reconstructed common ancestor of all the Slavic languages. It descends from Proto-Balto-Slavic, itself a branch of Proto-Indo-European. Because no written records of Proto-Slavic survive, its grammar and sound system are reconstructed by the comparative method from the attested Slavic languages, of which Old Church Slavonic (from the 9th century) preserves the most archaic features.

History

Scholars generally divide Proto-Slavic into an early period of broad unity and a late period (roughly 600–900 AD) in which dialectal differences accumulated as Slavic-speaking peoples expanded across eastern, central, and south-eastern Europe. By about the 10th century the common language had differentiated into the ancestors of the modern East, West, and South Slavic branches.

Phonology and grammar

Characteristic developments include the three palatalisations of velar consonants, the loss and vocalisation of the reduced vowels called yers, the rise of distinctive vowel quantity and a free mobile pitch accent, and the merger of several Proto-Indo-European vowels. Proto-Slavic nouns were inflected in three genders, three numbers (including a dual), and seven cases; verbs developed the characteristic Slavic system of perfective and imperfective aspect.
