Sterilization (microbiology)

Sterilization refers to any process that removes, kills, or deactivates all forms of life (in particular, referring to microorganisms such as fungi, bacteria, spores, and unicellular eukaryotic organisms) and other biological agents such as prions present in or on a specific surface, object, or fluid. Sterilisation can be achieved through various means, including heat, chemicals, irradiation, high pressure, and filtration.

Sterility assurance

Complete sterility is verified probabilistically through the sterility assurance level (SAL), typically 10−6 — at most one viable microorganism in a million sterilised items. Sterilisation differs from disinfection, which reduces but does not eliminate all microorganisms, and from pasteurisation, which targets pathogens in food.

Heat methods

Moist heat in an autoclave (typically 121 °C at 15 psi for 15–20 minutes) denatures proteins and is the standard for surgical instruments and culture media. Dry-heat ovens run hotter and longer (e.g. 170 °C for 1 hour) for glassware and powders.

Other methods

  • Chemical: ethylene oxide gas and hydrogen peroxide plasma for heat-sensitive devices
  • Radiation: gamma rays from cobalt-60, electron beams, and X-rays for single-use medical supplies
  • Filtration: 0.22 µm membranes remove bacteria from heat-labile liquids
  • Flaming and incineration: laboratory loops and waste disposal

Applications

Sterilisation is essential in surgery and medicine, pharmaceutical manufacturing, food canning, and spacecraft planetary-protection protocols. Resistant endospores of Geobacillus stearothermophilus serve as biological indicators for autoclaves.
