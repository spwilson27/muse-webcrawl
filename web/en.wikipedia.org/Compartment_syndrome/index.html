Compartment syndrome

Compartment syndrome is a condition in which increased pressure within a closed compartment of the body — usually a muscle compartment bounded by fascia in a limb — compromises circulation and the function of the tissues inside it. Acute compartment syndrome is a surgical emergency that most often follows fractures, crush injuries, or bleeding into a compartment; untreated, it causes muscle and nerve death within hours.

Signs and symptoms

The classic features are pain out of proportion to the injury, pain on passive stretching of the muscles in the compartment, a tense or "wood-like" compartment, paraesthesia, and pallor; loss of pulses is a late sign. Diagnosis is clinical and may be confirmed by measuring intracompartmental pressure.

Treatment

Treatment of acute compartment syndrome is emergency fasciotomy — surgical incision of the fascia to release the pressure — performed as soon as possible, ideally within six hours. Constricting casts or dressings are removed immediately. Delayed treatment risks Volkmann's contracture, rhabdomyolysis, kidney failure, amputation, or death.

Chronic compartment syndrome

Chronic exertional compartment syndrome causes exercise-induced pain that resolves with rest, typically in runners and military recruits; it is managed conservatively or by elective fasciotomy.
