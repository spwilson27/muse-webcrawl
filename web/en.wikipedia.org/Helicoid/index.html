Helicoid

A helicoid with α = 1, −1 ≤ ρ ≤ 1 and −π ≤ θ ≤ π.

The helicoid, also known as the helical ramp, is a surface in three-dimensional Euclidean space. It is the surface traced by an infinite line segment that moves simultaneously with a rotation around a fixed axis and a translation along the same axis. It is one of the ruled surfaces and was described by Euler in 1774 and by Jean Baptiste Meusnier in 1776.

The helicoid is a minimal surface, meaning that any small piece of it has the smallest possible area among all surfaces with the same boundary. Together with the plane, it is one of the Catalan surfaces, and it is the only non-planar ruled minimal surface. For many years the helicoid and the catenoid were the only known examples of complete embedded minimal surfaces of finite topology; the Costa surface and others were discovered later.

Description

The helicoid is a ruled surface: for every point on the surface there is a straight line lying entirely within the surface that passes through that point. In fact, it is swept out by a line segment that rotates about the axis while moving along it at a constant rate.

In cylindrical coordinates (ρ, θ, z), a helicoid with axis along the z-axis can be parametrized by:

x = ρ cos(αθ),   y = ρ sin(αθ),   z = θ,

where ρ and θ range over the real numbers and α is a constant. The pitch of the helicoid — the vertical distance covered in one full turn — is 2π/α in this parametrization. If α is positive the helicoid is right-handed, as in an ordinary screw.

Relation to the catenoid

The helicoid and the catenoid are members of a continuous family of minimal surfaces, sometimes called the associated family. One surface can be deformed isometrically into the other through a continuous family of minimal surfaces; the deformation is a local isometry, so lengths measured on a small patch are preserved even though the helicoid is ruled and the catenoid is not.

Applications

The helicoid shape appears in screw threads, augers, spiral staircases, and the ramps of some parking structures. In physics, helicoid-like surfaces appear in the study of liquid crystals and in models of DNA packing.
