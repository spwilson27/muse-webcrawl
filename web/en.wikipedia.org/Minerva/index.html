Minerva

Minerva (Latin: [mɪˈnɛr.wa]; Etruscan: Menrva) is the Roman goddess of wisdom, justice, law, victory, and the sponsor of arts, trade and strategy. She is the Roman equivalent of the Greek goddess Athena. With Jupiter and Juno she formed the Capitoline Triad, worshipped on Rome's Capitoline Hill, and her festival, the Quinquatrus (19–23 March), was celebrated by artisans and teachers.

Mythology

Following the Greek Athena myths, Minerva was born fully grown and armed from the head of Jupiter, who had swallowed her mother Metis. She was credited with inventing weaving, music and numbers, and figured prominently in stories of heroes such as Perseus, Odysseus (Ulysses) and Aeneas. Her contest with Neptune for the patronage of Athens gave the city its name (won, in the Roman retelling too, by her gift of the olive tree), while her punishment of Arachne, who challenged her at weaving, turned the girl into a spider.

Worship and legacy

Minerva was patroness of craftsmen, doctors, teachers and strategists; the owl of Minerva became a lasting emblem of wisdom — famously invoked by Hegel's remark that "the owl of Minerva spreads its wings only with the falling of the dusk." Her name and image have been widely used by universities, learned societies and institutions such as the Minerva Schools.
