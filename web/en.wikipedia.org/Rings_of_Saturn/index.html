The rings of Saturn are the most extensive and conspicuous ring system of any planet in the Solar System. They consist of countless small particles, ranging in size from micrometres to metres, almost entirely of water ice, orbiting Saturn in an extremely thin, flat disc. Despite spanning hundreds of thousands of kilometres, the main rings are typically only about ten metres thick.

History of observation

Galileo Galilei first observed the rings in 1610, but his low-resolution telescope showed them as two large "handles" or companion bodies flanking the planet. In 1659 Christiaan Huygens, with a better instrument, correctly proposed a thin flat ring encircling the planet without touching it. In 1675 Giovanni Domenico Cassini found the dark gap — the Cassini Division — separating the two brightest rings. In the 1850s James Clerk Maxwell proved the rings could not be a solid sheet and must be made of numerous small orbiting particles.

Structure

From the planet outward, the named regions are the dusty D ring, the relatively dark C ring, the bright broad B ring, the Cassini Division, the A ring (which contains the narrower Encke Gap), the thin knotted F ring, and the faint G and E rings; the E ring is a vast diffuse torus fed by geysers on the moon Enceladus. Gaps and fine structure are maintained by resonances with Saturn's moons and by small embedded "shepherd" moons such as Pan and Prometheus.

Composition and origin

Spectroscopy and the Cassini spacecraft showed the ring particles are about 99 per cent water ice. Cassini's final orbits (2017) measured the rings' total mass at roughly 40 per cent that of the moon Mimas and suggested the rings may be surprisingly young — perhaps only 100 million years old — though their origin (a shattered icy moon or comet) remains debated. Material constantly rains from the rings onto the planet, so the rings are slowly disappearing.
