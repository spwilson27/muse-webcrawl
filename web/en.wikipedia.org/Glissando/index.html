Glissando

A glissando notated as a diagonal line connecting two pitches.

In music, a glissando (plural: glissandi, abbreviated gliss.) is a glide from one pitch to another. It is an Italianized form of the French glisser, "to glide". In some contexts it is distinguished from the continuous portamento, though in practice the terms are often used interchangeably.

Performance

A glissando is easy to perform on instruments with continuously variable pitch, such as the trombone (using the slide), the violin and other unfretted strings (sliding a finger along the string), and the human voice. On the piano and harp, a glissando is produced by sweeping the fingers rapidly across the keys or strings, yielding a fast scale in the instrument's tuning. On the piano the white keys produce a C-major (or modal) scale and the black keys a pentatonic scale, effects famously exploited in works such as Maurice Ravel's Alborada del gracioso. Wind players can produce glissandi by combining embouchure bending with gradual fingering changes, most famously in the clarinet opening of George Gershwin's Rhapsody in Blue.

Notation

A glissando is indicated by a line — sometimes wavy — joining the initial and final notes, optionally with the abbreviation gliss. Composers may notate the first few and last few notes of the slide to clarify the intended scale or pitch content.

Portamento

Some writers reserve glissando for the rapid passage through discrete intermediate notes (as on the piano) and portamento for a truly continuous slide (as on the trombone or voice). The distinction is not consistently observed.
