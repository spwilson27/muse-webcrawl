Algebraic surface

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In mathematics, an algebraic surface is an algebraic variety of dimension two — roughly, a surface defined by polynomial equations. The theory of algebraic surfaces is far more complicated than that of algebraic curves, and their classification was a central achievement of the Italian school of algebraic geometry in the early twentieth century, completed in modern form by Kunihiko Kodaira.

Basic examples

The simplest algebraic surfaces are the projective plane itself and the quadric surfaces in projective 3-space, which include spheres, cones and hyperboloids. Cubic surfaces are famous for the theorem that a smooth cubic surface contains exactly 27 lines. Ruled surfaces are swept out by families of straight lines, while K3 surfaces and abelian surfaces occupy a middle ground of the classification, and surfaces of general type are the most numerous and least understood.

Classification

The Enriques–Kodaira classification organises smooth complex projective surfaces by their Kodaira dimension, which ranges from −∞ (rational and ruled surfaces) through 0 (K3, Enriques, abelian and bielliptic surfaces) and 1 (properly elliptic surfaces) to 2 (surfaces of general type). Key invariants used in the classification include the plurigenera, the irregularity, and the behaviour of the canonical divisor. Birational geometry — studying surfaces up to birational equivalence — simplifies the picture: unlike curves, surfaces can be blown up at points, and minimal models are the basic building blocks.
