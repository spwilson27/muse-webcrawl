The radial nerve is a nerve in the human body that supplies the posterior portion of the upper limb. It innervates the medial and lateral heads of the triceps brachii muscle of the arm, as well as all twelve muscles in the posterior osteofascial compartment of the forearm and the associated joints and overlying skin.

It originates from the brachial plexus, carrying fibers from the posterior cord with spinal roots C5, C6, C7, C8 and T1. The radial nerve and its branches provide motor innervation to the dorsal arm muscles (the triceps brachii and the anconeus) and the extrinsic extensors of the wrists and hands; it also provides cutaneous sensory innervation to most of the back of the hand, except the back of the little finger and the adjacent half of the ring finger.

Structure

From the axilla, the nerve descends behind the humerus in the spiral (radial) groove, accompanied by the profunda brachii artery, before piercing the lateral intermuscular septum to enter the anterior compartment of the arm. In front of the lateral epicondyle of the elbow it divides into a superficial sensory branch, which continues under the brachioradialis to the dorsum of the hand, and a deep motor branch — the posterior interosseous nerve — which passes through the supinator to supply the extensor muscles of the forearm.

Function

Motor supply includes the triceps brachii, anconeus, brachioradialis, extensor carpi radialis longus, and, via the posterior interosseous nerve, the remaining wrist and finger extensors and the supinator and abductor pollicis longus. Sensation is provided over the posterior arm and forearm and the dorsolateral hand and dorsal fingers (excluding the fingertips).

Clinical significance

Injury to the radial nerve in the spiral groove — classically from a mid-shaft humeral fracture or from prolonged compression ("Saturday night palsy", from falling asleep with the arm draped over a chair) — produces wrist drop: loss of wrist and finger extension with relatively preserved triceps function. Injury higher in the axilla (e.g., "crutch palsy") also weakens elbow extension. Most compression palsies recover spontaneously over weeks to months.
