Joint warfare

Joint warfare is a military doctrine in which the armed services of a nation — army, navy, air force, and others — fight together in integrated operations rather than separately. The concept emphasizes cooperation between different service branches to achieve synergistic effects, so that the combined effort is greater than the sum of its parts.

Principles

Joint operations are characterized by unity of command under a joint force commander, integrated planning, shared intelligence and communications, and combined arms across domains — land, sea, air, space, and cyberspace. Interoperability of equipment, doctrine, and terminology among the services is essential.

Development

Although combined operations occurred earlier — amphibious landings in the Second World War demanded close Army–Navy–Air Force coordination — jointness became formal doctrine after failures of interservice coordination, such as the 1980 Iran hostage rescue attempt and the 1983 invasion of Grenada. In the United States, the Goldwater–Nichols Act of 1986 strengthened the authority of unified combatant commands and made joint duty a requirement for senior officers. NATO and other alliances pursue similar integration among national contingents, known as combined or multinational operations.
