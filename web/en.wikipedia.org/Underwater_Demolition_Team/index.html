Underwater Demolition Team

Underwater Demolition Team
Active: 1942–1983
Country: United States
Branch: United States Navy
Role: Combat swimming, demolition, reconnaissance

The Underwater Demolition Teams (UDT) were United States Navy units originally created during World War II to conduct reconnaissance of enemy beaches, clear obstacles and mines, and guide landing craft during amphibious assaults. Their members, commonly called "frogmen", were the forerunners of the modern Navy SEALs.

History

The UDT concept arose after the costly assault on Tarawa in November 1943, where coral reefs and obstacles not detected beforehand caused heavy Marine casualties. The Navy thereafter formed demolition units drawn from Naval Construction Battalions and joint Army/Navy scout companies. UDTs first saw major action preparing the beaches for the invasion of Normandy and in the Pacific landings at Saipan, Guam, Peleliu, Iwo Jima, and Okinawa, often working in only swim trunks and fins under enemy fire.

Korean and Vietnam Wars

During the Korean War, UDTs expanded their missions to include inland raids on bridges and tunnels, mine hunting, and support of the Inchon landing. In Vietnam they operated with river patrol forces and pioneered many of the direct-action techniques later adopted by the SEALs, which were established in 1962. Many UDT operators transferred into the new SEAL teams.

Disestablishment

In 1983 the remaining UDTs were redesignated as SEAL or SEAL Delivery Vehicle teams, ending the UDT designation after four decades of service.
