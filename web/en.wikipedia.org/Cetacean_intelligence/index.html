Cetacean intelligence

Overview
Studied taxaDolphins, porpoises, and whales (infraorder Cetacea)
Noted abilitiesSelf-recognition, signature whistles, tool use, cultural transmission, cooperative hunting
Noted researchersJohn C. Lilly, Louis Herman, Diana Reiss, Lori Marino

Cetacean intelligence is the overall intelligence and derived cognitive ability of aquatic mammals belonging to the infraorder Cetacea, including dolphins, porpoises, and whales. Cetaceans are noted for their large brains — the sperm whale has the largest brain mass of any known animal — their complex social structures, and behaviours suggesting learning, communication, and in some species tool use and cultural transmission across generations.

Evidence

Research with bottlenose dolphins, particularly by Louis Herman, showed comprehension of artificial gestural and acoustic languages, including sensitivity to word order. Dolphins and elephants are among the few animals to pass the mirror test of self-recognition, as demonstrated by Diana Reiss and Lori Marino in 2001. Wild dolphins develop individual "signature whistles" functioning analogously to names, use marine sponges as foraging tools (a behaviour transmitted culturally, mainly mother to daughter), and engage in coordinated cooperative hunting. Orcas display distinct pod dialects and prey traditions considered a form of culture, while humpback whale songs evolve and spread across ocean basins.

Brains

Cetacean brains are highly convoluted and rich in von Economo (spindle) neurons, also found in great apes and elephants and associated with social cognition. Debate continues over how to compare intelligence across species with radically different sensory worlds and evolutionary histories — dolphins, for example, experience the world largely through echolocation.
