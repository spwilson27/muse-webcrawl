Planck units

Planck units are natural units built solely from fundamental constants

In particle physics and physical cosmology, Planck units are a system of units of measurement defined exclusively in terms of four universal physical constants: the speed of light in vacuum c, the gravitational constant G, the reduced Planck constant ħ, and the Boltzmann constant kB. The system is an example of natural units, in which these constants are normalised to 1 and physical quantities are expressed as multiples of the corresponding Planck unit. The units are named after Max Planck, who proposed them in 1899, soon after introducing the Planck constant.

Base units

The base Planck units are the Planck length (lP ≈ 1.616255×10−35 m), the Planck time (tP ≈ 5.391247×10−44 s, the time light takes to cross one Planck length), the Planck mass (mP ≈ 2.176434×10−8 kg), the Planck temperature (TP ≈ 1.416784×1032 K) and the Planck charge. Derived units include the Planck energy (≈ 1.956×109 J) and Planck density.

Significance

Planck units express the scales at which the effects of both quantum mechanics and general relativity become simultaneously important, so that a theory of quantum gravity is expected to be required to describe physics at, for example, the Planck length or the Planck epoch of the early universe (before one Planck time after the Big Bang). The Planck mass is unusually large by particle standards, roughly the mass of a flea egg, which illustrates how weak gravity is compared with the other forces. Because the units are derived without reference to any human artefact, they are sometimes suggested as a basis for communication with extraterrestrial intelligence.
