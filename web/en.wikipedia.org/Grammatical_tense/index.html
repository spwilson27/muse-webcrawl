Grammatical tense

In grammar, tense is a category that expresses time reference. Tenses are usually manifested by the use of specific forms of verbs, particularly in their conjugation patterns. The main tenses found in many languages include the past, present, and future. Some languages have only two distinct tenses (past and non-past, or future and non-future), and some, such as Mandarin Chinese and Indonesian, have no grammatical tense at all, relying on adverbs and context to locate events in time.

Tense systems

Tense locates the time of an event relative to a reference point — usually the moment of speaking (absolute tense) but sometimes another event (relative tense). A two-tense system contrasts past with non-past (English inflectional morphology is arguably such a system: walked vs. walk(s), with futurity expressed periphrastically by "will"). Three-tense systems distinguish past, present, and future. Some languages go further: several Bantu languages and some Indigenous languages of the Americas distinguish degrees of remoteness in the past and future — earlier today (hodiernal), yesterday, and remote past.

Tense, aspect, and mood

Tense interacts with aspect (whether an event is complete, ongoing, or habitual) and mood (its factual status). English traditional grammar names twelve "tenses" that are really tense–aspect combinations: past/present/future crossed with simple, progressive, perfect, and perfect progressive. Latin combined tense and aspect in forms such as the imperfect (past progressive/habitual) and the perfect (past completed).

Tenseless languages

Languages such as Mandarin, Vietnamese, Malay/Indonesian, and many others have no grammatical tense marking; temporal interpretation comes from aspect markers, time adverbs, and context. This shows that tense is not a universal of human language, though every language can communicate time reference.
