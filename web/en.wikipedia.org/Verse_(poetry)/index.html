Verse (poetry)

Verse is a term in poetry and literature with several related senses: a single line of poetry (in this sense a synonym of "line"), a stanza, or, most broadly, any composition written in metre — poetry as opposed to prose. The word derives from Latin versus, "a line, row, furrow," from the turning of the plough.

Forms of verse

Blank verse is unrhymed verse in a regular metre, classically iambic pentameter in English; it is the medium of Shakespeare's plays and Milton's Paradise Lost. Free verse abandons both regular metre and rhyme, relying on cadence, imagery, and line breaks for structure; it became dominant in much 20th-century poetry after pioneers such as Walt Whitman. Rhymed and stanzaic forms include the couplet, quatrain, sonnet, villanelle, and terza rima.

Metre

Metre is the rhythmic pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables, organised into feet (iambs, trochees, anapaests, dactyls) and counted in lines (tetrameter, pentameter, hexameter). Other traditions use quantitative metre based on syllable length (classical Greek and Latin), syllabic counts (French verse, Japanese haiku), or tonal patterns (classical Chinese verse).

Verse versus poetry

Because "verse" stresses craft and form, the word is sometimes used slightingly for technically regular but unremarkable verse — "mere verse" as opposed to "poetry" — though formally the two overlap, and modern usage treats verse as a neutral structural term.
