CONELRAD

CONELRAD (CONtrol of ELectromagnetic RADiation) was a method of emergency broadcasting to the public of the United States in the event of enemy attack during the Cold War. It was intended to allow continuous broadcast of civil defense information to the public using radio stations, while rapidly switching the transmitter stations to make the broadcasts unsuitable for Soviet bombers that might attempt to home in on the signals.

History

Before 1951, there was no systematic way for the U.S. government to communicate with citizens during an emergency. Existing emergency information broadcasts could be used by enemy planes as navigational aids, since radio transmitters broadcast steady signals. As a result, President Harry S. Truman established CONELRAD in 1951.

Under the system, radio stations were required to stop broadcasting at certain frequencies and then broadcast emergency information on either 640 or 1240 kHz. Stations took turns transmitting at these frequencies so that no single transmitter stayed on the air long enough to be used for direction finding.

Operation

During an alert, most television and FM radio stations were required to shut down. AM stations that stayed on the air would broadcast on the designated frequencies for short periods. Many radio receivers manufactured during the 1950s carried special markings at 640 and 1240 on the tuning dial, indicated by small "CD" (Civil Defense) triangles.

Replacement

CONELRAD was replaced by the Emergency Broadcast System (EBS) on August 5, 1963, which in turn was later replaced by the Emergency Alert System. The system's procedures proved slow to activate and generated complaints from broadcasters, contributing to its replacement.
