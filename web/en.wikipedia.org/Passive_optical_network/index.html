Passive optical network

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A PON tree: one feeder fibre is split passively among many subscribers

A passive optical network (PON) is a fibre-optic telecommunications technology for delivering broadband network access to end customers. It is called "passive" because the optical distribution network between the provider and the subscriber contains no electrically powered switching or amplification equipment: a single optical fibre from the central office is divided among many endpoints using unpowered optical splitters.

Architecture

A PON consists of an optical line terminal (OLT) at the service provider's central office, a tree of passive optical splitters, and optical network units (ONUs, or ONTs) at or near each subscriber's premises. Downstream traffic is broadcast from the OLT to all ONUs on one wavelength, each unit selecting the packets addressed to it; upstream traffic uses a different wavelength and is combined by time-division multiple access, with the OLT scheduling each ONU's transmission windows so that bursts do not collide at the splitter.

Standards

The main PON families are standardised by the ITU-T and the IEEE. APON and BPON were early ATM- and broadband-oriented versions. GPON (ITU-T G.984) offers nominal rates of 2.5 Gbit/s downstream and 1.25 Gbit/s upstream and is widely deployed for fibre-to-the-home services; its successor XG-PON and the symmetric XGS-PON raise rates to 10 Gbit/s, and 50G-PON has been standardised in turn. The IEEE Ethernet family includes EPON (802.3ah) and 10G-EPON. Typical split ratios are 1:32 or 1:64 over distances of up to about 20 km.

Applications

PONs are the dominant architecture for fibre-to-the-home (FTTH) broadband worldwide, and are also used for business services, mobile backhaul and in-building networks, because sharing the feeder fibre and OLT ports among many subscribers sharply reduces per-user cost compared with point-to-point fibre.
