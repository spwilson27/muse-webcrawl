Pamunkey

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Pamunkey are a federally recognised Native American tribe in Virginia, historically one of the leading tribes of the Powhatan paramount chiefdom (Tsenacommacah) that dominated the Virginia Tidewater at the time of English colonisation in 1607. Their reservation on the Pamunkey River in King William County, established by treaty in the seventeenth century, is among the oldest continuously held reservations in the United States.

History

The Pamunkey were closely related to the paramount chief Wahunsenacawh (Chief Powhatan) and his brother Opechancanough, and to Pocahontas (Matoaka), who belonged to the wider Powhatan world. Under the Treaty of 1677 (Middle Plantation), following Bacon's Rebellion, the Pamunkey — then led by the weroansqua Cockacoeske — secured confirmation of their lands and rights, and the tribe maintained the custom of paying an annual tribute of game to the Governor of Virginia, a ceremony that continues today.

Despite pressure on their land and attempts in the 20th century — notably under Virginia's Racial Integrity Act of 1924 and registrar Walter Plecker — to erase their legal identity, the Pamunkey retained their reservation and tribal government. After a lengthy petition process, the Pamunkey Indian Tribe received United States federal recognition in 2015 (effective 2016), the first Virginia tribe to gain recognition administratively; six other Virginia tribes were recognised by statute in 2018.

Culture

The tribe operates a museum and cultural centre on the reservation, and members maintain traditions including pottery — blackware pottery is a noted Pamunkey art — fishing and shad runs on the Pamunkey River, and the management of a fish hatchery. The Pamunkey language, an Eastern Algonquian language, ceased to be spoken fluently in the 19th century.
