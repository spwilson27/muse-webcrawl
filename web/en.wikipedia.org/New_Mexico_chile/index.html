New Mexico chile

The New Mexico chile (Capsicum annuum 'New Mexican') is a group of cultivars of the chile pepper developed by New Mexico State University. It is a staple of New Mexican cuisine and the state vegetable of New Mexico (along with the pinto bean). The flavor is distinctly earthy and medium-bodied, and the pepper is used both red (ripe, often dried into ristras or ground into powder) and green (unripe, roasted). The official state question of New Mexico, adopted in 1999, is "Red or Green?" — referring to the choice of chile sauce.

History

Chile peppers arrived in New Mexico with Spanish settlers in the late 16th century. Over centuries of selective breeding by Pueblo and Hispano farmers, local landraces developed. In 1888, Fabian Garcia at New Mexico State University began a breeding program that produced the modern, larger, smoother 'New Mexico No. 9' in 1913. Later work by Roy Harper and Roy Nakayama produced cultivars such as 'NuMex Big Jim' (once a Guinness record holder for largest chile) and the extra-hot 'NuMex Sandia'.

Cultivation and use

The Hatch Valley of southern New Mexico, centered on the village of Hatch, is famed for its chile production and hosts an annual Hatch Chile Festival each Labor Day weekend. Green chiles are roasted, peeled, and chopped into stews and sauces; red chiles are blended into a smooth sauce used on enchiladas and other dishes. Heat levels range from mild to extra hot, roughly 500 to 100,000 Scoville units depending on cultivar.
