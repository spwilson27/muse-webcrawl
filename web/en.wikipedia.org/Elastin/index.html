Elastin

Elastin is a highly elastic protein in the extracellular matrix of connective tissue that allows many tissues in the body to resume their shape after stretching or contracting. Elastin helps skin return to its original position when it is poked or pinched, and it gives arteries, lungs, ligaments, and the bladder their characteristic recoil.

Elastic fibres, rich in elastin, in the wall of an artery.

Composition and structure

Elastin is formed from the soluble precursor tropoelastin, which is rich in hydrophobic amino acids such as glycine, valine, alanine, and proline. The enzyme lysyl oxidase cross-links tropoelastin molecules through the unusual amino acids desmosine and isodesmosine, producing a durable, rubber-like network. In humans, elastin is encoded by the ELN gene.

Distribution and function

Elastin makes up roughly half the dry weight of large arteries such as the aorta, where it smooths out pulsatile blood flow from the heart. It is also abundant in the lungs, elastic ligaments (such as the ligamenta flava of the spine), skin, and cartilage of the ear. Most elastin is laid down before adulthood and is extremely long-lived, with a half-life of many decades.

Clinical significance

Mutations in ELN cause disorders including cutis laxa, supravalvular aortic stenosis, and Williams syndrome. Loss and fragmentation of elastin with age contribute to skin wrinkling, arterial stiffening, and emphysema, in which elastic fibres in the lungs are destroyed.
