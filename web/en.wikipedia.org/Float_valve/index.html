Float valve

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A float valve is a mechanical feedback mechanism that regulates the level of liquid in a tank by opening and closing an inlet valve according to the height of a buoyant float. The best-known example is the ballcock in a flushing toilet cistern: as water rises after a flush, the float (traditionally a hollow ball on an arm) rises and progressively closes the inlet until flow stops at the set level. As the tank empties, the float falls and reopens the valve.

Mechanism

In the classic ballcock, the float is attached to a pivoted lever arm; buoyancy acting on the float presses a washer against the inlet orifice. Modern fill valves instead use a compact float cup sliding on a vertical tower, or diaphragm designs in which the float actuates a small pilot valve. The same principle operates in livestock water troughs, evaporative coolers, carburettors (where a float chamber meters fuel), boiler feed systems, and the condensate traps of steam systems.

History

Float regulators are ancient: Ctesibius of Alexandria (3rd century BC) used float valves in water clocks, and Hero described several float-controlled devices. The ballcock for the water closet developed with the spread of the flushing toilet in the 18th and 19th centuries; designs by Thomas Crapper and others became the household standard.

Failure modes

Common faults include perished washers and diaphragms causing continuous running ("phantom flushing" and overflowing cisterns), waterlogged floats, and lime scale jamming the mechanism. A "silent" fill valve failing in a cistern can waste thousands of litres of water per year, making float-valve maintenance a standard target of water-conservation guidance.
