St Andrews (Latin: S. Andrea(s); Scots: Saunt Aundraes; Scottish Gaelic: Cill Rìmhinn) is a town on the east coast of Fife, in northeast Scotland, 10 miles (16 km) southeast of Dundee and 30 miles (48 km) northeast of Edinburgh. The town is home to the University of St Andrews, the third oldest university in the English-speaking world and the oldest in Scotland. It was also, until the Scottish Reformation, the centre of the Scottish church and the site of a great cathedral, now ruined.

History

According to legend, the relics of Saint Andrew were brought to the site in the 8th century, making it a major pilgrimage destination. The cathedral, founded in 1158 and consecrated in 1318, was the largest church in Scotland. During the Reformation the cathedral was sacked (1559) and fell into ruin. The university was founded in 1413 by a papal bull of Antipope Benedict XIII and became a centre of learning noted for figures such as John Knox, who preached there.

Golf

St Andrews is known worldwide as the "home of golf". The Old Course at St Andrews is among the oldest golf courses in the world — golf was played on the Links by 1552, and the game was briefly banned by an Act of the Scottish Parliament in 1457 as a distraction from archery practice. The Royal and Ancient Golf Club of St Andrews, founded in 1754, historically governed the rules of golf worldwide (except in the United States and Mexico) and still organises The Open Championship by its rules body, The R&A. The Old Course regularly hosts The Open, the Alfred Dunhill Links Championship, and the St Andrews Links Trophy.

The university today

Students make up a large share of the town's population. Traditions include the May Dip, Raisin Weekend, and academic families. Alumni include Prince William and Catherine, Princess of Wales, who met while studying there. The town also attracts visitors for its beaches — the West Sands featured in the opening scenes of the film Chariots of Fire — castle ruins, and the St Andrews Aquarium and Botanic Garden.
