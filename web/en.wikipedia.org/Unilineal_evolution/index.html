Unilineal evolution

Classical unilineal schemes arranged societies along a single line of development.

Unilineal evolution, also referred to as classical social evolution, is a 19th-century social theory about the evolution of societies and cultures. It was composed of many ideas from various sociologists and anthropologists, including Auguste Comte, Herbert Spencer, Lewis Henry Morgan, and Edward Burnett Tylor. The theory held that all cultures evolve through the same sequence of stages, from simple to complex.

The theory

Morgan's Ancient Society (1877) divided human history into three stages — savagery, barbarism, and civilization — each subdivided and marked by technological inventions such as fire, the bow and arrow, pottery, agriculture, and writing. Tylor, in Primitive Culture (1871), applied a similar progression to religion, from animism through polytheism to monotheism. Because all societies were thought to travel the same road, contemporary non-Western peoples were treated as "living fossils" representing earlier stages.

Criticism

In the early 20th century, Franz Boas and his students attacked unilineal evolution for its lack of empirical evidence, its assumption that change comes from internal development rather than diffusion and historical particular circumstances, and its implicit ranking of cultures. The Boasian school of historical particularism displaced it in American anthropology. Later theories of multilineal evolution, associated with Julian Steward, allowed different societies to follow different developmental paths.
