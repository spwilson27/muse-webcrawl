Transparency (behavior)

Transparency, as used in science, engineering, business, the humanities, and other social contexts, is operating in such a way that it is easy for others to see what actions are performed. Transparency implies openness, communication, and accountability.

In government and politics

Transparency in governance refers to public access to information about decision-making: published budgets, open meetings, freedom of information laws, asset declarations, and disclosure of lobbying. It is regarded as a key tool against corruption; the NGO Transparency International publishes the annual Corruption Perceptions Index.

In business

Corporate transparency covers financial reporting, disclosure of conflicts of interest, supply-chain visibility, and salary transparency. Radical transparency, practised by some firms, extends openness to internal communications and decision records. Critics note tensions with privacy, trade secrets, and information overload.

In technology

Algorithmic transparency concerns the ability to inspect how automated systems make decisions; open-source software is transparent in its code. Transparency reports published by technology platforms disclose government requests for user data and content removals.

Criticism

Scholars observe that transparency alone does not guarantee accountability: disclosed information may be incomprehensible, gamed, or voluminous enough to obscure what matters ("transparency washing").
