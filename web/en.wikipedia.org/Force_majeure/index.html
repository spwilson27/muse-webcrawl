Force majeure

Force majeure (French for "superior force") is a common clause in contracts which essentially frees both parties from liability or obligation when an extraordinary event or circumstance beyond the control of the parties — such as a war, strike, riot, crime, epidemic, or an event described by the legal term act of God — prevents one or both parties from fulfilling their obligations under the contract.

Application

In practice, most force majeure clauses do not excuse a party's non-performance entirely, but suspend it for the duration of the event. The precise scope depends on the wording of the clause and the governing law. In civil-law systems such as France's, force majeure is a recognised legal doctrine; in common-law jurisdictions such as England, there is no general doctrine, and parties must rely on the express terms of their contract or the narrower doctrine of frustration.

Notable invocations

Force majeure was widely invoked during the COVID-19 pandemic, when lockdowns disrupted events, construction and supply chains, and litigation followed over whether the pandemic fell within particular clauses. Shortages following natural disasters, embargoes and wars have likewise generated disputes about foreseeability and mitigation.
