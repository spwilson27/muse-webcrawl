Highland Boundary Fault

CountryScotland, United Kingdom
Lengthc. 240 km (150 mi)
TrendNorth-east to south-west
TypeStrike-slip / reverse fault (relict)

The Highland Boundary Fault is a major fault zone that traverses Scotland from Arran and Helensburgh on the west coast to Stonehaven in the east. It separates the Highlands to the north-west from the Midland Valley (Central Lowlands) to the south-east, and the dramatic change in topography along its line — from mountainous terrain to gentle lowland, often within a few hundred metres — is one of the most striking geological boundaries in Britain.

Geology

The fault marks the junction between two distinct geological terranes. To the north lie the ancient metamorphic rocks of the Dalradian Supergroup and the Grampian Highlands, deformed and metamorphosed during the Caledonian orogeny. To the south are the younger sedimentary rocks of Devonian and Carboniferous age that fill the Midland Valley graben. Along the fault itself runs the narrow Highland Border Complex, a sliver of Ordovician oceanic rocks — serpentinites, cherts and spilites — interpreted as remnants of the Iapetus Ocean floor scraped up during continental collision.

History of movement

The fault originated during the closure of the Iapetus Ocean in the Silurian and Devonian periods, initially as a thrust or reverse fault as the Midland Valley terrane docked against the Grampian block. Later movement was predominantly strike-slip, and the fault was reactivated periodically through the Carboniferous and into the Mesozoic. The present-day landscape contrast reflects the much greater resistance to erosion of the metamorphic rocks to the north.

Present day

The fault is largely inactive, though small earthquakes occasionally occur near its line, notably the swarm of minor tremors historically recorded around Comrie in Perthshire — once known as the "earthquake village". Settlements and features along the boundary include Loch Lomond, Aberfoyle, Callander, Comrie, Crieff, Dunkeld and Kirriemuir. The islands of Loch Lomond, including Inchcailloch and Conic Hill, trace the fault's course across the loch.
