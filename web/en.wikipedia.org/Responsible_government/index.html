Responsible government

Responsible government is a system in which the executive branch is accountable to an elected legislature and must retain its confidence to remain in office. It is a defining feature of the Westminster system and of parliamentary government in many Commonwealth countries.

Principles

The head of government and ministers are normally drawn from the legislature or are accountable to it. The government remains in power while it commands a majority, especially on votes of confidence and supply. If it loses confidence, it must resign, seek a new arrangement, or request an election. Ministers answer questions, defend policy, and can be compelled to provide information to parliamentary committees.

Ministerial responsibility

Collective ministerial responsibility requires cabinet members publicly to support government decisions or resign. Individual ministerial responsibility makes a minister answerable for the conduct of a department, although the expectation that every administrative error will produce a resignation varies by country and circumstance. Civil servants generally remain politically neutral while ministers take political responsibility.

Head of state

In constitutional monarchies and parliamentary republics, the formal head of state normally appoints the person most likely to command legislative confidence and acts on ministerial advice except in exceptional reserve-power situations. Responsible government therefore separates the formal authority of the Crown or president from day-to-day political accountability.

Historical development

The model developed in Britain as control over finance and ministries shifted toward Parliament. During the nineteenth century it was adopted in British settler colonies, notably in Canada, Australia, and New Zealand, where colonial executives had previously been responsible mainly to imperial authorities. Modern practice combines conventions, statutes, party discipline, and electoral accountability.
