Unreliable narrator

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Term coined byWayne C. Booth, The Rhetoric of Fiction (1961)
FieldNarratology, literary theory
Related conceptsImplied author, dramatic irony, frame narrative

An unreliable narrator is a narrator whose credibility is compromised. They can be found in fiction and film and can be employed by the author to invoke interesting techniques or to explore the subjectivity of storytelling. The term was coined in 1961 by Wayne C. Booth in The Rhetoric of Fiction, who defined a narrator as reliable when they speak or act in accordance with the norms of the work (i.e., the implied author's norms), and unreliable when they do not.

Types of unreliability

Scholars such as William Riggan have classified unreliable narrators into types, including the pícaro (a rogue exaggerator), the madman, the naïf whose limited perception distorts the account, and the deliberate liar. Unreliability may stem from ignorance, mental illness, self-deception, prejudice, or intentional deception, and may concern the facts of the story, the interpretation of events, or the narrator's values and judgments.

Notable examples

Classic examples include the governess in Henry James's The Turn of the Screw (1898), whose account may describe ghosts or hallucinations; the butler Stevens in Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day (1989), whose self-deception shapes his narration; Humbert Humbert in Vladimir Nabokov's Lolita (1955), a self-justifying liar; and the narrator of Agatha Christie's The Murder of Roger Ackroyd (1926), famous for its twist. In film, examples include Verbal Kint in The Usual Suspects (1995) and the narrator of Fight Club (1999).

Detection by the reader

Readers detect unreliability through internal contradictions, discrepancies between the narrator's account and other characters' views, stylistic clues, and conflicts between the narrator's norms and those implied by the work as a whole. The gap between narrator and implied author creates dramatic irony and invites rereading.
