Jet streams are fast flowing, narrow, meandering air currents in the atmosphere of some planets, including Earth. On Earth, the main jet streams are located near the altitude of the tropopause and are westerly winds (flowing west to east). Jet streams form where large temperature gradients exist, principally at the boundaries between polar and temperate air masses and between subtropical and temperate air.

Types

The strongest jet streams are the polar jets, located around 9–12 km above sea level at latitudes of roughly 40–60°, and the higher, somewhat weaker subtropical jets at about 10–16 km and around 30° latitude. Each hemisphere has one polar and one subtropical jet. Jet streams wander in latitude and develop meanders called Rossby waves, which strongly influence mid-latitude weather.

Discovery

Jet streams were first noticed in the 1920s by Japanese meteorologist Wasaburo Oishi, who tracked pilot balloons near Mount Fuji. They became widely known during the Second World War, when high-flying Allied bomber crews encountered unexpectedly strong headwinds and tailwinds over Japan and Europe.

Effects

Jet streams steer storm systems and can channel heat waves, cold snaps, and heavy rain. Airlines exploit the polar jet stream: eastbound flights ride the tailwind to save time and fuel, while westbound flights often route around it. Meandering of the jet stream has been linked to persistent weather patterns and extreme events, and its behavior under climate change is an active research topic.
