De Stijl

De Stijl (Dutch for "The Style"), also known as Neoplasticism, was a Dutch art and design movement founded in 1917 in Leiden. Advocates of De Stijl — including the painters Piet Mondrian and Theo van Doesburg, and the architect Gerrit Rietveld — sought to express a new utopian ideal of spiritual harmony and order through pure abstraction: straight horizontal and vertical lines, right angles, and only primary colours (red, blue, yellow) plus black, white, and grey.

Principles

De Stijl grew out of theosophically influenced ideas about cosmic order. Van Doesburg's journal De Stijl published the group's manifestos from 1918. Painters reduced form to rectangles of primary colour on white grounds, separated by black lines; architects applied the same vocabulary to buildings as arrangements of coloured planes. Mondrian called the painting doctrine "neoplasticism" and eventually left the group (1925) after van Doesburg introduced diagonal lines ("elementarism"), which Mondrian considered a betrayal of the principle of equilibrium.

Works and influence

Iconic De Stijl works include Mondrian's grid paintings such as Composition with Red, Blue and Yellow, Rietveld's Red and Blue Chair (designed 1917–18) and the Rietveld Schröder House in Utrecht (1924), now a UNESCO World Heritage Site, and J.J.P. Oud's housing at the Weissenhof Estate. The movement strongly influenced the Bauhaus, International Style architecture, graphic design, and later minimalism, and its colour-block aesthetic recurs in fashion and popular design. The journal ceased with van Doesburg's death in 1931, effectively ending the movement.
