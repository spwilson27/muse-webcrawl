Dodd–Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act

The Dodd–Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act, commonly referred to as Dodd–Frank, is a United States federal law that was enacted on July 21, 2010. The law overhauled financial regulation in the aftermath of the Great Recession and was intended to promote financial stability and end "too big to fail" bailouts.

Provisions

The act created the Financial Stability Oversight Council to monitor systemic risk, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau to regulate consumer financial products, and the Orderly Liquidation Authority for resolving failing large firms. The Volcker Rule restricted proprietary trading by banks, and the act imposed higher capital and stress-testing requirements on systemically important institutions, along with regulation of derivatives through central clearing and exchange trading.

Reactions and amendments

Supporters argued the law reduced the risk of another financial crisis, while critics maintained it burdened smaller banks and entrenched large institutions. In 2018, the Economic Growth, Regulatory Relief, and Consumer Protection Act raised the threshold for enhanced supervision from $50 billion to $250 billion in assets, partially rolling back Dodd–Frank.
