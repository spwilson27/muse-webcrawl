Mitosis


Image placeholder: the stages of mitosis

Mitosis is a part of the cell cycle in which one eukaryotic cell divides its duplicated chromosomes into two identical sets within two daughter nuclei. It is generally followed by cytokinesis, which divides the cytoplasm and organelles, producing two genetically identical daughter cells. Mitosis is the basis of growth, repair, and asexual reproduction in multicellular organisms. The process was described in detail by Walther Flemming in 1882.

Phases

Prophase: chromosomes condense and become visible; the mitotic spindle begins to form from microtubules, and the nucleolus disappears. Prometaphase: the nuclear envelope breaks down, and spindle microtubules attach to chromosomes at their kinetochores. Metaphase: chromosomes align along the metaphase plate at the cell's equator; the spindle checkpoint verifies correct attachment. Anaphase: sister chromatids separate and are pulled toward opposite poles as kinetochore microtubules shorten. Telophase: two new nuclear envelopes form around the separated chromosome sets, and the chromosomes decondense. Cytokinesis — cleavage furrow formation in animals, cell plate formation in plants — then divides the cell in two.

Regulation

Progression through mitosis is driven by cyclin-dependent kinases, especially CDK1 bound to cyclin B (the maturation-promoting factor), and is monitored by checkpoints that delay anaphase until every chromosome is properly attached to the spindle. Errors in chromosome segregation can produce aneuploidy, an abnormal chromosome number, which is associated with cancer and with disorders such as Down syndrome when it occurs in meiosis.

Mitosis and meiosis

Mitosis produces diploid daughter cells identical to the parent, whereas meiosis, which occurs only in reproductive cells, halves the chromosome number and reshuffles genetic material, producing haploid gametes. Together with interphase (G1, S, and G2 phases, in which DNA is replicated), mitosis makes up the cell division cycle.
