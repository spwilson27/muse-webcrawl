The Nerva–Antonine dynasty was a dynasty of seven Roman emperors who ruled the Roman Empire from AD 96 to 192: Nerva (96–98), Trajan (98–117), Hadrian (117–138), Antoninus Pius (138–161), Marcus Aurelius (161–180, with Lucius Verus as co-emperor until 169) and Commodus (177–192). The era of the first five is famously praised — Edward Gibbon called it the period "during which the condition of the human race was most happy and prosperous" — and they are conventionally known as the Five Good Emperors.

Adoptive succession

The dynasty's distinctive feature was adoptive succession: from Nerva through Marcus Aurelius, each emperor (lacking a surviving adult son, or choosing the practice) adopted a capable successor, generally presented as a meritocratic alternative to hereditary rule. Historians note the pattern was partly luck and necessity, but it delivered remarkably stable government for over 80 years. The break with the practice — Marcus Aurelius's elevation of his biological son Commodus — was followed by Commodus's tyranny and assassination, ending the dynasty amid civil war in the Year of the Five Emperors (193).

Highlights

Under Trajan the Empire reached its greatest territorial extent after the Dacian and Parthian wars. Hadrian consolidated the frontiers (including Hadrian's Wall in Britain) and toured the provinces. Antoninus Pius's long reign was peaceful and prosperous. Marcus Aurelius, the philosopher-emperor, spent much of his reign fighting the Marcomannic Wars on the Danube and facing the Antonine Plague, recording his Stoic reflections in the Meditations.
