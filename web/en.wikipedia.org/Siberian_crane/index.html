The Siberian crane (Leucogeranus leucogeranus), also known as the Siberian white crane or the snow crane, is a bird of the family Gruidae, the cranes. They are distinctive among the cranes: adults are nearly all snowy white, except for their black primary feathers, which are visible only in flight, and a naked red face and legs. They are among the most threatened of the cranes and, with the eastern population concentrated at a single wintering site, considered one of the world's most endangered birds.

Description

Adults of both sexes have a pure white plumage with black primaries, and a red face that extends from the bill to behind the eye, with white iris. Juveniles have feathered faces and buff plumage. The Siberian crane is the most aquatic of the cranes, spending most of its time wading in shallow water and probing in mud for food, and it has a distinctive serrated bill adapted to grasping slippery roots and small animals.

Distribution and migration

Two breeding populations are traditionally recognised. The eastern population breeds in north-eastern Siberia (Yakutia) and migrates some 5,000 km to winter overwhelmingly at Poyang Lake in China, where around 98 per cent of the species — some 3,500–4,000 birds — gather. The western/central population bred in western Siberia and wintered at Keoladeo National Park in India and in Iran; it has collapsed to near extinction, with only a single bird, known as Omid ("hope"), returning to Iran in recent years. During migration the cranes depend on a chain of staging wetlands along the flyway in Russia, Kazakhstan, and China.

Behaviour and ecology

Siberian cranes feed mainly on the tubers of aquatic plants, especially Vallisneria, in winter, and on plant material, insects, small rodents, and fish on the breeding grounds. Pairs mate for life and perform elaborate unison calls and dancing displays. Two eggs are usually laid, though typically only one chick survives. The species can live for decades in captivity.

Conservation

The Siberian crane is classified as Critically Endangered. Threats include habitat loss and hydrological changes at Poyang Lake — where proposed damming could alter the shallow wetlands on which nearly the whole species depends — hunting along migration routes, and drought at staging sites. Conservation efforts coordinated under the Convention on Migratory Species and the International Crane Foundation include captive breeding, wetland protection along the flyway, and (for the western population) releases guided by ultralight aircraft.

Cultural significance

The crane holds symbolic importance in the cultures of Siberia, Central Asia, India, and China, representing longevity and good fortune; the Yakut (Sakha) people regard the Siberian crane — the kыталык — as a sacred bird.
