Nutation

Nutation (from Latin nūtātiō, "nodding") is a small, periodic rocking or swaying in the axis of rotation of a spinning body, superimposed on its slower precession. In astronomy, the term usually refers to the nutation of Earth's axis, a nodding motion driven mainly by the gravitational torque of the Moon on Earth's equatorial bulge as the Moon's orbit itself precesses.

Astronomical nutation

Earth's nutation has a principal period of 18.6 years — the period of the precession of the Moon's orbital nodes — with an amplitude of about 17.24 arcseconds in longitude and 9.21 arcseconds in obliquity, plus many smaller terms arising from the Sun and planets. The astronomer James Bradley discovered the effect after two decades of precise stellar observations, announcing it in 1748; it provided early evidence for the Moon's gravitational influence on Earth's orientation. Modern values are defined by the IAU 2000A nutation model, which contains more than a thousand periodic terms and is essential for precise pointing of telescopes and spacecraft navigation.

In mechanics

For a gyroscope or spinning top, nutation is the transient wobble of the axis that occurs when the spin is disturbed; viscous friction usually damps it, leaving pure precession. The motion is one of the three Euler-angle motions of a rigid body, alongside precession and intrinsic rotation (spin).

Other uses

In botany, nutation (circumnutation) is the bending, circular, or oscillatory growth movement of plant organs such as tendrils and shoot tips, described by Charles Darwin in The Power of Movement in Plants (1880).
