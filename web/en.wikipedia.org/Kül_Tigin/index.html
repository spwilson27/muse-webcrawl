Kul Tigin (Old Turkic: 𐰚𐰇𐰠 𐱅𐰃𐰏𐰤; also spelled Kül Tigin, meaning roughly "glorious prince") was a prince and supreme military commander of the Second Turkic Khaganate. The younger son of the khaganate's founder, Ilterish Qaghan, and brother of Bilge Khagan, he is celebrated as the warrior-hero of the Old Turkic world, and the great runic inscription erected for him in 732 is one of the most important sources for early Turkic history and language.

Life

Kul Tigin was born in 685 and orphaned as a child when Ilterish died in 691. Raised in the court of his uncle Qapaghan Qaghan, he distinguished himself in battle from the age of sixteen, fighting the Oghuz, the Kyrgyz, the Türgesh and Tang Chinese forces. When Qapaghan was killed in 716 and a succession crisis followed, Kul Tigin led a coup that placed his elder brother Bilge on the throne as khagan; he himself became the realm's leading general and, in effect, co-ruler.

His campaigns secured the khaganate's frontiers: he defeated rebel tribes at battles recounted in loving detail by his inscription, routed the Türgesh and Karluks in the west, and campaigned as far as the Iron Gate near Samarkand. He died in 731, aged forty-six, mourned — his brother's inscription says — by envoys from China, Tibet, the Arabs and Byzantium.

The Kul Tigin inscription

In 732 Bilge Khagan erected a monumental stele in the Khöshöö-Tsaidam valley of the Orkhon river in present-day Mongolia. Carved in Turkic runes (with a Chinese text on the reverse commissioned by the Tang emperor), it narrates the history of the Turks from creation to Kul Tigin's death, warns future generations against Chinese seduction, and praises the dead prince's deeds. Together with the Bilge Khagan and Tonyukuk inscriptions, it forms the Orkhon corpus, deciphered in 1893 by Vilhelm Thomsen and the oldest extensive literature in any Turkic language.

Legacy

Kul Tigin remains a national hero in Turkey, Mongolia and the Turkic world; a famous sculpted head, believed to be his portrait from his memorial complex, is displayed in Mongolia, and his name appears on institutions, streets and a Turkish university in Kyrgyzstan (Kyrgyz-Turkish Manas University's partner commemorations).
