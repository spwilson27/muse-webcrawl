Paul Kruger (1825–1904)

Stephanus Johannes Paulus Kruger (10 October 1825 – 14 July 1904), better known as Paul Kruger and affectionately as Oom Paul ("Uncle Paul"), was one of the dominant political and military figures in 19th-century South Africa. He was President of the South African Republic (or Transvaal) from 1883 to 1900, and is remembered primarily for the role he played in the events leading up to and during the Second Boer War (1899–1902).

Early life

Kruger was born at Bulhoek, near Steynsburg in the Cape Colony, to tenant farmers Casper and Elsabe Kruger, of Prussian descent. In 1836 his family joined the Great Trek, the migration of Dutch-speaking colonists eastwards out of the British-ruled Cape. He survived malaria and pneumonia on the journey and settled in what later became the Transvaal. At sixteen he served as a commando in clashes with the Ndebele, and he was twice married — first to Maria du Plessis, who died young, then to Gezina du Plessis, his lifelong wife.

Rise to power

Kruger became a leading figure in the South African Republic, rising through the commandant-generalship during the civil wars of the 1860s. He negotiated with the British after the 1877 annexation of the Transvaal, leading deputations to London that failed to reverse it. In 1880 he helped form a triumvirate government that declared independence, triggering the First Boer War (1880–81), in which Boer forces defeated the British and regained autonomy under the Pretoria and London Conventions.

Presidency and the road to war

Elected president in 1883 and re-elected three times, Kruger presided over the Transvaal gold rush following the discovery of gold on the Witwatersrand in 1886. The influx of foreigners (Uitlanders) and British imperial ambitions under Cecil Rhodes poisoned relations. Kruger resisted British pressure, signed a defensive alliance with the Orange Free State, and armed his republic. The Jameson Raid of 1895–96 deepened the crisis; in October 1899 war broke out.

Exile and death

As British forces advanced, Kruger left South Africa in October 1900, travelling on the Dutch warship Gelderland to Europe, where he lobbied unsuccessfully for intervention. He died in Clarens, Switzerland, on 14 July 1904, never returning home. He was buried in Pretoria later that year. The Kruger National Park and the town of Krugersdorp are named after him, and his statue stands in Church Square, Pretoria.
