Leo IX, the reforming pope whose pontificate preceded the East–West Schism.

Pope Leo IX (born Bruno of Egisheim-Dagsburg; 21 June 1002 – 19 April 1054) was head of the Catholic Church and ruler of the Papal States from 12 February 1049 until his death. A German aristocrat and bishop of Toul, he was one of the most important popes of the Middle Ages: his energetic campaign against simony and clerical marriage inaugurated the great 11th-century reform of the Church, and his pontificate ended on the eve of the East–West Schism of 1054. He is venerated as a saint in the Catholic Church.

Early life and election

Bruno was born at Eguisheim in Alsace into the family of the counts of Nordgau, closely related to the Salian emperors. Educated at Toul, he became bishop there in 1027 and served Conrad II and Henry III as a diplomat and soldier-bishop. After the deposition of the scandal-tainted rival popes of 1046–1048, Emperor Henry III nominated Bruno at Worms in December 1048. Bruno famously accepted only on condition that the clergy and people of Rome elect him freely; travelling to Rome as a humble pilgrim, he was acclaimed and enthroned as Leo IX on 12 February 1049.

Reform

Leo brought to Rome a circle of reformers — including Humbert of Silva Candida, Frederick of Lorraine (later Pope Stephen IX), and the monk Hildebrand (later Pope Gregory VII) — and set about purifying the clergy. Insisting that reform begin at the centre, he travelled incessantly: holding synods across Italy, France, and Germany (including the councils of Reims and Mainz in 1049), deposing bishops guilty of buying their offices, and denouncing clerical marriage as concubinage. His legates carried the campaign as far as England and the Slavic lands. This model of an activist, centralising papacy directly prepared the Gregorian Reform of the later 11th century.

Normans and the road to schism

In southern Italy the pope confronted the encroaching Normans, who were seizing papal and Byzantine territory. Leading an army in person, Leo was defeated at the Battle of Civitate on 18 June 1053 and held in honourable captivity at Benevento for some nine months, until March 1054. Meanwhile his dispute with the Byzantine patriarch Michael Cerularius over Latin practices and papal primacy escalated: Leo sent Cardinal Humbert to Constantinople with a bull drafted in early 1054. Leo died in Rome on 19 April 1054 — three months before Humbert, his authority arguably lapsed, laid a bull of excommunication on the altar of Hagia Sophia (16 July 1054), the act traditionally taken to open the East–West Schism.
