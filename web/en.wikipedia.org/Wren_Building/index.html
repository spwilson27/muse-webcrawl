Wren Building

The Wren Building is the signature building of the College of William & Mary in Williamsburg, Virginia. Constructed between 1695 and 1700, it is the oldest college building still in use in the United States and anchors the college's Historic Campus. Its design is traditionally attributed to the English architect Sir Christopher Wren, though documentary evidence for his direct involvement is lacking.

History

The building was erected soon after the college received its royal charter in 1693, with the foundation laid in 1695. Thomas Jefferson studied and lived there in the 1760s. Fire damaged or gutted the interior on several occasions — in 1705, 1859 and 1862 — each time leaving the thick brick walls standing to be rebuilt within. During the American Revolution the building served as a hospital for French troops, and during the Civil War it was used by Confederate and Union forces.

Architecture

The three-storey brick building is organised around a central axis with a chapel in the south wing and the Great Hall in the north. Its Flemish-bond brickwork and symmetrical façade are among the finest surviving examples of colonial academic architecture in America. As part of the restoration of Colonial Williamsburg in the late 1920s, the building was returned to its colonial appearance under architect J. Stewart Barney's direction, guided by the Bodleian Plate, an engraved copperplate discovered at Oxford.

Use today

The Wren Building still hosts classes (particularly in religious studies), faculty offices, the Great Hall and the chapel, and traditions such as the annual Yule Log ceremony. Every William & Mary student passes through it during convocation and commencement traditions. It should not be confused with the bird family of the same name — see wren.
