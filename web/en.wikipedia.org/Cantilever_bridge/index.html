Cantilever bridge

Structural typeCantilever spans, often with a suspended central span
Typical materialStructural steel truss
Longest spanQuebec Bridge, 549 m
Notable examplesForth Bridge, Quebec Bridge, Howrah Bridge

A cantilever bridge is a bridge built using cantilevers: structures that project horizontally into space, supported on only one end. In the classic form, two cantilever arms extend from opposite piers toward the centre of the span, where they meet or support a shorter suspended span; on the shore side, anchor arms are held down by abutments or counterweights to resist the overturning forces. Because the structure can be erected outward from the piers without falsework in the water below, the cantilever principle was especially valuable for crossing deep, navigable, or fast-flowing channels.

History and examples

Simple timber cantilever bridges were built for centuries in Asia — Heinrich Gerber patented a modern articulated cantilever design in Germany in 1866. The type reached monumental scale in steel: the Forth Bridge in Scotland (1890), with two main spans of 521 metres, demonstrated the idea spectacularly. The Quebec Bridge in Canada, completed in 1917 after two catastrophic collapses during construction that killed 88 workers, remains the longest cantilever span in the world at 549 metres. Other major examples include the Howrah Bridge in Kolkata, the Commodore Barry Bridge in Pennsylvania, and Japan's Minato Bridge, one of the largest cantilever truss bridges.

Design considerations

The cantilever arrangement concentrates very large forces in the members near the piers, requiring deep trusses and substantial anchorages. Although cable-stayed and suspension designs have superseded cantilever bridges for the longest crossings, the type endures in steel truss construction, and "balanced cantilever" erection — building a concrete box-girder bridge symmetrically outward from each pier — remains a standard technique for medium spans worldwide.
