Borrelia burgdorferi is a bacterial species of the spirochete class in the genus Borrelia. It is the predominant causative agent of Lyme disease in North America, where it is transmitted to humans and other vertebrates through the bite of infected ticks of the genus Ixodes, principally the black-legged or deer tick (Ixodes scapularis) in the east and the western black-legged tick (I. pacificus) on the Pacific coast. (Related species, chiefly B. afzelii and B. garinii, cause most European cases.)

Discovery and genome

The bacterium was identified in 1981–1982 by entomologist Willy Burgdorfer and colleagues, who isolated spirochetes from ticks collected on Long Island, New York, during the investigation of the arthritis cluster around Lyme, Connecticut; the species was named in Burgdorfer's honor. Its genome, sequenced in 1997, was unusual among bacteria: a linear chromosome of roughly 910 kilobase pairs accompanied by numerous circular and linear plasmids — more than 20 in total — many of which encode surface lipoproteins essential for infection and immune evasion.

Biology and transmission

B. burgdorferi is a loosely coiled, motile spirochete 10–30 μm long that swims by means of internal flagella. It cycles between tick vectors and reservoir hosts such as the white-footed mouse; humans are incidental, dead-end hosts. The bacterium varies its outer surface protein VlsE by gene conversion, helping it evade the host immune system, and it alters protein expression (for example, OspA in the tick gut to OspC in the mammal) as it moves between hosts. Transmission generally requires the tick to remain attached for at least 36–48 hours.

Disease

Infection typically begins with the expanding "bull's-eye" rash erythema migrans and flu-like symptoms; untreated, it can progress to arthritis, carditis, and neurological involvement. Diagnosis is clinical in early disease and serological thereafter; treatment is with antibiotics such as doxycycline or ceftriaxone. A recombinant vaccine (LYMErix) was marketed in the United States from 1998 to 2002 before being withdrawn amid weak sales, and new vaccines have been in late-stage development.
