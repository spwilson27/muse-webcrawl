De Bruijn sequence

In combinatorial mathematics, a de Bruijn sequence of order n on an alphabet of size k is a cyclic sequence in which every possible string of length n over the alphabet appears exactly once as a contiguous subsequence. Such a sequence has length kn, the shortest possible length with that property. The sequences are named after the Dutch mathematician Nicolaas Govert de Bruijn, who studied them in 1946, though examples were known earlier, including in Sanskrit prosody.

Example

For a binary alphabet {0, 1} and n = 3, one de Bruijn sequence is 00010111. Read cyclically, its eight consecutive triples are 000, 001, 010, 101, 011, 111, 110 and 100 — every possible three-bit string exactly once.

Construction

De Bruijn sequences can be constructed by finding an Eulerian cycle in a de Bruijn graph, whose vertices are the strings of length n − 1 and whose edges represent strings of length n; equivalently, a Hamiltonian cycle in a related graph gives the same result. The number of distinct de Bruijn sequences for given k and n is (k!)kn−1 / kn.

Applications

De Bruijn sequences are used in rotary position encoders, where reading a short window of the sequence identifies an angular position; in card tricks based on cyclic codes; in neuroscience experiments; and, via de Bruijn graphs, in genome assembly from short DNA reads.
