A tornado is a violently rotating column of air that is in contact with both the surface of the Earth and a cumulonimbus cloud, or, in rare cases, the base of a cumulus cloud. It is often visible as a funnel of condensed water droplets, dust and debris extending from the cloud base to the ground. Tornadoes are among the most intense atmospheric phenomena on Earth: although usually a few hundred metres across and lasting minutes, the strongest produce the fastest winds known at the surface and can destroy well-built structures.

Formation

Most strong tornadoes form within supercell thunderstorms. Wind shear — winds changing speed and direction with height — creates horizontal rotation in the lower atmosphere; a strong updraft tilts this rotation into the vertical, forming a mesocyclone. Under conditions still not fully understood, rotation concentrates downward to the ground as a tornado. Weaker tornadoes can also form along gust fronts and squall lines (gustnadoes and QLCS tornadoes), and over water as waterspouts. Tornadoes occur on every continent except Antarctica, but the central United States — "Tornado Alley" — experiences the most, owing to the collision of warm moist Gulf air, dry air from the Rockies and cold polar air.

Intensity and damage

Since 2007, tornadoes in the United States have been rated on the Enhanced Fujita (EF) scale from EF0 (light damage) to EF5 (incredible damage, winds estimated above 200 mph / 322 km/h). The widest tornado on record, at El Reno, Oklahoma in 2013, reached 2.6 miles (4.2 km) across. The Tri-State Tornado of 18 March 1925 remains the deadliest in US history (695 deaths), while the 1989 Daulatpur–Saturia tornado in Bangladesh is the deadliest worldwide, killing an estimated 1,300 people.

Detection and safety

Doppler weather radar detects mesocyclones and tornadic debris signatures, enabling warnings typically minutes in advance; storm spotters and chasers supplement radar observations. Safety guidance is to shelter in a basement or small interior room on the lowest floor, away from windows; tornado forecasting is led in the US by the Storm Prediction Center, which issues outlooks and watches.
