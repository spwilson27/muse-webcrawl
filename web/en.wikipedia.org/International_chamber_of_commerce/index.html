The International Chamber of Commerce (ICC; French: Chambre de commerce internationale) is the largest and most representative business organization in the world. Founded in 1919 in the aftermath of the First World War, its network comprises millions of companies, chambers of commerce and business associations across more than 170 countries.

History

The ICC was founded in 1919 at a conference in Atlantic City, New Jersey, by business leaders from Belgium, Britain, France, Italy and the United States who styled themselves "merchants of peace". Its first president was Étienne Clémentel, a former French minister of commerce. The organization moved its headquarters to Paris in 1923, the same year it established its International Court of Arbitration.

Activities

The ICC promotes international trade and investment and helps businesses meet the challenges and opportunities of globalization. It is best known for a number of widely used instruments, including:

  • Incoterms — standard trade terms (such as FOB, CIF and EXW) defining the responsibilities of buyers and sellers in international transactions, first published in 1936 and revised periodically.
  • UCP 600 — the Uniform Customs and Practice for Documentary Credits, the rules governing letters of credit in trade finance.
  • International Court of Arbitration — the world's leading arbitral institution, administering commercial disputes between parties from different countries.
  • Model contracts and rules on marketing, banking and anti-corruption.

Structure and advocacy

Members join through national committees in their own countries. The ICC has held observer status at the United Nations since 1946 and is the only private-sector organization with that standing at the UN General Assembly. It also provides the business voice in forums such as the G20, the World Trade Organization and the UN climate negotiations.
