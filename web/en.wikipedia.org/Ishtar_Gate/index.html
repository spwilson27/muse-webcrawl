MaterialGlazed brick (lapis lazuli-coloured glaze, with gold and white rosettes)

The Ishtar Gate was the eighth gate to the inner city of Babylon, built around 575 BC on the orders of King Nebuchadnezzar II. Dedicated to the Babylonian goddess Ishtar, it formed the ceremonial entrance to the city along the Processional Way, used during the New Year festival.

Description

The gate was faced with brilliant blue glazed bricks — intended to evoke lapis lazuli — and decorated with alternating rows of relief animals: bulls, representing the storm god Adad, and sirrush (dragons) representing the chief god Marduk. Lions, sacred to Ishtar, lined the Processional Way leading to the gate. The structure was a double gate roughly 15 metres high; a dedicatory inscription by Nebuchadnezzar recorded his building works in cuneiform.

Excavation and reconstruction

German archaeologist Robert Koldewey excavated Babylon between 1904 and 1914 and recovered the gate's foundations and thousands of glazed brick fragments. Under an agreement with the Iraqi authorities, the materials were shipped to Berlin, where the front, smaller gate was reconstructed in the Pergamon Museum, opening in 1930. The reconstruction stands about 14 metres high and 30 metres wide.

Legacy and disputes

The Ishtar Gate is one of the most famous surviving monuments of the Neo-Babylonian Empire. Iraq has periodically called for the return of the gate and other antiquities removed during the colonial era. A full-size replica stands near the original site at Babylon in Iraq. Lions and brick fragments from the gate are held by museums in Istanbul, Detroit, Paris, Copenhagen and elsewhere.
