Plasmodium is a genus of unicellular eukaryotes that are obligate parasites of vertebrates and insects. The life cycles of Plasmodium species involve development in a blood-feeding insect host, which then injects parasites into a vertebrate host during a blood meal. The parasites grow within a vertebrate body tissue (often the liver) before entering the bloodstream to infect red blood cells. The destruction of red blood cells can result in the disease known as malaria. During this infection, some parasites are picked up by a blood-feeding insect, continuing the life cycle.

Species and hosts

More than 200 species of Plasmodium have been described, infecting mammals, birds, and reptiles. Five species regularly infect humans: P. falciparum, P. vivax, P. ovale, P. malariae, and the zoonotic P. knowlesi. P. falciparum causes the most severe and deadly form of malaria, while P. vivax and P. ovale can form dormant liver stages (hypnozoites) that reactivate months or years later.

Life cycle

The parasite is transmitted by female Anopheles mosquitoes. Sporozoites injected during a blood meal travel to the liver, where they multiply asexually (exoerythrocytic schizogony). Merozoites released from the liver invade red blood cells, undergoing repeated cycles of asexual multiplication (erythrocytic schizogony) that cause the periodic fevers of malaria. Some parasites develop into sexual forms, gametocytes, which are taken up by a mosquito and complete sexual reproduction (sporogony) in the insect's gut.

History

The parasites were first observed in the blood of malaria patients by Charles Louis Alphonse Laveran in 1880. In 1897, Ronald Ross demonstrated that mosquitoes transmit the parasite, work for which he received the Nobel Prize in 1902. The genus name, coined in 1885 by Marchiafava and Celli, refers to the multinucleate, amorphous appearance of some stages.
