Harlequinade

GenreTheatrical entertainment, pantomime
OriginCommedia dell'arte; 18th-century English theatre
Key figuresHarlequin, Columbine, Pantaloon, Clown

The harlequinade is a comic theatrical form that formed the slapstick second half of the eighteenth- and nineteenth-century English pantomime. It dramatised the elopement of Harlequin and Columbine, pursued by her father Pantaloon and his servants, including the Clown.

Origins

The form drew on the Italian commedia dell'arte. In London, John Rich popularised the harlequinade in the early 1700s, performing Harlequin himself under the name "Lun". Performances combined dance, acrobatics, silent mime, and spectacular transformation scenes achieved with trapdoors and trick scenery.

Development

Joseph Grimaldi, the great Clown of the early nineteenth century, expanded the clown's role and made the harlequinade the most popular part of the evening. Over time the opening "pantomime" story shrank, and by the late Victorian era the harlequinade dwindled into a brief afterpiece before disappearing.

Legacy

The harlequinade's stock characters, chases, and slapstick business passed into music hall, vaudeville, and modern pantomime tradition, where traces survive in contemporary British Christmas pantomimes.
