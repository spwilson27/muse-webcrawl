Reid technique

The Reid technique is a method of interrogation developed in the United States by police officer and polygraph expert John E. Reid in the 1950s and 1960s. It became the most widely taught interrogation method in North American law enforcement.

Procedure

The method begins with a non-accusatorial "behaviour analysis interview" to assess the suspect's truthfulness, followed — if the investigator concludes the suspect is deceptive — by a nine-step accusatorial interrogation. Steps include direct confrontation with assertions of guilt, theme development that offers morally acceptable rationalisations for the crime, handling denials, and presenting alternatives, one incriminating and one more sympathetic.

Criticism

Critics argue the technique is coercive and can produce false confessions, particularly from juveniles and vulnerable suspects. Research on false confessions has led some agencies and jurisdictions to restrict or abandon it in favour of information-gathering models such as PEACE.
