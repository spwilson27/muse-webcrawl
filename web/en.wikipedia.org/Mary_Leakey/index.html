Mary Leakey

British paleoanthropologist (1913–1996)

Mary Douglas Leakey (6 February 1913 – 9 December 1996) was a British paleoanthropologist who made some of the most important discoveries in the study of human evolution. Working largely in East Africa alongside her husband, Louis Leakey, she uncovered fossil evidence that pushed back the known antiquity of the human lineage and demonstrated that early hominins were considerably older than previously believed.

Early life

Born Mary Douglas Nicol in London, she spent much of her childhood travelling in France and Italy, where her father, an artist, took the family on painting trips. A visit to the prehistoric cave sites of the Dordogne kindled her interest in archaeology. She never completed a formal university degree, but she taught herself excavation and illustration, becoming a highly skilled field archaeologist and draughtswoman whose drawings of stone tools remain models of the craft.

Olduvai Gorge

From the 1930s the Leakeys worked at Olduvai Gorge in Tanzania. In July 1959 Mary discovered the robust australopithecine skull she nicknamed the "Nutcracker Man" (Zinjanthropus boisei, now Paranthropus boisei), dated to about 1.75 million years old — far older than expected for such a hominin, and a discovery that transformed the field and attracted major funding. She also developed the standard classification of the Oldowan stone tool industry found at the gorge.

Laetoli footprints

In 1978 Leakey's team at Laetoli, Tanzania, uncovered a trail of fossilised hominin footprints preserved in volcanic ash and dated to about 3.66 million years ago. The footprints provided the earliest direct evidence of bipedal walking in the human lineage, showing that upright walking long predated large brains. Many regard the Laetoli footprints as her most significant discovery.

Legacy

Mary Leakey continued fieldwork into her seventies and wrote several books, including Olduvai Gorge: My Search for Early Man (1979) and her autobiography Disclosing the Past (1984). Her sons, including Richard Leakey, carried the family's work into the next generation.
