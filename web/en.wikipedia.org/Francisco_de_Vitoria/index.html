Francisco de Vitoria

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Francisco de Vitoria OP (c. 1483 – 12 August 1546) was a Spanish Roman Catholic philosopher, theologian and jurist of the Renaissance, and the founder of the tradition in philosophy known as the School of Salamanca. He is noted especially for his contributions to the theory of just war and to the foundations of international law, earning him recognition as a "father of international law".

Life

Vitoria joined the Dominican Order in Burgos and studied and taught at the College Saint-Jacques in Paris, where he edited works of Aquinas and absorbed humanist and nominalist currents. In 1526 he won the prime chair of theology at the University of Salamanca, where his lecture courses (relectiones) attracted large audiences and where he taught until his death.

On the Indies and the law of war

In De Indis et de Iure Belli Relectiones (delivered 1539, published posthumously in 1557) Vitoria addressed the Spanish conquest of the Americas. He rejected the claims that the indigenous peoples of the New World lacked dominion over their lands merely because they were not Christians, that the Pope could grant their territories to Spain, or that refusal of the faith justified conquest. The Indians, he argued, possessed true ownership and reason, and could be dispossessed only on limited grounds such as violations of the law of nations — rights of travel, trade and peaceful preaching. In De Iure Belli he systematized conditions for just war that influenced Grotius and modern law of armed conflict.

Legacy

Through his students — among them Domingo de Soto and Melchor Cano — and his writings on law, rights, economics and the limits of imperial power, Vitoria shaped the School of Salamanca and early modern thought on international society. A hall at the United Nations headquarters in Geneva associated with lectures on international law bears his name.
