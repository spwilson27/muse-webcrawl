Policy debate

Policy debate is an American form of debate competition, usually for high school and college students. Policy debate advocates argue for and against a resolution that typically calls for policy change by the United States federal government. Teams of two debaters — the Affirmative, which supports the resolution by presenting a specific plan, and the Negative, which opposes it — alternate giving speeches that include evidence read from published sources, extensions of earlier arguments, and rebuttal.

Structure

A standard policy debate round consists of eight speeches: four constructive speeches in which arguments are introduced and four rebuttals, with cross-examination periods following each constructive speech. The affirmative case typically presents harms in the status quo, inherency (barriers to solving them), a plan, and solvency (the plan's ability to solve the harms), while claiming net advantages. The negative responds with on-case arguments and off-case positions such as disadvantages, counterplans, topicality arguments and kritiks.

Style and practice

Policy debate is known for its rapid delivery, colloquially called "spreading" (speed reading), and its heavy reliance on researched evidence, which debaters organise into files and read as "cards". Judging paradigms vary; many debates are decided on a line-by-line evaluation recorded on a flowsheet. The annual resolution is selected by the National Speech and Debate Association and other governing bodies.

History

Policy debate developed in American universities in the early twentieth century and spread to high schools after the Second World War. The National Debate Tournament, founded in 1947, is the principal college championship.
