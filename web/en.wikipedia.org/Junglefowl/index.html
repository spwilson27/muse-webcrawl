Junglefowl are the four living species of birds in the genus Gallus, in the pheasant family Phasianidae. They are native to South and Southeast Asia. The red junglefowl (Gallus gallus) is the primary wild ancestor of the domestic chicken, one of the most numerous domesticated animals in the world.

Species

The four junglefowl species are:

  • Red junglefowl (Gallus gallus) — widespread from northern Pakistan and India to southern China and Indonesia.
  • Grey junglefowl (Gallus sonneratii) — peninsular India; also contributed genes to the domestic chicken, including the yellow-skin trait.
  • Green junglefowl (Gallus varius) — Java and nearby Indonesian islands.
  • Sri Lankan junglefowl (Gallus lafayettii) — endemic to Sri Lanka, where it is the national bird.

Description and behaviour

Male junglefowl (cocks) are larger and far more colourful than females (hens), with iridescent plumage, fleshy combs and wattles, and long arching tail feathers. Females are camouflaged in browns for nesting on the ground. Junglefowl are omnivorous, scratching in leaf litter for seeds, insects and small invertebrates. They roost in trees at night and are capable of short bursts of flight. Males crow to advertise territory and status.

Domestication

Genetic studies confirm the red junglefowl as the principal ancestor of domestic chickens, domesticated several thousand years ago in Southeast Asia, with later contributions from the grey junglefowl and possibly other species. Junglefowl hybridise readily with domestic chickens where their ranges overlap, which is a conservation concern for wild populations.
