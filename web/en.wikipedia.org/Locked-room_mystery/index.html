Locked-room mystery

The locked room: a crime seemingly impossible to commit or escape

The locked-room mystery (or impossible crime) is a subgenre of detective fiction in which a crime — almost always murder — is committed in circumstances under which it appears impossible for the perpetrator to have entered or left the scene. The classic form involves a victim found dead inside a room locked and bolted from the inside, with no other means of entry or exit.

History

The earliest canonical example is Edgar Allan Poe's "The Murders in the Rue Morgue" (1841), in which C. Auguste Dupin solves the killing of two women in a room locked from within. The genre was developed by writers including Israel Zangwill (The Big Bow Mystery, 1891), Gaston Leroux (The Mystery of the Yellow Room, 1907), and G. K. Chesterton.

The acknowledged master of the form is John Dickson Carr, whose The Three Coffins (1935; also published as The Hollow Man) contains the famous "locked-room lecture," in which Dr Gideon Fell systematically classifies the possible methods by which such crimes can be committed — mechanical devices, illusion and misdirection, hidden passages, delayed-action mechanisms, and imposture, among others. Other notable practitioners include Clayton Rawson, Hake Talbot (Rim of the Pit, 1944), and Soji Shimada, a leader of the Japanese shin honkaku school.

Characteristics

Locked-room stories are a pure form of the "fair play" whodunit puzzle: the interest lies less in discovering who committed the crime than in how it could have been done. Solutions generally fall into categories such as the room not truly being sealed, the murder occurring before or after the room was sealed, mechanical or string-based devices, or the victim being killed by some delayed or natural mechanism disguised as murder.
