Glycation

Glycation is the covalent attachment of a sugar to a protein, lipid or nucleic acid molecule without the controlling action of an enzyme. It contrasts with glycosylation, the enzyme-directed attachment of sugars that is essential to normal protein function. Glycation is a haphazard process that impairs the functioning of biomolecules and is implicated in ageing and in the complications of diabetes mellitus.

Chemistry

Glycation begins when a reducing sugar such as glucose reacts with a free amino group of a protein to form an unstable Schiff base, which rearranges into a more stable Amadori product — the blood test HbA1c measures the Amadori product formed on haemoglobin. Over weeks to years, further oxidation, dehydration and cross-linking reactions convert these early products into a heterogeneous group of compounds called advanced glycation end-products (AGEs), such as carboxymethyllysine and the fluorescent crosslink pentosidine. The same family of reactions between sugars and amino acids during cooking is the Maillard reaction, responsible for the browning and flavour of cooked foods.

Biological effects

AGEs accumulate in long-lived proteins such as collagen, stiffening tissues and blood vessels. They also bind to the receptor RAGE, triggering inflammatory signalling. Chronic high blood glucose in diabetes accelerates glycation, and AGE accumulation is linked to diabetic kidney disease, retinopathy, neuropathy and cardiovascular disease, as well as to aspects of normal ageing and neurodegenerative disease.

Dietary AGEs

Foods cooked at high temperature by grilling, frying or roasting contain substantial AGEs formed by the Maillard reaction. A proportion of dietary AGEs is absorbed, and their contribution to disease is an active area of research; moist, lower-temperature cooking methods such as steaming and boiling produce far fewer.
