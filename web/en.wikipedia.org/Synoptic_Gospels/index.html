The Synoptic Gospels are the first three gospels of the New Testament — Matthew, Mark, and Luke. They are called "synoptic" (from the Greek syn, "together", and opsis, "view") because they include many of the same stories, often in the same sequence and sometimes in identical wording, so that they can be laid out in parallel columns and "seen together". The term was introduced by Johann Jakob Griesbach, who published a synopsis of the three gospels in 1776.

Shared material

Mark contains 661 verses, of which about 600 appear in Matthew and about 350 in Luke; Matthew and Luke also share some 200 verses of teaching material — sayings of Jesus such as much of the Sermon on the Mount — that are absent from Mark. Each synoptic gospel also contains material unique to itself: for example, only Matthew has the visit of the Magi, and only Luke has the parables of the Good Samaritan and the Prodigal Son. The Gospel of John differs so greatly in structure, chronology, and style that it is not counted among the Synoptics.

The Synoptic problem

The pattern of agreements and disagreements poses the "Synoptic problem": what literary relationship explains them? The majority scholarly view, the two-source hypothesis, holds that Mark was written first and used by Matthew and Luke, who independently combined Mark with a lost sayings source called Q. Rival proposals include the Augustinian hypothesis (Matthew first, then Mark, then Luke, in canonical order), the Farrer hypothesis (Mark first; Luke used Matthew, no Q), and oral-tradition explanations.
