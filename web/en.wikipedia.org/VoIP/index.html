Voice over IP

Voice over Internet Protocol (VoIP), also called IP telephony, is a method and group of technologies for delivering voice communications and multimedia sessions over Internet Protocol networks, such as the Internet. Instead of the circuit-switched public telephone network, VoIP converts the voice signal into digital data packets, transmits them over packet-switched networks, and reassembles them at the destination. It underlies services from enterprise phone systems to consumer apps such as Skype, Zoom, WhatsApp calls and FaceTime Audio.

How it works

A VoIP endpoint digitizes speech with a codec — compressing it from 64 kbit/s (G.711) down to a few kbit/s with efficient codecs such as Opus — and sends it in RTP packets. A signalling protocol, most commonly the Session Initiation Protocol (SIP), sets up, manages and tears down calls, while directories and registrars locate users. Gateways connect VoIP networks to the traditional PSTN so calls can reach ordinary phone numbers. Quality depends on bandwidth, latency, jitter and packet loss, and is managed with quality-of-service techniques.

History and adoption

Early experiments date to the 1970s ARPANET, and the first commercial internet phone software, VocalTec's Internet Phone, appeared in 1995. Through the 2000s, falling broadband costs let businesses replace PBX systems with IP telephony, while services such as Skype (2003) popularized free computer-to-computer calling. Mobile VoIP over Wi-Fi and 4G/5G — including carrier voice-over-LTE — has since made packet voice the default for much of the world's telephony, and legacy circuit-switched networks are being retired in many countries.

Advantages and issues

VoIP typically costs less than conventional telephony, integrates voice with video and messaging, and makes features like conferencing and voicemail-to-email trivial. Its drawbacks include dependence on power and internet connectivity, variable call quality, difficulties with precise caller location for emergency calls, and security concerns such as eavesdropping and toll fraud, mitigated by encryption (SRTP, TLS).
