Cirrhosis
DefinitionEnd-stage liver fibrosis with regenerative nodules
Major causesAlcohol, viral hepatitis B and C, metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease
ComplicationsAscites, variceal bleeding, hepatic encephalopathy, hepatocellular carcinoma

Cirrhosis is the late stage of chronic liver disease in which healthy liver tissue is replaced by scar tissue (fibrosis) and regenerative nodules, distorting the organ's architecture and blood flow. The word comes from the Greek kirrhos, "tawny", describing the colour of the diseased liver, a term introduced by René Laennec.

Causes

Worldwide the leading causes are chronic hepatitis B and C infection, long-term alcohol misuse, and fatty liver disease associated with obesity and diabetes. Less common causes include autoimmune hepatitis, primary biliary cholangitis, haemochromatosis and Wilson's disease.

Course and complications

Cirrhosis may remain compensated and symptom-free for years. Decompensation begins with portal hypertension and liver failure: ascites, bleeding from oesophageal varices, hepatic encephalopathy, jaundice, coagulopathy and increased susceptibility to infection. Cirrhosis also greatly raises the risk of hepatocellular carcinoma. Severity is graded with the Child–Pugh score and the MELD score, the latter used to prioritise liver transplantation — the only definitive cure.

Treatment

Treating the underlying cause (antivirals, abstinence from alcohol, weight loss) can halt or partly reverse disease. Complications are managed with diuretics, paracentesis, band ligation of varices, lactulose for encephalopathy, and surveillance ultrasound for cancer.
