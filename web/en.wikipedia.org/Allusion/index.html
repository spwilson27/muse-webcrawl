Allusion

An allusion is a figure of speech that references a person, place, thing, or idea of historical, cultural, literary, or political significance, without describing it in detail. It is brief and indirect, relying on the reader's prior knowledge to make the connection.

Allusion
Type: Figure of speech
From Latin: alludere, "to play with, to refer to"
Related: Intertextuality, quotation

Functions

Allusions enrich meaning economically: a single phrase such as "He met his Waterloo" evokes defeat; "a Good Samaritan" evokes compassion. Writers use biblical, classical, Shakespearean, and historical allusions to create resonance, irony, humour, or characterisation. T. S. Eliot's The Waste Land (1922) is famously dense with allusion.

Types

Scholars distinguish topical allusions (to current events), personal allusions, metaphorical allusions, and imitative allusions that evoke a predecessor's style. Unlike a quotation, an allusion does not reproduce its source verbatim and usually does not name it explicitly.

Reception

Because allusion depends on shared cultural knowledge, it can exclude readers unfamiliar with the source — a point debated in literary pedagogy — but it also rewards rereading and creates a sense of literary community.
