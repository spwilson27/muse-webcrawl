Volcanic Explosivity Index

The Volcanic Explosivity Index (VEI) is a scale used to measure the size of explosive volcanic eruptions. It was devised by Christopher G. Newhall of the United States Geological Survey and Stephen Self of the University of Hawaii in 1982. The scale is logarithmic, with each interval representing a tenfold increase in the volume of erupted material.

Definition

Volume of products, eruption cloud height, and qualitative observations (using terms ranging from "gentle" to "mega-colossal") are used to determine the explosivity value. The scale is open-ended, but eruptions larger than VEI 8 are not known in the geologic record of the last several hundred million years. The primary measure is the bulk volume of tephra (ash, pumice, and other fragmented material) ejected during the eruption.

Limitations

The VEI primarily measures explosive eruptions; effusive eruptions that produce enormous volumes of lava—such as flood basalt events—score low on the index despite their great size. The index also depends on estimates of bulk tephra volume, which for ancient eruptions must be inferred from surviving deposits and is subject to substantial uncertainty. Sulfur dioxide output, which drives climatic effects, is not directly captured by the VEI: the 1991 eruption of Pinatubo (VEI 6) injected far more sulfur into the stratosphere than the similarly rated 1982 eruption of El Chichón relative to their volumes in some comparisons, leading to different climate impacts.

Largest eruptions

The largest known eruption of the Holocene epoch was that of Mount Tambora in 1815 (VEI 7), which caused the "Year Without a Summer" in 1816. The Toba eruption of about 74,000 years ago (VEI 8) is the largest known eruption of the Quaternary period and has been linked to a global volcanic winter and a possible genetic bottleneck in human populations.
