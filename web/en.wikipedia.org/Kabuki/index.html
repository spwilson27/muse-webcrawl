Kabuki

Kabuki
Origin: Japan, early 17th century
Medium: Theatre
UNESCO: Intangible Cultural Heritage (2008)

Kabuki (歌舞伎) is a classical form of Japanese theatre that combines dramatic performance with traditional dance. Known for its highly stylized acting, elaborate make-up (kumadori), ornate costumes, and dynamic stage devices, kabuki developed in the early 1600s and remains one of Japan's principal traditional performing arts. In 2008 UNESCO inscribed it on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

History

Kabuki is traditionally credited to Izumo no Okuni, a shrine maiden who began performing comic dances and skits in Kyoto around 1603. Her all-female troupe was hugely popular, but in 1629 the Tokugawa shogunate banned women from the stage, and kabuki evolved into an all-male form in which female roles are played by specialist actors called onnagata. During the Edo period kabuki flourished as entertainment for townspeople, with plays depicting historical dramas (jidaimono), domestic stories (sewamono), and dance pieces.

Stagecraft

Kabuki theatres feature the hanamichi, a walkway extending through the audience, revolving stages, and trap lifts that allow dramatic entrances. Actors strike expressive poses called mie at climactic moments while wooden clappers accent the action. Famous plays include Kanadehon Chūshingura (the tale of the forty-seven rōnin), Yoshitsune Senbon Zakura, and Sugawara Denju Tenarai Kagami.

Modern kabuki

Leading venues include the Kabuki-za in Tokyo. Star actors such as those of the Ichikawa and Nakamura families enjoy celebrity status, and contemporary productions occasionally blend kabuki conventions with modern stories and technology.
