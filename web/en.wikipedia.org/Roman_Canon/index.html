Roman Canon

The Roman Canon, also known as the Canon of the Mass and, since the post-conciliar revision, as Eucharistic Prayer I, is the oldest eucharistic prayer of the Roman Rite of the Catholic Church. It was the only anaphora (eucharistic prayer) used in the Roman Rite from late antiquity until the liturgical reforms following the Second Vatican Council introduced additional eucharistic prayers.

History

The prayers that make up the Canon took shape over several centuries, broadly between the 4th and 7th centuries, and its text was essentially fixed by the time of Pope Gregory the Great (590–604). In the Tridentine Mass codified in 1570 it stood at the center of the liturgy, recited silently by the priest. The 1970 Roman Missal retained it as the first of several eucharistic prayers, with some modifications, including an audible recitation.

Structure

The Canon begins with the Te igitur, in which the Church offers the sacrifice for the Pope, the local bishop, and all the faithful, followed by the Memento of the living and the Communicantes, invoking the communion of saints. After the Hanc igitur and the Quam oblationem, the institution narrative (Qui pridie) recounts the Last Supper and contains the words of consecration. The anamnesis (Unde et memores), the offering (Supra quae), the epiclesis-like Supplices te rogamus, a Memento of the dead, and the Nobis quoque peccatoribus follow, concluding with the doxology Per ipsum and the Great Amen.

Use

The Roman Canon remains in use in both the Ordinary Form (as Eucharistic Prayer I) and the Extraordinary Form of the Mass, and is particularly associated with solemn celebrations and the Chrism and Easter liturgies.
