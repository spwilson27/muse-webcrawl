Microorganism

A cluster of Escherichia coli bacteria, magnified many thousands of times.

A microorganism, or microbe, is an organism of microscopic size, which may exist in single-celled form or as a colony of cells. Microorganisms include all bacteria and archaea, most protists, some fungi (such as yeasts), and algae; viruses are usually considered separately as non-living particles.

Discovery

Microorganisms were first observed by Antonie van Leeuwenhoek in the 1670s using single-lens microscopes of his own design; he called them "animalcules." In the 19th century, Louis Pasteur and Robert Koch established the germ theory of disease and the foundations of microbiology.

Diversity and habitats

Microbes inhabit almost every environment on Earth, from deep-sea hydrothermal vents and subsurface rock to the upper atmosphere, and they vastly outnumber the cells of the human body in and on it. They drive global nutrient cycles — fixing nitrogen, decomposing organic matter, and producing much of the world's oxygen through photosynthesis.

Importance

Microorganisms are essential to food production (bread, beer, yogurt, cheese), biotechnology and antibiotic production, and waste treatment; they also cause infectious diseases and food spoilage. The human microbiome is increasingly recognised as a major influence on health.
