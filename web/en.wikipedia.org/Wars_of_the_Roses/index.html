Wars of the Roses

The Wars of the Roses, known at the time and in following centuries as the Civil Wars, were a series of civil wars fought over control of the English throne from 1455 to 1487. The wars were fought between supporters of the House of Lancaster, whose heraldic symbol was a red rose, and supporters of the House of York, whose symbol was a white rose. Both houses were branches of the royal House of Plantagenet.

Origins

The conflict grew from the weak rule of Henry VI, whose mental illness and costly loss of English possessions in France undermined royal authority, and from the rivalry of great magnates. Richard, Duke of York, asserted his claim to the throne as a descendant of Edward III through the senior line, and open fighting began at the First Battle of St Albans in May 1455.

Course of the wars

After early Yorkist successes, Richard of York was killed at the Battle of Wakefield in 1460, but his son seized the crown as Edward IV after the bloody Battle of Towton in 1461, the largest battle fought on English soil. Edward’s marriage and his favouritism split the Yorkists: the Earl of Warwick, “the Kingmaker”, briefly restored Henry VI in 1470, but Edward returned to win the battles of Barnet and Tewkesbury in 1471, where Henry’s son Edward of Westminster was killed; Henry VI died in the Tower soon after.

On Edward IV’s death in 1483 his brother Richard, Duke of Gloucester, took the throne as Richard III, displacing Edward’s young sons — the Princes in the Tower — who disappeared. Richard’s usurpation alienated many Yorkists, and in August 1485 the Lancastrian claimant Henry Tudor defeated and killed him at the Battle of Bosworth Field. Henry VII married Elizabeth of York, uniting the two houses under the Tudor rose. The last battle of the wars was fought at Stoke Field in 1487 against the pretender Lambert Simnel.

Consequences

The wars destroyed much of the old feudal nobility and strengthened the monarchy, paving the way for the Tudor dynasty. Henry VII ruled until 1509 and was succeeded by his son Henry VIII. The name “Wars of the Roses” was popularised by Sir Walter Scott in the 19th century; Shakespeare’s history plays made the conflict’s kings and queens enduring figures of English literature.
