Shade (mythology)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In literature and poetry, a shade (sometimes wraith) is the spirit or ghost of a dead person residing in the underworld. The notion descends from ancient Greek and Roman belief: in Homer's Odyssey, the dead in Hades are shadowy remnants of their living selves — the Greek word is skia, "shadow" — lacking strength and full consciousness unless they drink sacrificial blood, as when Odysseus summons the shade of the prophet Tiresias in the nekyia of Book 11.

Ancient usage

Roman authors likewise called the dead umbrae ("shades"), and Virgil's Aeneid, Book 6, depicts Aeneas visiting the underworld and meeting the shade of his father Anchises. Shades were thought to retain the appearance and wounds of their living bodies but to be insubstantial, flitting like smoke when grasped. In this sense the dead of Sheol in Hebrew tradition and the shades of many later literatures share the image of a diminished half-life after death.

Later literature

The term persists in English poetry and fantasy: shades appear in Dante's Inferno, in Milton, and in modern fantasy fiction, where "shade" may denote a distinct class of undead or shadow-creature. The word also survives in phrases such as "shades of night" and in the institution of lamplight "shades" only by separate development.
