Sodium fluoroacetate

Sodium fluoroacetate is the organofluorine chemical compound with the formula FCH2CO2Na. This colourless salt is used as a pesticide, especially against mammal pests. It is best known in New Zealand and Australia by the brand name 1080 (pronounced "ten eighty"), and is banned or tightly restricted in many countries because of its toxicity and lack of a specific antidote.

Occurrence and mechanism

Fluoroacetate occurs naturally in several plants, notably Dichapetalum cymosum (gifblaar) in South Africa and some Australian Gastrolobium species, to which some native fauna have evolved tolerance. In the body, fluoroacetate is converted to fluorocitrate, which blocks the enzyme aconitase in the citric acid cycle, halting cellular respiration — a process termed "lethal synthesis".

Use in pest control

1080 baits are used extensively in New Zealand to control introduced brushtail possums, rats, and stoats threatening native birds, and in Australia against foxes, wild dogs, and feral pigs. Aerial application is controversial because of risks to non-target animals, especially dogs, and it has drawn sustained protest, though conservation agencies maintain it remains essential for protecting native species.

Toxicity

The compound is highly toxic to mammals and insects; symptoms in humans include nausea, convulsions, and cardiac failure. There is no proven antidote, and treatment is supportive.
