Erinyes

The Erinyes, also known as the Furies, were female chthonic deities of vengeance in ancient Greek religion and mythology. The Latin word Furies combines the Greek Erinyes, from Erinys, literally meaning "the implacable ones."

Origins

According to Hesiod's Theogony, the Erinyes sprang from the drops of blood shed by Uranus when his son Cronus castrated him. Other traditions made them daughters of Nyx (Night) or of Gaia.

The three Furies

The Erinyes were traditionally three: Alecto ("endless anger"), Megaera ("jealous rage"), and Tisiphone ("vengeance for murder"). They were depicted as hideous women with snakes for hair, dogs' heads, coal-black bodies, bats' wings, and blood-shot eyes.

Role

The Erinyes pursued those guilty of crimes against the natural order, particularly homicide, crimes against parents, oath-breaking, and inhospitality. Their most famous victim was Orestes, pursued after he murdered his mother Clytemnestra. In Aeschylus' Eumenides, Athena intervenes and the Erinyes are transformed into the Eumenides, the "Kindly Ones."
