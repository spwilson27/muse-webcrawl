Gary Stanley Becker (1930–2014) was an American economist at the University of Chicago, known for applying economic analysis to areas traditionally studied by sociologists and demographers, including discrimination, crime, family decisions, addiction and education.

Work

His doctoral research became The Economics of Discrimination (1957). Human Capital (1964) treated education and training as investments that raise productivity and earnings. His 1968 paper on crime modelled offending as a rational response to expected benefits and punishments, influencing deterrence policy.

Family and social interaction

In A Treatise on the Family (1981) and later work on social interactions, Becker analysed marriage, fertility, altruism within households and the effects of preferences and social norms on individual choices.

Honours and criticism

Becker received the Nobel Memorial Prize in 1992 for extending microeconomic analysis to human behaviour and interaction. Critics have questioned whether rational-choice models capture power, culture and bounded rationality, but his approach reshaped labour economics and public policy.
