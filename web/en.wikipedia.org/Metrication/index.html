Metrication

Metrication is the act or process of converting to the metric system of measurement. The metric system originated in France during the French Revolution and has since become the dominant system of measurement worldwide. Today, only a few countries — principally the United States, Liberia and Myanmar — have not fully adopted the metric system as their official system, though all use it extensively in science, medicine and many industries.

History

France adopted the metric system in 1795, and its use spread through Europe during the 19th century. The Metre Convention of 1875 established international coordination. The United Kingdom began official metrication in 1965, a process largely completed in most sectors though imperial units remain in some everyday uses, such as miles on road signs. Australia, New Zealand, Canada and South Africa metricated during the 1970s.

Arguments

Supporters cite the metric system's decimal coherence, ease of calculation, and global standardisation. Opponents in the United States have raised the cost of conversion and public familiarity with customary units. The U.S. Metric Conversion Act of 1975 declared metric the "preferred" system but made conversion voluntary.

Modern status

The International System of Units (SI) is used in science everywhere. In the United States, customary units are legally defined in terms of SI units, so even non-metric measurements rest on metric definitions.
