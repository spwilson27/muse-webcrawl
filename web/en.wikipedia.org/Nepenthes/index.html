Nepenthes is a genus of carnivorous plants, also known as tropical pitcher plants or monkey cups, in the monotypic family Nepenthaceae. The genus comprises roughly 170 species and numerous natural and cultivated hybrids, native mainly to the humid tropics of Southeast Asia, with the greatest diversity on Borneo and Sumatra.

Pitcher traps

The plants catch prey — mostly insects, but occasionally small vertebrates — in cup-shaped pitfall traps called pitchers, which develop from the tips of the leaves: the tendril at the end of the leaf blade swells and forms a hollow pitcher topped by a lid (operculum). Nectar glands attract prey to a slippery rim (peristome); once wetted, the peristome becomes extremely slick, and insects fall into digestive fluid in the lower pitcher, where enzymes and symbiotic microbes break them down and the plant absorbs nutrients such as nitrogen and phosphorus. In nutrient-poor soils this carnivory compensates for scarce minerals.

Many species produce two forms of trap: stout lower pitchers resting on the ground and slender upper pitchers produced by climbing stems. Some species have remarkable mutualisms — tree shrews and rats deposit nitrogen-rich feces in the large pitchers of species such as N. lowii and N. rajah while feeding on their secretions, and the woolly bat Kerivoula hardwickii roosts in pitchers of N. hemsleyana.

Distribution and ecology

Nepenthes species inhabit environments from coastal mangroves to mossy montane forests above 3,000 m, typically on acidic, nutrient-poor substrates. Centers of diversity include Borneo (about 40 species), Sumatra, and the Philippines — many species are narrow endemics of single mountains, making them vulnerable to habitat loss and poaching for the horticultural trade.

History and cultivation

The genus name, coined by Linnaeus in 1737, alludes to the drug nepenthe of Greek myth, a potion that banished sorrow. Madagascan species were described as early as the 17th century by Étienne de Flacourt. Nepenthes were prized in Victorian glasshouses, and today hundreds of species and hybrids are cultivated by specialists; all species are listed under CITES (Appendix I or II).
