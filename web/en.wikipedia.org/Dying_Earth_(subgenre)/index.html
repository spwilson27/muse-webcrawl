Dying Earth (subgenre)

Dying Earth is a subgenre of science fiction and fantasy set in the far future, when the Earth is near the end of its life and the Sun is aged and dim. Themes of exhaustion, entropy and melancholy predominate, and the boundary between magic and forgotten technology is often blurred.

History

Early examples include H. G. Wells's The Time Machine (1895), whose traveller visits a dying Earth under a bloated red sun, and William Hope Hodgson's The Night Land (1912). Clark Ashton Smith's Zothique stories of the 1930s depicted Earth's last continent. The subgenre takes its name from Jack Vance's Dying Earth sequence, begun in 1950, whose far-future world of waning magic and decadent societies became hugely influential — Vance's system of memorised spells directly inspired the magic of Dungeons & Dragons.

Later works

Gene Wolfe's The Book of the New Sun (1980–83), set on an ancient Earth ("Urth") lit by a failing sun, is widely regarded as a masterpiece of the form. Other notable entries include M. John Harrison's Viriconium sequence and the far-future tales of Michael Moorcock.

Themes

Common motifs include the ruins of immensely old civilisations, humanity diminished or transformed, indifferent remnants of advanced technology treated as magic, and a pervasive sense of cosmic weariness.
