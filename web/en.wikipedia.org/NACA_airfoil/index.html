The NACA airfoils are standardised wing cross-sections developed by the United States National Advisory Committee for Aeronautics (NACA) in the 1930s and 1940s. Each profile is defined by a short numeric code from which the airfoil coordinates can be computed by simple formulas, and the sections were systematically tested in wind tunnels, making them a common reference for aircraft design.

Four-digit series

In the four-digit series, illustrated by the NACA 2412, the first digit gives the maximum camber as a percentage of chord (2%), the second the position of maximum camber in tenths of chord (at 40% back), and the last two the maximum thickness as a percentage of chord (12%). Symmetric sections have codes beginning with 00, such as the NACA 0012. The five-digit series (e.g. NACA 23012) refined camber line shapes, while the later 6-series sections were designed to maintain laminar flow over more of the chord, reducing drag — sections used on aircraft such as the P-51 Mustang.

Legacy

Although modern design uses computational methods and custom supercritical sections, NACA profiles remain standard teaching examples and are still used in light aircraft, model aircraft, wind turbines and fans.
