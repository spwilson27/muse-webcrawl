Bottom trawling

Type: Commercial fishing method
Gear: Weighted nets dragged on seabed
Targets: Demersal fish and shellfish (cod, haddock, shrimp)
Concerns: Habitat damage, bycatch

Bottom trawling is a fishing method in which trawlers tow a large net along the sea floor. It is used to catch demersal species such as cod, haddock, flatfish and shrimp, and accounts for a substantial share of the world's marine catch.

Method

Trawl nets are held open by heavy doors (otter boards) and the lower edge of the net, often fitted with chains or rollers, skims or digs into the seabed. Modern industrial trawlers may drag nets hundreds of metres wide at depths from shallow coastal waters to over a kilometre.

Environmental impact

Bottom trawling can damage seabed habitats, including cold-water coral reefs and sponge beds, resuspend sediments and release stored carbon, and catch large amounts of non-target species (bycatch). Many jurisdictions restrict trawling areas and gear, and some have banned it in sensitive zones; debate continues over its sustainability and reform.
