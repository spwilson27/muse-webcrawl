Observer-expectancy effect

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The observer-expectancy effect (also called the experimenter-expectancy effect) is a form of reactivity and cognitive bias in which a researcher's expectations about the outcome of an experiment subtly influence participants' behaviour or the recording and interpretation of results, tending to confirm the hypothesis. It is a central justification for blinded and double-blind experimental designs.

Classic demonstrations

The classic case is Clever Hans, the horse that appeared to do arithmetic but was responding to unconscious cues from questioners. In the 1960s Robert Rosenthal demonstrated expectancy effects experimentally: students told that certain rats were bred to be "maze-bright" obtained better maze performance from genetically similar animals, and in the Rosenthal–Jacobson "Pygmalion in the classroom" study, pupils randomly labelled as about to bloom showed greater IQ gains.

Mechanisms and controls

Expectations leak through tone of voice, encouragement, differential handling and selective recording of ambiguous observations. Standard controls include blinding experimenters and observers to condition or hypothesis, automated data collection, standardised protocols, and pre-registration of analyses.
