Laffer curve

Laffer curve
Popularised byArthur Laffer
FieldPublic finance, supply-side economics

The Laffer curve illustrates the relationship between tax rates and the resulting level of government tax revenue. It suggests that revenue is zero at a 0 percent rate and also at a 100 percent rate (where no one would produce taxable income), and therefore that there is a revenue-maximising rate in between — and that cutting rates above that point could increase revenue.

History

The idea is old: the 14th-century scholar Ibn Khaldun described how excessive taxation can reduce revenue, and Keynes and others noted the same logic. Economist Arthur Laffer popularised the curve in the 1970s — legend has it sketched on a napkin for Dick Cheney and Donald Rumsfeld in 1974 — as an intellectual foundation of supply-side economics and the tax cuts of the Reagan administration.

Analysis

The curve's shape and the location of its peak depend on the elasticity of taxable income. Empirically, most economists place the revenue-maximising rate for labour income well above the rates prevailing in most developed economies when Laffer's argument was invoked, and studies generally find that the 1980s US tax cuts did not pay for themselves. The curve remains a standard teaching device about incentives and tax bases.

Criticism

Critics argue the curve is often used rhetorically to imply any tax cut raises revenue, an inference the model does not support unless rates are above the peak. Supporters respond that at high marginal rates, avoidance and reduced effort make the effect real.
