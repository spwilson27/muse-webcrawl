Michelangelo Merisi da Caravaggio (29 September 1571 – 18 July 1610), generally known simply as Caravaggio, was an Italian painter active in Rome, Naples, Malta and Sicily between about 1592 and 1610. His paintings combine a realistic observation of the human state — physical and emotional — with a dramatic use of lighting, which had a formative influence on Baroque painting. This technique, in which forms emerge from dark backgrounds with intense illumination, is known as tenebrism or chiaroscuro in its Caravaggesque form.

Caravaggio employed close physical observation with a dramatic use of light, an approach that made him a controversial figure: the Church rejected some of his altarpieces for their unidealised, street-cast models, even as collectors competed for his work.

Early life and Rome

Born in Milan and apprenticed at thirteen to the painter Simone Peterzano, Caravaggio arrived in Rome in the early 1590s nearly destitute. He painted still lifes and half-length figures of musicians, cardsharps and fortune tellers — among them The Cardsharps and Bacchus — before attracting the patronage of Cardinal Francesco Maria del Monte, who lodged him in the Palazzo Madama. For the Contarelli Chapel in San Luigi dei Francesi he painted his first great public cycle, The Calling of Saint Matthew, The Inspiration of Saint Matthew and The Martyrdom of Saint Matthew (1599–1602), which made his reputation. Works such as Judith Beheading Holofernes, The Conversion of Saint Paul and The Crucifixion of Saint Peter for the Cerasi Chapel, and the Supper at Emmaus followed.

Flight and final years

Caravaggio's life in Rome was punctuated by brawls, arrests and lawsuits. On 28 May 1606, during a street fight arising from a dispute over a game of pallacorda, he killed a young man, Ranuccio Tomassoni, and fled Rome with a death sentence on his head. He reached Naples, then Malta, where he was inducted into the Order of Saint John and painted the enormous Beheading of Saint John the Baptist — the only work he ever signed, in the Baptist's blood — before being imprisoned and expelled from the Order as a "foul and rotten member". Escaping to Sicily, he painted in Syracuse, Messina and Palermo, then returned to Naples, where in 1609 he was ambushed and badly wounded. He died in July 1610 at Porto Ercole while travelling toward Rome in expectation of a papal pardon, in circumstances that remain debated — fever, sepsis from his wounds, lead poisoning and malaria have all been proposed.

Legacy

Famous in his lifetime, Caravaggio was nearly forgotten by the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries; his reputation was revived by twentieth-century scholarship, notably Roberto Longhi. His influence on the Baroque, through followers known as the Caravaggisti — including Orazio and Artemisia Gentileschi, Bartolomeo Manfredi and, indirectly, Ribera, Georges de La Tour and Rembrandt — was profound, and his stark realism and spotlighted drama have been repeatedly invoked in photography and cinema.
