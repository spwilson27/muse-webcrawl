Posterior vitreous detachment

A posterior vitreous detachment (PVD) is a condition of the eye in which the vitreous membrane separates from the retina. The vitreous is the clear gel filling the back of the eye; with age it liquefies and shrinks, eventually peeling away from the retinal surface. PVD is very common with ageing — a majority of people over 70 have experienced it — and is more frequent in the short-sighted and after cataract surgery.

Symptoms

The classic symptoms are a sudden increase in floaters — including a single large ring-shaped floater (a Weiss ring) where the vitreous detaches from around the optic disc — and flashes of light (photopsia) in the peripheral vision, caused by the gel tugging on the retina as it separates.

Complications

Most PVDs complete without harm, and floaters usually become less noticeable over months. However, in a minority of cases the vitreous pulls hard enough to tear the retina, which can lead to retinal detachment — a sight-threatening emergency. For this reason, sudden onset of floaters and flashes warrants prompt dilated retinal examination, and patients are advised to return immediately if they notice a shower of new floaters, persistent flashing, or a shadow or curtain across the vision.

Treatment

No treatment is needed for uncomplicated PVD; management consists of examination and safety advice. Retinal tears found during the acute period are typically sealed with laser photocoagulation or cryotherapy to prevent detachment.
