Sarcophagus

A sarcophagus (plural sarcophagi) is a box-like funeral receptacle for a corpse, most commonly carved in stone and usually displayed above ground, though it may also be buried. The word comes from the Greek sarx ("flesh") and phagein ("to eat"), referring to a type of limestone thought to consume the flesh of corpses placed in it.

History

Sarcophagi were used throughout the ancient world. In Egypt, elaborately decorated stone sarcophagi enclosed the mummified bodies of pharaohs and nobles, often nested inside wooden coffins. Phoenician and Etruscan sarcophagi were frequently carved with a reclining figure of the deceased on the lid. Greek and Roman sarcophagi, especially from the 2nd century AD onward, carried rich relief sculpture depicting mythological scenes, battles, and episodes from the life of the deceased.

Early Christian and medieval use

Early Christian sarcophagi adapted Roman forms to biblical imagery, carving scenes such as Jonah and the whale or the Good Shepherd. In the Middle Ages sarcophagi were less common in northern Europe, where burial in simple coffins or tombs predominated, but stone sarcophagi continued in use around the Mediterranean.

Famous examples

Notable sarcophagi include the Alexander Sarcophagus from Sidon, the marble sarcophagi of the Roman emperors porphyry tombs in Constantinople, and the granite sarcophagus in the King's Chamber of the Great Pyramid of Giza. The term is also applied to the steel and concrete structure built over the destroyed reactor at Chernobyl.
