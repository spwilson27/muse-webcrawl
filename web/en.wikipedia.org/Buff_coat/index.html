Buff coat

A buff coat is a type of thick leather garment worn by soldiers during the 17th century, particularly in the English Civil War and the Thirty Years' War. Made of thick, softened leather — originally buffalo or ox hide — the coat reached to the knees and was worn as light armor, either on its own or under a steel cuirass.

Design

Buff coats were typically made of hide about 3–5 mm thick, tanned with oil, which gave the leather a characteristic pale yellow-buff color. They fastened at the front with hooks and eyes or laces, and many had sleeves of thinner leather or fabric. The thick hide could turn a sword cut and offered some protection against spent projectiles, though it could not stop a musket ball at close range.

Use

Buff coats were especially associated with cavalry, including Oliver Cromwell's Ironsides and the harquebusiers of the period. Officers often wore elaborately decorated examples with silver or gold lace. As firearms improved and armor declined, the buff coat fell from military use in the late 17th and early 18th centuries, but it survived as a fashionable waistcoat style.

Surviving examples

Notable surviving buff coats are held by the Royal Armouries and the Metropolitan Museum of Art, including examples said to have been worn by participants in the English Civil War.
