Ethernet

Ethernet (/ˈiːθərnɛt/) is a family of wired computer networking technologies commonly used in local area networks (LAN), metropolitan area networks (MAN) and wide area networks (WAN). It was commercialized in 1980 and first standardized in 1983 as IEEE 802.3. Ethernet has since largely replaced competing wired LAN technologies such as Token Ring and ARCNET.

History

Ethernet was developed at Xerox PARC between 1973 and 1974 by Robert Metcalfe, David Boggs, Chuck Thacker, and Butler Lampson, inspired in part by ALOHAnet's radio packet system (the name derives from the "luminiferous aether" once thought to pervade space). Xerox, DEC, and Intel promoted the "DIX" (Ethernet II) specification; IEEE standardisation followed as 802.3.

Technology

Ethernet originally ran on coaxial cable using CSMA/CD (carrier-sense multiple access with collision detection) and frames encapsulated with a 48-bit source and destination MAC address. Modern Ethernet predominantly uses structured cabling (twisted pair or fibre) with switches creating "collision domains" of one device per port. Speeds have risen over generations: 10 Mbit/s (1980s) → 100 Mbit/s (Fast Ethernet, 1995) → 1 Gbit/s (Gigabit Ethernet) → 10/25/40/100/400 Gbit/s standards today. Power over Ethernet (PoE) allows suitably designed cables to also supply DC power.
