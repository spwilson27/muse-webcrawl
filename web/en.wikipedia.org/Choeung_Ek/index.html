Choeung Ek (Khmer: ជើងឯក) is a former orchard and Chinese cemetery about 17 kilometres south of Phnom Penh, Cambodia, which became the best known of the country's "Killing Fields" — sites where the Khmer Rouge regime collectively murdered and buried more than a million people between 1975 and 1979. Choeung Ek served as the principal execution and burial site for prisoners of the S-21 security prison (Tuol Sleng) in Phnom Penh.

History

Under the regime of Pol Pot, prisoners — former officials, professionals, intellectuals, ethnic minorities, monks, and entire families including infants — were transported blindfolded from S-21 by truck, typically at night. To save bullets, they were killed with blows from farm implements, axes, and sharpened palm fronds, and thrown into mass graves. Loudspeakers mounted on a tree played revolutionary music to mask the sounds. The so-called "killing tree" was used to murder children, and another tree held speakers. In total, an estimated 17,000 people were killed at Choeung Ek, one of roughly 300 killing fields across Cambodia.

Memorial

After the regime's fall in 1979, investigators exhumed 129 mass graves at the site, recovering some 8,985 bodies. A tall white Buddhist stupa, built in 1988, houses more than 8,000–9,000 skulls arranged by sex and age behind glass panels. Bone fragments and scraps of victims' clothing still emerge from the ground during the rainy season. Today Choeung Ek is a major memorial and educational site, visited alongside the Tuol Sleng Genocide Museum, with an audio guide narrated by survivors and historians.
