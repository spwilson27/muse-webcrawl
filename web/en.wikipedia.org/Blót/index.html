Blót

Blót is the term for a sacrificial feast in Old Norse religion and in modern Germanic neopaganism (Heathenry). The word derives from Old Norse blót, related to a verb meaning "to sacrifice" or "to worship with sacrifice". In pre-Christian Scandinavia, blóts were communal ceremonies in which animals were slaughtered, their blood offered to the gods, and the meat shared in a ritual feast accompanied by ceremonial toasts.

Historical practice

Medieval Icelandic sagas and histories describe blóts held by kings and chieftains, for example at Lade and Mære in Norway, in which horses and other livestock were sacrificed and the blood was sprinkled on altars, temple walls, and participants using a twig (hlautteinn). The flesh was boiled and eaten at the feast, while toasts were drunk to the gods — Odin for victory, Njörðr and Freyr for peace and good seasons — and to ancestors and the king. Adam of Bremen's account of the temple at Uppsala describes a great festival held every nine years. Major seasonal blóts included the autumn Winter Nights, Yule at midwinter, and Sigrblót at the start of summer.

Modern practice

Since the 20th-century revival of Germanic paganism, the blót has been revived as the central rite of Heathen groups in Iceland (Ásatrúarfélagið), Scandinavia, Britain, and North America. Modern blóts generally replace animal blood with offerings of mead or ale: a horn is filled, blessed, and passed around so participants may hail the gods, ancestors, and land spirits; a portion is poured out as an offering. Sumbel, a related ritual of successive rounds of toasting and oath-making, is often combined with the blót.
