Animal rights

Animal rights holds that nonhuman animals are entitled to fundamental protections.

Animal rights is the philosophy according to which many or all sentient animals have moral worth independent of their utility to humans, and that their most basic interests—such as avoiding suffering—should be afforded the same consideration as similar interests of human beings. Broadly speaking, and particularly in popular discourse, "animal rights" is often used synonymously with "animal protection" or "animal liberation". More narrowly, "animal rights" refers to the idea that many animals have fundamental rights—such as the right to life and freedom from suffering—that should be protected by law.

Advocates argue that the animal industry and other institutions treat animals as mere commodities or resources. Critics of the animal rights movement argue that nonhuman animals cannot enter into a social contract, make moral choices, or understand their rights, and therefore cannot possess rights in the way humans do.

History

The idea that animals might have rights has ancient roots, but the modern movement took shape in the 1970s. Richard Ryder coined the term "speciesism" in 1970, and Peter Singer's Animal Liberation (1975) is widely regarded as the founding text of the animal liberation movement. Singer's utilitarian argument holds that the capacity to suffer, rather than rationality or species membership, is the relevant criterion for moral consideration. Tom Regan's The Case for Animal Rights (1983) offered a deontological alternative, arguing that animals who are "subjects-of-a-life" possess inherent value and rights that cannot be traded away for aggregate welfare.

Earlier antecedents include Henry Salt's Animals' Rights: Considered in Relation to Social Progress (1892) and the nineteenth-century anti-vivisection movement. In ancient philosophy, Pythagoras and Plutarch defended kindness toward animals, while Aristotle and later René Descartes treated animals as lacking reason or even feeling.

Philosophical approaches

Utilitarianism

Singer and other utilitarians argue for equal consideration of interests: the suffering of any being counts equally regardless of species. This approach does not strictly require "rights" language but often reaches similar practical conclusions, including support for vegetarianism or veganism.

Rights-based approaches

Regan and Gary Francione argue that sentient animals possess basic rights—at minimum, the right not to be treated as property. Francione's abolitionist approach rejects the regulation of animal use in favour of ending animals' status as property altogether.

Capabilities and other views

Martha Nussbaum's capabilities approach argues that each creature should be supported in flourishing according to its species-specific form of life.

In law

Most legal systems classify animals as property, though many jurisdictions have enacted animal cruelty statutes and welfare regulations. Some have gone further: in 2021 the United Kingdom's Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act recognised animals as sentient beings in law, and courts in Argentina and India have entertained habeas corpus or personhood claims on behalf of individual animals such as the orangutan Sandra and the elephant Happy (the latter rejected by New York's Court of Appeals in 2022).

Criticism

Philosophers such as R. G. Frey and Carl Cohen have argued that rights depend on membership in a moral community capable of reciprocal obligations, which animals cannot join. Others argue that animal rights claims conflict with human interests in medicine, food, and livelihoods, or that welfare reform is a more practical path than abolition.
