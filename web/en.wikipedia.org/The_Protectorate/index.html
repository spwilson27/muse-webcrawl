The Protectorate

Period16 December 1653 – 25 May 1659
Lord ProtectorOliver Cromwell (1653–58); Richard Cromwell (1658–59)

The Protectorate was the period of British and Irish history between 1653 and 1659 during which the Commonwealth of England, Scotland and Ireland was governed by a Lord Protector as head of state. It followed the dissolution of the Rump Parliament and the short-lived nominated Barebone's Parliament, and it ended with the fall of Richard Cromwell and the unravelling that led to the Restoration of Charles II in 1660.

Constitutions

The Protectorate was established by the Instrument of Government (1653), Britain's first written constitution, which vested executive authority in Oliver Cromwell as Lord Protector, advised by a Council of State, with legislative power shared with a single-chamber Parliament elected on a reformed franchise. In 1657 a more conservative settlement, the Humble Petition and Advice, offered Cromwell the Crown — which, under pressure from the army, he declined — while granting him powers to nominate his successor and creating a second parliamentary chamber (the Other House) and a more courtly regime.

Government and policies

The regime combined religious toleration for most Protestant dissenters (with informal readmission of the Jews to England) with energetic administration. In 1655–56 England and Wales were divided into districts under army major-generals, an experiment in godly discipline that proved unpopular and was abandoned. Abroad, Cromwell's navy under Robert Blake fought the Dutch and Spanish, seized Jamaica from Spain in 1655, won the Battle of the Dunes and acquired Dunkirk in 1658, and raised Britain's standing as a naval power.

End

On Oliver Cromwell's death on 3 September 1658 his son Richard succeeded peacefully, but lacked authority over the army and Parliament; he was forced from office in May 1659. The restored Commonwealth descended into factional chaos, prompting General George Monck's march from Scotland and the Convention Parliament's invitation to Charles II.
