Wola massacre

The Wola massacre was the systematic murder of tens of thousands of Polish civilians by German forces in the Wola district of Warsaw in early August 1944, during the Warsaw Uprising of World War II. Between 40,000 and 50,000 people were killed between August 5 and August 12, 1944, making it one of the largest massacres of civilians in a single episode in the war.

DateAugust 5–12, 1944
LocationWola, Warsaw, occupied Poland
DeathsAn estimated 40,000–50,000 civilians
PerpetratorsGerman SS, police, and auxiliary units under Heinz Reinefarth and Oskar Dirlewanger

Background

When the Polish Home Army rose against the German occupation on August 1, 1944, Heinrich Himmler ordered that Warsaw be destroyed and its population exterminated. SS-Gruppenführer Heinz Reinefarth's battle group, including the notorious Dirlewanger Brigade and RONA units, advanced into Wola from the west.

The killings

From August 5 — remembered as "Black Saturday" — German units went house to house, rounding up residents regardless of age or sex and shooting them at execution sites such as factory yards and the Górczewska Street railway embankment. Hospitals were burned with patients inside. Captured Poles were forced to stack and burn the bodies to conceal the crime. The mass murder was scaled back after August 12, when orders shifted toward deporting surviving civilians to concentration and labor camps instead.

Aftermath

Despite the scale of the atrocity, few perpetrators were punished; Reinefarth lived out his life in West Germany and was never convicted for Wola. The massacre is commemorated by numerous memorials in Wola and remains a central symbol of Warsaw's wartime suffering, in which some 150,000–200,000 Polish civilians died during the uprising overall.
