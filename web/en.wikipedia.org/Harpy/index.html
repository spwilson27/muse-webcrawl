Harpy

In Greek and Roman mythology, a harpy (plural harpies; Greek: ἅρπυια, harpyia) is a half-human and half-bird, often believed to be a personification of storm winds. Harpies are generally depicted as birds with the heads of maidens, faces pale with hunger, and long claws on their hands. They were agents of punishment, carrying evildoers to the Erinyes, and were known for snatching food and defiling it.

Mythology

In the earliest traditions, the harpies were beautiful winged maidens, swift as the wind; Hesiod described them as the daughters of Thaumas and the Oceanid Electra, naming Aello ("storm-swift") and Ocypete ("swift-wing"). Virgil and later writers transformed them into hideous monsters.

The most famous myth concerns King Phineus of Thrace, who, as punishment (in one version, for abusing his prophetic gift), was given a feast from which the harpies would snatch the food, fouling what remained. The Argonauts Zetes and Calais, the winged sons of Boreas, drove the harpies away, and were stopped from killing them by the goddess Iris, who promised the harpies would trouble Phineus no more. In Virgil's Aeneid, the harpy Celaeno curses Aeneas and his Trojans during their voyage. Dante placed harpies in the seventh circle of Hell in the Inferno, where they nest in the Wood of the Suicides and feed on the trees' leaves, causing them pain.

Later usage

The word "harpy" has entered English as a term for a grasping or shrewish person, particularly a woman. Harpies appear widely in later literature, art and heraldry — including in Shakespeare's Much Ado About Nothing and in cephalopod-free bestiaries — and have given their name to the harpy eagle of Central and South America. In heraldry, the harpy (German Jungfrauenadler) appears as a charge in East Frisian coats of arms.
