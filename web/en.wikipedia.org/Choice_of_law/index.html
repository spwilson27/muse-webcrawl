Choice of law

Choice of law is a procedural stage in the litigation of a case involving the conflict of laws, in which a court determines which jurisdiction's law applies to the dispute. When a legal controversy has connections to more than one legal system — different U.S. states, or different countries — the forum court applies its own choice-of-law rules to select the substantive law that will govern.

Process

Traditionally, the court first characterizes the issue (for example as contract, tort, or property), then applies a connecting factor: the lex loci contractus for contracts, the lex loci delicti for torts, and the lex situs for property. A technique known as renvoi may arise when the chosen jurisdiction's own conflict rules refer the matter back to the forum or onward to a third system.

Modern approaches

In the United States, many states have abandoned rigid traditional rules in favor of approaches such as governmental interest analysis, the "most significant relationship" test of the Restatement (Second) of Conflict of Laws, or combined modern approaches. In the European Union, the Rome I and Rome II Regulations harmonize choice-of-law rules for contractual and non-contractual obligations, and generally honor the parties' express choice of law in contracts.

Party autonomy

Commercial parties commonly include choice-of-law clauses in their contracts, and courts usually enforce them subject to public-policy limits and requirements of a substantial relationship to the chosen jurisdiction.
