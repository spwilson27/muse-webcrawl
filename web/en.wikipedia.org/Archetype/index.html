Archetype

An archetype is a concept, a pattern of behaviour, a prototype, or a model upon which other things are copied, patterned, or emulated. The term, from the Greek arkhetypon ("first-moulded"), appears in philosophy, psychology, and literary criticism.

In philosophy

The idea of archetypes descends from Plato's theory of Forms, in which sensible objects are copies of eternal, perfect patterns. Later thinkers, including Philo and the Neoplatonists, treated archetypes as ideas in the divine mind, a notion taken up by medieval scholastics and by John Locke's usage of the term for ideas not derived from experience.

In Jungian psychology

Carl Gustav Jung used "archetype" for universal, archaic patterns and images residing in the collective unconscious. Archetypes such as the Self, the Shadow, the Anima and Animus, the Persona, the Mother, the Hero, and the Trickster manifest in dreams, myths, and religions across cultures. Jung distinguished the archetype itself, which is not directly representable, from archetypal images, its culturally shaped representations.

In literature

Literary critics influenced by Jung and by works such as James George Frazer's The Golden Bough and Northrop Frye's Anatomy of Criticism identify archetypal characters (the hero, the mentor, the scapegoat), plots (the quest, the descent and rebirth), and symbols recurring across traditions. Joseph Campbell's monomyth in The Hero with a Thousand Faces popularized archetypal narrative analysis.

Other uses

In biology the term historically described an idealized body plan from which a group of organisms was thought to be derived; in systems analysis and marketing, archetypes describe typical users, brands, or personalities modeled on universal themes.
