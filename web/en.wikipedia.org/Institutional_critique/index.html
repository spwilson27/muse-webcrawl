Institutional critique


Institutional critique examines the museum and gallery system from within.

Institutional critique is an approach in contemporary art in which artists systematically examine and expose the structures of the institutions — museums, galleries, funding bodies and the art market — that present and legitimise art. Rather than treating the gallery as a neutral container, institutional critique reveals how display conventions, architecture, corporate sponsorship and curatorial choices shape the meaning and value of artworks. The term is associated with work from the late 1960s onward.

Artists and works

Marcel Broodthaers founded his fictional Musée d'Art Moderne, Département des Aigles (1968–72) to parody museum authority. Daniel Buren's striped works and writings argued that the frame of the institution produces the work's meaning. Hans Haacke exposed the business interests of museum trustees, most famously in works that led to the cancellation of his 1971 Guggenheim exhibition. Michael Asher altered gallery architecture itself, and Andrea Fraser later performed the roles of museum guides and patrons to dramatise institutional rhetoric. The critic Benjamin Buchloh helped theorise the movement, and the artist and writer Andrea Fraser distinguished successive "generations" of the practice.

Themes

Recurring concerns include the influence of donors and sponsors, the labour conditions of art workers, the exclusion of women and minority artists from collections, colonial histories of museum holdings, and the contradiction that critique itself is readily absorbed and exhibited by the institutions it attacks.
