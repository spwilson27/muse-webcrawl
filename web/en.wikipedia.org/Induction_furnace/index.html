Induction furnace

An induction furnace is an electrical furnace in which heat is applied by induction heating of metal. A water-cooled copper coil carrying high-frequency alternating current surrounds a refractory crucible; the alternating magnetic field induces eddy currents in the metal charge, melting it. Furnace capacities range from a few kilograms in jewellery and dental laboratories to tens of tonnes in foundries and steel plants, and the induced currents also stir the melt, promoting uniform composition.

A coreless induction furnace pouring molten metal.

Types and operation

Coreless furnaces use a crucible fully encircled by the coil and operate at frequencies from mains (50/60 Hz) to many kilohertz, with lower frequencies giving deeper penetration for large charges. Channel furnaces maintain a loop of molten metal through an induction unit and are used mainly for holding and superheating cast iron. Compared with arc furnaces and fuel-fired furnaces, induction furnaces offer precise temperature control, low metal loss, and no combustion emissions at the point of use, though they depend on electricity supply and have high capital cost.

History

Induction melting was developed in the early 20th century — Edwin Northrup built an early high-frequency furnace in 1916 — and became a mainstay of the foundry industry after the Second World War with the advent of solid-state power supplies.
