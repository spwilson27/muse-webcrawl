Body piercing

Body piercings including ear, nostril, and lip piercings

Body piercing is a form of body modification consisting of puncturing or cutting a part of the human body to create an opening in which jewelry may be worn or an implant inserted. The word piercing can refer to the act or practice, to the opening created, or to the jewelry itself. Ear piercing and nose piercing have been widespread in many cultures throughout recorded history, while a much wider range of piercings became fashionable in Western culture in the late 20th century.

History

Body adornment by piercing is ancient. Mummified remains over 5,000 years old have been found with earrings, and nose piercing is depicted in Middle Eastern art from around 1500 BCE. Lip and tongue piercing were practiced by Aztec and Maya cultures, often for religious purposes. In the late 1970s, piercing beyond the earlobe was popularized in the West by subcultural movements and figures such as Jim Ward, founder of the Gauntlet in Los Angeles, often described as the first body piercing shop in the United States.

Common types

Widely practiced piercings include the earlobe and ear cartilage, nostril, septum, eyebrow, lip and labret, tongue, navel, nipple, and various genital piercings. Surface piercings and dermal anchors sit on or under the skin of flat areas of the body. Jewelry forms include studs, barbells, captive bead rings, and plugs for stretched piercings.

Procedure and healing

Professional piercings are usually performed with a hollow, sterilized needle rather than a piercing gun. Healing times vary from a few weeks for earlobes to nine months or more for some cartilage and navel piercings. Proper aftercare — typically cleaning with sterile saline and avoiding unnecessary handling — reduces complications.

Risks

Complications can include infection, allergic reaction to jewelry metals such as nickel, scarring and keloid formation, rejection or migration of the jewelry, and, for oral piercings, damage to teeth and gums. Regulation of piercing studios varies by jurisdiction; the Association of Professional Piercers promotes safe practice standards.
