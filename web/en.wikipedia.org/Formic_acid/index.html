Formic acid

Formic acid is the simplest carboxylic acid.

Formic acid (systematically methanoic acid) is the simplest carboxylic acid, with the chemical formula HCOOH and structure H−C(=O)−OH. It is an important intermediate in chemical synthesis and occurs naturally, most notably in the venom of bee and ant stings — its name comes from the Latin formica, meaning ant, since it was first isolated by the distillation of ant bodies in the seventeenth century.

Natural occurrence

Many stinging insects of the order Hymenoptera produce formic acid; ants of the subfamily Formicinae spray it as a defence, and in some species it makes up over half the venom volume. It is also found in nettle stings and in trace quantities in honey. The substance was described by naturalists including John Ray, who distilled it from ants in 1671.

Production and uses

Industrially, formic acid is produced mainly by the hydrolysis of methyl formate, itself made from methanol and carbon monoxide; it is also a significant co-product of acetic acid manufacture. Annual production is on the order of a million tonnes. Major uses include preservative and antibacterial treatment of livestock feed and silage, leather tanning, textile dyeing and descaling. Beekeepers use it to control varroa mites. Because it decomposes to hydrogen and carbon dioxide under catalysis, formic acid is studied as a liquid hydrogen-storage medium for fuel cells.

Safety

Formic acid is corrosive: concentrated solutions cause skin burns and eye damage, and ingestion or inhalation is dangerous. It is nonetheless far less toxic than methanol, into which it can be metabolized — formic acid accumulation is largely responsible for methanol poisoning's damage to the optic nerve.
