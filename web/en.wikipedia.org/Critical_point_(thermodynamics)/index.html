Critical point (thermodynamics)

Critical point of water
Temperature: 374 °C (647 K)
Pressure: 218 atm (22.1 MPa)
Above this point liquid water and steam become indistinguishable.

In thermodynamics, a critical point (or critical state) is the end point of a phase equilibrium curve. The most prominent example is the liquid–vapour critical point, the end point of the pressure–temperature curve that designates conditions under which a liquid and its vapour can coexist. At higher temperatures, the gas cannot be liquefied by pressure alone. At the critical point, defined by a critical temperature Tc and a critical pressure pc, phase boundaries vanish and the densities of the liquid and gas become equal.

A typical phase diagram: the green line (liquid–gas boundary) terminates at the critical point

Overview

As a liquid–vapour mixture approaches the critical temperature, the properties of the gas and liquid phases converge, and only one phase — a supercritical fluid — exists above it. Near the critical point, physical properties change dramatically: density fluctuations occur on all length scales, producing the milky phenomenon of critical opalescence. The concept was developed in the 19th century through the experiments of Thomas Andrews on carbon dioxide, and the van der Waals equation provided an early theoretical account predicting a critical point from its constants.

Critical constants of selected substances

For water the critical point is at 647 K and 22.06 MPa; for carbon dioxide, 304.1 K and 7.38 MPa — readily attainable values that make CO2 a common supercritical solvent; for nitrogen, 126.2 K and 3.39 MPa; for helium-4, 5.2 K and 0.227 MPa, among the lowest known.

Supercritical fluids

Beyond the critical point, a substance forms a supercritical fluid with properties intermediate between those of a gas and a liquid: it expands to fill its container like a gas but dissolves materials like a liquid. Supercritical carbon dioxide is used industrially for decaffeination and dry cleaning, and supercritical water and CO2 are used in advanced power-generation cycles.

Other critical points

The term also applies to other phase transitions: liquid–liquid critical points in mixtures, the Curie point of ferromagnets, and critical points in the theory of phase transitions generally, where quantities such as heat capacity and compressibility diverge according to universal critical exponents — an insight formalised in the renormalisation group theory of Kenneth Wilson.
