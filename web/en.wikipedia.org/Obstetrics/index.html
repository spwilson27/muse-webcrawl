Obstetrics

Obstetrics is the field of study concentrated on pregnancy, childbirth, and the postpartum period. As a medical specialty, it is usually combined with gynaecology as obstetrics and gynaecology (OB/GYN). Obstetric care is provided by obstetricians, midwives, family physicians, and specialist nurses, and covers prenatal (antenatal) care, management of labour and delivery, and care of complications such as pre-eclampsia, haemorrhage, and obstructed labour.

Prenatal care

Antenatal care includes screening for chromosomal and structural fetal abnormalities (by ultrasound and blood tests), monitoring maternal blood pressure and glucose (for gestational diabetes), immunisation, and counselling. Ultrasound dating and anomaly scans at around 11–14 and 18–22 weeks are standard in many health systems. High-risk pregnancies may be managed by subspecialists in maternal–fetal medicine.

Labour and delivery

Labour is conventionally divided into three stages: dilation of the cervix, delivery of the baby, and delivery of the placenta. Delivery may be vaginal—spontaneous or assisted by forceps or vacuum extraction—or by caesarean section, whose rates vary widely among countries. Pain relief ranges from non-pharmacological methods to epidural anaesthesia. Major complications include postpartum haemorrhage, eclampsia, sepsis, and shoulder dystocia; historically, childbed fever (puerperal sepsis) was a leading cause of maternal death until Ignaz Semmelweis demonstrated the value of hand disinfection in 1847.

History

Childbirth was traditionally attended by midwives; male surgeons and "man-midwives" entered practice with instruments such as the Chamberlen forceps in the 17th–18th centuries. Anaesthesia (chloroform, famously used by Queen Victoria in 1853), antisepsis, safe caesarean technique, antibiotics, blood transfusion, and neonatal care transformed outcomes in the 19th and 20th centuries. Global maternal mortality has fallen dramatically but remains high in parts of sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia.
