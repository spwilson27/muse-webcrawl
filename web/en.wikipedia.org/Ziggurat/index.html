Ziggurat

TypeStepped temple tower
RegionAncient Mesopotamia
Period4th millennium BCE – 6th century BCE
Notable examplesZiggurat of Ur, Etemenanki, Chogha Zanbil

A ziggurat (Akkadian: ziqqurratu, "to build on a raised area") is a massive stepped structure built in ancient Mesopotamia. It has the form of a terraced compound of successively receding levels. Notable ziggurats include the Great Ziggurat of Ur near Nasiriyah, the ziggurat of Aqar Quf, Chogha Zanbil in Khuzestan, and Sialk in Kashan.

Ziggurats were built by ancient Sumerians, Akkadians, Elamites, Eblaites and Babylonians for local religions. Each ziggurat was part of a temple complex that included other buildings. The precursors of the ziggurat were raised platforms or terraces that date from the Ubaid period during the 6th millennium BCE. The ziggurats began as a platforms, usually with two or three levels.

History

The ziggurat was an artificial mountain raised for the worship of the gods. Unlike the pyramids of Egypt, the outside of a ziggurat was not smooth but tiered to accommodate the work that took place on each level. The Sumerian king Ur-Nammu built the ziggurat at Ur in the 21st century BCE, dedicating it to the moon god Nanna. It consisted of three tiers resting on a platform, and the whole was sheathed in fired brick set in bitumen.

Description

The core of the ziggurat was made of mud brick covered by an outer layer of baked brick. A triple flight of stairs led to the shrine at the top, where it was believed the god descended to meet the priests. Unlike Egyptian pyramids, ziggurats were not tombs; they functioned as high terraces for temples, and only priests had access to the summit.

Notable ziggurats

The best-preserved ziggurat is that of Chogha Zanbil in modern Iran, an Elamite structure built around 1250 BCE. The ziggurat of Babylon, Etemenanki, has been associated with the biblical Tower of Babel; it stood about 91 metres (300 ft) square and reached a similar height before falling into ruin by the time of Alexander the Great.
