Penrose process

The Penrose process is a mechanism predicted by general relativity by which energy can be extracted from a rotating black hole. Proposed by Roger Penrose in 1969, it exploits the ergosphere — the region outside the event horizon where spacetime is dragged around with the hole — within which an object can have negative energy as measured from far away.

Mechanism

A particle entering the ergosphere splits into two: one fragment falls through the event horizon along a negative-energy trajectory, decreasing the black hole's mass and angular momentum, while the other escapes to infinity with more energy than the original particle carried in. The black hole's rotational energy pays the difference. At most about 29 percent of a maximally rotating hole's mass-energy can be extracted in this way (for a single step, the emerging particle gains up to about 20.7 percent more energy than the incoming one).

Significance

Although impractical as an engineering scheme, the Penrose process is theoretically important: it led to the concept of black hole area and superradiance, and related wave-scattering and magnetic mechanisms (such as the Blandford–Znajek process) are invoked to power the relativistic jets of active galactic nuclei.
