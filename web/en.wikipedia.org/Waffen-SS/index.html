The Waffen-SS was the armed wing of the Nazi Party's Schutzstaffel (SS) organisation. It grew from three regiments in the 1930s into a multinational, multi-ethnic military force of nearly a million men by 1944, fighting alongside—but never formally part of—the Wehrmacht. Its units served on every major front of the Second World War in Europe and were also deeply involved in the crimes of the Nazi regime, including the Holocaust and numerous massacres of civilians and prisoners of war.

Origins

The Waffen-SS originated in the SS-Verfügungstruppe, formed in 1934 from political paramilitary units, together with Hitler's bodyguard regiment, the Leibstandarte SS Adolf Hitler, and the Totenkopfverbände, which administered the concentration camps. The force received the name Waffen-SS in 1940. Under the overall authority of Reichsführer-SS Heinrich Himmler, it developed its own recruitment, ranks, and training, and jealously guarded its independence from the army.

Expansion and foreign volunteers

Originally restricted to volunteers meeting the SS's racial criteria, the Waffen-SS expanded rapidly during the war. Himmler's organisation recruited "Germanic" volunteers from occupied and allied countries—Dutch, Flemish, Norwegian, Danish, and others—and later, as manpower needs grew, conscripted and recruited among ethnic Germans across Europe and eventually among non-Germanic populations, including Bosnian Muslims, Ukrainians, Cossacks, and even a small Indian legion. Of the 38 nominal divisions raised, many existed largely on paper or were at cadre strength.

Combat record and crimes

Early divisions such as Das Reich, Totenkopf, and Wiking fought in the campaigns in France, the Balkans, and the Soviet Union, and Waffen-SS formations were committed at Kharkov, Kursk, Normandy, the Ardennes, and the defence of Berlin. At the same time, Waffen-SS units were responsible for atrocities including the Le Paradis and Wormhoudt massacres of British prisoners (1940), the Marzabotto massacre in Italy (1944), the Oradour-sur-Glane massacre in France (1944, by Das Reich), and the Malmedy massacre of American prisoners during the Battle of the Bulge (1944). Personnel rotated between front-line units, the camp system, and the Einsatzgruppen, embedding the Waffen-SS in the machinery of the Holocaust.

Aftermath

At the Nuremberg trials, the International Military Tribunal declared the SS—including the Waffen-SS—a criminal organisation, exempting only those conscripted who had committed no crimes. The verdict, veterans' later campaigns for rehabilitation, and the myth of a "clean" Waffen-SS that fought honourably apart from Nazi crimes have been extensively examined by historians, who regard the organisation as an integral part of the regime's apparatus of war and genocide.
