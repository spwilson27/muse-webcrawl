Special economic zones of China

Special economic zones of China
Established: 1980
Original zones: Shenzhen, Zhuhai, Shantou, Xiamen
Later additions: Hainan (1988), Pudong (1990), others

The special economic zones (SEZs) of China are designated areas with more free-market-oriented economic policies and more flexible governmental measures than the rest of the country, designed to attract foreign investment, technology and managerial expertise. First established in 1980 under Deng Xiaoping's reform and opening up, they served as laboratories and engines of China's economic transformation.

The original zones

In May 1980 four coastal cities were designated SEZs: Shenzhen and Zhuhai in Guangdong (adjacent to Hong Kong and Macau respectively), Shantou, also in Guangdong, and Xiamen in Fujian, facing Taiwan. The locations were chosen to draw on overseas Chinese capital and trade links. Shenzhen grew from a fishing region of some 30,000 people into a metropolis of well over 17 million and a global technology hub.

Expansion

The whole island province of Hainan became the largest SEZ in 1988, and in 1990 the Pudong New Area in Shanghai was opened on similar principles. Fourteen additional coastal cities were designated "open cities" in 1984, and economic and technological development zones, bonded zones and free trade zones were later created across the country, including the Shanghai Pilot Free Trade Zone (2013) and the Guangdong–Hong Kong–Macau Greater Bay Area initiative.

Impact

SEZs pioneered market mechanisms — land-use rights sales, foreign joint ventures, flexible labour contracts — that were later generalised nationwide. Scholars credit them with a major share of China's exports, foreign direct investment and employment growth during the reform era, and the model has been studied and imitated by many other countries.
