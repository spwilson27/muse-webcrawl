Mammary gland

Cross-section of the human mammary gland showing lobules, ducts and the nipple.

A mammary gland is an exocrine gland in humans and other mammals that produces milk to feed young offspring. Mammals get their name from the Latin word mamma, "breast". The mammary glands are arranged in organs such as the breasts in primates, the udder in ruminants and the mammary papillae of marsupials. A complex network of branching ducts and secretory lobules, the glands respond to hormonal signals — principally oestrogen, progesterone, prolactin and oxytocin — to develop at puberty and pregnancy and to secrete milk during lactation.

Structure

The mature gland consists of 15–20 lobes arranged radially around the nipple, each lobe containing many lobules of milk-secreting alveoli. Alveoli are lined by secretory epithelial cells and surrounded by myoepithelial cells that contract to eject milk. Ducts from the lobules converge into lactiferous ducts that open at the nipple; in humans they widen into lactiferous sinuses beneath the areola. The gland is embedded in adipose tissue and supported by fibrous suspensory ligaments (of Cooper).

Development

Mammary glands develop embryologically along the "milk lines" running from axilla to groin. In humans only the thoracic pair normally persists. The gland remains rudimentary until puberty, when oestrogen drives ductal growth; full lobuloalveolar development occurs in pregnancy under progesterone, prolactin and placental hormones. After menopause the glandular tissue involutes and is replaced by fat.

Lactation

Milk secretion begins after birth, when the fall in progesterone permits prolactin to act. Suckling triggers oxytocin release, causing the milk-ejection ("let-down") reflex. Colostrum, the antibody-rich first secretion, precedes mature milk. Weaning leads to involution of the secretory tissue.

Clinical significance

Disorders of the mammary gland include mastitis (inflammation, often during breastfeeding), fibrocystic change, benign tumours such as fibroadenoma, and breast cancer — one of the most common cancers in women worldwide. Male mammary tissue can also be affected by gynaecomastia and, rarely, cancer.
