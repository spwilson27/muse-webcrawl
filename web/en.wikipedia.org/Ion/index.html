Ion

An ion (/ˈaɪ.ɒn, -ən/) is an atom or molecule with a net electrical charge. The charge of an electron is considered negative by convention, while the charge of a proton is considered positive. An atom that has lost one or more electrons becomes positively charged and is called a cation; an atom that has gained electrons becomes negatively charged and is called an anion. Because of their opposite charges, cations and anions attract each other and readily form ionic compounds.

Characteristics

Ions consisting of only a single atom are monoatomic ions, while those composed of two or more atoms are polyatomic ions. Ions can be created physically by radiation, heating, or electrical discharge, or chemically by reactions that add or remove electrons. A compound consisting of oppositely charged ions held together by electrostatic attraction is called an ionic compound, such as common table salt, sodium chloride (NaCl).

History

The word ion was coined by the English polymath William Whewell in 1834, from the Greek ἰόν, present participle of ἴεναι, "to go", for instances of substances appearing at opposite electrodes in the electrochemical experiments of Michael Faraday. Faraday's laws of electrolysis, published in the 1830s, first quantified the relationship between electric charge and the amount of material deposited at electrodes.

Net charge

The symbol for an ion includes the element symbol or molecular formula followed by a superscript indicating the number and sign of the charge, e.g. Na+, Ca2+, and SO42−. Ions are central to the behavior of plasmas, electrolytes, and the operation of batteries and mass spectrometers.
