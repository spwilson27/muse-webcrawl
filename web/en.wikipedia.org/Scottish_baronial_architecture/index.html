Scottish baronial architecture

Scottish baronial or Scots baronial architecture is a style of building that became popular in the nineteenth century as part of the Gothic Revival. It drew on the features of medieval Scottish castles and tower houses, combining them with the comforts expected of a modern country house.

Characteristics

Typical features include asymmetrical plans and elevations, crow-stepped gables, corbelled turrets (bartizans) with conical roofs, battlements, and small, irregularly placed windows. Interiors often featured a great hall, dark wood panelling, and heraldic decoration.

History

The style was popularised by the novelist Sir Walter Scott, whose home at Abbotsford (completed in the 1820s) set the pattern for romantic Scottish domestic architecture. The rebuilding of Balmoral Castle for Queen Victoria and Prince Albert in the 1850s gave the style royal endorsement. Architects such as William Burn and David Bryce brought it to maturity, designing dozens of baronial country houses across Scotland and beyond.

Decline

The style fell out of fashion after the First World War as country-house building declined, though baronial elements continued to appear in Scottish public and institutional buildings well into the twentieth century.
