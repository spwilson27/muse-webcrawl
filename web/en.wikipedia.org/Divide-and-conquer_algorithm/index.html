In computer science, divide and conquer is an algorithm design paradigm. A divide-and-conquer algorithm recursively breaks down a problem into two or more sub-problems of the same or related type, until these become simple enough to be solved directly. The solutions to the sub-problems are then combined to give a solution to the original problem.

Structure

The paradigm proceeds in three steps at each level of recursion: divide the problem into smaller subproblems; conquer the subproblems by solving them recursively (or directly if small enough — the base case); and combine the solutions into a solution of the original problem. The running time of such algorithms is typically expressed as a recurrence relation and analysed with the master theorem or the Akra–Bazzi method.

Examples

Classic examples include merge sort and quicksort for sorting; binary search; the Karatsuba algorithm for fast multiplication; Strassen's algorithm for matrix multiplication; and the Cooley–Tukey fast Fourier transform. Divide-and-conquer algorithms are naturally suited to parallel execution, since subproblems can be solved independently, and form the basis of the MapReduce model. The name evokes the Latin phrase divide et impera ("divide and rule").

Relation to other techniques

Divide and conquer differs from dynamic programming in that subproblems are disjoint rather than overlapping; when subproblems overlap, memoisation or tabulation converts the recursion into dynamic programming. A variant, decrease and conquer, reduces the problem to a single smaller subproblem, as in binary search.
