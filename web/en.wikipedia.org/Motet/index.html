Motet

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In Western classical music, a motet is mainly a vocal musical composition, of highly diverse form and style, from high medieval music to the present. The motet was one of the pre-eminent polyphonic forms of Renaissance music, and the word is used generally for short sacred choral works in Latin, often unaccompanied (a cappella).

History

The motet emerged in 13th-century France from the clausulae of Notre Dame organum: words (French mot, "word") were added to the previously melismatic upper voices of a chant-based tenor, producing polytextual pieces in which different voices sang different texts simultaneously. By the 14th century, composers such as Philippe de Vitry and Guillaume de Machaut developed the isorhythmic motet, built on repeating rhythmic patterns (talea) in the tenor.

In the Renaissance the motet became the central sacred genre, typically a continuous imitation-based setting of a Latin text for four to six voices, cultivated by composers including Josquin des Prez, Orlando di Lasso and Giovanni Pierluigi da Palestrina. In the Baroque era the term broadened to include works with instruments; J. S. Bach's six surviving motets, BWV 225–230, are masterpieces of the form. Later composers from Mozart and Mendelssohn to Brahms, Bruckner and Poulenc continued to write motets.
