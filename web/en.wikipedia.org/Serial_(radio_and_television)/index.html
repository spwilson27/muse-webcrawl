Serial (radio and television)

Radio serials were a staple of 1930s broadcasting

In television and radio programming, a serial is a show that has a continuing plot that unfolds in sequential episodes, in contrast to a series, in which episodes are largely self-contained. Serials typically follow a single continuing storyline across many episodes, encouraging viewers to follow the programme from episode to episode to follow the unfolding narrative.

History

The serial form developed in early radio drama. Daytime serial dramas sponsored by soap manufacturers gave rise to the term soap opera. Programmes such as Painted Dreams (1930) and The Guiding Light (1937) established the open-ended daily serial, a format that migrated to television in the 1950s.

Evening television initially favoured episodic series, but prime-time serials such as Peyton Place (1964–69), Dallas (1978–91) and Dynasty demonstrated the commercial power of continuing storylines and season-ending cliffhangers. The famous "Who shot J.R.?" storyline in Dallas became a cultural phenomenon.

Modern serial drama

From the 1990s onward, serial storytelling became dominant in prestige television, in series such as The Sopranos, The Wire, Breaking Bad and Game of Thrones. The rise of streaming services, which release entire seasons at once and encourage binge-watching, further favoured heavily serialized narratives over standalone episodes. Many modern shows blend the two forms, combining a serialized seasonal arc with semi-standalone episodes.

Film serials

A related form, the film serial or chapter play, flourished in American cinemas from the 1910s to the 1950s, presenting stories in weekly chapters ending in cliffhangers. Popular examples featured characters such as Flash Gordon and were a major influence on later adventure filmmaking.
