Sankin-kōtai

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sankin-kōtai (Japanese: 参勤交代, "alternate attendance") was a policy of the Tokugawa shogunate during most of the Edo period in Japan. The purpose was to strengthen central control over the daimyōs (major feudal lords). It required feudal lords to move back and forth between their domains and Edo, the shogunal capital, typically spending every other year in Edo, while their wives and heirs remained in Edo as virtual hostages.

History

Practices of hostage-keeping and alternate residence existed under Toyotomi Hideyoshi, but the system was formalised by the third shogun, Tokugawa Iemitsu, in the 1634–1635 revisions of the Laws for the Military Houses (Buke shohatto), and was extended to nearly all daimyō by 1642. The system remained in force, with relaxations in its later years, until it was effectively abolished in 1862, shortly before the end of the shogunate.

Operation and effects

Daimyō travelled to Edo in great processions (daimyō gyōretsu) whose size was regulated according to rank and revenue; the journeys along highways such as the Tōkaidō and Nakasendō could involve hundreds or thousands of retainers. The obligation to maintain lavish Edo residences and fund these processions consumed a large share of domain revenues — often estimated at half or more — limiting the lords' ability to finance military rebellion. At the same time, sankin-kōtai stimulated road networks, post towns, commerce, and the spread of Edo culture throughout Japan, and helped turn Edo into one of the largest cities in the world.
