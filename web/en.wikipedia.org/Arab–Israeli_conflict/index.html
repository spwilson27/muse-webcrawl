The Arab–Israeli conflict is the phenomenon involving political tension, military conflicts, and disputes between Arab countries and Israel, which escalated during the 20th century. The roots of the conflict are often traced to the birth of Zionism and Arab nationalism towards the end of the 19th century, as both movements asserted claims to the territory of the former Ottoman district of southern Syria, later Mandatory Palestine. The conflict formally began with the end of the British Mandate and the establishment of the State of Israel in 1948.

Background

Under the Ottoman Empire, Palestine was majority Arab and Muslim, with smaller Jewish, Christian, and Druze communities. Jewish immigration, spurred by European antisemitism and the Zionist movement founded by Theodor Herzl, accelerated under the British Mandate (1920–1948). The 1917 Balfour Declaration committed Britain to support a "national home for the Jewish people" in Palestine, while promises made to Arab leaders during the First World War raised competing expectations. Violence between the communities grew through the 1920s and 1930s, including riots in 1929 and the Arab revolt of 1936–1939. In 1947 the United Nations proposed partitioning the Mandate into Jewish and Arab states; Jewish leaders accepted, Arab leaders rejected the plan.

Major wars

The principal interstate wars of the conflict include:

  • 1948 Arab–Israeli War — fought after Israel's declaration of independence; Israel survived and expanded beyond the partition borders, while over 700,000 Palestinian Arabs fled or were expelled in what Palestinians call the Nakba ("catastrophe").
  • Suez Crisis (1956) — Israel, Britain, and France attacked Egypt after Gamal Abdel Nasser nationalised the Suez Canal; international pressure forced a withdrawal.
  • Six-Day War (1967) — Israel captured the West Bank, Gaza Strip, East Jerusalem, the Sinai Peninsula, and the Golan Heights.
  • War of Attrition (1967–1970) — artillery and air combat along the Suez Canal.
  • Yom Kippur War (1973) — Egypt and Syria attacked Israel on Yom Kippur; initial Arab gains were reversed, but the war paved the way for Egyptian–Israeli diplomacy.
  • Lebanon Wars (1982, 2006) — Israeli interventions against the PLO and later Hezbollah in Lebanon.

Peace process

The Camp David Accords of 1978 led to the Egypt–Israel peace treaty of 1979, the first between Israel and an Arab state. The Madrid Conference (1991) and the Oslo Accords (1993–1995) opened direct Israeli–Palestinian negotiations and created the Palestinian Authority. Israel and Jordan signed a peace treaty in 1994. The Arab Peace Initiative of 2002 offered collective Arab normalisation in exchange for full withdrawal and a Palestinian state. In 2020 the Abraham Accords normalised Israel's relations with the United Arab Emirates, Bahrain, Morocco, and (nominally) Sudan, decoupling normalisation from a prior Palestinian settlement.

Current status

The Israeli–Palestinian dimension of the conflict — over borders, Jerusalem, settlements, security, and refugees — remains unresolved. Fighting between Israel and Hamas in Gaza, most destructively after the Hamas-led attack of 7 October 2023 and the ensuing Gaza war, has kept the conflict at the centre of regional politics, alongside tensions with Hezbollah in Lebanon and Iran, which supports armed groups opposed to Israel. Several Arab states that once formed the conflict's core belligerents now maintain peace or quiet security cooperation with Israel, even as public opinion in the region remains broadly sympathetic to the Palestinian cause.
