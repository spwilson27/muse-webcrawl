Open-source intelligence

Open-source intelligence (OSINT)
TypeIntelligence collection discipline
SourcesMedia, public records, social media, satellite imagery, academic publications
RelatedHUMINT, SIGINT, GEOINT, IMINT

Open-source intelligence (OSINT) is the collection and analysis of data gathered from open sources—information that is publicly available—to produce actionable intelligence. It is one of the core intelligence disciplines, alongside human intelligence (HUMINT), signals intelligence (SIGINT), and geospatial intelligence (GEOINT).

Open sources include news media, government reports and records, academic publications, commercial data, satellite imagery available for purchase, and the vast body of user-generated content on social media platforms. OSINT is legal to collect because the underlying material is public, though its analysis may be classified.

History

Governments have monitored foreign media since the Second World War; the United States established the Foreign Broadcast Monitoring Service in 1941 to translate foreign radio broadcasts and newspapers. The end of the Cold War and, above all, the growth of the internet dramatically expanded the volume and importance of open sources. In 2005 the U.S. Director of National Intelligence created the Open Source Center, later renamed the Open Source Enterprise. The wars and crises of the 2010s and 2020s—particularly the Russian invasion of Ukraine in 2022—showcased OSINT's value, as commercial satellite imagery, dashcam footage, and social media posts documented military movements in near real time.

The OSINT cycle

OSINT work typically follows the intelligence cycle: planning and direction, collection, processing and exploitation (including translation and geolocation), analysis and production, and dissemination. Distinctive techniques include geolocation of photographs by matching landmarks and shadows, chronolocation using sun angles and weather records, and verification of imagery to detect manipulation or miscaptioning.

Civilian and investigative use

Collectives such as Bellingcat, founded in 2014, have applied OSINT methods to investigate the downing of Malaysia Airlines Flight 17, chemical weapons use in Syria, and extremist networks. Journalists, human-rights researchers, and law-enforcement agencies increasingly rely on open-source methods, while cybersecurity professionals use OSINT for reconnaissance assessment and threat intelligence.

Challenges

Analysts must contend with information overload, deliberate disinformation, doctored imagery and deepfakes, and the ethical and privacy implications of aggregating public data about private individuals. The ease of collection also places pressure on tradecraft: verifying provenance is essential before conclusions are drawn.
