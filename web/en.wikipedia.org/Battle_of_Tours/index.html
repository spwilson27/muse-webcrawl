The Battle of Tours (also called the Battle of Poitiers, in Arabic the Battle of the Palace of the Martyrs) was fought in October 732 between Frankish and Aquitanian forces under Charles Martel and an Umayyad army led by Abd al-Rahman al-Ghafiqi, governor of al-Andalus, at a site between the cities of Tours and Poitiers in west-central France. The Frankish victory halted the great Umayyad raid into Gaul and killed Abd al-Rahman.

Background

Umayyad forces had held Narbonne and Septimania since 719 and raided deep into Gaul; Odo of Aquitaine had crushed one invasion at Toulouse in 721 but was defeated on the Garonne in 732 (see Battle of the River Garonne), after which he appealed to his rival Charles Martel. Abd al-Rahman's army plundered Bordeaux and Poitiers and advanced toward the rich abbey of Saint Martin at Tours. Charles positioned his veteran infantry on wooded high ground between the raiders and Tours.

The battle

After several days of skirmishing, the Umayyad cavalry charged the Frankish shield-wall repeatedly. The Mozarabic Chronicle of 754 says the Franks stood "like a wall of ice" and cut down the attackers. During the fighting, word spread in the Umayyad camp that Frankish scouts were plundering the accumulated booty, drawing troops back from the line; Abd al-Rahman was killed attempting to rally his men. The following morning the Franks found the enemy camp abandoned—the army had withdrawn south under cover of night.

Significance

Later generations, from Gibbon onward, hailed Tours as the battle that saved Christian Europe, though modern historians debate how far a large raiding expedition intended permanent conquest; Umayyad forces remained in Septimania until 759. The victory secured Charles Martel's supremacy in Francia and laid the groundwork for the Carolingian dynasty founded by his son Pepin the Short.
