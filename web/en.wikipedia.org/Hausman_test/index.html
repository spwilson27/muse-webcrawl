Hausman test

The Hausman test, also called the Hausman specification test, is a statistical hypothesis test in econometrics named after Jerry A. Hausman. The test evaluates the significance of an estimator compared to an alternative estimator, helping to decide between two models: one that is consistent and efficient under the null hypothesis but inconsistent under the alternative, and another that remains consistent under both.

Principle

The test compares two estimators: an estimator b0 that is efficient (and consistent) under the null hypothesis but inconsistent under the alternative, and an estimator b1 that is consistent under both hypotheses but not efficient under the null. Under the null hypothesis, the probability limit of both estimators is the same, so the difference between them should be close to zero; a systematic difference indicates that the null is false. The test statistic is a quadratic form in the difference of the estimators, weighted by the inverse of the variance of that difference, and is asymptotically distributed as a chi-squared distribution under the null.

Applications

The most famous application is in panel data analysis, where the Hausman test is used to choose between the fixed effects model (consistent under correlation between individual effects and regressors) and the random effects model (efficient if that correlation is zero). It is also used to test for endogeneity, by comparing ordinary least squares with instrumental variables estimates, in a form closely related to the Durbin–Wu–Hausman test.

History

Hausman introduced the test in his 1978 Econometrica paper "Specification Tests in Econometrics", which became one of the most widely cited contributions to econometric methodology.
