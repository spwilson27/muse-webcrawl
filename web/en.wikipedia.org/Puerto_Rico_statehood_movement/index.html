The Puerto Rico statehood movement (Spanish: estadidad) seeks the admission of Puerto Rico — an unincorporated territory of the United States since 1898 — as a U.S. state, giving its roughly 3.2 million residents full voting representation in Congress, the right to vote in presidential elections, and equal treatment under federal programmes. The movement is led principally by the New Progressive Party (PNP, Partido Nuevo Progresista).

Background

Puerto Ricans have held U.S. citizenship since 1917 but cannot vote for president and elect only a non-voting Resident Commissioner to the House of Representatives. Debate over the island's status — statehood, the current commonwealth arrangement, free association, or independence — has structured Puerto Rican politics for decades. Status plebiscites were held in 1967, 1993, 1998, 2012, 2017, 2020, and 2024; statehood finished first in the disputed 2012 two-part vote, won 97% in 2017 amid an opposition boycott, and secured 52.5% in a straight yes/no referendum in 2020, a slim majority.

Arguments and obstacles

Supporters argue statehood is a matter of civil rights and would bring economic benefits, including full access to federal benefits, while retaining Spanish language and Puerto Rican culture. Opponents raise the island's debt crisis, tax implications, cultural identity, and preferences for other options. Because admission requires an act of the U.S. Congress, and bills such as the Puerto Rico Status Act have stalled, the referendums remain non-binding and the island's status unresolved.
