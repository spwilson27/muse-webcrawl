Documentary photography

Documentary photography usually refers to photography used to chronicle events and images of the real world, recording people, places and social conditions—often with the intention of informing the public, bearing witness or promoting social change. The term was popularised in the 1930s; the genre encompasses photojournalism, social documentary, street photography, war photography and long-form photographic essays, and it is characterised by an ambition to present subject matter truthfully and objectively, notwithstanding the photographer's interpretive choices.

History

Documentary uses of photography emerged soon after the medium's invention in the nineteenth century: Roger Fenton's Crimean War photographs, Mathew Brady's studio's American Civil War images, and Jacob Riis's How the Other Half Lives (1890) and Lewis Hine's photographs of child labour all used the camera as a tool of record and reform. In the 1930s, during the Great Depression, the US Farm Security Administration (FSA) employed photographers including Dorothea Lange, Walker Evans and Gordon Parks to document rural poverty; Lange's Migrant Mother (1936) became an icon. After the Second World War, picture magazines such as Life and agencies such as Magnum Photos, founded in 1947, sustained the documentary tradition.

Styles and practitioners

Documentary photography ranges from direct, observational approaches associated with Henri Cartier-Bresson and the "decisive moment" to the subjective, diaristic work of photographers such as Robert Frank, whose book The Americans (1958) reshaped the genre. Other major figures include Sebastião Salgado, Mary Ellen Mark, James Nachtwey and Don McCullin. Contemporary practitioners increasingly combine photography with audio, video and text in multimedia projects.

Ethics

Because documentary photographs claim to tell the truth, questions of staging, manipulation, captioning and consent have long accompanied the genre. Digital editing and artificial intelligence have intensified debates about photographic truth, and professional codes of ethics for photojournalism restrict alteration of documentary images beyond basic processing.
