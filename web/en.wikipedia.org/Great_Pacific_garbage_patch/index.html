Great Pacific garbage patch

The Great Pacific garbage patch, also described as the Pacific trash vortex and the North Pacific Garbage Patch, is a gyre of marine debris particles in the central North Pacific Ocean. It is located roughly from 135°W to 155°W and 35°N to 42°N. The collection of plastic and floating trash originates from the Pacific Rim, including countries in Asia, North America, and South America. The gyre is divided into two areas, the "Eastern Garbage Patch" near California and the "Western Garbage Patch" near Japan.

Discovery

The existence of a garbage patch in the North Pacific was predicted in a 1988 paper published by the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA). The prediction was based on results obtained by Alaska-based researchers between 1985 and 1988 measuring neustonic plastic in the North Pacific. The patch was brought to wider public attention by oceanographer and racing boat captain Charles J. Moore, who encountered it in 1997 while returning from the Transpac sailing race.

Formation

Like other areas of concentrated marine debris in the world's oceans, the Great Pacific garbage patch formed because rotating ocean currents — in this case the North Pacific Gyre — slowly draw in and concentrate floating debris. An estimated 1.15 to 2.41 million tonnes of plastic enter the oceans each year from rivers, and once plastic enters the gyre it is unlikely to leave until it degrades into smaller pieces.

Composition and size

A 2018 study published in Scientific Reports estimated the patch at about 1.6 million square kilometres — roughly twice the size of Texas or three times the size of France — containing an estimated 1.8 trillion pieces of plastic weighing some 80,000 tonnes. The study found that microplastics accounted for about 94% of the pieces but only about 8% of the mass; discarded fishing gear, including nets, ropes and crates, made up a large share of the mass. Contrary to popular depictions, the patch is not a visible solid island of rubbish; much of it consists of microplastic particles suspended in the upper water column, giving the water a cloudy, soup-like quality.

Environmental effects

Marine debris harms wildlife through ingestion and entanglement. Sea turtles may mistake floating plastic bags for jellyfish, a common food item; albatrosses and other seabirds feed plastic pellets to their chicks, which can starve with stomachs full of indigestible material. Microplastics enter food webs as filter feeders and small fish consume them, and plastic debris can transport invasive species across ocean basins.

Cleanup efforts

The Ocean Cleanup, a non-profit founded by Dutch inventor Boyan Slat, has deployed floating barrier systems towed through the patch to concentrate and remove plastic. Governments and researchers also emphasise prevention — reducing plastic entering the oceans from rivers and fisheries — as the primary long-term remedy.
