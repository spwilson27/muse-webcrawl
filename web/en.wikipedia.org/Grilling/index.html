Grilling

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Grilling is a form of cooking that involves dry heat applied to the surface of food, commonly from above or below. It usually consists of a significant amount of direct, radiant heat and is used for cooking meat and vegetables quickly over a grill — an apparatus of parallel bars or a gridiron heated by charcoal, wood, gas burners or electric elements. The high temperatures, typically 260–370 °C (500–700 °F) or more, produce browning through the Maillard reaction and characteristic char marks.

Methods and terminology

In North American usage, grilling means fast cooking over direct high heat, and is distinguished from barbecuing, which is slower cooking with indirect heat and smoke. In British and Commonwealth usage, "grilling" usually means cooking under a top heating element (called "broiling" in North America), while outdoor cooking over charcoal is barbecuing. Related techniques include pan-grilling on a ridged griddle, plank grilling, and the use of a hibachi or yakitori grill. Foods are often marinated or basted to retain moisture during the short cooking time.

Health considerations

Grilling meat at high temperatures can form heterocyclic amines and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, compounds associated in laboratory studies with increased cancer risk; risk is reduced by avoiding charring, marinating, pre-cooking, and turning food frequently. Grilled vegetables and leaner meats generate fewer of these compounds.
