Mont de piété

A mont de piété (from the Italian monte di pietà, "mount of piety") is a charitable pawnbroking institution that lends money at low interest against pledged movable goods, originally established to protect the poor from usurers. The first montes were founded in 15th-century Italy by Franciscan preachers, notably Bernardino of Feltre, and the model spread across Catholic Europe.

A mont de piété served as a charitable alternative to private moneylenders.

Origins

The first monte di pietà was established in Perugia in 1462. Franciscans campaigned for institutions that would lend small sums to the needy, secured by pledges, charging only enough interest to cover operating costs. Early foundations in some cities lent entirely without interest, supported by alms and endowments.

In France

In France, monts de piété were founded in many cities from the 17th century onward; the Paris institution dates to 1637, founded by the journalist and philanthropist Théophraste Renaudot. The Paris mont de piété was reorganised in the 19th century and became the Crédit Municipal de Paris, which still operates today. Affectionately nicknamed ma tante ("my aunt"), it traditionally allowed people in temporary difficulty to pawn valuables discreetly; unredeemed pledges were sold at public auction.

Modern institutions

Surviving monts de piété and their successors, such as the French crédits municipaux, combine pawnbroking with banking services and social lending. The concept influenced later microfinance movements by demonstrating that small, collateralised loans could serve borrowers excluded from conventional credit.
