Vasopressin

Vasopressin, also called antidiuretic hormone (ADH), arginine vasopressin (AVP) or argipressin, is a hormone synthesized from the AVP gene as a peptide prohormone in neurons in the hypothalamus of the brain, and then converted to AVP. It is then transported down the axon terminating in the posterior pituitary, and released into the bloodstream in response to extracellular fluid hyperosmolality (hypernatremia) and hypovolemia.

Function

Vasopressin has two primary functions. First, it increases the amount of solute-free water reabsorbed back into the circulation from the filtrate in the kidney tubules of the nephrons, acting through V2 receptors that insert aquaporin-2 channels into collecting-duct cells. Second, at high concentrations it constricts arterioles via V1 receptors, raising arterial blood pressure — hence the name "vasopressin". It is released when osmoreceptors detect rising plasma osmolality or when baroreceptors sense falling blood volume or pressure.

Clinical significance

Deficiency of ADH causes diabetes insipidus, with excretion of large volumes of dilute urine; inappropriate excess (SIADH) causes water retention and dangerous hyponatremia. The synthetic analogue desmopressin is used to treat central diabetes insipidus and some bleeding disorders. Vasopressin itself is used as a pressor drug in vasodilatory shock.
