Salone dei Cinquecento

LocationPalazzo Vecchio, Florence, Italy
Built1494–1495
ArchitectSimone del Pollaiolo (il Cronaca), with contributions by others
Dimensions54 m long, 23 m wide, 18 m high
DecorationGiorgio Vasari and workshop (1563–1565)

The Salone dei Cinquecento (Hall of the Five Hundred) is the largest and most famous chamber of the Palazzo Vecchio in Florence, Italy. Measuring about 54 metres long, 23 metres wide and 18 metres high, it is one of the largest rooms in Italy built for the management of a civic government, and it is celebrated for its monumental fresco cycle celebrating Florentine military triumphs.

History

The hall was built in 1494–1495, during the Florentine Republic led by the Dominican friar Girolamo Savonarola, to house the Consiglio Maggiore (Great Council) of five hundred members established by the new constitution. Simone del Pollaiolo, known as il Cronaca, directed the construction, raising the great timber-trussed ceiling that still spans the room.

In the early 1500s the republic commissioned two of the greatest artists of the Renaissance to decorate the hall with battle scenes: Leonardo da Vinci was to paint the Battle of Anghiari and Michelangelo the Battle of Cascina. Neither work was completed — Leonardo's experimental technique failed and Michelangelo was called away to Rome — and both are known today only through drawings and copies, Leonardo's famously as the "lost Leonardo".

After Florence became a duchy, Cosimo I de' Medici made the Palazzo Vecchio his residence and had Giorgio Vasari remodel the hall between 1555 and 1572, raising the ceiling by seven metres and covering the walls and ceiling with frescoes glorifying Cosimo and Florentine victories over Pisa and Siena. Vasari's Allegories of the quarters and districts of Florence decorate the ceiling, and huge canvases of battles and ceremonies fill the walls.

Description

Along the walls stand sculptures including Michelangelo's Genius of Victory and works by Baccio Bandinelli and Vincenzo de' Rossi depicting the labours of Hercules and episodes of Medici history. At the far end is the raised platform known as the Udienza, originally intended for the Signoria. The hall continues to host official ceremonies, exhibitions and cultural events in Florence.
