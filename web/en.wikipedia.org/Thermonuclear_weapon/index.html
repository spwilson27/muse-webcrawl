A thermonuclear weapon, fusion weapon or hydrogen bomb (H bomb) is a second-generation nuclear weapon design. Its greater sophistication affords it vastly greater destructive power than first-generation nuclear bombs, a more compact size, a lower mass, or a combination of these benefits. Characteristics of nuclear fusion reactions make possible the use of non-fissile depleted uranium as the weapon's main fuel, thus allowing more efficient use of scarce fissile material such as uranium-235 or plutonium-239.

Design principles

Modern thermonuclear weapons use the Teller–Ulam design, in which a fission primary stage and a physically separate fusion secondary stage are enclosed in a radiation case. X-rays from the primary are contained and used to compress and heat the secondary — radiation implosion — igniting nuclear fusion of hydrogen isotopes (deuterium and tritium, the latter often generated in situ from lithium-6 deuteride). The secondary's fusion neutrons and, in many designs, a fissionable "tamper" or "pusher" that itself undergoes fast fission contribute much of the total yield. Stages can in principle be chained, and there is no inherent upper limit on yield. The design's specifics remain closely held secrets, though its broad outline has been publicly known since the late 1970s.

History

The possibility of fusion weapons was discussed during the Manhattan Project; Edward Teller championed the "Super" from 1942 onward, but early designs proved unworkable. The breakthrough came in 1951 with the Teller–Ulam configuration, demonstrated by the Greenhouse George shot and then at full scale by Ivy Mike (10.4 Mt, November 1, 1952), a cryogenic liquid-deuterium device too large to be a deliverable weapon. The dry-fuel Castle Bravo test (March 1, 1954) yielded 15 Mt — two and a half times the prediction, because of unanticipated lithium-7 reactions — and spread dangerous fallout over inhabited atolls and the Japanese fishing boat Lucky Dragon No. 5. The Soviet Union tested a true staged device (RDS-37) in 1955 and the 50-megaton Tsar Bomba in 1961, the most powerful explosive ever detonated. The United Kingdom (1957), China (1967), and France (1968) followed; India claimed a thermonuclear test in 1998, and North Korea claimed one in 2017.

Yields and deployment

Thermonuclear warheads form the backbone of the strategic arsenals of the nuclear-weapon states, arming intercontinental and submarine-launched ballistic missiles and gravity bombs, with yields typically from about 100 kilotons to over a megaton. Their development, combined with multiple independently targetable reentry vehicles (MIRVs), drove the logic of Cold War deterrence and arms-control negotiations.
