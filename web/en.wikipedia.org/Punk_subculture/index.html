The punk subculture is based around punk rock music and a confrontational, anti-establishment ethos that emerged in the mid-1970s in the United States, the United Kingdom and Australia. Closely associated first with bands such as the Ramones in New York and the Sex Pistols and the Clash in London, punk combined fast, stripped-down music with a do-it-yourself (DIY) ethic, distinctive fashion and visual art, and a broad politics of rebellion against authority, consumerism and social conformity.

History

Punk drew on earlier garage rock, the New York Dolls, the Stooges and the Velvet Underground, as well as 1960s counterculture and avant-garde movements. The term "punk rock" was applied in the early 1970s to 1960s garage bands, and by 1976–77 a self-conscious punk scene had crystallised around venues such as CBGB in New York and the 100 Club in London. The Sex Pistols' notorious career, managed by Malcolm McLaren, and their single "God Save the Queen" (1977) made punk a focus of moral panic in Britain.

In the late 1970s and 1980s punk fragmented into subgenres and scenes including hardcore punk, anarcho-punk, Oi!, post-punk and new wave. The 1990s saw punk-influenced pop punk and alternative rock reach mass audiences, while underground scenes continued worldwide.

Characteristics

Punk culture encompasses music, fashion (torn clothing, leather jackets, spiked and dyed hair, mohawks, safety pins), visual art and zines, dance (pogoing, moshing, stage diving), and literature. Ideologically, punks commonly emphasise anti-authoritarianism, anti-corporatism, individual freedom and direct action; many identify with anarchism, though political views within punk vary widely.
