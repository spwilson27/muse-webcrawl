Twee pop is a subgenre of indie pop characterised by deliberately simple, melodic songs, sweet or naive lyrical themes, jangling guitars and a do-it-yourself aesthetic. The word "twee" — British slang for excessively dainty or cute — was initially used derisively but was adopted by fans and bands themselves.

Origins

The genre coalesced around the British C86 cassette released by the NME in 1986, which showcased bands such as The Pastels, Primal Scream (in their early incarnation) and The Shop Assistants. Centred on independent labels such as Sarah Records of Bristol — home to The Field Mice, Heavenly and Talulah Gosh — twee emphasised emotional directness, boy-girl harmonies and a rejection of rock machismo, often paired with a childlike visual style.

American scene and legacy

In the United States, Calvin Johnson's K Records in Olympia, Washington, promoted an "International Pop Underground" with bands such as Beat Happening, and twee merged with riot grrrl and lo-fi movements. The Scottish band Belle and Sebastian brought the style to wide acclaim in the late 1990s, and its influence persists in indie pop acts such as Camera Obscura and The Pains of Being Pure at Heart.
