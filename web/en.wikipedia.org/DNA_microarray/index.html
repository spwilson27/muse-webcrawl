DNA microarray

A DNA microarray (also commonly known as a DNA chip or biochip) is a collection of microscopic DNA spots attached to a solid surface. Scientists use DNA microarrays to measure the expression levels of large numbers of genes simultaneously or to genotype multiple regions of a genome. Each DNA spot contains picomoles of a specific DNA sequence, known as a probe.

Principle

The core principle behind microarrays is hybridisation between two DNA strands: complementary nucleic acid sequences pair with each other by hydrogen bonding. In a typical expression experiment, mRNA from samples is converted to labelled cDNA, applied to the array, and allowed to hybridise with the probes. After washing away non-specific binding, the array is scanned; the fluorescence intensity at each spot reflects the abundance of the corresponding transcript in the sample.

Fabrication

Microarrays can be manufactured by spotting pre-synthesised probes onto the surface, or by synthesising oligonucleotides in situ using photolithography, as in the GeneChip arrays produced by Affymetrix. Two-channel spotted arrays compare two samples labelled with different fluorophores (commonly Cy3 and Cy5) on the same chip.

Applications

Microarrays have been widely used in gene expression profiling, cancer classification, pharmacogenomics, and the detection of single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNP genotyping). From the late 2000s, RNA sequencing progressively displaced microarrays for transcriptome analysis, though arrays remain in use for genotyping and clinical cytogenetics.
