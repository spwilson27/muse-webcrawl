Internet privacy

Internet privacy
ConcernsTracking, profiling, data breaches, surveillance
Key lawsGDPR (EU), CCPA/CPRA (California), and others
ToolsEncryption, VPNs, private browsing, Tor

Internet privacy involves the right or mandate of personal privacy concerning the storing, repurposing, provision to third parties, and display of information pertaining to oneself via the Internet. It is a subset of data privacy and has become a major public concern as online services routinely collect browsing histories, locations, communications and purchasing behaviour.

Threats to privacy

Privacy online can be compromised by web tracking technologies such as cookies, device fingerprinting and tracking pixels, which enable advertisers and data brokers to build profiles across sites; by data breaches exposing personal records; by malware and phishing; and by lawful or covert surveillance by governments, a concern amplified by the 2013 disclosures of global surveillance programmes. Social networking services raise additional issues, as users voluntarily disclose information that can be aggregated, resold or used for manipulation, as in the Facebook–Cambridge Analytica scandal.

Legal frameworks

The European Union's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), applicable since 2018, grants individuals rights of access, rectification, erasure and data portability and requires a lawful basis for processing. California's CCPA and its successor the CPRA created similar rights in the United States, which otherwise relies on sectoral laws such as HIPAA for health data and COPPA for children's data. Many other jurisdictions, including Brazil (LGPD) and China (PIPL), have enacted comprehensive regimes.

Protective measures

Individuals can reduce exposure with end-to-end encrypted messaging, virtual private networks, privacy-respecting browsers and search engines, tracker blockers, strong unique passwords and two-factor authentication. At the technical level, privacy-enhancing technologies include differential privacy, onion routing (Tor) and zero-knowledge proofs.
