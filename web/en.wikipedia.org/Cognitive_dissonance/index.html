Cognitive dissonance

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In psychology, cognitive dissonance is the mental discomfort experienced by a person who holds two or more contradictory beliefs, values or attitudes, or who behaves in a way that conflicts with their beliefs. People are motivated to reduce this discomfort — typically by changing their attitudes, acquiring new information that reconciles the conflict, or trivialising the inconsistency. The theory was proposed by Leon Festinger in his 1957 book A Theory of Cognitive Dissonance.

Festinger's theory

Festinger argued that cognitions (beliefs, knowledge, opinions) can be consonant, dissonant or irrelevant to one another, and that dissonance creates psychological tension experienced as unpleasant arousal, which people strive to reduce much as they reduce hunger or thirst. The magnitude of dissonance depends on the importance of the cognitions involved and the proportion of dissonant to consonant relations.

Classic studies

  • When Prophecy Fails (1956): Festinger and colleagues studied a cult whose prophecy of a flood failed; rather than abandoning their belief, committed members proselytised more vigorously, reducing dissonance by gaining social support.
  • Induced compliance (Festinger & Carlsmith, 1959): participants paid only $1 to tell a lie about a boring task later rated the task as more enjoyable than those paid $20 — the small payment provided insufficient external justification, so attitudes shifted.
  • Effort justification: people who undergo severe initiations value a group more highly (Aronson & Mills, 1959).
  • Post-decision dissonance: after choosing between alternatives, people spread apart their evaluations, upgrading the chosen option and downgrading the rejected one.

Criticism and developments

Alternative accounts, such as Daryl Bem's self-perception theory, explain some classic results without positing aversive arousal. Later research refined the theory: dissonance is strongest when behaviour has aversive consequences, implicates the self-concept, or threatens a person's sense of competence and morality. Neural studies associate dissonance with activity in the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex and anterior insula.
