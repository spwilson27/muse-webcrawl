Torque wrench

Torque wrench
PurposeApply a specific torque to a fastener
Common typesBeam, click, dial, digital/electronic, hydraulic
UnitsNewton-metres (N·m); pound-feet (lbf·ft)
PatentedBeam type patented 1918 (Conrad Bahr)

A torque wrench is a tool used to apply a specific torque to a fastener such as a nut, bolt or lag screw. It is usually in the form of a socket wrench with an indicating, limiting or measuring mechanism. Applying the specified torque ensures that an assembly is tightened enough to carry its load and distribute clamping force evenly, without over-tightening that could strip threads, distort parts or break the fastener — critical in engine work, wheel fitting, aerospace and structural bolting.

Types

The beam wrench, the oldest and simplest type, has a deflecting beam and a pointer over a scale; it is inexpensive and durable but must be read straight-on. The click (micrometer) wrench is preset to a torque value and its internal clutch slips with an audible click when that torque is reached, signalling the operator to stop; it sacrifices some accuracy if not stored at its lowest setting and recalibrated periodically. Dial and digital wrenches read applied torque directly, and electronic models can log data and measure angle as well. Large industrial torques are applied with hydraulic torque wrenches or torque multipliers, while very small torques use torque screwdrivers.

Use and calibration

Specified torque assumes particular friction conditions; lubricating threads changes the tension achieved for a given torque, so manufacturer instructions govern. Best practice is to pull smoothly, in the middle 20–100% of the wrench's range, and to have the tool calibrated at intervals — typically every 5,000 cycles or 12 months for professional use, against standards such as ISO 6789.
