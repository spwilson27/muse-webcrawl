The Bourbon Restoration was the period of French history following the fall of Napoleon in 1814 and again in 1815, when the Bourbon monarchy was restored under Louis XVIII and then his brother Charles X. It lasted until the July Revolution of 1830 swept the senior Bourbon line from the throne.

The settlement of 1814–1815

After Napoleon's abdication in April 1814, Louis XVIII returned to France and granted the Charter of 1814, a constitution preserving many revolutionary gains — legal equality, the Napoleonic Code, and the land settlement — while restoring the monarchy. Napoleon's return during the Hundred Days (1815) interrupted the Restoration; after Waterloo, a harsher "Second Restoration" followed, marked by the White Terror against former revolutionaries and the ultra-royalist "Chambre introuvable".

Louis XVIII and Charles X

Louis XVIII governed pragmatically, balancing liberals and ultra-royalists, though the assassination of the duc de Berry in 1820 strengthened reaction. His brother Charles X (reigned 1824–1830) was an uncompromising ultra: he compensated émigrés for confiscated lands, passed a law on sacrilege, and pursued a more authoritarian course. The conquest of Algiers in 1830 failed to restore his popularity.

Fall

In July 1830 Charles issued the July Ordinances, suspending press freedom and dissolving the newly elected chamber. Paris rose in the "Trois Glorieuses" (27–29 July); Charles abdicated, and the crown passed to his Orléanist cousin Louis Philippe, inaugurating the July Monarchy. The senior Bourbon line never ruled France again.
