History of whaling

The history of whaling spans thousands of years, from Neolithic subsistence hunts depicted in petroglyphs to the industrial pelagic fleets of the 20th century. Whales supplied meat, blubber oil for lamps and lubrication, baleen for corsets and springs, and ambergris for perfume. Commercial hunting devastated whale populations worldwide; an international moratorium on commercial whaling took effect in 1986, though limited hunting continues under various exemptions.

Prehistoric and early whaling

The Bangudae petroglyphs in Korea (6000–1000 BCE) show large-scale whale hunting with boats and harpoons, among the earliest evidence anywhere. Indigenous peoples of the Arctic — Inuit, Yupik, and Chukchi — hunted bowhead and beluga whales for millennia, and similar subsistence traditions existed among the Ainu of Japan, the Makah of the Pacific Northwest, and peoples of Indonesia and the Azores. Strandings of dead whales were exploited wherever they occurred.

Basque and early commercial whaling

The Basques of the Bay of Biscay were the first great commercial whalers, hunting North Atlantic right whales from at least the 11th century and establishing shore stations along the coasts of Spain, France, and, by the 16th century, Labrador (Red Bay). Basque techniques — lookout towers, fast rowing boats, and tryworks for rendering blubber — spread to Dutch, English, and French fleets that hunted bowheads around Spitsbergen and Greenland in the 1600s. The Dutch port of Smeerenburg flourished briefly as a whaling boomtown.

The age of sail: sperm and right whales

In the 18th and 19th centuries the industry centred on sperm whales, whose spermaceti and high-quality oil were prized, and on right and bowhead whales for baleen and oil. Nantucket and later New Bedford, Massachusetts, dominated the American fishery, sending ships on voyages of three to five years across the Pacific; Herman Melville's Moby-Dick (1851) memorialised this world. Yankee whalers opened grounds off Japan, in the Bering Sea, and around New Zealand, while British, French, and colonial fleets worked the South Seas. Petroleum (from 1859) and the American Civil War hastened the industry's decline, as did the loss of ships to Arctic ice disasters.

Modern industrial whaling

The Norwegian Svend Foyn revolutionised whaling in the 1860s–70s with the steam catcher boat and the explosive grenade harpoon, making fast blue and fin whales huntable for the first time. Shore stations spread from Norway to Iceland, the Faroes, and eventually South Georgia (Grytviken, 1904). Pelagic factory ships, developed in the 1920s with stern slipways, freed fleets from land stations and opened the Antarctic, which by the 1930s supplied most of the world's whale oil — increasingly for margarine and soap rather than lighting. Catches peaked catastrophically: some 29,000 blue whales were killed in the 1930–31 season alone, and blue, fin, sei, and sperm whale populations collapsed in sequence.

Regulation and the moratorium

The International Whaling Commission, created by a 1946 convention, set quotas that long proved too high; protection came species by species (blue whales in the 1960s, humpbacks, then others) as environmental campaigns and whale-watching tourism changed public attitudes. In 1982 the IWC voted a moratorium on commercial whaling, effective in 1986. Norway and Iceland continued or resumed commercial hunting under objection or reservation, Japan hunted for decades under a scientific-permit programme before leaving the IWC in 2019 and resuming commercial whaling in its own waters. Aboriginal subsistence whaling continues under IWC quotas in Alaska, Greenland, Russia, and elsewhere. Many whale populations are recovering, though the North Atlantic right whale remains critically endangered.
