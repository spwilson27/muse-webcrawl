Epitope

An epitope, also known as antigenic determinant, is the part of an antigen that is recognised by the immune system, specifically by antibodies, B cells, or T cells. Although epitopes are usually non-self proteins, sequences derived from the host that can be recognised are also epitopes.

Types

Conformational (discontinuous) epitopes are formed from amino acids brought together by protein folding; they are recognised by B-cell receptors and most antibodies. Linear (continuous) epitopes are contiguous stretches of amino acids; they are the epitopes presented to T cells bound to MHC molecules.

Most epitopes recognised by antibodies or B cells can be thought of as three-dimensional surface features of an antigen molecule. Epitopes recognised by T cells are short peptide fragments presented on the cell surface by MHC proteins.

Applications

Epitopes are central to vaccine design: a vaccine should present the protective epitopes of a pathogen so that immune memory can recognise the real organism. Epitope mapping identifies the binding sites of antibodies on their target antigens, and is used to develop monoclonal antibodies, diagnostics, and epitope-based vaccines.
