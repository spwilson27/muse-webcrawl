Akinetopsia

Akinetopsia, also known as motion blindness, is a rare neuropsychological disorder in which a patient can see stationary objects normally but cannot perceive movement in the visual field. Affected individuals experience the moving world as a series of static "stills," like frames of a film: a pouring drink appears frozen, then half-full, then overflowing, without the intervening flow. The condition arises from lesions to the visual motion-processing areas of the brain, particularly area V5 (MT) and surrounding cortex in the posterior parietal region.

The case of LM

The best-known case is that of "LM," a patient studied by Josef Zihl and colleagues in Munich in the 1980s after bilateral damage to the lateral occipital cortex following a stroke. LM reported that pouring tea was difficult because the liquid appeared frozen, and crossing streets was frightening because cars appeared far away and then suddenly near, with no perceived movement between. Her colour, form, and object recognition remained largely intact, demonstrating that motion perception is a separable visual function.

Causes and related phenomena

Akinetopsia has been reported after stroke, head injury, and neurodegenerative disease, and transient motion-perception deficits can be induced experimentally with transcranial magnetic stimulation over area V5. Related conditions include akinetopsia-like effects in some patients taking certain antidepressants. There is no established cure; patients adapt with strategies such as relying on sound and avoiding fast-moving environments.
