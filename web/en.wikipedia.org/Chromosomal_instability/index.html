Chromosomal instability

Field: Genetics, oncology

Effect: Frequent gains and losses of chromosomes

Chromosomal instability (CIN) is a tendency for cells to gain or lose whole chromosomes, or large parts of them, at an abnormally high rate during division. It is a common feature of solid tumours and contributes to genetic diversity within cancers.

Mechanisms

Defects in the mitotic spindle checkpoint, centrosome number, chromosome cohesion, kinetochore attachment, DNA replication, and repair can cause missegregation. The resulting aneuploidy may promote growth, metastasis, and drug resistance, although severe instability can also kill cells.

Clinical relevance

CIN is associated with prognosis in several cancers and is studied as a therapeutic target. It differs from microsatellite instability, which involves small DNA sequence changes caused mainly by defective mismatch repair.
