Public art

Public art is media whose form, function and meaning are created for the general public through a public process. It is artwork in the public realm, usually installed outdoors and accessible to all, though it may also be sited in publicly accessible interiors. Public art can take many forms — monuments, memorials, civic statuary, murals, mosaics, fountains, land art, integrated architectural features, and temporary installations or performances — and may be permanent or ephemeral.

History

Art in communal space is ancient: triumphal columns, equestrian statues and religious monuments were central to classical and medieval cities. The modern notion of public art expanded with 20th-century government programmes, including New Deal arts projects in the United States and, from the 1960s, "percent for art" schemes that dedicate a fraction of public construction budgets to artworks. Works such as Picasso's Chicago sculpture (1967) and Richard Serra's Tilted Arc (1981) — removed in 1989 after public controversy — became landmarks in debates about expertise, taste and the public's voice.

Themes and debate

Public art is often commissioned to commemorate historical figures or events, express civic identity, revitalise districts, or provoke reflection. Because it occupies shared space and is often publicly funded, it is a frequent site of controversy, notably over war memorials, statues of colonial or Confederate figures, and abstract works perceived as alien to their surroundings. Contemporary practice increasingly emphasises community participation, social practice and temporary or performative interventions rather than the permanent monument.
