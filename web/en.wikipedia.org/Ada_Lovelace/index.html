Ada Lovelace

Born: 10 December 1815, London, England
Died: 27 November 1852 (aged 36)
Known for: Notes on the Analytical Engine; first published algorithm
Parents: Lord Byron and Annabella Milbanke

Augusta Ada King, Countess of Lovelace (née Byron; 10 December 1815 – 27 November 1852) was an English mathematician and writer, chiefly known for her work on Charles Babbage's proposed mechanical general-purpose computer, the Analytical Engine. She is often regarded as the first computer programmer, because her notes on the engine include the first algorithm intended to be carried out by a machine.

Life

Ada was the only legitimate child of the poet Lord Byron, who left England months after her birth and died when she was eight; she never met him. Her mother, Annabella Milbanke, encouraged mathematics and logic in the hope of suppressing any poetic temperament — a plan that only partly succeeded, as Ada described her own approach as "poetical science". She was tutored by, among others, Augustus De Morgan, and corresponded with Babbage from 1833, when she first saw his Difference Engine. In 1835 she married William King, later Earl of Lovelace, with whom she had three children.

The Notes

In 1842–43 Lovelace translated an article by the Italian engineer Luigi Menabrea describing the Analytical Engine, and appended a set of notes (labelled A to G) three times longer than the article itself. Note G contains a detailed method for computing Bernoulli numbers on the engine — the algorithm now celebrated as the first computer program. Beyond the algorithm, she grasped the machine's broader potential: she wrote that it could compose music and produce graphics if the relations of pitched sounds or other phenomena could be expressed symbolically, while also observing that "the Analytical Engine has no pretensions whatever to originate anything" — a remark later discussed by Alan Turing in his paper on machine intelligence.

Legacy

Lovelace died of uterine cancer at 36, the same age as her father. Her notes were largely forgotten until republished in the 1950s; since then she has become an icon of women in science and computing. The programming language Ada is named after her, and Ada Lovelace Day is celebrated internationally each October to honour women's achievements in STEM.
