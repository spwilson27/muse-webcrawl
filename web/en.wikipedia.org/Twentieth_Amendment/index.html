Twentieth Amendment

ConstitutionUnited States
ProposedMarch 2, 1932
RatifiedJanuary 23, 1933
Nickname"Lame Duck Amendment"

The Twentieth Amendment to the United States Constitution establishes the beginning and ending dates of the terms of federal elected officials. It moved the start of presidential and vice-presidential terms from March 4 to January 20, and of congressional terms to January 3, greatly shortening the "lame duck" period between an election and the new officeholders taking power.

Provisions

Section 1 sets the term dates. Section 2 requires Congress to assemble at least once a year, beginning on January 3. Section 3 provides that if the president-elect dies before inauguration, the vice president-elect becomes president, and empowers Congress to legislate for cases where neither a president-elect nor vice president-elect has qualified. Section 4 addresses deaths among candidates in contingent elections held by the House or Senate.

Background

Under the original Constitution, four months separated the November election from the March inauguration — a gap suited to 18th-century travel but dangerous in crises, as the secession winter of 1860–61 and the banking emergency of 1932–33 showed. Senator George W. Norris championed the amendment for over a decade before its ratification.

Application

The new dates first applied to Congress in 1935 and to the presidential inauguration of Franklin D. Roosevelt's second term on January 20, 1937.
