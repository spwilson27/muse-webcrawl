Sèvres syndrome

FieldInternational relations, Turkish politics
Named afterTreaty of Sèvres (1920)

Sèvres syndrome (Turkish: Sevr Sendromu) is a term used in Turkish political discourse to describe a persistent fear or belief that foreign powers are secretly plotting to weaken, divide or partition Turkey. It takes its name from the Treaty of Sèvres of 1920, which proposed carving up the Ottoman Empire after the First World War.

Background

The Treaty of Sèvres, signed between the Allies and the Ottoman government, envisaged an independent Armenia, possible Kurdish autonomy or independence, Greek administration of Smyrna (İzmir) and international control of the Straits. The treaty was never implemented: the Turkish War of Independence, led by Mustafa Kemal Atatürk, overturned it, and it was replaced by the Treaty of Lausanne in 1923, which recognised the modern Republic of Turkey.

Use of the term

Analysts use "Sèvres syndrome" to describe the suspicion with which some Turkish officials and commentators view Western policies, minority rights demands, and foreign criticism, interpreting them as steps toward a renewed partition. The concept is frequently invoked in debates over Kurdish issues, European Union accession, and the Armenian genocide recognition campaigns. Critics of the term argue it can be used to dismiss legitimate concerns or dissent as foreign manipulation.
