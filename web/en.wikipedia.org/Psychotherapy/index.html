Psychotherapy, also called talk therapy or counselling, is the use of psychological methods — particularly regular personal interaction — to help a person change behaviour, increase happiness, and overcome problems such as depression, anxiety, and relationship difficulties. It is practised by clinical psychologists, psychiatrists, counsellors, and other licensed professionals.

History and schools

Modern psychotherapy developed from psychoanalysis, founded by Sigmund Freud in the 1890s around free association and interpretation of unconscious material. Later schools include Carl Jung's analytical psychology, Carl Rogers's person-centred (humanistic) therapy, the behaviour therapies, and Aaron Beck's cognitive therapy. From the 1960s these combined into cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT), now among the most extensively researched approaches. Other major modalities include psychodynamic therapy, dialectical behaviour therapy, EMDR, family and systemic therapies, and integrative approaches drawing on several schools.

Effectiveness

Decades of outcome research and meta-analyses find that psychotherapy is effective for many conditions, with benefits comparable to or exceeding medication for several disorders and fewer side effects; the strength of the therapeutic relationship ("common factors") predicts outcomes across schools. Sessions typically last about 50 minutes and may run from a few meetings to several years. Regulation of titles and practice varies widely between countries.
