Treaty rights

Treaty rights are the rights reserved to, or recognised for, Indigenous peoples under treaties with colonial and national governments, principally in the United States, Canada, and New Zealand. They commonly include rights to hunt, fish, and gather on traditional territories, rights to reservation lands and resources, and various forms of tribal self-government.

United States

Between 1778 and 1871 the United States concluded hundreds of treaties with Native American tribes. Although Congress ended treaty-making in 1871, existing treaties remain in force and are described in American law as the "supreme law of the land". Because tribes are treated as having reserved all rights not expressly ceded, treaty rights have been upheld by the Supreme Court in cases such as United States v. Winans (1905), concerning fishing access, and the Boldt decision of 1974, which upheld treaty tribes' right to half the salmon harvest in Washington State. In McGirt v. Oklahoma (2020) the Court held that the Muscogee (Creek) reservation in Oklahoma had never been disestablished.

Canada

In Canada, treaty rights are recognised and affirmed by section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982. They arise from the historic numbered treaties, the Maritime peace and friendship treaties, and modern comprehensive land claims agreements. Canadian courts have developed principles that treaties are to be interpreted as the Indigenous parties would naturally have understood them, and that the Crown has fiduciary obligations in implementing them.

New Zealand

In New Zealand, the Treaty of Waitangi (1840) underpins the claims and settlements process, under which the Crown has apologised for historical breaches and provided compensation to Māori iwi.
