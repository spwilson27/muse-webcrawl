Chinatown is a 1974 American neo-noir mystery film directed by Roman Polanski from a screenplay by Robert Towne. It stars Jack Nicholson as private detective J. J. "Jake" Gittes and Faye Dunaway as Evelyn Mulwray, with John Huston as the villainous Noah Cross. Set in 1937 Los Angeles, the story was inspired by the California water wars: Gittes, hired to investigate an adultery case, uncovers a conspiracy to steal water and land, and a far darker family secret. The film is widely considered one of the greatest ever made, and Towne's screenplay — which won the Academy Award for Best Original Screenplay — is frequently cited as the finest in Hollywood history.

Plot

Gittes is hired by a woman claiming to be Evelyn Mulwray to tail her husband Hollis, chief engineer of the city's water department. After Hollis is found drowned during a drought, the real Evelyn Mulwray appears with a lawyer, and Gittes realizes he was set up. Digging deeper, he discovers that land in the northwest valley is being bought up cheaply by shell buyers while water is dumped at night — a scheme by Evelyn's father, Noah Cross, to profit once a new dam irrigates the land. Gittes also learns the devastating truth about Evelyn's relationship with her younger "sister" Katherine. In the climax in Chinatown — where Gittes once failed to protect a woman he loved as a young policeman — Evelyn is shot dead by police while fleeing with Katherine, and Cross takes the girl. Gittes's associate delivers the famous closing line: "Forget it, Jake. It's Chinatown."

Themes and legacy

The film is a landmark of New Hollywood, combining classic film-noir style with a bleak, downbeat ending that Polanski insisted upon against Towne's more hopeful draft. It was nominated for 11 Academy Awards. In 1991 it was selected for preservation in the U.S. National Film Registry, and it consistently ranks near the top of AFI and critics' lists of the greatest American films and screenplays.

Sequel

A sequel, The Two Jakes (1990), directed by Nicholson himself from Towne's script, was a commercial and critical disappointment. Towne had envisioned a trilogy about Los Angeles — water, oil, and land development.
