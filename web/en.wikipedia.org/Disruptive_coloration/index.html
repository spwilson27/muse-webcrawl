Disruptive coloration

Disruptive coloration (also known as disruptive patterning or disruptive camouflage) is a form of camouflage that works by breaking up the outlines of an animal, soldier or military vehicle with a strongly contrasting pattern. It is often combined with other methods of crypsis, including background colour matching and countershading. The pattern appears conspicuous out of context but makes the wearer hard to see when in place.

Principle

Vision systems detect objects largely by their edges. A pattern of sharply contrasting patches placed at the body's margin creates false edges inside the silhouette while destroying the real outline, so the viewer's visual system groups the pattern's elements instead of recognising the animal's shape. The naturalist Hugh Cott, in his 1940 book Adaptive Coloration in Animals, called this principle "maximum disruptive contrast".

History

The American artist and naturalist Abbott Handerson Thayer described disruptive patterning in his 1909 book Concealing-Coloration in the Animal Kingdom, although his wider claims about animal colour were controversial. During the First World War, navies adopted "dazzle" painting on ships, using disruptive patterns to confuse enemy range-finders about a vessel's heading and speed. Military uniforms from the mid-twentieth century onward — such as splinter, flecktarn and pixelated patterns — apply the same idea on land.

In animals

Many animals use disruptive coloration, including frogs, moths, cuttlefish, giraffes, zebras and numerous fish. The bold black-and-white pattern of the giant panda has also been interpreted as disruptive at a distance, supplemented by background matching at close range. Experimental work with artificial prey presented to wild birds has shown that patterns with high-contrast patches at the edge of the body survive predation better than patterns whose patches lie inside the outline.

Related effects

Disruptive coloration is related to, but distinct from, masquerade (resembling an uninteresting object) and from simple background matching. Some disruptive patterns are also aposematic, advertising unprofitability rather than hiding the animal.
