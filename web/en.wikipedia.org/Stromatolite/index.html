Stromatolite

Modern stromatolites in Shark Bay, Western Australia, among the few places living examples persist.

Stromatolites (from Ancient Greek στρῶμα strôma "layer, stratum" and λίθος líthos "rock") are layered sedimentary formations that are created mainly by the growth of layer upon layer of cyanobacteria, a single-celled photosynthesizing microbe. These structures are among the oldest evidence of life on Earth, with fossil stromatolites dating back more than 3.5 billion years.

Fossilized stromatolites provide records of ancient life on Earth, and today living stromatolites can still be found in a few places around the world, most famously at Shark Bay in Western Australia and in certain hypersaline lagoons and alkaline lakes.

Morphology

Stromatolites are layered, biochemical accretionary structures formed in shallow water by the trapping, binding and cementation of sedimentary grains in biofilms (microbial mats), especially cyanobacteria. They exhibit a variety of shapes and structures, including conical, stratiform, branching, domal, and columnar types that can grow to a metre or more in height.

Formation

Time-lapse photography of modern microbial mats shows three mechanisms by which stromatolites form: trapping and binding of sediment grains by sticky microbial filaments; precipitation of minerals, particularly calcium carbonate, induced by microbial metabolic activity; and the repeated alternation of these processes, which produces the characteristic fine internal lamination.

Fossil record

Stromatolites were much more abundant on the Earth in Precambrian times. While older Archean fossil remains are considered to be colonies of cyanobacteria, younger (that is, Proterozoic) fossils may be primordial forms of the eukaryote chlorophytes (that is, green algae). One genus of stromatolite very common in the geologic record is Collenia. The earliest stromatolite of confirmed microbial origin dates to around 2.7 billion years ago, though structures from the Pilbara and Barberton regions claimed to be 3.4–3.5 billion years old remain debated.

The decline of stromatolites in the late Precambrian and Phanerozoic is often attributed to the rise of grazing and burrowing animals, whose activities disrupted the microbial mats; their reduced abundance in modern oceans reflects both grazing pressure and competition.

Modern occurrence

Modern stromatolites occur mainly in hypersaline or otherwise extreme environments where grazing animals are excluded. Notable localities include Hamelin Pool in Shark Bay, Western Australia; Lagoa Salgada in Brazil; Cuatro Ciénegas in Mexico; and Pavilion Lake in British Columbia, where freshwater microbialites are studied as analogues for early life and for the search for life on other planets.
