Earth's crust

Earth's crust is the planet's thin, outermost solid shell of rock. Together with the uppermost mantle it forms the lithosphere, which is broken into tectonic plates. The crust makes up less than 1% of Earth's volume and differs markedly between continents and oceans.

Types

Oceanic crust underlies the ocean basins. It is relatively thin (about 5–10 km), dense (about 3.0 g/cm³), young (nowhere older than about 200 million years) and composed mainly of basalt and gabbro produced at mid-ocean ridges; it is continually created at spreading centres and destroyed by subduction.

Continental crust is thicker (typically 30–50 km, and up to about 70 km beneath young mountain belts), less dense (about 2.7 g/cm³), compositionally similar on average to andesite or granodiorite, and can be extremely old: rocks in Canada and Australia exceed 4 billion years. It is not readily subducted and so preserves a long record of Earth's history.

Composition and boundary

The crust is dominated by oxygen (about 47% by mass), silicon (about 28%), aluminium, iron, calcium, sodium, potassium and magnesium. Its lower boundary is the Mohorovičić discontinuity (the Moho), identified in 1909 by Andrija Mohorovičić from a sharp increase in seismic wave velocity marking the transition to mantle peridotite. Plate tectonic processes — volcanism, mountain building, erosion and sedimentation — continually reshape the crust.
