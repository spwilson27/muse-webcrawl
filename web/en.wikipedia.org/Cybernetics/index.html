Cybernetics

Feedback — information about a system's output returned to control its behaviour — is the central concept of cybernetics.

Cybernetics is the transdisciplinary study of systems, especially of communication and automatic control in both machines and living organisms. The field was named by the American mathematician Norbert Wiener, who derived it from the Greek kybernētikḗ ("governance" or "steersmanship") and defined it in his 1948 book Cybernetics: Or Control and Communication in the Animal and the Machine. Its core concepts include feedback, information, black boxes, and the regulation of systems towards goals.

Origins

Cybernetics crystallised out of wartime work on anti-aircraft fire control, prediction, and computing, and from a seminal 1943 paper by Arturo Rosenblueth, Wiener and Julian Bigelow, "Behavior, Purpose and Teleology". The Macy Conferences (1946–1953) brought together figures such as John von Neumann, Claude Shannon, Warren McCulloch, Margaret Mead and Gregory Bateson, linking control engineering, information theory, neurophysiology, psychology and anthropology.

Key ideas

  • Feedback — negative feedback stabilises a system around a goal (as in a thermostat or a governor); positive feedback amplifies change.
  • Requisite variety — W. Ross Ashby's law that a controller must command at least as much variety as the system it regulates.
  • Homeostasis — the self-regulation of essential variables, developed by Ashby in his homeostat and by earlier physiology.
  • Black box — studying a system by its inputs and outputs without reference to its internal structure.

Second-order cybernetics

In the 1970s Heinz von Foerster and others developed "second-order cybernetics", which includes the observer within the system being described, influencing fields from family therapy to epistemology and the theory of autopoiesis proposed by Humberto Maturana and Francisco Varela. Stafford Beer applied cybernetic ideas to management, notably in the Chilean Cybersyn project (1971–1973).

Influence

Cybernetics shaped control theory, systems theory, computer science, robotics, cognitive science and artificial intelligence, and its vocabulary — feedback, input/output, "cyber-" — entered everyday language even as cybernetics itself fragmented into more specialised disciplines.
