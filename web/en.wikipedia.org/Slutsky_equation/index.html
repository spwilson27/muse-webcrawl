Slutsky equation
FieldMicroeconomics, consumer theory
Named afterEugen Slutsky (1880–1948)

The Slutsky equation (or Slutsky identity), named after Russian economist Eugen Slutsky, is a fundamental result in microeconomic consumer theory. It decomposes the effect of a price change on the demand for a good into a substitution effect (the change holding utility constant) and an income effect (the change due to the altered purchasing power of the consumer's income).

Statement

For Marshallian (uncompensated) demand xi(p, w) and Hicksian (compensated) demand hi(p, u), the equation states:

∂xi/∂pj = ∂hi/∂pj − xj · ∂xi/∂w

The first term on the right is the substitution effect; the second is the income effect, proportional to how much of good j the consumer buys and to the good's income sensitivity.

Implications

The equation shows that the own-price substitution effect is always non-positive, while the total price effect can be positive for strongly inferior goods—the case of the Giffen good. The matrix of substitution effects (the Slutsky matrix) is symmetric and negative semidefinite, results central to the theory of revealed preference and integrability. Slutsky's 1915 paper, published in Italian, went largely unnoticed until its rediscovery in the 1930s by Hicks, Allen, and Schultz.
