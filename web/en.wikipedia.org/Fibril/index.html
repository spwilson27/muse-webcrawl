Fibril

Fibril
FieldCell biology, materials science
ScaleTypically 1–100 nm in diameter
ExamplesCollagen fibrils, myofibrils, amyloid fibrils, cellulose fibrils

A fibril is a fine, thread-like structural component found in biological and synthetic materials, intermediate in scale between individual molecules or filaments and larger fibres. The term derives from the Latin fibrilla, a diminutive of fibra (fibre).

Biological fibrils

In connective tissue, collagen fibrils — typically 20–100 nm across — are assembled from tropocollagen molecules packed in a staggered array and bundled into the fibres that give tendons and skin their tensile strength. Within muscle cells, myofibrils are the contractile rods built from repeating sarcomeres of actin and myosin. Plant cell walls are reinforced by cellulose microfibrils, while pathological amyloid fibrils — insoluble, β-sheet-rich protein aggregates — are characteristic of diseases such as Alzheimer's disease. A fibril should not be confused with fibrin, the specific blood-clotting protein.

Formation and properties

Fibrillogenesis — the self-assembly of fibrils — is governed by molecular packing, cross-linking, and templating proteins. The hierarchical arrangement of fibrils into fibres and tissues underlies the toughness of biological materials such as bone, silk, and cartilage, and inspires biomimetic materials design.
