In geometry, stellation is the process of extending a polygon in two dimensions, or a polyhedron in three dimensions, to form a new figure. Starting with the original figure, the process extends specific elements — such as its edges or face planes — usually in a symmetrical way, until they meet each other again to form the boundary of a new figure.

Stellation of polyhedra

When a polyhedron is stellated, its faces are extended until they intersect to form new cells. Johannes Kepler first studied the process, discovering in 1619 the two Kepler–Poinsot polyhedra — the small stellated dodecahedron and the great stellated dodecahedron — obtained by stellating the regular dodecahedron. Augustin-Louis Cauchy later proved that these, together with the great dodecahedron and great icosahedron, are the only regular stellations of the Platonic solids.

The icosahedron

The most famous stellation study concerns the regular icosahedron. In The Fifty-Nine Icosahedra (1938), H. S. M. Coxeter and co-authors applied rules proposed by J. C. P. Miller and enumerated 58 stellations of the icosahedron, plus the icosahedron itself — 59 in total. These include the compound of five octahedra and the echidnahedron, the final stellation.

Miller's rules

Miller's rules restrict permissible stellations to those preserving the full symmetry of the original solid, requiring that all faces lie in the original face planes, that cells be simply connected, and excluding some degenerate cases; without such rules the number of stellations of the icosahedron runs into millions. The dual process of stellation is faceting, in which parts of a polyhedron are removed without creating new vertices.
