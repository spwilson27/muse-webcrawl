Capital structure

In corporate finance, capital structure is the particular combination of debt and equity used by a company to finance its overall operations and growth. Debt comes in the form of bond issues or loans, while equity may come from common stock, preferred stock, or retained earnings. Short-term debt is also considered part of the capital structure.

Theory

The Modigliani–Miller theorem, published in 1958, argued that in a frictionless market the value of a firm is unaffected by how it is financed. Later refinements incorporated taxes, bankruptcy costs, and agency costs, giving rise to the trade-off theory, in which firms balance the tax benefits of debt against the costs of financial distress. The pecking order theory instead suggests firms prefer internal financing first, then debt, and issue equity only as a last resort.

Key concepts

  • Leverage — the use of borrowed funds to finance assets.
  • Weighted average cost of capital (WACC) — the blended cost of the firm's debt and equity.
  • Optimal capital structure — the mix that minimizes WACC and maximizes firm value.
