Spider silk

Spider silk is a protein fibre spun by spiders from spidroin proteins stored in silk glands. Spiders use their silk to make webs and other structures, which function as sticky nets to catch other animals, as nests or cocoons to protect their offspring, and to wrap and restrain prey before eating it.

Properties

Spider silk is remarkable for its combination of light weight, strength and elasticity. Dragline silk has a tensile strength comparable to high-grade alloy steel on a per-weight basis and far greater toughness, absorbing large amounts of energy before breaking. The fibres are also fine — a few micrometres in diameter — and are thermal conductors of unusual efficiency for a biological material.

Types

A single spider may produce up to seven types of silk from different glands: major ampullate (dragline) silk for web frames and lifelines, capture-spiral silk that is extremely stretchy and coated in glue droplets, tubuliform silk for egg sacs, and others for wrapping prey and building shelters.

Human uses

Because spiders are territorial and cannibalistic, they cannot be farmed like silkworms, so researchers have pursued synthetic production of spidroins in yeast, bacteria, plants and transgenic goats. Spider silk textiles have historically been made by laboriously collecting silk from wild golden orb-weavers, including a famous Madagascan cape exhibited in the 21st century. Potential applications include sutures, artificial ligaments, protective fabrics and biodegradable composites.
