Taylor series

In mathematics, the Taylor series of a function is an infinite sum of terms expressed in terms of the function's derivatives at a single point. For many common functions the series converges to the function itself over some interval, so the function can be approximated by a finite partial sum, called a Taylor polynomial.

f(x) = Σn=0∞ f(n)(a) / n! · (x − a)n

Definition

The Taylor series of a real or complex function f(x) that is infinitely differentiable at a point a is the power series above, where n! denotes the factorial of n and f(n)(a) is the nth derivative of f evaluated at a. When a = 0, the series is called a Maclaurin series. Examples include ex = Σ xn/n!, sin x = x − x3/3! + x5/5! − …, and the geometric series 1/(1−x) = Σ xn for |x| < 1.

History

Ideas behind the series go back to Archimedes' method of exhaustion and to 14th-century work by Madhava of Sangamagrama, who derived series for sine, cosine, and arctangent. The general series was published by Brook Taylor in his Methodus Incrementorum (1715), and Colin Maclaurin popularised the special case centred at zero. Taylor's theorem, which bounds the error of a truncated expansion, was given rigorous form by Lagrange and Cauchy.

Applications

Taylor expansions are fundamental in analysis and applied mathematics: they underlie polynomial approximation of functions in calculators and software, the solution of differential equations, asymptotic analysis in physics, and the linearisation of models throughout science and engineering. A function equal to its Taylor series on an interval is called analytic.
