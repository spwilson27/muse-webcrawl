In mathematics, Puiseux series are a generalisation of power series that allow for negative and fractional exponents of the indeterminate. For example, the series

x−2 + 2x−3/2 + x−1 + 2x−1/2 + 3x1/3 + …

is a Puiseux series in x. Puiseux series were first described in Isaac Newton's work of 1671 (published 1736) in connection with the Newton polygon method, and were systematically introduced by Victor Puiseux in 1850.

Definition

A Puiseux series in a variable x over a field K is a formal series whose exponents are rational numbers with a common denominator and are bounded below: that is, a series of the form Σ an xn/q for some fixed positive integer q, with n ranging over integers bounded below. Equivalently, a Puiseux series is a Laurent series in x1/q for some q. The set of all Puiseux series over an algebraically closed field K of characteristic zero forms a field, and a fundamental theorem (the Puiseux theorem) states that this field is the algebraic closure of the field of formal Laurent series K((x)); hence every algebraic equation over K((x)) has solutions expressible as Puiseux series.

Newton–Puiseux theorem and applications

The Newton–Puiseux theorem guarantees that the solutions of a polynomial equation F(x, y) = 0, with F(0, 0) = 0, can locally be written as Puiseux series in x (possibly after exchanging x and y). This gives a complete description of the branches of a plane algebraic curve singularity: the fractional exponents encode the ramification of each branch, and the Newton polygon of F computes the leading exponents. Puiseux expansions are fundamental in singularity theory, the resolution of singularities, tropical geometry, and the asymptotics of algebraic functions, and they generalise to Hahn series with well-ordered real exponents.
