Seventh Crusade

Date1248–1254
LocationEgypt (Nile Delta)
LeaderLouis IX of France
ResultCrusader defeat; capture and ransom of Louis IX

The Seventh Crusade (1248–1254) was the first of the two crusades led by Louis IX of France. Aiming to strike at the heart of Muslim power, it targeted Ayyubid Egypt as a route to recovering Jerusalem, which had been retaken by Muslim forces in 1244. The expedition ended in disaster and the capture of the king himself.

The campaign

Louis sailed from the purpose-built port of Aigues-Mortes in August 1248, wintered in Cyprus, and landed in Egypt in June 1249, quickly seizing the port of Damietta. Advancing south toward Cairo, the crusaders won a costly engagement at Mansoura (February 1250), in which the king's brother Robert of Artois was killed, but disease, supply failure, and the flooding of the Nile forced a retreat. At Fariskur (April 1250) the crusader army was destroyed and Louis captured.

Aftermath

Louis was released for an enormous ransom of 800,000 bezants and the surrender of Damietta. He spent four further years in the Holy Land strengthening the defences of Acre, Jaffa, and other coastal strongholds before returning to France in 1254 upon his mother's death. During the crusade the Mamluks overthrew the Ayyubid sultanate in Egypt, a shift with lasting consequences for the region. Louis's piety during the expedition contributed to his canonisation in 1297; he led one final crusade, the Eighth, in 1270.

Sources

The chief narrative source is Jean de Joinville's Life of Saint Louis, written by a seneschal who accompanied the king and left a vivid eyewitness account.
