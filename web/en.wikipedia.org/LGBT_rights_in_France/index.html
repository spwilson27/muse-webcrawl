Lesbian, gay, bisexual and transgender (LGBT) people in France enjoy many of the same rights as non-LGBT people. France was among the first countries in the world to decriminalise homosexuality, in 1791, and is generally regarded as one of the most socially liberal countries on LGBT issues, with same-sex marriage and adoption legal since 2013.

History

The Revolutionary Penal Code of 1791 omitted sodomy from the criminal law, and the Napoleonic Code of 1810 preserved this silence, a legacy exported across much of Europe. Discriminatory provisions nonetheless accumulated in the 20th century: a 1942 Vichy law set a higher age of consent for same-sex acts, and the 1960 Mirguet amendment classed homosexuality among "social scourges", doubling penalties for public indecency. These measures were repealed in 1980 and 1982, when the age of consent was equalised at 15 and the last discriminatory statutes were removed.

In 1999 France created the pacte civil de solidarité (PACS), a civil partnership open to same-sex and different-sex couples alike, which proved enormously popular. The campaign organisation Ni Putes Ni Soumises-era activism and debates of the 2000s were followed by the election pledge of François Hollande to open marriage to same-sex couples.

Marriage and family

The law of 17 May 2013 — the "loi Taubira", named after Justice Minister Christiane Taubira — legalised same-sex marriage and joint adoption, making France the 14th country worldwide to do so, after large public protests both for and against. Medically assisted reproduction (PMA), long available to heterosexual couples, was extended to lesbian couples and single women by the bioethics law of 2021. Surrogacy remains prohibited for all couples.

Discrimination and social attitudes

Discrimination on grounds of sexual orientation has been prohibited in employment since 2001 and comprehensively since 2008; hate crimes motivated by homophobia or transphobia carry aggravated penalties, and "conversion therapies" were banned in 2022. Successive polls show large majorities supporting equal rights, and Paris — home to the Marais district and one of Europe's biggest Pride marches — is a major centre of LGBT life, though rights groups continue to report persistent homophobic and transphobic violence.

Transgender rights

A 2016 law removed the requirement of sterilisation and medical treatment for legal gender recognition, substituting a court procedure; in 2017 France ceased to classify transgender identity as a mental illness in its official diagnostic practice, having been the first country to depathologise it at the national level in 2010.
