Bias (statistics)

In statistics, the bias of an estimator is the difference between the estimator's expected value and the true value of the parameter being estimated. An estimator with zero bias is called unbiased. Bias is distinct from variance: an estimator may be unbiased yet imprecise, or highly precise yet systematically off target.

Definition

For an estimator θ̂ of a parameter θ, bias is defined as Bias(θ̂) = E[θ̂] − θ. The mean squared error of an estimator decomposes as MSE = variance + bias², so a small amount of bias can be worth accepting if it buys a large reduction in variance — a trade-off central to modern statistics and machine learning (the bias–variance tradeoff).

Examples

The sample mean is an unbiased estimator of the population mean. The maximum-likelihood estimator of the population variance, dividing by n, is biased downward; dividing by n − 1 (Bessel's correction) gives an unbiased estimator for independent identically distributed observations. Shrinkage estimators, such as the James–Stein estimator, deliberately introduce bias to reduce total error.

Sources of bias in studies

Beyond estimator bias, statisticians use "bias" for systematic distortions in data and inference: selection bias, sampling bias, recall bias, publication bias, and confounding. Randomization, blinding, and representative sampling are the standard defences in experimental and survey design.
