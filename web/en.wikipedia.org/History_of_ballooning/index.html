History of ballooning


The first manned ascent, Paris, 21 November 1783

The history of ballooning begins with unmanned hot-air and hydrogen balloons in the 18th century and the first manned free flight in 1783. Balloons were humanity's first successful flying technology, preceding powered aircraft by more than a century, and were used for scientific observation, military reconnaissance, sport and exploration, including stratospheric and record-breaking long-distance flights.

Early experiments

Small paper hot-air balloons had flown in China for centuries as Kongming lanterns, used for signalling. In 1709 the Portuguese priest Bartolomeu de Gusmão demonstrated a small hot-air device before the court in Lisbon. The decisive breakthrough came in France in 1783: the paper-makers Joseph-Michel and Jacques-Étienne Montgolfier sent up a large unmanned hot-air balloon at Annonay on 4 June 1783, and on 19 September a sheep, a duck and a rooster flew before the court at Versailles. Meanwhile the physicist Jacques Charles launched an unmanned hydrogen balloon from Paris on 27 August 1783.

First manned flights

On 21 November 1783, Pilâtre de Rozier and the Marquis d'Arlandes made the first untethered manned flight, travelling about 9 km over Paris in a Montgolfier balloon. Ten days later, on 1 December, Charles and Nicolas-Louis Robert flew in a hydrogen balloon from the Tuileries. The first woman to fly was Élisabeth Thible in 1784. On 7 January 1785 Jean-Pierre Blanchard and John Jeffries made the first crossing of the English Channel by air. Pilâtre de Rozier died later that year attempting a Channel crossing in a hybrid hot-air/hydrogen design, the first fatality in aviation history.

Scientific and military use

Balloons quickly found scientific use: in 1804 Joseph Louis Gay-Lussac ascended to about 7,000 m to sample air and measure magnetism. Militaries adopted observation balloons from the French Revolutionary Wars (the company of Aérostiers at Fleurus, 1794) through the American Civil War and the siege of Paris (1870–71), when balloons carried mail and passengers out of the encircled city. Tethered observation balloons were used extensively in the First World War, and barrage balloons defended cities in the Second.

Dirigibles and sport

Steerable balloons — airships — culminated in the rigid Zeppelins of the early 20th century. Free ballooning remained a sport: Gordon Bennett Cup races began in 1906. In 1932 Auguste Piccard reached the stratosphere at about 16,200 m in a pressurised gondola. High-altitude research continued with projects such as the US Navy's Skyhook and the manned Manhigh and Excelsior programmes; in 1960 Joseph Kittinger parachuted from about 31,300 m.

Modern era

The modern hot-air balloon, with a nylon envelope and propane burner, was developed by Ed Yost and Raven Industries around 1960, reviving recreational ballooning. Long-distance records followed: Double Eagle II made the first transatlantic crossing in 1978, and in 1999 Bertrand Piccard and Brian Jones completed the first non-stop circumnavigation in the Breitling Orbiter 3. Steve Fossett made the first solo circumnavigation in 2002. In 2012 Felix Baumgartner ascended by helium balloon to about 39 km before his record freefall, and in 2014 Alan Eustace reached about 41.4 km. Unmanned balloons remain vital for weather forecasting and atmospheric research, with radiosondes launched daily worldwide.
