Road surface marking

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Road surface marking is any kind of device or material that is used on a road surface in order to convey official information; they are commonly placed with road marking machines (also referred to as road marking equipment or pavement marking equipment). They can also be applied in other facilities used by vehicles to mark parking spaces or designate areas for other uses. In some countries and areas (France, Italy, Spain, the UK), road markings are conceived as horizontal traffic signs, as opposed to vertical traffic signs placed on posts.

Road surface markings are used on paved roadways to provide guidance and information to drivers and pedestrians. Uniformity of the markings is an important factor in minimizing confusion and uncertainty about their meaning, and efforts exist to standardize such markings across borders. However, countries and areas categorize and specify road surface markings in different ways—white lines are called white mechanical, non-mechanical, or temporary.

Mechanical markers

Mechanical devices may be raised or recessed into the road surface, and either reflective or non-reflective. Most are permanent; some are movable. Cat's eyes, invented in 1933, are used all over the UK and many parts of the world. They consist of a reflective lens that is embedded in a durable housing that protrudes from the road surface. In North America, Botts' dots and raised reflective markers such as Stimsonite markers are common, while rumble strips provide both tactile and audible feedback to drifting drivers.

Non-mechanical markers

Paint was the first material to be used for road markings, applied with brushes or spray equipment. Modern materials include thermoplastic, preformed polymer tape, and epoxy. Thermoplastic is applied hot and fuses with the road surface, offering durability and retroreflectivity when combined with glass beads. Paint remains the least expensive option but wears fastest, particularly in areas subject to snowploughs.

Colors

In most of Europe and Australia, road markings are white, though yellow may be used to denote restrictions on waiting or stopping. In North America, white markings separate traffic flowing in the same direction and mark the right edge of the roadway, while yellow lines separate opposing traffic and mark the left edge on divided highways. Temporary markings in work zones are often orange, as in Germany, or yellow in much of the United States.
