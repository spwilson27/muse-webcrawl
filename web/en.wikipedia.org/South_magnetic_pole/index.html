South magnetic pole

South magnetic pole
Location (2020)Approx. 80.65° S, 72.68° E (off the Adélie Coast, Antarctica)
First reached16 January 1909 (Edgeworth David, Douglas Mawson, Alistair Mackay)
MovementAbout 10–15 km per year toward the north-west
CounterpartNorth magnetic pole

The south magnetic pole is the point on Earth's surface where the planet's magnetic field points vertically downward — that is, where a freely suspended magnetised needle dips straight down. It is the southern "dip pole" of the geomagnetic field, distinct from both the geographic South Pole and the south geomagnetic pole defined by the best-fitting dipole model. Because Earth's field is generated by the moving liquid outer core, the pole wanders, and it has drifted off the Antarctic coast into the Southern Ocean.

History

James Clark Ross calculated the pole's approximate position in 1841 but did not reach it. The first party to reach it was the Northern Party of Ernest Shackleton's Nimrod Expedition: Edgeworth David, Douglas Mawson and Alistair Mackay claimed the position on 16 January 1909 at about 72°25′ S, 155°16′ E, on the Antarctic plateau. Later surveys relocated the pole repeatedly as it migrated northward; by 2000 it lay offshore near Dumont d'Urville Station, and it continues to drift away from the geographic pole toward Australia's side of the continent.

Geomagnetic poles

The geomagnetic poles are antipodal points where the axis of Earth's best-fitting magnetic dipole intersects the surface. The south geomagnetic pole (near Vostok Station) differs from the dip pole because the real field is not a perfect dipole. Magnetic poles also swap entirely during geomagnetic reversals, the most recent being the Brunhes–Matuyama reversal about 780,000 years ago.

Expeditions and navigation

Unlike the geographic poles, the magnetic poles matter practically: compass needles point toward them, and charts must be corrected for magnetic declination, which is extreme near the poles. The south magnetic pole's drift is tracked by the World Magnetic Model, updated every five years and used in navigation systems worldwide.
