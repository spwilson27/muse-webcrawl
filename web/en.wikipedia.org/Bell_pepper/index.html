Bell pepper

The bell pepper (also known as sweet pepper, pepper, or capsicum) is the fruit of plants in the Grossum Group of the species Capsicum annuum. Cultivars of the plant produce fruits in different colours, including red, yellow, orange, green, white, chocolate, candy cane striped, and purple. Bell peppers are sometimes grouped with less pungent chili varieties as "sweet peppers". While they are fruits — botanically classified as berries — they are commonly used as a vegetable ingredient or side dish.

Description

The bell pepper is native to the Americas, where it was domesticated more than 6,000 years ago. Peppers were introduced to Europe and Asia by Spanish and Portuguese traders after the Columbian exchange. The fruit is blocky, typically three- or four-lobed, with a thick, crisp flesh. Green bell peppers are unripe versions of the red fruit; left to ripen, the pepper turns yellow, orange, and finally red, becoming progressively sweeter.

Nutrition

Bell peppers are an excellent source of vitamin C — richer, gram for gram, than citrus fruit — as well as vitamin A, vitamin B6, folate, and potassium. They contain no capsaicin, the compound responsible for the heat of other peppers, because of a recessive gene that eliminates its production; their Scoville rating is therefore zero.

Culinary uses

Bell peppers are eaten raw in salads and crudités, or cooked in stir-fries, fajitas, soups, stews, and sauces. Stuffed peppers — filled with rice, meat, or cheese — appear in many cuisines. Green peppers are a key ingredient in Louisiana's "holy trinity" of Creole cooking alongside onion and celery, and in the Catalan sofrito and French ratatouille traditions.

Production

China is by far the largest producer of peppers, followed by Mexico, Indonesia, Spain, Turkey, and the United States. In North America most bell peppers are grown in Florida and California.
