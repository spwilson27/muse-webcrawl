The Great Library of Alexandria, in Alexandria, Egypt, was one of the largest and most significant libraries of the ancient world. Part of the larger research institution known as the Mouseion, it was built under the Ptolemaic dynasty in the early third century BC and became the centre of Greek scholarship for centuries, attracting thinkers such as Euclid, Eratosthenes (who calculated the Earth's circumference there), Aristarchus of Samos, and the geographer and librarian Callimachus, whose Pinakes was an early bibliographic catalogue.

Collection

The Ptolemies aimed to gather all the world's knowledge. Ships docking at Alexandria were searched for scrolls, which were copied and confiscated into the library — borrowers received the copies while the originals, labelled "from the ships," were kept. Estimates of the collection range from 40,000 to as many as 400,000 scrolls at its height. A smaller "daughter library" was housed in the Serapeum temple.

Decline and destruction

The library declined over centuries rather than in a single catastrophe. Julius Caesar's fire in 48 BC burned warehouses of scrolls during the Alexandrine War. Ptolemy VIII had expelled many scholars around 145 BC, dispersing its intellectual community. The Mouseion faded under Roman rule; the Serapeum was destroyed in AD 391 amid anti-pagan riots under Bishop Theophilus, and the scholar Hypatia was murdered in 415. The popular story that Caliph Omar ordered the remaining books burned after the Arab conquest of 642 is a much later legend, not found in early sources.

Legacy

The library remains the archetype of the universal library and a symbol of lost knowledge. In 2002 Egypt opened the Bibliotheca Alexandrina near the presumed site, a modern library and cultural centre designed by Snøhetta.
