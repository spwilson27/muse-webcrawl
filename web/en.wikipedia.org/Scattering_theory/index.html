Scattering theory


A particle scattered by a target

In mathematics and physics, scattering theory is a framework for studying and understanding the scattering of waves and particles. Classically, a wave sent toward a target is deflected in directions determined by the interaction; measuring the outgoing pattern reveals information about the target. Scattering theory underlies much of experimental particle, nuclear and atomic physics, as well as acoustics, seismology and optics.

Concepts

Key quantities include the scattering amplitude, which gives the probability amplitude for scattering into a particular direction, and the differential cross section, proportional to the modulus squared of the amplitude, which describes the intensity of scattering at each angle. Integrating over all angles gives the total cross section. In quantum mechanics the incoming and outgoing states are described by wave packets, and the time evolution between them is captured by the S-matrix, which connects initial states in the distant past to final states in the distant future.

Methods

For weak interactions the Born approximation treats the scattered wave as a small perturbation of the incident wave. When a potential has special symmetry, the partial wave expansion decomposes the scattering into contributions of definite angular momentum. Optical theorems relate the total cross section to the imaginary part of the forward scattering amplitude.

Applications

Rutherford's gold-foil experiment, analysed with classical scattering theory, revealed the atomic nucleus. Modern particle accelerators, radar, medical ultrasound and materials science techniques such as neutron and X-ray scattering all rely on the theory.
