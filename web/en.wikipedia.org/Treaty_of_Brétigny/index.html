Treaty of Brétigny

Treaty of Brétigny
Signed8 May 1360 (preliminaries); ratified at Calais, 24 October 1360
LocationBrétigny, near Chartres, France
SignatoriesEngland and France
ContextHundred Years' War, first phase

The Treaty of Brétigny was an agreement signed on 8 May 1360 at Brétigny, a village near Chartres, between King Edward III of England and King John II of France, and subsequently ratified at Calais. It brought the first phase of the Hundred Years' War to a close, granting England vast territories in France in return for Edward's renunciation of his claim to the French crown.

Background

The English victory at the Battle of Poitiers in 1356 had captured John II himself, and France descended into disorder with the Jacquerie revolt and the revolt of Paris under Étienne Marcel. Two earlier drafts negotiated in London (1358 and 1359) demanded even larger concessions and were rejected by the French. Edward's campaign of 1359–1360, intended to force a decision, failed to take Reims or Paris, and a devastating hailstorm near Chartres in April 1360 (the "Black Monday" storm) helped push both sides to settle at Brétigny.

Terms

France ceded to England, in full sovereignty, an enlarged Aquitaine — including Gascony, Poitou, Saintonge, the Limousin, Périgord, Quercy, the Agenais and other lands amounting to roughly a third of France — together with Calais, Guînes and Ponthieu in the north. France agreed to pay a ransom of three million gold écus for John II, with hostages, including royal princes, held as surety for payment. In return Edward III renounced his claim to the French throne and his claims to Normandy, Anjou, Maine and suzerainty over Brittany and Flanders. Crucially, the mutual renunciations — Edward's of the French crown and John's of sovereignty over the ceded lands — were deferred to a later exchange at Calais and were never formally completed, a legal loophole later used to justify the resumption of war.

Aftermath

John II was released after the first ransom instalment, but when his son Louis of Anjou broke parole as a hostage in 1363, the king famously returned to captivity in England, where he died in April 1364. Under Charles V and his constable Bertrand du Guesclin, France avoided pitched battle and steadily recovered the ceded territories after war resumed in 1369; by 1375 England retained little beyond Calais and a strip around Bordeaux and Bayonne. The treaty is remembered both for the peak extent of English rule in France and for the unfinished renunciations that undermined its permanence.
