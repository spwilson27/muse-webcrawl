Bayesian probability

Bayesian probability is an interpretation of the concept of probability in which probability measures a degree of belief or knowledge, rather than a frequency or propensity of some phenomenon. It takes its name from the 18th-century statistician and theologian Thomas Bayes, whose theorem provides the mathematical rule for updating beliefs in the light of new evidence. Bayesian probability is one of the most widely used interpretations in statistics, machine learning, and the philosophy of science, contrasted especially with the frequentist interpretation, which defines probability as the long-run frequency of repeated events.

Updating a prior belief with a likelihood to obtain a posterior

Bayes' theorem

The central rule of Bayesian inference is Bayes' theorem:

P(H | E) = P(E | H) · P(H) / P(E)

Here P(H) is the prior probability of a hypothesis before seeing evidence E; P(E | H) is the likelihood of the evidence given the hypothesis; P(E) is the marginal likelihood or evidence; and P(H | E) is the posterior probability — the updated belief after the evidence is taken into account. Repeated updating, in which today's posterior becomes tomorrow's prior, allows beliefs to converge as data accumulate.

History

Bayes's essay was published posthumously in 1763 by Richard Price. Pierre-Simon Laplace independently developed and popularised the method in the early 19th century. During the early 20th century, frequentist methods of Fisher, Neyman, and Pearson became dominant in mainstream statistics, while Bayesians such as Bruno de Finetti and Leonard J. Savage developed the subjective theory of probability. From the 1980s, advances in computation — notably Markov chain Monte Carlo methods — made Bayesian models practical for complex problems, producing a broad revival across science and industry.

Applications

Bayesian methods are used in spam filtering (naive Bayes classifiers), medical diagnosis, A/B testing, robotics and sensor fusion, phylogenetics, astrophysics, and Bayesian networks for reasoning under uncertainty. In machine learning, Bayesian approaches quantify uncertainty in model predictions, and hierarchical Bayesian models pool information across related groups.

Criticism and the prior problem

The chief philosophical objection concerns the choice of prior: different analysts may choose different priors and reach different posteriors from the same data. Bayesians reply that priors are explicit and checkable, that "objective" (weakly informative) priors can reduce arbitrariness, and that with enough data the influence of the prior washes out. Debates between Bayesian and frequentist schools remain a staple of statistical foundations.
