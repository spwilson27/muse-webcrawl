Goodness of fit

The goodness of fit of a statistical model describes how well it fits a set of observations. Measures of goodness of fit typically summarise the discrepancy between observed values and the values expected under the model in question. Such measures can be used in statistical hypothesis testing, for example to test whether sample data are compatible with a specified probability distribution.

Tests and measures

The chi-squared goodness-of-fit test compares observed and expected frequencies in categories, using a chi-squared statistic whose significance is assessed against the chi-squared distribution. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test compares a sample's empirical distribution function with a reference distribution, and the Anderson–Darling test gives greater weight to the tails. The Shapiro–Wilk test is widely used to test for normality specifically.

Regression models

For regression models, the coefficient of determination (R²) measures the proportion of variance in the dependent variable explained by the model. Adjusted R² and information criteria such as the Akaike information criterion (AIC) and Bayesian information criterion (BIC) penalise unnecessary complexity when comparing models. In logistic regression, pseudo-R² measures and the Hosmer–Lemeshow test are commonly used.

Interpretation

A good fit does not prove that a model is correct: with small samples, poor models may not be rejected, while with very large samples even trivial deviations become statistically significant. Goodness-of-fit assessment is therefore combined with residual analysis, subject-matter judgement and out-of-sample validation.
