Resonance is a phenomenon that occurs when an object or system is driven by an external periodic force whose frequency matches the system's natural frequency. The system then oscillates with larger amplitude than when driven at other frequencies, because energy is transferred to it efficiently and accumulates over many cycles.

Mechanics

For a damped driven harmonic oscillator, the steady-state amplitude peaks near the natural frequency ω₀; the sharpness of the peak is measured by the quality factor Q, high Q meaning low damping and a narrow, tall resonance. Everyday examples include pushing a child on a swing in time with its natural period, the sympathetic vibration of a tuning fork, and the Helmholtz resonance of air in a bottle neck.

Engineering significance

Resonance can be destructive: soldiers break step when crossing bridges to avoid exciting structural resonances, and wind-induced oscillations contributed to the collapse of the Tacoma Narrows Bridge in 1940 (a complex aeroelastic flutter phenomenon often popularised as simple resonance). Engineers design structures so their natural frequencies avoid expected excitation, and add damping devices such as tuned mass dampers in skyscrapers. Conversely, resonance is exploited in clocks, musical instruments, radio tuners (electrical resonance in LC circuits), magnetic resonance imaging, and lasers.

Other domains

In acoustics, resonance of air columns and cavities shapes the timbre of instruments and voices. In chemistry, "resonance" denotes the delocalisation of electrons among equivalent Lewis structures, as in benzene. In particle physics, short-lived particles appear as resonance peaks in scattering cross-sections. Orbital resonance describes commensurate periods of orbiting bodies, as with Jupiter's moons Io, Europa, and Ganymede in a 1:2:4 Laplace resonance.
