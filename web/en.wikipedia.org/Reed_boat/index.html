Reed boat

Totora reed boats are still made and used on Lake Titicaca in Peru and Bolivia.

A reed boat (also reed ship) is a type of watercraft built from bundles of reeds or papyrus lashed together, among the earliest forms of boat known. Reed boats were used in ancient Egypt on the Nile, in Mesopotamia, on Lake Titicaca in the Andes, on Lake Tana in Ethiopia, and by other cultures where reeds grew beside navigable water. Bundles are tied tightly and shaped with upturned bow and stern; the buoyant stems keep the craft afloat even as it slowly absorbs water, so boats are periodically hauled out to dry or rebuilt.

Ancient Egypt and Mesopotamia

Egyptian papyrus boats appear in tomb paintings thousands of years old, used for fishing, hunting in the marshes, ferrying and ritual voyages; larger ceremonial versions in wood imitated their shape. In Mesopotamia, round coracles (quffa) of reeds coated with bitumen were used on the Tigris and Euphrates, and reed-bundle craft (keleks and mashhufs) served in the southern marshes of Iraq, where Marsh Arabs still pole slender reed canoes called taradas.

Lake Titicaca

The Uros people of Lake Titicaca build boats from totora reeds, which also form the floating islands on which they live. Larger two-hulled reed sailing craft were once used to cross the lake. In the 20th and 21st centuries the Norwegian explorer Thor Heyerdahl commissioned Titicaca boatbuilders for his experimental reed ships Ra, Ra II and Tigris, demonstrating that such vessels could survive long ocean voyages — Ra II crossed the Atlantic in 1970 — and supporting his theories of ancient transoceanic contact.

Other traditions

On Lake Tana, Ethiopian tankwa boats of papyrus remain in everyday use. Aboriginal peoples of Tasmania and mainland Australia made bundled-reed canoes, and North American peoples such as the tule-boat builders of California's marshes used similar craft. Modern replicas continue to be built for festivals, tourism and experimental archaeology.
