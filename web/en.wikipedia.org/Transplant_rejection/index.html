Transplant rejection

Transplant rejection occurs when transplanted tissue is rejected by the recipient's immune system, which destroys the transplanted tissue. Rejection arises because the recipient's immune system recognizes donor antigens — principally the major histocompatibility complex (MHC, in humans the HLA system) — as foreign. Rejection can be lessened by determining the molecular similarity between donor and recipient and by immunosuppressant drugs after transplant.

Types

Hyperacute rejection occurs within minutes to hours, caused by preformed antibodies against donor antigens, and requires immediate removal of the graft. Acute rejection develops over days to months and is mediated mainly by T lymphocytes; it is treatable with intensified immunosuppression. Chronic rejection is a slow, progressive loss of graft function over months to years, involving fibrosis and vascular changes, and remains a leading cause of late graft failure.

Prevention and treatment

Prevention relies on HLA matching, crossmatching, and maintenance immunosuppression with combinations of calcineurin inhibitors (such as tacrolimus and ciclosporin), antiproliferative agents, and corticosteroids. The introduction of ciclosporin in the 1980s dramatically improved graft survival. Rejection must be balanced against the risks of immunosuppression, including infection and malignancy. In hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, the related phenomenon of graft-versus-host disease occurs when donor immune cells attack the recipient.
