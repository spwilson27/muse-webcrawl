Gymnosperms are a group of seed-producing plants whose seeds are "naked" — not enclosed within an ovary or fruit, but typically borne on the scales of cones. Together with the flowering plants (angiosperms) they make up the seed plants. The living gymnosperms comprise about 1,000 species in four groups: conifers (by far the largest), cycads, the single species Ginkgo biloba, and the gnetophytes (Gnetum, Ephedra, Welwitschia).

Life cycle and reproduction

Gymnosperms are heterosporous: male cones produce pollen, which is carried by wind to female cones, where ovules develop into seeds after fertilisation. Unlike angiosperms they undergo no double fertilisation, and their nutritive seed tissue is haploid. Most are woody trees or shrubs with needle-like or scale-like leaves adapted to conserve water.

Evolution and importance

Seed plants arose in the Devonian, and gymnosperms dominated terrestrial flora through the Mesozoic before angiosperms eclipsed them in diversity during the Cretaceous. Conifers still dominate the vast boreal forests of the Northern Hemisphere and supply most of the world's softwood timber and paper pulp. Individual gymnosperms include the tallest (coast redwood) and oldest (bristlecone pine) living trees.
