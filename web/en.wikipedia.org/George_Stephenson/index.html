George Stephenson (9 June 1781 – 12 August 1848) was an English civil and mechanical engineer renowned as the "Father of Railways". Born in Wylam, Northumberland, he built the first public inter-city railway line in the world to use steam locomotives, the Liverpool and Manchester Railway, which opened in 1830.

Early life

Stephenson was the second child of Robert and Mabel Stephenson, neither of whom could read or write. He worked at collieries from a young age, became an engineman, and taught himself literacy at night school. In 1811 he fixed a pumping engine at Killingworth so successfully that he was made enginewright, specialising in steam-driven machinery for mines.

Locomotives and railways

His first locomotive, the Blücher, hauled coal at Killingworth in 1814. He was engineer of the Stockton and Darlington Railway (1825), the first public railway to carry passengers by steam. At the Rainhill Trials of 1829, the Rocket, built by George and his son Robert, won decisively. Stephenson also championed the 4 ft 8½ in gauge, which became the world standard.

Safety lamp

In 1815 Stephenson developed a miner's safety lamp (the "Geordie lamp"), tested in fiery mines at Killingworth — at the same time as Sir Humphry Davy's lamp — leading to a long-running priority dispute.

Later life

Stephenson advised on railway projects in Belgium, Spain and elsewhere, retired to Tapton House near Chesterfield, and died of pleurisy in 1848. He is buried in Holy Trinity Church, Chesterfield.
