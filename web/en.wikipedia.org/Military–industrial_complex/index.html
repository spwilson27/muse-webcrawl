Military–industrial complex
Coined by: President Dwight D. Eisenhower, farewell address, 17 January 1961
Meaning: The network of relationships between armed forces, defence industry and government

The military–industrial complex describes the relationship between a country's armed forces, its defence industry, and political institutions that together supply and fund military capability. Critics argue that this network creates a vested interest in continued or expanded military spending and can exert undue influence over public policy.

Origin of the term

The phrase was popularised by US President Dwight D. Eisenhower in his farewell address of 17 January 1961. Eisenhower warned that "we must guard against the acquisition of unwarranted influence, whether sought or unsought, by the military-industrial complex," noting that the conjunction of a permanent military establishment with a large arms industry was new in American experience. Drafts of the speech reportedly first read "military-industrial-congressional complex."

Analysis

Scholars and journalists have adapted the term to other countries and to related pairings such as the military-industrial-media and prison-industrial complexes. Mechanisms of influence commonly discussed include lobbying, campaign contributions, the "revolving door" between the Pentagon and contractors, and the distribution of defence jobs across congressional districts. Defenders respond that close cooperation between state and industry is necessary for national defence and technological innovation.
