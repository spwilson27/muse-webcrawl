The Tigris is the eastern of the two great rivers that define Mesopotamia, the other being the Euphrates. It flows some 1,850 kilometres from the mountains of eastern Turkey through Iraq, joining the Euphrates at al-Qurnah to form the Shatt al-Arab, which empties into the Persian Gulf.

Course

The Tigris rises near Lake Hazar in the Taurus Mountains of Turkey, flows past Diyarbakır, and briefly forms the border between Turkey and Syria before entering Iraq. It passes Mosul, Samarra and the capital Baghdad, receiving tributaries from the Zagros Mountains, including the Great Zab, Little Zab and Diyala rivers. South of Baghdad the river slows, splitting into distributaries across the Mesopotamian plain.

History

The Tigris valley was central to ancient civilisations: the Assyrian capitals Nineveh, Assur and Nimrud stood on its banks, as did the Parthian and Sasanian capital Ctesiphon and, from 762 CE, Baghdad. Seasonal flooding — less regular than the Nile's — drove the development of irrigation agriculture in Sumer and Akkad. In Mesopotamian religion the river's waters were linked to the god Ea, and myth held that the Tigris flowed from the eye of the slain goddess Tiamat.

Modern issues

Dams in Turkey (including the Ilısu Dam), Syria and Iraq have reduced downstream flows and flooded archaeological sites such as Hasankeyf. Marshes near the river's lower course, drained in the 1990s, have been partly reflooded since 2003.
