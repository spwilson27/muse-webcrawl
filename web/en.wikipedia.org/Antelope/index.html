Antelope

Antelope are even-toed ungulates of the family Bovidae, mostly native to Africa and Eurasia.

The term antelope refers to numerous species of even-toed ruminant ungulates in the family Bovidae. It is not a strict taxonomic grouping: antelope comprise the various bovid species that are neither cattle, sheep, nor goats—including gazelles, impala, springbok, oryx, wildebeest, kudu, eland, and the diminutive dik-diks and duikers. Most antelope species—about 71 of the roughly 91—live in Africa; the remainder occur in Asia.

Characteristics

Antelope are typically slender, long-legged grazers or browsers adapted for speed and vigilance on open ground. Most species have permanent, unbranched horns (unlike the shed antlers of deer), present in both sexes in many species. Sizes range from the royal antelope of West Africa, standing only about 25 cm at the shoulder, to the giant eland, which can weigh up to 900 kg. Many species are highly social, forming herds from a handful of individuals to the vast migratory aggregations of wildebeest and springbok.

Behaviour and ecology

Antelope are important prey for large carnivores such as lions, leopards, cheetahs, and wild dogs. Their defences include keen senses, camouflage, flight—some species can sprint at 80–90 km/h and leap several metres—and behaviours such as pronking (springbok) or the evasive zigzag of gazelles. Territorial males of many species hold mating territories, including the leks of Uganda kob and topi relatives.

Relationship with humans

Antelope appear in the art, heraldry, and mythology of many cultures and have long been hunted for meat and trophies. Several species are threatened; the scimitar oryx became extinct in the wild before reintroduction efforts, and the saiga suffered catastrophic die-offs. The North American pronghorn is often called an antelope but belongs to its own family, Antilocapridae.
