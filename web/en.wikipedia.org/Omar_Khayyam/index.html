Omar Khayyam

Omar Khayyam
Born18 May 1048, Nishapur, Persia (Seljuk Empire)
Died4 December 1131 (aged 83), Nishapur
FieldsMathematics, astronomy, philosophy, poetry
Known forRubaiyat; classification of cubic equations; Jalali calendar; contributions to the parallel postulate

Omar Khayyam (1048–1131) was a Persian polymath: mathematician, astronomer, philosopher, and poet. In the Islamic world of his lifetime he was renowned chiefly as a scientist; in the West he became famous above all for the Rubaiyat of Omar Khayyam, the collection of quatrains rendered into English by Edward FitzGerald in 1859.

Mathematics

In his Treatise on Demonstration of Problems of Algebra (1070), Khayyam gave a systematic classification of cubic equations and solved them geometrically by intersecting conic sections, noting that arithmetic solutions would require further development — a problem completed centuries later in Renaissance Italy. He also worked on Euclid's parallel postulate, developing ideas on non-Euclidean geometry, and gave an early statement related to the binomial theorem and Pascal's triangle.

Astronomy and the calendar

Under Sultan Malik-Shah I, Khayyam directed the observatory at Isfahan and led the team that produced the Jalali calendar (1079), a solar calendar of remarkable accuracy — by some calculations more accurate than the Gregorian calendar, with a year length measured at 365.24219858156 days. The modern Iranian (Solar Hijri) calendar descends from it.

Poetry

Khayyam is credited with about a thousand ruba'i (quatrains) meditating on mortality, fate, wine, and the brevity of life, though scholars debate how many are authentically his. FitzGerald's free translation made them a Victorian sensation, influencing literature across the English-speaking world — "A Book of Verses underneath the Bough, / A Jug of Wine, a Loaf of Bread — and Thou." His mausoleum stands in Nishapur, Iran.

Legacy

The lunar crater Omar Khayyam and the asteroid 3095 Omarkhayyam bear his name. He is also the legendary third member, with vizier Nizam al-Mulk and Hassan-i Sabbah, of a schoolboy pact of friendship — almost certainly a later legend.
