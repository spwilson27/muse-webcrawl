The Yellow River (Chinese: 黄河; pinyin: Huáng Hé) is the second-longest river in China, after the Yangtze, and the sixth-longest in the world, at approximately 5,464 kilometres. Originating in the Bayan Har Mountains of Qinghai province on the Tibetan Plateau, it flows east through nine provinces and empties into the Bohai Sea. Its basin was the birthplace of ancient Chinese civilisation and has often been called the "Mother River of China". The river takes its name from the vast quantities of yellow loess sediment it carries from the Loess Plateau, which give its waters a muddy yellow colour.

Course

The upper reaches descend from the Tibetan Plateau through deep gorges in Qinghai and Gansu, passing Lanzhou. The river then makes a great northern loop, the Ordos Loop, around the Ordos Desert through Ningxia and Inner Mongolia, before turning south to form the Shanxi–Shaanxi border, passing the Hukou Waterfall, the largest waterfall on the river. The lower reaches cross the North China Plain through Henan and Shandong, where the river, choked with silt and confined by levees, flows in places more than ten metres above the surrounding land — a "suspended river" — before reaching the Bohai Sea near Dongying.

Flooding and course changes

The Yellow River is notorious for devastating floods, earning it the historical epithet "China's Sorrow". Its lower course has shifted dramatically many times: major avulsions in 1194 and 1855 moved its mouth hundreds of kilometres, from south of the Shandong Peninsula to north of it, where it remains today. The 1931 and 1938 floods rank among the deadliest natural disasters in recorded history, each killing on the order of a million people; the 1938 flood was deliberately caused by the Nationalist government breaching the levees at Huayuankou to slow the Japanese advance. Flood control — dykes, reservoirs such as Xiaolangdi, and sediment management — remains a central task of Chinese water policy.

History and culture

The fertile, easily worked loess lands of the middle basin supported some of the earliest Chinese Neolithic cultures, including Yangshao and Longshan, and the heartlands of the Xia, Shang and Zhou dynasties. Control of the river was bound up with the legitimacy of rulers: the legendary Yu the Great was said to have earned the throne by taming the floods. The idiom "when the Yellow River runs clear" signifies a virtuous age.

Modern issues

Intensive irrigation and industrial demand from the basin's roughly 120 million inhabitants led the river to fail to reach the sea for extended periods in the 1990s, most severely in 1997. Since 1999 unified dispatch of water has prevented dry-ups, but water scarcity, pollution and the management of the river's extraordinary sediment load — over a billion tonnes a year historically — remain major challenges.
