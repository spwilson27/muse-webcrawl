Felon disenfranchisement in the United States

Felon disenfranchisement in the United States is the restriction of voting rights for people convicted of felony crimes. Because elections are administered by the states, the rules vary widely: some states restore voting rights automatically upon release from prison or completion of sentence, while others impose longer or indefinite restrictions.

History

Disenfranchisement for criminal offenses has roots in English common law "civil death" and colonial-era practices. Provisions expanded in many states after the Civil War and during the Jim Crow era, in some cases designed to reduce African American voting following ratification of the Fifteenth Amendment. Section 2 of the Fourteenth Amendment was interpreted by the Supreme Court in Richardson v. Ramirez (1974) to permit states to disenfranchise felons.

State variation

As of the 2020s, Maine, Vermont, and the District of Columbia allow citizens to vote even while incarcerated. Most states restore rights upon release or after parole/probation. A smaller number of states require individual petitions, payment of fines and fees, or executive clemency. Florida's Amendment 4 (2018), passed by referendum, restored rights to most people who completed their sentences, though subsequent legislation conditioned restoration on payment of legal financial obligations.

Scale and debate

Estimates by organizations such as The Sentencing Project place the number of disenfranchised Americans in the millions, with rates significantly higher among Black Americans. Supporters argue disenfranchisement is a legitimate consequence of serious crime; critics argue it undermines rehabilitation, democratic representation, and racial equity.
