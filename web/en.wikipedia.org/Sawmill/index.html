Sawmill

A sawmill (or lumber mill) is a facility where logs are cut into lumber. Modern sawmills use motorised saws — bandsaws, circular saws and increasingly computer-optimised cutting lines — to break logs down into boards, beams and other sawn timber products, which are then dried, planed and graded.

History

The earliest mechanised sawmills were water-powered. A sawmill appears in a sketch by Villard de Honnecourt around 1250, and water-driven frame saws spread through medieval Europe; the Dutch later developed wind-powered sawmills, which transformed shipbuilding timber production in the 17th century. Steam power in the 19th century brought large mills to the forest frontiers of North America and Australasia, and the circular saw and bandsaw greatly increased throughput. Portable sawmills now allow small-scale milling on site.

Process

Logs arriving at the mill are debarked, scanned and sorted. A head rig (or primary breakdown saw) makes the first cuts, converting the log into cants; resaws and edgers then produce boards of standard dimensions, and trimmers cut them to length. Computerised scanners evaluate each log's shape and defects to select a cutting pattern that maximises the value recovered. Offcuts are chipped for paper pulp, particleboard or fuel, and sawdust and bark are used for energy or animal bedding, so that little of the log is wasted. The sawn timber is sorted by grade, kiln-dried or air-dried, and dressed.

Safety and environment

Sawmills were historically among the most dangerous workplaces; modern mills have greatly reduced injuries through mechanisation, guarding and log-handling automation. Mill siting and expansion are frequently bound up with debates over logging, forest management and the carbon stored in wood products.
