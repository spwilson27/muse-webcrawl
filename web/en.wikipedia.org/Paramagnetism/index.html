Paramagnetism

Liquid oxygen, a paramagnetic liquid, suspended between the poles of a strong magnet.

Paramagnetism is a form of magnetism whereby some materials are weakly attracted by an externally applied magnetic field, and form internal, induced magnetic fields in the direction of the applied field. Paramagnetic materials include most chemical elements and some compounds; they have a relative magnetic permeability slightly greater than 1 and a small positive magnetic susceptibility (typically 10−5 to 10−3). In contrast, diamagnetic materials are repelled by magnetic fields, and ferromagnetic materials exhibit strong, permanent attraction.

Cause

Paramagnetism arises from atoms, ions or molecules with unpaired electrons, whose spins create permanent magnetic moments. In the absence of a field, thermal agitation randomises these moments so the material shows no net magnetisation; an applied field partially aligns them against thermal disorder. Some metals show a weak, temperature-independent paramagnetism (Pauli paramagnetism) from their conduction electrons.

Curie's law

For low field strengths and temperatures well above any ordering temperature, the magnetisation of a paramagnet follows Curie's law, formulated by Pierre Curie in 1895: the susceptibility is inversely proportional to temperature (χ = C/T). Materials with interactions between moments obey the modified Curie–Weiss law. At sufficiently low temperatures many paramagnetic salts approach magnetic saturation, a behaviour described by the Brillouin function.

Examples and applications

Paramagnetic substances include oxygen (O2 is a rare paramagnetic gas), aluminium, platinum, and salts of iron and the rare earths. Paramagnetic properties are exploited in electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy, in magnetic refrigeration by adiabatic demagnetisation, and in paramagnetic oxygen analysers. Curie himself used paramagnetic balances to study magnetism systematically.
