West African cuisine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

West African cuisine encompasses the diverse cooking traditions of West Africa, a region of more than a dozen countries including Nigeria, Ghana, Senegal, Mali and Côte d'Ivoire. Its characteristic pattern is a starchy staple — often pounded into a dough-like accompaniment — served with richly flavoured soups and stews built on tomatoes, onions, chillies, palm oil or groundnuts, with leafy greens, okra and dried or smoked fish.

Staples and dishes

Fufu — a smooth, elastic dough of pounded cassava, yam or plantain — is eaten with soups such as egusi (melon seed), okra and bitter-leaf. Jollof rice, rice cooked in a blended tomato and pepper base, is the region's most famous party dish and the subject of a friendly rivalry, the "jollof wars", between Nigerian and Ghanaian versions; its Senegalese ancestor is thieboudienne (ceebu jën), Senegal's national dish of fish, rice and vegetables. Groundnut (peanut) stew, known as maafe in Mali and Senegal, is widespread, as are grilled meats such as Nigerian suya, skewers coated in spiced peanut powder.

Ingredients and influences

Indigenous crops — yams, millet, sorghum, fonio, African rice — were joined after the Columbian exchange by cassava, maize, chillies, tomatoes and groundnuts from the Americas, which became foundational. Trade across the Sahara and along the coast brought spices and techniques; Islamic and later colonial influences also shaped urban foodways. Heat typically comes from Scotch bonnet peppers, and fermented seasonings such as soumbala (from African locust bean) and ogiri provide deep umami flavours.

Global spread

Through the African diaspora, West African foodways profoundly influenced the cuisines of the Americas — gumbo, jambalaya and black-eyed-pea dishes among them — and West African restaurants and dishes have gained growing international attention in the twenty-first century.
