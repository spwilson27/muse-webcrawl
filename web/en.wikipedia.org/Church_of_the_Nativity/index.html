The Church of the Nativity, or Basilica of the Nativity, is a basilica located in Bethlehem, in the West Bank. The grotto beneath it holds a prominent religious significance as the traditional birthplace of Jesus. The church is one of the oldest continuously operating churches in the world.

History

The first basilica on the site was commissioned by Constantine the Great and his mother Helena around 327–335 and completed about 339. It was destroyed by fire during the Samaritan revolts of the sixth century and rebuilt by Emperor Justinian I, largely in the form seen today.

Unlike many churches in the region, the basilica survived the Persian invasion of 614 and the early Islamic period, and it was a major pilgrimage destination throughout the Middle Ages. During the Crusades it was embellished with mosaics and paintings, some of which survive.

The Grotto of the Nativity

Beneath the chancel lies the Grotto of the Nativity, a rectangular cave where a silver star set into the marble floor marks the traditional spot of Jesus's birth, inscribed in Latin: "Here Jesus Christ was born of the Virgin Mary."

Status Quo

Rights and possession of the church are shared among the Greek Orthodox Patriarchate, the Armenian Apostolic Church, and the Roman Catholic Custody of the Holy Land under the Status Quo arrangements confirmed in the nineteenth century. The church was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2012 and underwent a major restoration programme in the 2010s.
