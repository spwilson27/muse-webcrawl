The First Libyan Civil War, also known as the Libyan Revolution or 17 February Revolution, was an armed conflict in 2011 in the North African country of Libya that was fought between forces loyal to Colonel Muammar Gaddafi and rebel groups seeking to oust his government. The war was preceded by protests in Benghazi beginning on 15 February 2011, part of the wider Arab Spring, which escalated into a full-scale rebellion as the regime responded with military force.

International intervention

As loyalist forces advanced on Benghazi, the UN Security Council passed Resolution 1973 (17 March 2011), authorising a no-fly zone and "all necessary measures" to protect civilians. A coalition led first by France, the United Kingdom and the United States — and from 31 March by NATO as Operation Unified Protector — conducted airstrikes that destroyed much of the regime's armour and air defences, while Qatar and others supplied the rebels.

Fall of the regime

After months of seesaw fighting along the coastal road and in the Nafusa Mountains, rebels entered Tripoli in August 2011 (Operation Mermaid Dawn), effectively ending 42 years of Gaddafi rule. Gaddafi fled to his hometown of Sirte, where he was captured and killed on 20 October 2011. The National Transitional Council declared liberation on 23 October.

Aftermath

The war ended Gaddafi's rule but left Libya fragmented among militias; failure to stabilise the country led to renewed fighting in the Second Libyan Civil War (2014–2020), and the conflict's consequences included regional weapons proliferation and a migration crisis across the Mediterranean.
