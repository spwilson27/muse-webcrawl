Two-body problem

In classical mechanics, the two-body problem is to predict the motion of two massive objects which are abstractly viewed as point particles. The problem assumes that the two objects interact only with one another; the only force affecting each object arises from the other one, and all other objects are ignored. The most prominent example is the gravitational problem, in which the two bodies attract each other according to Newton's law of universal gravitation.

Reduction to one-body problem

The two-body problem can be reduced to two separate one-body problems: the motion of the center of mass, which moves uniformly in a straight line, and the relative motion of the two bodies about their center of mass. The relative motion is equivalent to the motion of a single particle with the reduced mass μ = m1m2/(m1 + m2) moving in a central force field. Because the force is central, angular momentum is conserved and the motion lies in a plane.

Keplerian orbits

For an attractive inverse-square force such as gravity, the solutions are conic sections: the bodies follow elliptical orbits (with the center of mass at a focus of each ellipse) when the total energy is negative, parabolic or hyperbolic trajectories when it is zero or positive. These results underlie Kepler's laws of planetary motion and form the foundation of celestial mechanics and orbital mechanics. By contrast, the three-body problem generally has no closed-form solution and exhibits chaotic behavior.
