Spontaneous combustion

Spontaneous combustion
TypeCombustion phenomenon
CauseSelf-heating from slow oxidation, heat accumulation exceeding dissipation
Common materialsHay, coal, oily rags, compost, linseed oil-soaked cloth

Spontaneous combustion or spontaneous ignition is a type of combustion which occurs by self-heating (increase in temperature due to exothermic internal reactions), followed by thermal runaway (self-heating which rapidly accelerates to high temperatures) and finally, autoignition.

Causes

Spontaneous combustion can occur when a substance with a relatively low ignition temperature (such as hay, straw, peat, or coal) releases heat through slow oxidation. If the material is stored in bulk so that heat cannot dissipate, the temperature rises until it exceeds the ignition point. Microbial activity can drive the early stages of heating in damp biological material such as haystacks and compost piles; above roughly 70–80 °C, chemical oxidation takes over. Oily rags — particularly those soaked in drying oils such as linseed oil, which polymerize exothermically — are a well-documented fire hazard.

Prevention

Prevention focuses on ventilation, temperature monitoring of bulk stores, proper drying of hay before baling, and safe disposal of oil-soaked materials in sealed metal containers.

Spontaneous human combustion

Spontaneous human combustion (SHC) is the pseudoscientific concept of the combustion of a living (or recently deceased) human body without an apparent external source of ignition. A small number of historical cases have been attributed to it, but forensic investigations generally find explanations involving an external ignition source combined with the "wick effect", in which body fat renders into clothing and sustains a slow, localized burn. No scientifically verified mechanism or case of SHC exists.
