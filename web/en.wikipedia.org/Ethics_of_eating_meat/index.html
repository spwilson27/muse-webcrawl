Ethics of eating meat

The ethics of eating meat is debated in part over conditions in intensive animal farming

The ethics of eating meat concerns whether it is morally permissible to eat the flesh of animals. The question involves the moral status of animals, the suffering and death caused by meat production, and the environmental consequences of livestock farming. Supporters of meat eating and ethical vegetarians and vegans advance competing arguments in moral philosophy, and positions vary across religious and cultural traditions.

Arguments against eating meat

Utilitarian philosophers such as Peter Singer argue that the capacity of animals to suffer gives their interests equal moral consideration, and that the pleasure humans gain from eating meat is outweighed by the suffering inflicted, particularly in factory farming. Others argue from animal rights that sentient animals have inherent value and should not be treated as property or resources, so killing them for food is wrong even if painless. Environmental arguments cite livestock's contribution to greenhouse gas emissions, deforestation, and water use. Some ethicists add that meat consumption by affluent societies is unnecessary for health, weakening any justification.

Arguments in favour

Defenders of meat eating argue that moral status depends on capacities such as rationality or moral agency that non-human animals lack; that humans are omnivores for whom meat eating is natural; that well-managed livestock farming need not involve cruelty, giving rise to "ethical meat" positions permitting meat from humanely raised animals; and that some land is suitable only for grazing. The "logic of the larder" argument holds that farm animals owe their existence to the demand for meat. Critics of vegetarianism also point to the animals killed in crop production and question whether plant agriculture is harm-free.

Intermediate positions

Many people adopt intermediate stances: vegetarianism (avoiding meat but eating eggs and dairy), pescatarianism, flexitarianism, or eating only certain animals — distinctions some critics, such as Singer, challenge by asking what morally relevant difference separates, for example, a fish from a pig. Religious traditions including Jainism, Hinduism, Buddhism, and Seventh-day Adventism have long-standing vegetarian teachings or practices.
