Freedom Charter

The Freedom Charter is a statement of core principles adopted by the South African Congress Alliance on June 26, 1955, at the Congress of the People in Kliptown, Soweto. Drafted from thousands of demands collected from ordinary people across South Africa, it remains a foundational document of the African National Congress (ANC) and the anti-apartheid struggle, opening with the declaration that "South Africa belongs to all who live in it, black and white."

Contents

The Charter demanded universal suffrage and a democratic state based on the will of all the people; equal rights regardless of race, colour, or sex; the sharing of the country's wealth, including nationalization of mines, banks, and monopoly industry; redistribution of the land; equal work, pay, and education; free and compulsory education; decent housing, healthcare, and social security; and peace and friendship in international affairs. Its clauses on nationalization made it controversial both with the apartheid government — which charged its signatories with treason in the 1956 Treason Trial — and, later, with free-market critics within and outside the ANC.

Legacy

The Charter broke with purely racial definitions of the nation by proclaiming a non-racial South Africa, a position that contributed to the breakaway of Africanist critics who formed the Pan Africanist Congress in 1959. After the ANC came to power in 1994, many of the Charter's principles were incorporated into the 1996 Constitution of South Africa and its Bill of Rights, although the ANC in government did not implement wholesale nationalization. The document continues to be invoked in South African debates over land reform and economic transformation.
