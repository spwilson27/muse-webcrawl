Tricuspid valve

LocationBetween right atrium and right ventricle
LeafletsThree (anterior, posterior, septal)
FunctionPrevents backflow of blood into the right atrium

The tricuspid valve, or right atrioventricular valve, is a flap-like structure in the heart that lies between the right atrium and the right ventricle. Its function is to allow deoxygenated blood to flow from the right atrium into the right ventricle during diastole and to prevent its backflow (regurgitation) into the atrium when the ventricle contracts during systole.

Structure

As its name suggests, the valve normally has three leaflets or cusps — anterior, posterior, and septal — attached to a fibrous annulus. The leaflets are anchored by chordae tendineae to papillary muscles in the wall of the right ventricle, which tense during contraction to keep the valve from prolapsing into the atrium. The tricuspid orifice is the largest of the heart's valve openings, reflecting the lower pressures on the right side of the heart.

Clinical significance

Tricuspid regurgitation, leakage of the valve, is most often functional — caused by dilation of the right ventricle and annulus secondary to pulmonary hypertension or left-sided heart disease — and can lead to right heart failure, with jugular venous distension, liver congestion, and peripheral oedema. Tricuspid stenosis, narrowing of the valve, is rarer and usually rheumatic. Infective endocarditis of the tricuspid valve is particularly associated with intravenous drug use. The congenital malformation Ebstein's anomaly involves downward displacement of the septal and posterior leaflets. Severe disease may be treated by annuloplasty repair or valve replacement, and transcatheter therapies have emerged for high-risk patients.
