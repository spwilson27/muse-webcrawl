Geodesy

Geodesy is the science of accurately measuring and understanding the Earth's geometric shape, its orientation in space, and its gravitational field. Historically, geodesists used ground-based measurement tools, but modern geodesy relies on space-based techniques such as GPS/GNSS, satellite laser ranging, very-long-baseline interferometry (VLBI), and dedicated gravity missions. The discipline underpins surveying, mapping, navigation, and the monitoring of tectonic motion and sea-level change.

Definition and tasks

The fundamental tasks of geodesy are the determination of the positions of points on the Earth's surface, the figure of the Earth, and its external gravity field, all as functions of time. The International Association of Geodesy organizes the field within the International Union of Geodesy and Geophysics.

The figure of the Earth

The Earth approximates an oblate spheroid flattened at the poles. Its true equipotential surface, the geoid, deviates from a smooth reference ellipsoid by up to about 100 meters because of uneven mass distribution. Modern reference ellipsoids such as the one used by WGS 84 have an equatorial radius of 6,378,137 m and a flattening of about 1/298.257.

Datums and reference frames

Coordinates are meaningless without a datum: a defined origin, orientation, and ellipsoid. Regional datums (such as NAD83 in North America) fit the geoid locally, while geocentric frames like the International Terrestrial Reference Frame (ITRF) support global positioning. Heights present a special problem: GPS yields heights above the ellipsoid, whereas maps generally show orthometric heights above the geoid (approximately mean sea level).

History

Eratosthenes estimated the Earth's circumference around 240 BC by comparing shadows at Syene and Alexandria. Islamic astronomers refined the measurement; in the 17th–18th centuries, arc measurements in Peru and Lapland confirmed Newton's prediction of polar flattening. Nineteenth-century triangulations — the Great Trigonometrical Survey of India among them — produced the ellipsoids of Bessel and Clarke and revealed the geoid's irregularity.

Modern techniques

Today GNSS receivers fix positions to millimeters, VLBI observations of distant quasars define the terrestrial reference frame and measure Earth rotation, and satellites such as GRACE and GOCE map temporal changes in the gravity field — tracking ice-sheet loss and groundwater depletion. Geodesy provides the precise orbit and timing frameworks on which satellite navigation depends.
