Melanin (from Ancient Greek μέλας (mélas) 'black, dark') is a family of biomolecules organized as oligomers or polymers, which among other functions provide the pigments of many organisms. Melanin pigments are produced in a specialized group of cells known as melanocytes. The most common type of melanin is eumelanin, which is produced by the oxidation of the amino acid tyrosine, followed by polymerization. Eumelanin is present in the skin, hair, and eyes, and is responsible for brown and black coloration. A second, sulfur-containing form, pheomelanin, gives red and yellow hues and predominates in red hair and freckles. A third form, neuromelanin, is found in the substantia nigra and locus coeruleus of the brain.

Biosynthesis

The first step of the biosynthetic pathway for both eumelanins and pheomelanins is catalysed by the copper-containing enzyme tyrosinase, which hydroxylates tyrosine to L-DOPA and oxidises it to dopaquinone. In the absence of cysteine, dopaquinone cyclises to form dihydroxyindole compounds that polymerise into eumelanin. When cysteine is present, dopaquinone instead forms cysteinyl-DOPA intermediates that lead to benzothiazine units and ultimately pheomelanin. Melanin is packaged into organelles called melanosomes, which in skin are transferred from melanocytes to surrounding keratinocytes.

Functions

Melanin absorbs ultraviolet radiation across a broad spectrum and dissipates it as heat, protecting DNA in skin cells from UV-induced damage. The amount and type of melanin in the skin is the principal determinant of human skin colour, and darker skin confers significant protection against sunburn and skin cancer. Melanin also acts as an antioxidant and free-radical scavenger, binds certain drugs and metal ions, and — in the inner ear and in neuromelanin-containing brain nuclei — may play roles in hearing and neuronal vulnerability. In many animals melanin underlies camouflage, warning coloration, and sexual display.

Disorders

Absence or reduction of melanin production causes forms of albinism (oculocutaneous and ocular), most often through mutations in the tyrosinase gene or other melanogenesis genes. Excess or disordered pigmentation produces conditions such as melasma and post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation, while loss of melanocytes causes vitiligo. Melanoma, a malignant cancer of melanocytes, is unrelated to melanin quantity per se but arises in pigment-producing cells; fair-skinned individuals with low eumelanin are at higher risk of UV-related skin cancers. Degeneration of neuromelanin-containing neurons in the substantia nigra is a hallmark of Parkinson's disease.

Melanin in other organisms

Melanins occur across all kingdoms of life. In fungi, melanin strengthens cell walls and protects against radiation and desiccation — melanised fungi famously thrive in high-radiation environments. In cephalopods, melanin is the principal pigment of defensive ink. Melanin-like pigments in feathers produce much of the black and brown coloration of birds, often combined with structural colour.
