Rudolf Diesel

Rudolf Christian Karl Diesel (18 March 1858 – 29 September 1913) was a German inventor and mechanical engineer, best known for inventing the diesel engine, which burns diesel fuel. His compression-ignition engine, dramatically more efficient than the steam engines and early internal combustion engines of his day, transformed marine propulsion, rail transport, heavy road vehicles and stationary power generation.

Career

Born in Paris to Bavarian immigrant parents and raised partly in London after the Franco-Prussian War, Diesel studied at the Royal Bavarian Polytechnic in Munich, where he attended Carl von Linde's lectures on thermodynamics and conceived the ambition of an engine approaching the efficiency of the ideal Carnot cycle. While working for Linde's refrigeration company, he developed his "rational heat motor": air compressed to a very high pressure becomes hot enough to ignite fuel injected near the end of the compression stroke. He patented the design in 1892, and on 10 August 1893 his first prototype ran under its own power in Augsburg with the support of Maschinenfabrik Augsburg (later MAN). A production-ready engine followed in 1897, achieving thermal efficiency around 26% — more than double that of steam engines.

Death

Diesel disappeared from the deck of the mail steamer SS Dresden in the English Channel on the night of 29 September 1913, en route to a meeting in London; his body was found ten days later. Whether his death was suicide (he was deeply in debt) or murder (conspiracy theories involve oil and military interests) remains unresolved. His engine, meanwhile, powered the submarines, ships, locomotives and, from the 1930s onwards, trucks and cars that bear his name in the fuel itself: diesel.
