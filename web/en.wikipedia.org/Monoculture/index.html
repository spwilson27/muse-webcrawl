Monoculture

In agriculture, monoculture is the practice of growing one crop species in a field at a time. Monoculture is widely used in intensive farming and in organic farming: in both bulk and organic contexts, it allows farmers to specialise in a crop, matching machinery and methods to its requirements, and can support substantial economies of scale.

The term is also used in animal husbandry, forestry (where it refers to stands of a single tree species), and in computing and sociology by analogy.

Advantages

Growing a single crop allows a farm to optimise every operation — planting, pest control, harvesting — around one plant's biology, and simplifies the use of specialised machinery. Uniform stands ripen at the same time, easing harvest scheduling.

A monoculture wheat field: a single genotype covers the whole area.

Risks

Because the plants in a monoculture are genetically similar, they share susceptibility to pests and diseases, which can spread rapidly through an entire crop. Monocultures can also deplete specific soil nutrients and increase dependence on fertilisers and pesticides. Loss of genetic diversity is a central concern: the Irish Potato Famine of the 1840s was worsened by reliance on a single susceptible potato variety, and the collapse of the Gros Michel banana to Panama disease led the export industry to switch to the Cavendish cultivar.

Historical examples

The spread of Panama disease (Fusarium wilt) through Gros Michel banana plantations in the mid-20th century is a classic case of monoculture vulnerability. Coffee leaf rust similarly devastated the coffee industry of Ceylon (Sri Lanka) in the 19th century, prompting a shift to tea cultivation.

Alternatives

Polyculture, intercropping, and crop rotation diversify plantings across species or time, interrupting pest cycles and balancing nutrient use. Diversity farming systems attempt to retain some of monoculture's efficiency while reducing its risks.
