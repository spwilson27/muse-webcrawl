Planck's law

Planck's law, formulated by German physicist Max Planck in 1900, describes the spectral density of electromagnetic radiation emitted by a black body in thermal equilibrium at a given temperature. It was a founding result of quantum mechanics.

The law

The law gives the spectral radiance of radiation at frequency ν as a function of temperature: it rises to a peak and falls at high frequency, avoiding the "ultraviolet catastrophe" predicted by the classical Rayleigh–Jeans law. Planck derived it by assuming that electromagnetic energy can be emitted only in quantised form, in units of hν, where h is the Planck constant.

Related laws

Planck's law contains the Stefan–Boltzmann law as its integral over all frequencies, and Wien's displacement law — which locates the peak wavelength — as a derived result. At low frequencies it reduces to the Rayleigh–Jeans law, and at high frequencies to Wien's approximation.

Significance

Planck's reluctant introduction of energy quanta in 1900 is generally taken as the birth of quantum theory, later developed by Einstein, Bohr and others.
