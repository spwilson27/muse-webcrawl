Trotskyism is the body of Marxist theory and practice advocated by the Russian revolutionary Leon Trotsky and his supporters, developed in opposition to Stalinism. Trotsky, a leader of the October Revolution and founder of the Red Army, was expelled from the Communist Party of the Soviet Union in 1927, exiled in 1929, and assassinated in Mexico City in 1940 by an agent of Stalin's NKVD. Trotskyists consider themselves the heirs of Lenin and the Bolshevik tradition, identifying with the Left Opposition.

Core ideas

The theory of permanent revolution, first developed by Trotsky in 1905–1906, holds that in economically backward countries the tasks of the bourgeois-democratic revolution can only be completed by the working class taking power, and that such a revolution must spread internationally to survive — a direct rejection of Stalin's doctrine of "socialism in one country". Trotsky analyzed the USSR as a "degenerated workers' state" whose planned economy should be defended against imperialism while its bureaucratic ruling caste was overthrown by political revolution.

The Fourth International

After concluding that the Comintern was irreformable following Hitler's unopposed rise in 1933, Trotsky's supporters founded the Fourth International in 1938, adopting the Transitional Program. After Trotsky's death the movement fragmented into numerous competing organizations and tendencies. Trotskyist parties and currents have nonetheless influenced labor and anti-colonial movements in many countries, notably in France, Britain, Sri Lanka, Vietnam, and Bolivia.
