Syngas

Syngas, or synthesis gas, is a mixture of hydrogen (H2) and carbon monoxide (CO), often with some carbon dioxide (CO2). The name comes from its use as an intermediate in creating synthetic natural gas and in producing ammonia and methanol. Syngas is combustible and is often used as a fuel in internal combustion engines or as an intermediate in industrial chemical processes.

Production

Syngas is produced by several methods. Steam reforming of natural gas reacts methane with steam over a nickel catalyst at high temperature: CH4 + H2O → CO + 3 H2. Gasification converts carbonaceous materials such as coal, petroleum coke, or biomass into syngas by reacting them with a controlled amount of oxygen and/or steam at high temperatures. Partial oxidation burns hydrocarbons with a sub-stoichiometric amount of oxygen. The water–gas shift reaction (CO + H2O ⇌ CO2 + H2) is used to adjust the H2:CO ratio for downstream processes.

Applications

The hydrogen-to-carbon-monoxide ratio determines syngas's uses. The Fischer–Tropsch process converts syngas with a ratio near 2 into liquid hydrocarbons such as synthetic diesel and waxes. Methanol synthesis requires a ratio of about 2 as well. Ammonia production uses syngas as a source of hydrogen, after shifting and CO2 removal. Syngas also feeds hydroformylation ("oxo") processes and can be burned in integrated gasification combined cycle (IGCC) power plants.

History

Related gas mixtures have a long history: "town gas" produced from coal lit cities in the 19th century, and water gas was widely used for heating and lighting. German chemists Franz Fischer and Hans Tropsch developed their synthesis in the 1920s, and syngas chemistry became strategically important during the Second World War. Modern interest centres on biomass gasification and carbon capture as routes to lower-carbon fuels and chemicals.
