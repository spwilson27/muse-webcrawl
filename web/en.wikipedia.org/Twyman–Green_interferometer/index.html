Twyman–Green interferometer

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Twyman–Green interferometer is a variant of the Michelson interferometer used principally for precision testing of optical components. It was invented and patented in 1916 by Frank Twyman and Arthur Green of the British firm Adam Hilger Ltd.

Design

In the Michelson interferometer one arm contains the optical element under test — for example a lens, prism or mirror — while the other arm provides a reference beam reflected from a reference flat. The key difference from Michelson's original instrument is the use of a collimated, extended monochromatic (today usually laser) light source: a point source is expanded by a collimating lens into a parallel beam covering the full aperture of the test piece, so that a complete two-dimensional map of wavefront error is obtained at once rather than point by point.

Use

Deviations of the test optic from perfection distort the recombined interference fringes; each fringe displacement of one fringe spacing corresponds to a wavefront error of half a wavelength. Manufacturers of telescope mirrors, camera lenses and lithography optics use the instrument for quantitative quality control. Modern versions use phase-shifting and computer analysis to convert interferograms into surface-height maps accurate to a small fraction of a wavelength of light.
