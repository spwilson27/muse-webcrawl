Fugacity

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In chemical thermodynamics, the fugacity of a real gas is an effective partial pressure which replaces the true partial pressure in an accurate computation of chemical equilibrium. It is equal to the pressure of an ideal gas which has the same temperature and molar Gibbs free energy as the real gas. The concept was introduced by the American chemist Gilbert N. Lewis in 1901, from the Latin fugere, "to flee", reflecting a substance's tendency to escape or expand.

Definition

Fugacity f is defined so that the chemical potential μ of a real gas takes the same form as for an ideal gas:

μ = μ° + RT ln(f / p°)

The ratio of fugacity to pressure, φ = f/p, is the fugacity coefficient, which equals 1 for an ideal gas and deviates from unity as intermolecular forces become significant. For a pure real gas, fugacity can be computed from an equation of state by integrating the departure of the molar volume from ideal behaviour over pressure.

Applications

Fugacity is widely used in chemical engineering for modelling phase equilibrium in high-pressure systems, such as gas processing and supercritical extraction, where ideal-gas assumptions fail badly. In geology, the fugacity of oxygen in rocks and magmas — measured against standard buffers — records the oxidation conditions under which minerals formed. For condensed phases, fugacity is closely related to activity, the analogous "effective concentration" used for liquids and solids.
