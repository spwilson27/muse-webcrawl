United States debt ceiling

The United States debt ceiling or debt limit is a legislative limit on the amount of national debt that can be incurred by the U.S. Treasury, thus limiting how much money the federal government may pay on the debt it already borrowed. The debt ceiling is an aggregate figure that applies to the gross debt, which includes debt in the hands of the public and in intra-government accounts.

The U.S. federal debt has repeatedly been capped by the statutory debt limit.

History

Before 1917, Congress authorized each borrowing individually. The Second Liberty Bond Act of 1917, passed to finance American involvement in World War I, set an aggregate ceiling on bonds, and in 1939 Congress created the first overall limit covering nearly all federal debt. Since World War II the ceiling has been raised scores of times under presidents of both parties. The United States is one of the few countries with a statutory debt ceiling expressed in nominal currency terms; Denmark maintains a similar but largely symbolic limit.

Effects and controversies

When the Treasury reaches the limit, it employs "extraordinary measures" — accounting maneuvers such as suspending reinvestment of federal retirement funds — to continue paying obligations until Congress acts. Political standoffs, notably in 2011 and 2023, brought the government close to default and prompted credit-rating actions: S&P downgraded U.S. debt in August 2011, and Fitch did so in 2023. Economists warn that an actual default would disrupt global markets, though some legal scholars argue the Fourteenth Amendment's public debt clause could authorize continued payment.
