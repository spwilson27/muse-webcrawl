Anglesey (Welsh: Ynys Môn) is an island off the north-west coast of Wales. With an area of 714 square kilometres it is the largest island in Wales and the seventh largest in the British Isles. It forms the principal area of the Isle of Anglesey County, which also includes Holy Island and smaller islets. About 70,000 people live on the island, and it is one of the most strongly Welsh-speaking parts of Wales.

Geography and transport

Anglesey is separated from the mainland by the Menai Strait, a narrow tidal channel crossed by two famous 19th-century bridges: Thomas Telford's Menai Suspension Bridge (1826) and Robert Stephenson's Britannia Bridge (1850), which carries the North Wales Coast railway and road traffic. The island is mostly low and gently rolling farmland. Its largest town is Holyhead on Holy Island, the ferry port for Ireland; the administrative centre is Llangefni. The village of Llanfairpwllgwyngyll (in full, Llanfairpwllgwyngyllgogerychwyrndrobwllllantysiliogogogoch) is famed for having one of the longest place names in the world.

History

Anglesey — Môn, "mother of Wales" for its grain harvests — was a stronghold of the druids, attacked by the Roman general Suetonius Paulinus in AD 60, and later a seat of the princes of Gwynedd at Aberffraw. It was annexed with the rest of Wales by Edward I, whose castle at Beaumaris is the last and largest of his Welsh fortresses. In the 18th century the great copper mine at Parys Mountain made Anglesey a world centre of copper production.

Today

The economy rests on tourism, agriculture and the port of Holyhead; the Wylfa nuclear power station, now decommissioning, was a major employer. The 200-km Anglesey Coastal Path circles the island's cliffs, beaches and heritage sites.
