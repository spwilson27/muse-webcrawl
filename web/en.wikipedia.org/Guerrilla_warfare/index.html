Guerrilla warfare

Guerrilla fighters typically rely on terrain, mobility and civilian support rather than set-piece battle.

Guerrilla warfare is a form of irregular warfare in which small groups of combatants, such as paramilitary personnel, armed civilians or irregulars, use military tactics including ambushes, sabotage, raids, petty warfare, hit-and-run tactics and mobility to fight a larger and less-mobile traditional military. The word guerrilla comes from the Spanish diminutive of guerra ("war"), meaning "little war"; the term became widespread during the Peninsular War (1808–1814), when Spanish and Portuguese irregulars fought Napoleon's occupying armies.

History

Guerrilla tactics are ancient: the Scythians used them against Darius the Great, and Fabius Maximus employed delaying and harassing strategies against Hannibal. In the modern era, guerrilla warfare was central to the American Revolutionary War, the Spanish resistance to Napoleon, the Boer Wars, the Chinese Civil War, the Vietnam War, the Cuban Revolution and numerous anti-colonial struggles in Africa and Asia. Mao Zedong systematised guerrilla doctrine in works such as On Guerrilla Warfare (1937), comparing guerrillas to fish swimming in the sea of the people.

Tactics and strategy

Successful guerrilla campaigns usually depend on popular support or at least passive acquiescence, knowledge of local terrain, and the ability to disperse and reassemble. Guerrillas avoid decisive battle, attack supply lines and isolated outposts, and seek to erode the enemy's political will over a protracted conflict. Counterinsurgency forces respond with population control, intelligence gathering and "hearts and minds" programmes. Related concepts include asymmetric warfare, insurgency and partisan warfare.
