Sago

Sago starch is extracted from the pith of the sago palm and often sold as pearls.

Sago is a starch extracted from the spongy centre, or pith, of various tropical palm stems, especially those of Metroxylon sagu, the sago palm. It is a major staple food for the lowland peoples of New Guinea and the Maluku Islands, where it is eaten as a starchy paste (papeda) or baked into pancakes. Commercial sago is most often sold dried in the form of small white "pearls," similar in appearance and use to tapioca pearls.

Production

A mature sago palm is felled just before flowering, when its trunk is richest in starch; the pith is rasped, kneaded, and washed to extract the starch, which settles and is dried. A single palm can yield 150–300 kilograms of dry sago. The crop thrives in swampy lowlands unsuitable for rice, making it a resilient food source. Malaysia (particularly Sarawak) and Indonesia are the principal exporters.

Culinary use

Sago pearls are boiled until translucent and used in puddings and desserts across South and Southeast Asia — such as sago pudding, gulab jamun variants, and the Filipino drink sago't gulaman — and in drinks akin to bubble tea. In India, sago (sabudana) is eaten during religious fasts. Nutritionally sago is almost pure carbohydrate with negligible protein, so traditional diets pair it with fish and vegetables.

Health note

Raw sago palm pith contains toxins that traditional processing removes. In Guam and the Marianas, consumption of flour from the related cycad Cycas micronesica (sometimes called sago) has been investigated for a possible link with the ALS/PDC neurodegenerative complex, though the hypothesis remains debated.
