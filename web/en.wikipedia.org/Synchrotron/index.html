Synchrotron

A synchrotron is a particular type of cyclic particle accelerator, descended from the cyclotron, in which the accelerating particle beam travels around a fixed closed-loop path. The magnetic field that bends the beam is increased in step (“in synchrony”) with the particles' rising kinetic energy, allowing the orbit radius to stay constant and the vacuum chamber to be a comparatively narrow torus rather than a huge disc.

Principle and history

Particles are accelerated by radio-frequency cavities each time they pass, while dipole magnets bend their trajectory and quadrupole magnets focus the beam. The principle of phase stability was published independently in 1944–45 by Vladimir Veksler in the Soviet Union and Edwin McMillan in the United States; McMillan coined the name “synchrotron” and built the first electron synchrotron at Berkeley in 1949. Proton synchrotrons such as the Cosmotron, the CERN Proton Synchrotron (1959), and the Tevatron pushed energies ever higher; the Large Hadron Collider is a superconducting proton synchrotron reaching 6.8 TeV per beam.

Synchrotron radiation

A charged particle forced onto a curved path emits electromagnetic radiation tangentially — synchrotron radiation. Once considered an energy-sapping nuisance in high-energy physics, this intense, collimated light, especially in the X-ray range, became so valuable that dedicated facilities called synchrotron light sources now serve thousands of researchers in physics, chemistry, biology, and medicine.
