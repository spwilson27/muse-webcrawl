Common brushtail possum

Scientific nameTrichosurus vulpecula
FamilyPhalangeridae
OrderDiprotodontia
Native toAustralia; introduced to New Zealand

The common brushtail possum (Trichosurus vulpecula, from the Greek for "fiery tailed" and the Latin for "little fox") is a nocturnal, semi-arboreal marsupial of the family Phalangeridae. It is the largest of the possums and one of the most familiar Australian mammals, native to much of mainland Australia and Tasmania and introduced to New Zealand, where it is regarded as a serious pest.

Description

The common brushtail possum is about the size of a domestic cat, with a head-and-body length of roughly 32–58 cm and a bushy prehensile tail of 24–40 cm. Its fur ranges from silver-grey to brown, black, or golden; males tend to have reddish shoulders. It has large ears, sharp claws for climbing, and a naked patch on the underside of the tail that aids grip.

Behaviour and diet

Brushtail possums are solitary and territorial, communicating by scent-marking and by loud coughs, growls, and hisses. They shelter by day in tree hollows, roofs, or other cavities and emerge at night to feed. Although chiefly herbivorous — eating leaves, especially eucalyptus, as well as flowers and fruit — they also take insects, eggs, and occasionally small animals. Females give birth to a single joey after a gestation of about 17 days; the young develops in the pouch for four to five months.

In New Zealand

Possums were introduced to New Zealand from 1837 onward to establish a fur industry. Without natural predators, the population grew to tens of millions, damaging native forests by browsing and preying on native birds and invertebrates; they also spread bovine tuberculosis to cattle and deer. Possums are therefore subject to extensive control there, while in Australia the species is protected.
