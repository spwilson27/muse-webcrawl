Tiberias

Tiberias (Hebrew: טבריה, Tverya; Arabic: طبريا, Ṭabariya) is an Israeli city on the western shore of the Sea of Galilee in the Lower Galilee region of northern Israel. Lying about 200 metres below sea level, it is one of the lowest cities in the world and a popular resort, known for its hot springs and lakeside setting. Its population is roughly 50,000.

History

The city was founded around AD 20 by Herod Antipas, tetrarch of Galilee, who named it in honour of the Roman emperor Tiberius. Because it was built partly over an ancient burial ground, many Jews initially considered it ritually impure, but it grew into an important urban centre of Roman and Byzantine Palestine. After the Bar Kokhba revolt, Tiberias became a principal centre of Jewish life: the Sanhedrin moved there, and the city was home to the rabbis who completed the Jerusalem Talmud (c. AD 400) and developed the Tiberian system of vowel points (nikkud) for Hebrew. It is counted among Judaism's Four Holy Cities, alongside Jerusalem, Hebron and Safed.

Tiberias passed through Muslim, Crusader, Mamluk and Ottoman rule; the Jewish community was revived under Ottoman rule with the support of Doña Gracia Mendes Nasi in the sixteenth century. The city contains the traditional tombs of Rabbi Akiva, Rabbi Meir Baal HaNes and Maimonides. In 1948 the Arab population fled or was expelled during the war, and Tiberias became a predominantly Jewish city.

Today

Modern Tiberias is a hub for tourism in the Galilee, with hotels along the lakefront, marinas, the Hamat Tiberias hot springs, and boat trips on the Sea of Galilee. It also serves as a base for visiting Christian holy sites around the lake and Jewish pilgrimage sites in the city.
