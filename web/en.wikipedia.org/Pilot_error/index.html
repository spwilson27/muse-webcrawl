Pilot error

Pilot error generally refers to an action or decision made by a pilot that is a major contributing factor in an aviation accident or incident. It includes errors of judgement, failures to follow procedures, loss of situational awareness, and mishandling of the aircraft. Historically, pilot error has been cited as a factor in a large share of aviation accidents, though modern safety analysis emphasises that such errors usually arise within a wider system of contributing causes.

Modern flight decks are designed to reduce the chance and impact of pilot error.

Types of error

Investigators distinguish decision errors (poor choices, such as continuing into bad weather), skill-based errors (breakdowns in basic flight technique or procedural execution), and perceptual errors (misjudging altitude, speed, or orientation, sometimes caused by visual illusions). Violations of rules or procedures, whether routine or exceptional, form a further category.

Human factors

Fatigue, stress, distraction, fixation, and poor crew communication are common underlying factors. Crew resource management (CRM) training, introduced after accidents in the 1970s showed how hierarchical cockpits suppressed vital warnings, teaches crews to communicate and cross-check effectively.

Notable examples

The Tenerife airport disaster of 1977, the deadliest accident in aviation history, involved a takeoff attempted without clearance in fog. The crash of Air France Flight 447 in 2009 involved inappropriate control inputs and loss of situational awareness following airspeed sensor failure. Such cases illustrate how design, training, weather, and organisational factors combine with human error.

Prevention

Mitigation includes standard operating procedures, checklists, automation, ground proximity and traffic collision warning systems, rigorous training and checking, and confidential incident reporting systems that encourage learning from mistakes without blame.
