Gravel road

A gravel road is a type of unpaved road surfaced with gravel that has been brought to the site from a quarry or stream bed. Such roads are common in less-developed parts of the world and in rural regions of countries including the United States, Canada, Australia and New Zealand, where they serve farms, forests and remote communities. In the United States there are roughly 1.3–1.6 million miles of unpaved roads, most of them gravel-surfaced.

Construction and maintenance

A well-built gravel road has three layers: a subgrade of native soil, a sub-base, and a surface course of crushed stone mixed with fines and clay that bind the surface when compacted. Maintenance is dominated by motor-grader blading, which restores the road's crown and smooths the corrugations known as "washboarding" that develop under traffic. Gravel is periodically replenished, since material is lost to traffic spray, weather and dust; dust-control treatments such as calcium chloride are sometimes applied.

Driving characteristics

Gravel surfaces provide far less traction than sealed pavement and behave inconsistently: loose stone, washboarding and dust-reduced visibility demand lower speeds and greater following distances. Loose gravel is a particular hazard for motorcycles and bicycles. In Scandinavian countries, gravel roads are also used as special stages in rallying, including Rally Finland and Rally Sweden.

Comparison with other unpaved roads

Gravel roads differ from dirt roads, which are formed simply by grading native soil and compact poorly and become impassable in wet weather, and from improved surfaces such as macadam or chipseal. Agencies weigh the lower construction cost of gravel against its higher routine maintenance and user costs; paving is usually justified only above a certain daily traffic volume.
