Wet nurse

A wet nurse is a woman who breastfeeds and cares for another's child. Wet nurses have been employed throughout history when a mother was unable or chose not to nurse her own infant, or when breastfeeding was considered incompatible with her social position or health. A woman can only act as a wet nurse if she is lactating, a state induced and maintained by recent childbirth and continued nursing.

History

Wet nursing is attested in ancient Egypt, Greece, and Rome, where it was common among wealthy families and was regulated by law and medical advice — the physician Soranus wrote criteria for choosing a good nurse. In early modern Europe, particularly in France, large numbers of urban infants were sent to wet nurses in the countryside, a practice associated with high infant mortality. In royal and aristocratic households a wet nurse could hold an influential position; wet nurses of monarchs were sometimes richly rewarded, and children nursed by the same woman were regarded as "milk siblings," a relationship recognized in Islamic law as a bar to marriage.

Decline

The practice declined sharply in the 19th and 20th centuries with the development of safe infant formula, pasteurization, feeding bottles, and changing social attitudes toward breastfeeding, though wet nursing persists in some form today through milk banks and informal milk sharing. Cross-nursing in emergencies — for example, after the death of a mother in childbirth — remains occasionally practised.
