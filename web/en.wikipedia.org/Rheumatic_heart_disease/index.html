Rheumatic heart disease

Rheumatic heart disease is a condition in which the heart valves have been permanently damaged by rheumatic fever, an inflammatory disease that can develop after an infection with group A Streptococcus bacteria (such as strep throat or scarlet fever). The valve damage may persist long after the acute illness, or develop progressively over years of recurrent episodes. The mitral valve is most commonly affected, followed by the aortic valve, causing stenosis, regurgitation, or both, and potentially leading to heart failure, stroke and infective endocarditis.

Pathophysiology

Rheumatic fever is an autoimmune response: antibodies and T cells raised against streptococcal M protein cross-react with proteins in the heart (molecular mimicry). During acute rheumatic carditis, inflammation affects the pericardium, myocardium and endocardium; the characteristic Aschoff bodies may be found in the myocardium. Repeated or severe valvulitis leads to scarring, leaflet thickening, commissural fusion and chordal shortening, producing chronic rheumatic heart disease.

Epidemiology

Once common in industrialised countries, rheumatic heart disease declined sharply there with improved living conditions and the antibiotic era, but it remains a major cause of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality in low- and middle-income countries, affecting an estimated 30–40 million people worldwide and causing several hundred thousand deaths per year. It particularly affects children and young adults in sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia and the Pacific, and Indigenous populations in Australia and New Zealand.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on clinical findings of a heart murmur and is confirmed by echocardiography; criteria published by the World Heart Federation standardise echocardiographic diagnosis, including of subclinical ("borderline") disease detected by screening. Treatment includes long-term antibiotic prophylaxis (typically intramuscular benzathine penicillin every four weeks) to prevent recurrences of rheumatic fever, management of heart failure and atrial fibrillation, and, for severe valve disease, balloon valvuloplasty or surgical valve repair or replacement.

Prevention

Prompt antibiotic treatment of group A streptococcal pharyngitis prevents most initial attacks of rheumatic fever (primary prevention); continuous prophylaxis after an attack prevents recurrences and progression (secondary prevention). In 2018 the World Health Assembly adopted a resolution calling for a global response to rheumatic fever and rheumatic heart disease.
