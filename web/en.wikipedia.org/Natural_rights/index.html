Natural rights

See also: Natural law and Human rights.

Natural rights are rights that are held to exist independently of human laws, customs, or governments — rights people possess by virtue of their nature as human beings, rather than by grant of any authority. The concept developed within the tradition of natural law and was central to Enlightenment political philosophy, influencing the American Declaration of Independence (1776) and the French Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen (1789). Natural rights are typically contrasted with legal rights, which are created and enforced by legal systems, and are often described as universal, inalienable, and pre-political.

Historical development

Ideas of natural justice appear in ancient Greek and Roman thought and in medieval scholasticism, where Thomas Aquinas grounded natural law in eternal law. Hugo Grotius and Samuel Pufendorf developed modern natural law theory in the 17th century. John Locke's Two Treatises of Government (1689) argued that in the state of nature individuals possess rights to life, liberty, and property (estate), and that governments derive their legitimacy from a social contract to protect these rights — a theory that profoundly shaped Anglo-American political thought. Thomas Jefferson's formulation "life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness" adapted Locke's triad.

Content and criticism

Proposed lists of natural rights vary, but commonly include rights to life, liberty, property, and the pursuit of happiness; some theorists derive further rights such as self-defense and freedom of conscience. Critics from Jeremy Bentham — who called natural rights "nonsense upon stilts" — to legal positivists have argued that rights exist only as creations of law. In the 20th century, the language of natural rights largely merged into the discourse of universal human rights, embodied in the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948).
