The National Health Service (NHS) is the umbrella term for the publicly funded healthcare systems of the United Kingdom. Founded on 5 July 1948 by the post-war Labour government of Clement Attlee, with Health Minister Aneurin Bevan as its architect, the NHS provides healthcare — including hospital treatment, general practice, and emergency care — largely free at the point of use, funded through general taxation. It is among the largest employers in the world.

Founding principles

The NHS was founded on three core principles: that it meet the needs of everyone, that it be free at the point of delivery, and that it be based on clinical need, not ability to pay. Its creation drew on the Beveridge Report (1942), which identified "Disease" as one of the five giant evils to be defeated by the welfare state. The launch brought nearly all hospitals in Britain into public ownership.

Organisation

Since devolution, healthcare has been run separately in each nation: NHS England (overseen by NHS England and integrated care boards), NHS Scotland, NHS Wales, and Health and Social Care in Northern Ireland. Most patients access care through general practitioners (GPs), who act as gatekeepers to specialist services. Prescription charges apply in England but were abolished in Wales (2007), Northern Ireland (2010), and Scotland (2011); dentistry and optometry carry charges in parts of the UK.

Challenges

The NHS faces persistent pressures from an ageing population, rising chronic disease, workforce shortages, and waiting lists that grew sharply after the COVID-19 pandemic. It nevertheless consistently polls as one of Britain's most valued institutions; its prominence in national life was symbolised by its central place in the opening ceremony of the 2012 London Olympics.
