The I Ching or Yijing (易經, "Classic of Changes") is an ancient Chinese divination text that is among the oldest of the Chinese classics. Originally a divination manual in the Western Zhou period (1000–750 BC), the I Ching was transformed over the Warring States and early imperial periods into a cosmological text with a series of philosophical commentaries known as the "Ten Wings". Reading the I Ching became a hallmark of literate culture, and it was canonised as one of the Five Classics of Confucianism.

Structure

The core of the text, the Zhouyi, consists of 64 chapters, each headed by a hexagram — a figure of six lines, each line either broken (yin) or unbroken (yang). Hexagrams are traditionally built from combinations of the eight trigrams. Each chapter gives an oracle, the "judgment", followed by texts for each of the six lines. A consultation traditionally involves generating a hexagram by manipulating yarrow stalks or tossing coins, then reading the judgment and any "moving lines" and the hexagram they transform it into.

History

Tradition attributes the trigrams to the culture hero Fu Xi and the line texts to King Wen of Zhou and the Duke of Zhou. Divination with milfoil (yarrow) stalks appears in Zhou records alongside turtle-shell oracle divination. The Ten Wings commentaries — including the Great Treatise (Xici zhuan), a key statement of Chinese cosmology — are now dated by most scholars to the Warring States and Han periods rather than to Confucius himself. The 2nd-century BC Mawangdui silk manuscripts contain an early version with differing hexagram order and commentaries.

Philosophy and influence

The I Ching treats change as the fundamental feature of reality: situations are described by hexagrams that are always liable to transform into others through their moving lines. Its ideas underlie the yin–yang cosmology and much of Chinese thought on statecraft, medicine and strategy. In the West the text became widely known through Richard Wilhelm's German translation (1923), rendered into English by Cary Baynes with a foreword by Carl Jung, who used it to illustrate his concept of synchronicity. Leibniz, who corresponded with Jesuit missionaries in China, saw in the hexagrams a precedent for his binary arithmetic. The composer John Cage used the I Ching to generate chance operations in his music.
