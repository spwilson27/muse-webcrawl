Strain gauge

A typical metallic foil strain gauge

A strain gauge (also sometimes spelled strain gage) is a sensor whose electrical resistance varies in proportion to the amount of strain in the device. The most widely used type is a metallic foil pattern bonded to a flexible insulating backing, which is glued to the object under test. As the object deforms, the foil lengthens or shortens, and its resistance changes are measured, usually with a Wheatstone bridge.

History

The relation between strain and resistance change in conductors was noted by Lord Kelvin in the 1850s. Practical strain gauges were developed in the 1930s by Edward E. Simmons and Arthur C. Ruge, whose bonded wire gauge of 1938 became the standard instrument in structural testing.

Principle

The gauge factor, the ratio of relative resistance change to strain, is about 2 for metallic foil gauges; semiconductor gauges have much higher gauge factors but greater temperature sensitivity. Quarter-, half-, and full-bridge arrangements provide temperature compensation and amplified output.

Applications

Strain gauges are used to measure load, torque, pressure, and deformation in load cells, bridges, aircraft structures, rail track, and experimental stress analysis, as well as in geotechnical monitoring.
