Technological singularity

The technological singularity—or simply the singularity—is a hypothetical future point in time at which technological growth becomes uncontrollable and irreversible, resulting in unforeseeable consequences for human civilization. According to the most popular variant of the singularity hypothesis, an upgradable intelligent agent (initially a weak artificial general intelligence) will eventually enter a positive feedback loop of self-improvement cycles, each new and more intelligent generation appearing more rapidly, causing an "explosion" in intelligence and resulting in a powerful superintelligence that qualitatively far surpasses all human intelligence.

Origins

The concept is often traced to mathematician John von Neumann, who in the 1950s spoke of an "ever accelerating progress of technology" approaching "some essential singularity in the history of the race beyond which human affairs, as we know them, could not continue." Science fiction author Vernor Vinge popularized the term in his 1993 essay "The Coming Technological Singularity," predicting such an event within a few decades. Futurist Ray Kurzweil later argued, in books such as The Singularity Is Near (2005), that accelerating returns in computing would bring the singularity around 2045.

Intelligence explosion

Mathematician I. J. Good speculated in 1965 that an ultraintelligent machine could design even better machines, leading to an "intelligence explosion" that would leave human intelligence far behind. This recursive self-improvement argument is central to most singularity scenarios.

Criticism

Critics argue that predictions of a singularity rely on naive extrapolation of exponential trends, underestimate physical and economic limits, and conflate narrow measures of computing power with general intelligence. Others dispute the timeline while accepting the possibility of transformative AI. The concept remains a subject of debate among researchers in artificial intelligence and futures studies.
