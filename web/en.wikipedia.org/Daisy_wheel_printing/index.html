Daisy wheel printing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Daisy wheel printing is an impact printing technology invented in 1970 by Andrew Gabor at Diablo Systems. It uses interchangeable pre-formed type elements — a wheel with a character embossed at the end of each "petal" — which a servo motor rotates into position before a hammer strikes it against an inked ribbon and the paper. The result was fully formed, typewriter-quality ("letter quality") output.

History

Diablo's HyType I printer (1972) and the widely adopted HyType II brought daisy wheel printing to minicomputer and word-processing markets. After Xerox acquired Diablo, the technology spread through office systems; dedicated word processors and electronic typewriters from Brother, Olivetti, and others used daisy wheels extensively through the 1970s and 1980s. Qume and NEC (with its thimble-shaped Spinwriter element, a close relative) were other major manufacturers.

Characteristics

Daisy wheel printers produced crisp text comparable to a good typewriter, could print in proportional spacing with suitable wheels, and handled multi-part carbon forms thanks to impact printing. Their limitations were significant: each wheel carried a single typeface in one size (typically 96 or 130 characters), graphics were essentially impossible except by laborious overstriking and micro-spacing plots, and speeds — roughly 30 to 55 characters per second — were far below later technologies. They were also notoriously noisy.

Decline

By the late 1980s, 24-pin dot-matrix printers matched daisy wheel quality while adding graphics, and affordable laser and inkjet printers soon displaced impact printing entirely. Daisy wheel machines survived longest in typewriter-like niches and some form-filling applications.
