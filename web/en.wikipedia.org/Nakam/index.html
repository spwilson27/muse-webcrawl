Nakam

Jewish partisans and survivors in post-war Europe formed Nakam

Nakam (Hebrew: נקם, "vengeance") was a paramilitary organisation of about fifty Holocaust survivors which, in 1945, sought to avenge the murder of the Jews during the Holocaust. Led by Abba Kovner, a former partisan commander from the Vilnius Ghetto, the group — also known as Dam Yisrael Noter ("the blood of Israel avenges"; DIN, from the initials of its Hebrew name) — plotted mass reprisals against Germans after the end of the Second World War.

Plan A: mass poisoning

Concluding that legal justice would be inadequate, Kovner travelled to Mandatory Palestine in 1945 seeking poison from contacts in the Haganah and Hebrew University, intending to poison the water supplies of major German cities such as Munich and Nuremberg and kill six million Germans in retribution. Kovner was arrested by the British on his return voyage and the poison was thrown overboard; the "Plan A" was never carried out, and its feasibility is debated by historians.

Plan B: the Nuremberg bread poisoning

The group turned to a narrower target: SS prisoners held in American camps. On 13 April 1946, Nakam operatives who had infiltrated the bakery supplying Stalag 13 near Nuremberg painted arsenic onto thousands of loaves of bread destined for SS prisoners. More than 2,200 prisoners became ill; reports of deaths vary, with the Associated Press reporting at least several hundred hospitalised and later accounts suggesting no confirmed large death toll was ever established. American authorities investigated but made no arrests.

Disbanding and legacy

As surviving members left for Palestine to fight in the 1948 Arab–Israeli War, Nakam dissolved. Its story remained obscure for decades until research and memoirs in the 1990s and 2000s — including historian Dina Porat's studies — brought it to wider attention. In 2016 German prosecutors opened and then closed an investigation into two elderly former members on grounds of the statute of limitations and lack of evidence.
