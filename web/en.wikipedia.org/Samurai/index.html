Samurai (侍) were the hereditary military nobility and officer caste of medieval and early-modern Japan, from the late 12th century until their abolition in the late 1870s during the Meiji era. They were the well-paid retainers of the daimyo, the great feudal landholders, and were granted particular privileges, most famously the right to wear two swords (the daishō). Their conduct was governed by the code later called bushido, emphasizing loyalty, martial skill and honour unto death.

History

Armed warriors rose to prominence in the Heian period as provincial clans such as the Taira and Minamoto amassed power. Minamoto no Yoritomo's victory in the Genpei War led to the establishment of the Kamakura shogunate in 1192, beginning nearly 700 years of military rule. Samurai fought off the Mongol invasions of 1274 and 1281, endured the Sengoku period of civil war, and were consolidated as a distinct hereditary caste under the Tokugawa shogunate (1603–1868), when many became administrators and scholars during a long peace.

Weapons and armour

The samurai's iconic weapons were the katana and the shorter wakizashi, worn together as a badge of rank. Earlier samurai were primarily mounted archers using the long yumi bow, and they also fought with polearms such as the yari and naginata. Armour evolved from the lamellar ō-yoroi to later plate-and-mail designs influenced by European imports in the 16th century, when firearms (tanegashima) transformed Japanese warfare.

Decline and abolition

The Meiji Restoration of 1868 dismantled the feudal order. A conscript army replaced samurai forces, stipends were commuted to bonds, and the 1876 Haitōrei edict banned the wearing of swords. Discontent culminated in the Satsuma Rebellion of 1877, led by Saigō Takamori, whose defeat by the conscript army marked the end of the samurai as a class. Their image, however, became central to modern Japanese identity and to popular culture worldwide.
