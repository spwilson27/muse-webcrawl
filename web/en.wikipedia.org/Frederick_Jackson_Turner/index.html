Frederick Jackson Turner (November 14, 1861 – March 14, 1932) was an American historian best known for the "frontier thesis," one of the most influential interpretations of American history. He taught for most of his career at the University of Wisconsin and later at Harvard.

The frontier thesis

In his 1893 address "The Significance of the Frontier in American History," delivered at the American Historical Association meeting in Chicago, Turner argued that American democracy and national character had been shaped by the experience of the moving frontier: the availability of free land, the shedding of European customs, and repeated rebirth of society at the edge of settlement. Reacting to the 1890 census declaration that the frontier line was closed, he asked what would become of American development without it.

Influence and criticism

Turner's thesis dominated American historical writing for decades and trained a generation of historians. From the mid-twentieth century, critics challenged its vagueness about the word "frontier," its neglect of Native Americans, women, and the environment, and its nationalism; "New Western historians" reframed the West as a place of conquest and continuity rather than a process of free land. Turner also wrote on sectionalism in The Significance of Sections in American History (1932), which won a posthumous Pulitzer Prize.
