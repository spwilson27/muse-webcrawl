Genghis Khan (born Temüjin, c. 1162 – 18 August 1227) was the founder and first khan of the Mongol Empire, which became the largest contiguous land empire in history. Uniting the nomadic tribes of the Mongolian plateau, he launched invasions that conquered much of Central Asia and northern China.

Early life and unification

Temüjin's father, a minor chieftain, was poisoned when Temüjin was a boy, and the family was abandoned by their clan. Through hardship, alliance-building and warfare — aided by his anda (blood brother) turned rival Jamukha's eventual defeat — Temüjin subdued or allied with the steppe peoples. In 1206 a kuriltai (assembly) proclaimed him Genghis Khan, universal ruler of the Mongols. He reorganised his followers into a decimal military system, promoted by merit rather than birth, and issued laws later associated with the Yassa code.

Conquests

Genghis Khan subjugated the Western Xia (1211–1219) and conquered the Jin dynasty of northern China. After the Khwarazmian Empire executed his envoys, he invaded Central Asia (1219–1221), destroying cities such as Samarkand and Bukhara with notorious brutality; many regions' populations were devastated. His armies ranged from the Caspian Sea to the Pacific. He died in 1227 during a final campaign against Western Xia; the location of his tomb remains unknown.

Legacy

The empire passed to his son Ögedei and was later divided among his descendants. Mongol rule (the Pax Mongolica) secured the Silk Road, promoted trade and religious tolerance, and reshaped Eurasian geopolitics. Genghis Khan is revered in Mongolia as a national founder, while in many conquered lands he is remembered as a merciless destroyer; historians continue to debate the demographic toll of his wars.
