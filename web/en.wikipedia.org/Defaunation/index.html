Defaunation is the global, local, or functional extinction of animal populations or species from ecological communities. The term, coined by analogy with deforestation, was popularised in the 1990s and 2000s by conservation biologists to describe the loss of animals not only through outright species extinction but also through severe population declines that leave animals ecologically absent even where they still survive.

Causes

Defaunation is driven primarily by human activity: hunting and poaching for meat, trophies, and traditional medicine (including the bushmeat trade); habitat destruction and fragmentation from logging, agriculture, and roads, which expose previously remote wildlife to hunters; the introduction of invasive predators and diseases; pollution; and climate change. Large-bodied species — primates, ungulates, large carnivores, elephants, and big frugivorous birds — are typically affected first and most severely because they reproduce slowly and are preferentially hunted.

Ecological consequences

Because animals perform essential functions, their loss cascades through ecosystems. The disappearance of large seed dispersers reduces recruitment of large-seeded trees, shifting forest composition and lowering the carbon stored in tropical forests. Declines in predators release herbivore populations, while losses of pollinators, scavengers, and dung beetles disrupt reproduction, nutrient cycling, and disease dynamics. The resulting landscape is the "empty forest" described by Kent Redford in 1992: a forest that looks intact from above but has been stripped of its fauna.

Scale and responses

Studies of defaunation, including global analyses of mammal populations since 1970, indicate losses on the order of half or more of the individuals in many monitored populations, a phenomenon linked to the broader biodiversity crisis sometimes called the sixth mass extinction. Responses include protected areas with effective enforcement, hunting regulation, wildlife corridors, and rewilding projects that reintroduce lost species and restore their ecological roles.
