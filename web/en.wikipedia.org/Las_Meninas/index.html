Las Meninas (Spanish for "The Maids of Honour") is a 1656 painting by Diego Velázquez, the leading artist of the Spanish Golden Age. Housed in the Museo del Prado in Madrid, it is regarded as one of the most famous and analysed paintings in Western art, renowned for its complex composition and its meditation on representation, illusion, and the status of the painter.

Description

The scene is set in Velázquez's studio in the Alcázar of Madrid. At centre stands the five-year-old Infanta Margaret Theresa, attended by two maids of honour (meninas), a chaperone, a bodyguard, two dwarfs, and a dog. To the left, Velázquez himself appears at work on a large canvas, brush and palette in hand, looking out toward the viewer. In a mirror on the back wall are reflected King Philip IV and Queen Mariana — the subjects, it seems, of the painting within the painting, standing where the viewer stands. A chamberlain pauses in the lit doorway at the rear.

Analysis and legacy

The painting's play between reality and representation has generated an enormous literature, including Michel Foucault's celebrated reading in The Order of Things (1966), which treats it as a representation of representation itself. The red cross of the Order of Santiago on Velázquez's chest — reportedly added after his admission to the order — asserted the nobility of painting and of the painter. Pablo Picasso painted a celebrated series of 58 reinterpretations of the work in 1957. Luca Giordano called the picture the "theology of painting".
