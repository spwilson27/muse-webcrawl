Dementia

Dementia is a syndrome — a group of related symptoms — involving progressive decline in memory, thinking, language and the ability to carry out everyday activities, severe enough to interfere with daily life. It is not a single disease nor a normal part of ageing, though risk rises strongly with age.

Causes

The most common cause is Alzheimer's disease, accounting for roughly 60–70% of cases, followed by vascular dementia, dementia with Lewy bodies and frontotemporal dementia. These diseases involve abnormal protein accumulation and death of brain cells; mixed pathology is common in older people. A small proportion of cases result from treatable conditions such as vitamin deficiency, thyroid disorders or normal-pressure hydrocephalus.

Symptoms, diagnosis and care

Early symptoms include forgetfulness, difficulty finding words, disorientation and changes in mood and personality; advanced dementia brings loss of independence and need for full-time care. Diagnosis combines cognitive testing, history, brain imaging and blood tests. There is no cure for the major neurodegenerative dementias; cholinesterase inhibitors and memantine modestly relieve symptoms, and anti-amyloid antibodies such as lecanemab can slightly slow early Alzheimer's. Worldwide, more than 55 million people live with dementia, a number projected roughly to triple by 2050 as populations age, making it a leading cause of disability and care costs.
