Eugene Shoemaker

BornApril 28, 1928, Los Angeles, California
DiedJuly 18, 1997 (aged 69), near Alice Springs, Australia
FieldsPlanetary geology, impact cratering

Eugene Merle Shoemaker (April 28, 1928 – July 18, 1997) was an American geologist and one of the founders of planetary science. He is best known for co-discovering Comet Shoemaker–Levy 9 with his wife Carolyn Shoemaker and David Levy; the comet collided with Jupiter in July 1994, providing the first direct observation of an extraterrestrial collision between Solar System objects.

Career

Shoemaker earned his doctorate at Princeton University under Harry Hammond Hess. Working for the U.S. Geological Survey, he proved that Barringer Crater (Meteor Crater) in Arizona was formed by an asteroid impact by identifying the shocked mineral coesite. In 1960 he founded the USGS Astrogeology Research Program in Flagstaff, Arizona, and trained Apollo astronauts in lunar geology; he was expected to be the first geologist to walk on the Moon before Addison's disease disqualified him.

He later joined Caltech, where with Eleanor Helin he conducted sky surveys for Earth-crossing asteroids, helped establish the modern understanding of impact cratering rates, and argued that impacts have played a major role in Earth's geological and biological history.

Death and lunar burial

Shoemaker died in a car crash in Australia in 1997 while prospecting for impact craters. On July 31, 1999, a portion of his ashes was carried to the Moon aboard NASA's Lunar Prospector spacecraft, which was deliberately crashed into a crater near the lunar south pole — making him the only person whose remains have been placed on the Moon. The crater was subsequently named Shoemaker crater in his honor.
