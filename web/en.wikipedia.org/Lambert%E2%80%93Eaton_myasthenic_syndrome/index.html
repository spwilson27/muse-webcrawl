Lambert–Eaton myasthenic syndrome

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Lambert–Eaton myasthenic syndrome (LEMS), also called Lambert–Eaton syndrome or Eaton–Lambert syndrome, is a rare autoimmune disorder of the neuromuscular junction in which antibodies attack voltage-gated calcium channels on the presynaptic nerve terminal, reducing the release of acetylcholine and causing muscle weakness.

Signs and symptoms

Weakness typically affects the proximal muscles of the legs first, making it hard to climb stairs or rise from a chair. Unlike myasthenia gravis, strength may improve briefly with exercise (facilitation), and reflexes are reduced but may return after contraction. Autonomic symptoms such as dry mouth, erectile dysfunction, and constipation are common. About half of cases are associated with an underlying cancer, most often small-cell lung cancer, in which case LEMS is a paraneoplastic syndrome.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on clinical features, electrodiagnostic studies showing incremental responses on high-frequency repetitive stimulation, and detection of anti–voltage-gated calcium channel antibodies. Treatment includes searching for and treating any tumour, and symptomatic therapy with amifampridine (3,4-diaminopyridine); immunosuppression and plasma exchange are used in more severe cases.
