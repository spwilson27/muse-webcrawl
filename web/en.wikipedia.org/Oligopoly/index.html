Oligopoly

An oligopoly is a market structure in which a market or industry is dominated by a small number of large sellers (oligopolists). Because each firm is large relative to the market, the actions of one firm — its prices, output or advertising — directly affect its rivals, so firms are strategically interdependent and must take competitors' likely reactions into account. Oligopolies often arise where economies of scale, high entry barriers, patents or control of resources limit competition.

Characteristics and theory

Oligopolistic markets typically feature a few sellers of an identical or differentiated product, substantial barriers to entry, and price-setting power tempered by the threat of retaliation. Economists model oligopoly with game theory: in the Cournot model firms compete in quantities, in the Bertrand model they compete in prices, and in the Stackelberg model a leader moves first. The kinked demand curve model explains price rigidity: firms fear that raising prices will cost them customers while cutting prices will start a price war. Outcomes range from tacit collusion, which can reproduce monopoly prices, to vigorous competition; explicit collusion in a cartel is illegal in most jurisdictions under antitrust law.

Examples

Commonly cited examples include the global markets for large commercial aircraft (Boeing and Airbus — a duopoly, the limiting case of two firms), commercial operating systems, credit cards, mobile telecommunications in many countries, major oil companies, and the "Big Four" accounting firms. The Herfindahl–Hirschman Index (HHI), which sums the squares of firms' market shares, is used by regulators to measure such concentration.
