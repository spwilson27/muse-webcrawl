Guaraní people

The Guaraní are an Indigenous people of South America whose traditional territory covers parts of present-day Paraguay, northeastern Argentina, southern Brazil and southeastern Bolivia. They speak Guaraní, a Tupian language that is co-official in Paraguay — where it is spoken by the majority of the population, including most non-Indigenous Paraguayans — and is one of the most widely spoken Indigenous languages in the Americas.

History

Before European contact the Guaraní were semi-sedentary farmers organised in extended-family villages led by chiefs and shamans, united by a shared language and a religious quest for the "Land Without Evil". From 1609 Jesuit missionaries established reductions among the Guaraní of the Paraná–Paraguay region, creating large mission towns famed for their music, crafts and militias that defended against slave-raiding bandeirantes from São Paulo. After the Jesuits' expulsion in 1767 the missions declined and Guaraní communities dispersed.

Modern Guaraní

Today distinct Guaraní groups include the Ava-Guaraní, Mbyá, Kaiowá and Ñandeva. In Brazil and Paraguay many communities face ongoing conflicts over ancestral land occupied by cattle ranching and soy farming, and Guaraní land rights remain a major human-rights issue. Guaraní culture has deeply shaped Paraguayan national identity, cuisine and language.
