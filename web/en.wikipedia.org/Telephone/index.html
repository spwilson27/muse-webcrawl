Telephone

A telephone is a telecommunications device that permits two or more users to conduct a conversation when they are too far apart to be heard directly. It converts sound, typically the human voice, into electronic signals that are transmitted over wires or radio links and reconverted to sound at the receiving end.

History

The telephone was developed in the 1870s; Alexander Graham Bell received the first US patent for it in 1876, while Elisha Gray, Antonio Meucci and others made rival claims or contributions. Early telephones were connected by manual switchboards operated by human operators; automatic exchanges, beginning with Almon Strowger's electromechanical switch (patented 1891), progressively automated dialling.

How it works

In a classic landline telephone, a microphone (originally a carbon transmitter) converts sound to a varying electric current carried over a copper pair to the telephone exchange, which switches the call to the destination line, where a receiver converts the current back to sound. Digital telephony converts voice to data packets, as in mobile networks and Voice over IP (VoIP).

Modern telephones

Mobile phones, using cellular radio, overtook fixed lines in the 2000s, and smartphones combined telephony with computing and internet access, becoming the dominant personal communications device worldwide.
