Grazing

In agriculture, grazing is a method of animal husbandry in which livestock are allowed to roam outdoors and feed on wild vegetation, in contrast to being confined and fed harvested fodder. More broadly, grazing is a form of feeding in which a herbivore consumes plant matter, especially grasses. Grazing animals — grazers — include cattle, sheep, goats, horses, deer, rabbits, kangaroos and many invertebrates such as grasshoppers and sea urchins.

Grazing systems

Grazing management systems range from continuous grazing of a single paddock, through rotational grazing (moving stock between subdivided pastures so that forage can recover), to intensive strip grazing and rangeland grazing over large natural grasslands. "Mob grazing" and adaptive multi-paddock grazing use high stock densities and long rest periods, and are promoted by advocates of regenerative agriculture as ways to build soil carbon, though the evidence is debated.

Ecological effects

Grazers shape ecosystems: grazing maintains grasslands against encroaching shrubs, recycles nutrients in dung and urine, and creates habitat mosaics used by other species. Conversely, overgrazing — grazing beyond the land's capacity to recover — compacts and exposes soil, promotes erosion and invasive species, and contributes to desertification. Grazing pressure by wild herbivores is a central topic in ecology, including classic examples such as sea otters controlling sea-urchin grazing of kelp forests in trophic cascades.

History

Pastoral grazing is among the oldest forms of food production, with domesticated sheep, goats and cattle grazed across Eurasia and Africa for roughly ten thousand years. Transhumance — the seasonal movement of herds between lowland and mountain pastures — structured landscapes from the Alps to the Sahel, and grazing rights on common land were a recurring source of social conflict, notably during the English enclosure movement.
