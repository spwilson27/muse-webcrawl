Carl David Tolmé Runge (1856–1927)

Carl David Tolmé Runge (German: [ˈʁʊŋə]; 30 August 1856 – 3 January 1927) was a German mathematician, physicist, and spectroscopist. He was co-developer and co-eponym of the Runge–Kutta method (German pronunciation: [ˈʀʊŋə ˈkʊta]), in the field of what is today known as numerical analysis.

Life and work

Runge spent the first years of his life in Havana, where his father Julius Runge was the Danish consul. In 1880, he received his Ph.D. in mathematics at Berlin, where he studied under Karl Weierstrass. In 1886, he became a professor at the Technical University of Hanover. His interests included mathematics, spectroscopy, geodesy, and astrophysics.

In Hanover he was involved in the investigation of spectral lines of elements other than hydrogen. The spectra he studied were expressed by a law he developed independently but simultaneously with Heinrich Kayser (the Kayser–Runge law). His work on spectra led, in 1900, to his contributions to numerical solutions of ordinary differential equations.

In 1904, on the initiative of Felix Klein, he received a call to the University of Göttingen, the first chair of applied mathematics in Germany. He developed the Runge–Kutta method of numerical integration, later notable for its use in solving differential equations numerically. He also investigated the behaviour of polynomial interpolation, discovering that equally spaced interpolation points can cause wild oscillations near the ends of an interval — a phenomenon now known as Runge's phenomenon.

Honours

The crater Runge on the Moon is named after him. The Schumann–Runge bands of molecular oxygen are named after him and Victor Schumann.
