High-fructose corn syrup

High-fructose corn syrup (HFCS), also known as glucose–fructose, isoglucose and glucose–fructose syrup, is a sweetener made from corn starch. As in the production of conventional corn syrup, the starch is broken down into glucose by enzymes. To make HFCS, the corn syrup is further processed by D-xylose isomerase to convert some of its glucose into fructose, producing a sweeter compound. HFCS was first marketed in the early 1970s by the Clinton Corn Processing Company and the Japanese Agency of Industrial Science and Technology.

Varieties and uses

The most common forms are HFCS 42 (42% fructose), used in processed foods and baked goods, and HFCS 55 (55% fructose), used primarily in soft drinks; HFCS 90 is used in small quantities for some specialty applications. Because it is cheaper and more stable than sucrose in many applications (and because of US sugar import quotas and corn subsidies), HFCS became the dominant sweetener in US processed foods and beverages from the late 1970s onward, displacing sugar in products such as Coca-Cola and Pepsi in the United States. Its use is much lower in most other countries.

Health debate

HFCS consumption has been debated in connection with obesity, type 2 diabetes, and metabolic disease. A widely cited 2004 commentary proposed a link between rising HFCS use and the obesity epidemic, but subsequent reviews by bodies such as the American Medical Association and the US Food and Drug Administration concluded that HFCS is nutritionally similar to sucrose and that there is insufficient evidence it is uniquely harmful beyond the effects of excessive sugar intake generally. US per-capita HFCS consumption peaked around 1999 and has since declined.
