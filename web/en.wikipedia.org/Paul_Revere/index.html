Paul Revere

BornJanuary 1, 1735 (baptised), Boston, Massachusetts Bay
DiedMay 10, 1818 (aged 83), Boston, Massachusetts, U.S.
OccupationsSilversmith, goldsmith, engraver, industrialist
Known forThe "Midnight Ride" of April 18–19, 1775

Paul Revere (January 1, 1735 – May 10, 1818) was an American silversmith, military officer, and industrialist who became a folk hero of the American Revolution for his "midnight ride" of April 18, 1775, warning the Massachusetts militia of approaching British forces before the battles of Lexington and Concord. A leading artisan in colonial Boston and a central figure in the city's patriot intelligence network, Revere also produced the most famous engraving of the Boston Massacre and, after the war, pioneered American industries in copper rolling, bell founding, and cannon casting.

Early life and craft

Revere was born in Boston's North End, the son of Apollos Rivoire, a French Huguenot immigrant who anglicised his name to Paul Revere, and Deborah Hitchbourn. Apprenticed to his father, a goldsmith (in the period, a worker in silver as well as gold), Revere inherited the shop at age 19 and became one of colonial America's finest silversmiths; his silver hollowware is now prized in museum collections. He served in the French and Indian War and was an active Freemason and member of the Sons of Liberty.

Revolutionary activity

Revere participated in the Boston Tea Party in December 1773 and served as an express rider for the Boston Committee of Correspondence, carrying news of the crisis to New York and Philadelphia. His 1770 engraving of the Boston Massacre, adapted from a design by Henry Pelham, became an enduring piece of patriot propaganda.

The Midnight Ride

On the night of April 18–19, 1775, Dr. Joseph Warren charged Revere with riding to Lexington to warn Samuel Adams and John Hancock that British troops were marching to seize military stores at Concord and arrest the patriot leaders. Rowed across the Charles River to Charlestown—having arranged the famous lantern signal in the Old North Church steeple ("one if by land, two if by sea")—Revere rode through Medford and Menotomy, warning households along the way. Joined by William Dawes and Dr. Samuel Prescott, he was captured by a British patrol before reaching Concord; Prescott escaped and completed the mission. The militia mobilisation that followed led to the first battles of the Revolutionary War the next morning.

Revere later served as a lieutenant colonel of artillery in the Massachusetts militia; his reputation suffered after the failed Penobscot Expedition of 1779, though he was eventually cleared of wrongdoing. Henry Wadsworth Longfellow's 1861 poem "Paul Revere's Ride" transformed him into a national icon, though it simplified the historical ride.

Industrial career

After the war Revere expanded into iron founding, cast church bells (his firm, later Revere Copper Company, survived for two centuries), and in 1801 opened the first copper rolling mill in North America at Canton, Massachusetts, supplying copper sheathing for ships including USS Constitution and the dome of the Massachusetts State House. He died in Boston in 1818.
