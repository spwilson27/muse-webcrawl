Gossan

[ Image placeholder: A rusty, iron-stained gossan outcrop above a weathered sulphide deposit ]

Gossan, also written gozzan and gossaniferous, is intensely oxidised, weathered or decomposed rock, usually the upper and exposed part of an ore deposit or mineral vein. In the classic gossan or iron cap, all that remains is iron oxides and quartz, often in the form of a boxwork — a lacy framework retaining the shape of the dissolved sulphide minerals. In other cases, quartz and iron oxides, largely limonite and goethite, form the gossan, which can be identified by its characteristic reddish, orange or yellow-brown staining.

Formation

Gossans form in the oxidising zone above the water table, where percolating oxygenated water attacks sulphide minerals such as pyrite, chalcopyrite and sphalerite. The sulphides are oxidised and their metal content is commonly leached downward, sometimes reprecipitating below as an enriched supergene zone. The residual iron remains as hematite, goethite and other iron oxides near the surface.

Prospecting significance

Because gossans mark the weathered tops of sulphide bodies, they have long served as surface guides for prospectors searching for copper, lead, zinc, gold and silver deposits below. The colour, boxwork textures and trace metal content of a gossan can indicate the composition of the primary ore at depth; experienced geologists distinguish "true" gossans over sulphide bodies from iron-stained rocks of other origins.

Etymology

The word derives from the Cornish miner's term goos, meaning blood — a reference to the blood-red staining of iron oxides on the outcrop.
