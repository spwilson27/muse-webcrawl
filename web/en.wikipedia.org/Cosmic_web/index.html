Cosmic web

The cosmic web is the largest known structure in the universe, consisting of galaxies and galactic clusters connected by filaments of mostly dark matter and gas, separated by giant voids. The term was coined in a 1996 paper by J. Richard Bond, Lev Kofman, and Dmitri Pogosyan. The web formed as gravity amplified the slight density fluctuations present in the early universe, imprinted in the cosmic microwave background, over billions of years.

Components

  • Filaments — thread-like bridges of galaxies, gas, and dark matter, tens to hundreds of megaparsecs long, which contain a large fraction of the universe's ordinary matter;
  • Walls or sheets — flattened structures such as the Great Wall and the Sloan Great Wall, hundreds of megaparsecs across;
  • Clusters and nodes — the dense intersections of filaments where galaxy clusters sit;
  • Voids — vast, nearly empty regions tens of megaparsecs in diameter occupying most of the universe's volume.

Evidence and observation

Galaxy redshift surveys, from the CfA Redshift Survey that revealed the Great Wall in 1989 to the Sloan Digital Sky Survey and 2dF Galaxy Redshift Survey, mapped the web directly, while N-body simulations such as the Millennium Simulation reproduce its pattern from ΛCDM cosmology. In 2019, astronomers for the first time directly imaged faint hydrogen emission from filaments linking galaxies at redshift z ≈ 3 using the MUSE instrument on the Very Large Telescope, and quasar absorption studies trace the diffuse intergalactic medium along the web.
