French horn

Horn
Classification: Brass aerophone
Key: F/B♭ (double horn)
Range: About three and a half octaves
Related: Natural horn, cor d'harmonie

The French horn (since the 1930s often called simply the horn in professional music circles) is a brass instrument made of about 12–13 feet (3.7–4.0 m) of tubing wrapped into a coil with a large flared bell. Pitch is controlled by the player's embouchure, by rotary valves operated with the left hand, and by the right hand in the bell, a technique inherited from the natural horn.

History

The horn descends from hunting horns (cors de chasse) used in France, and entered the orchestra in the early 18th century as the natural horn, limited to the notes of the harmonic series, with crooks inserted to change key. Players used hand-stopping to fill in missing notes, a technique refined by Anton Joseph Hampel. The addition of valves in the 19th century made the instrument fully chromatic. The modern double horn, patented by Fritz Kruspe and Ed. Kruspe's workshop around 1900 and developed further by others, combines two instruments in one — in F and in B♭ — switched by a thumb valve, giving security in the high register while retaining the F horn's mellow tone.

Repertoire and role

The horn holds a central place in the orchestra, bridging the woodwind and brass sections, and in chamber groups such as the wind quintet. Notable concertos include four by Mozart, two by Richard Strauss, and works by Haydn, Weber, and Hindemith; famous orchestral solos include the nocturne in Mendelssohn's A Midsummer Night's Dream incidental music and the horn calls in Wagner and Mahler. The instrument's name is something of a misnomer — the orchestral horn is largely German in development — but "French horn" remains its common English name.
