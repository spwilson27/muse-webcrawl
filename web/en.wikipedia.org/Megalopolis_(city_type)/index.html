Megalopolis (city type)

A megalopolis (from Greek megas, "large", and polis, "city") is a group of two or more roughly adjacent metropolitan areas that, through interlocking systems of transportation, economy and social life, function as a single urban region. The term was popularized by the French geographer Jean Gottmann in his 1961 study of the northeastern United States, describing the chain of cities from Boston to Washington, D.C. — often called BosWash — as a single, integrated megalopolis.

Examples

Other commonly cited megalopolises include the Taiheiyō Belt in Japan (Tokyo–Nagoya–Osaka–Kobe), the Blue Banana in western Europe (from north-west England through the Low Countries and the Rhineland to northern Italy), the Yangtze River Delta and Pearl River Delta in China, the Great Lakes megalopolis in the United States and Canada, and the urban corridor from São Paulo to Rio de Janeiro in Brazil.

Characteristics

Megalopolises are characterized by dense populations (often tens of millions of inhabitants), multinucleated structure in which distinct cities retain their identities, and intense flows of commuters, goods and information along shared transport corridors. Planning across such regions raises challenges because they typically span many municipal and even national jurisdictions.
