From naked-eye star charts to space telescopes: astronomy is among the oldest sciences.

The history of astronomy is the history of humanity's attempts to understand the motions and nature of celestial objects. Astronomy is one of the oldest natural sciences: early cultures used the sky for calendars, navigation and ritual, and left records of eclipses, comets and planetary positions that are still scientifically useful.

Ancient astronomy

Babylonian astronomers developed mathematical schemes to predict lunar and planetary phenomena and identified the zodiac. Egyptians aligned monuments to the stars and tracked the heliacal rising of Sirius for their calendar. Greek astronomy transformed the field into a geometric science: Aristarchus proposed a heliocentric model and measured the relative distances of Sun and Moon; Hipparchus compiled a star catalogue and discovered precession; and Ptolemy's Almagest (2nd century AD) codified the geocentric system of deferents and epicycles that dominated for 1,400 years. Indian and Chinese astronomers maintained continuous records and refined computational methods, and Islamic astronomers of the medieval period preserved, criticised and advanced the Greek tradition with major observatories at Baghdad, Maragheh and Samarkand.

The Copernican revolution

Nicolaus Copernicus's De revolutionibus (1543) revived heliocentrism. Tycho Brahe's precise naked-eye observations enabled Johannes Kepler to discover the elliptical orbits and three laws of planetary motion (1609–1619). Galileo Galilei's telescopic discoveries (1609–1610) — the moons of Jupiter, the phases of Venus, lunar mountains — undermined the Aristotelian cosmos, and Isaac Newton's law of universal gravitation (1687) supplied the physical theory unifying celestial and terrestrial mechanics.

Modern astronomy

The 19th century added stellar parallax (measuring interstellar distances), spectroscopy (revealing the chemical composition of stars) and photography. In the 20th century astronomy expanded beyond visible light (radio, infrared, X-ray and gamma-ray astronomy), established that the Milky Way is one galaxy among many (Hubble, 1920s), discovered the expanding universe and cosmic microwave background, and developed astrophysics and cosmology. Space observatories such as the Hubble and James Webb telescopes, exoplanet surveys, and gravitational-wave detectors mark the current era.
