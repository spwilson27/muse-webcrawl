Battery (electricity)

First production1800 (voltaic pile)

An electric battery is a source of electric power consisting of one or more electrochemical cells with external connections for powering electrical devices. When a battery is supplying power, its positive terminal is the cathode and its negative terminal is the anode. The terminal marked negative is the source of electrons that will flow through an external electric circuit to the positive terminal. When a battery is connected to an external electric load, a redox reaction converts high-energy reactants to lower-energy products, and the free-energy difference is delivered to the external circuit as electrical energy.

History

The first electrical battery was the voltaic pile, invented by Alessandro Volta in 1800, consisting of alternating discs of zinc and copper separated by brine-soaked cloth. In 1859, Gaston Planté invented the lead–acid battery, the first rechargeable type, still used in automobiles. Georges Leclanché's wet cell (1866) led to the dry cell, and Waldemar Jungner's nickel–cadmium battery appeared in 1899. Lithium-ion batteries, commercialized by Sony in 1991 following work by Whittingham, Goodenough, and Yoshino (Nobel Prize in Chemistry 2019), came to dominate portable electronics and electric vehicles.

Types and chemistry

Primary batteries are single-use: common chemistries include zinc–carbon and alkaline (zinc–manganese dioxide) cells, and lithium cells. Secondary (rechargeable) batteries include lead–acid, nickel–cadmium, nickel–metal hydride, and lithium-ion and lithium polymer types. Cells are standardized in sizes such as AAA, AA, C, D, and 9-volt, and are characterized by voltage, capacity (in ampere-hours), energy density, self-discharge rate, and cycle life.

Applications and disposal

Batteries power devices from hearing aids and watches to electric vehicles and grid-scale storage systems. Spent batteries are a regulated waste stream in many jurisdictions — the subject of international controls on their transboundary movement under conventions such as the Basel Convention — and recycling recovers lead, lithium, nickel, cobalt, and other materials. A battery management system monitors and protects larger rechargeable packs in operation.

Not to be confused with an artillery battery.
