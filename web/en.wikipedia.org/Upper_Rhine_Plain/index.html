Upper Rhine Plain

The Upper Rhine Plain (German: Oberrheinische Tiefebene or Oberrheingraben; French: Plaine du Rhin supérieur) is a major rift valley and plain around the upper course of the Rhine in West-Central Europe. It stretches about 350 kilometres (220 mi) from Basel in Switzerland in the south to Frankfurt am Main in Germany in the north, and is 20–40 kilometres wide, lying between the Vosges Mountains in France to the west and the Black Forest in Germany to the east.

Geology

The plain occupies the Upper Rhine Graben, a rift that began forming in the Eocene and Oligocene epochs, some 35–45 million years ago, as the European Cenozoic Rift System developed in the foreland of the Alps. The graben floor has subsided several kilometres relative to the flanking highlands and is filled with thick sediments deposited by the Rhine and its tributaries. The Kaiserstuhl, a low volcanic massif near Freiburg, rises from the plain. The region remains seismically active and has significant geothermal energy potential.

Climate and land use

Sheltered by the Vosges and the Black Forest, the plain is one of the warmest and driest regions of Germany, with a long growing season. It is intensively farmed, known for wine (Alsace, Baden, Palatinate and Rheinhessen wine regions), asparagus, tobacco, maize and fruit. The Rhine Auen — the river's floodplain forests and wetlands — include protected areas of international importance.

Cities and transport

Major cities in or at the edge of the plain include Basel, Mulhouse, Colmar, Strasbourg, Freiburg im Breisgau, Karlsruhe, Heidelberg, Mannheim, Ludwigshafen, Mainz, Wiesbaden and Darmstadt. The plain is a principal European transport corridor, carrying the Rhine navigation, motorways and high-speed rail lines linking France, Germany and Switzerland.

History

The Rhine has formed a cultural and political frontier since Roman times, when the Limes ran along it, and the plain has been contested between France and German states for centuries, notably in Alsace. Nineteenth-century engineering under Johann Gottfried Tulla straightened and channelled the Rhine, reducing flooding but lowering the water table and altering the floodplain ecology; later projects partially restored side channels and retention areas.
