The Cascade Volcanoes (also known as the Cascade Volcanic Arc) are a chain of volcanoes extending about 1,130 km (700 mi) from southwestern British Columbia through Washington and Oregon to Northern California. The arc formed by subduction of the Juan de Fuca and Gorda plates beneath the North American Plate and contains more than a dozen major volcanic centres and thousands of smaller vents.

Major volcanoes

The principal peaks include Mount Rainier (the highest, at 4,392 m), Mount Shasta, Mount Hood, Mount Adams, Mount Baker, Glacier Peak, Three Sisters, Mount Jefferson, Newberry Volcano, Crater Lake (Mount Mazama), Medicine Lake Volcano, Lassen Peak and, in Canada, Mount Garibaldi, Mount Meager and Mount Cayley.

Eruption history

The most destructive recent eruption in the contiguous United States was that of Mount St. Helens on 18 May 1980, when a lateral blast devastated 600 km², killed 57 people and produced the largest debris avalanche in recorded history. Lassen Peak's 1914–1917 eruptions were the only other Cascade eruptions of the 20th century on the US mainland. About 7,700 years ago Mount Mazama erupted cataclysmically, expelling some 50 km³ of material and collapsing to form the caldera now holding Crater Lake. Geologists regard most major Cascade centres as active and potentially hazardous; the arc is monitored by the USGS Cascades Volcano Observatory.

Geology

The arc sits above a subduction zone where oceanic crust descends and partially melts, generating andesitic and dacitic magmas that build the characteristic steep stratovolcanoes. Volcanism began in the region tens of millions of years ago, but the modern High Cascades date mainly from the last several million years. Hazards include lava flows, pyroclastic flows, lahars (volcanic mudflows) — a particular threat to communities in valleys draining Mount Rainier — and volcanic ash.
