North Atlantic oscillation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The North Atlantic oscillation (NAO) is a climatic phenomenon in the North Atlantic Ocean consisting of fluctuations in the difference of atmospheric sea-level pressure between the Icelandic Low and the Azores High. Through this "seesaw" of atmospheric mass, the NAO controls the strength and direction of westerly winds and storm tracks across the North Atlantic, and is the dominant mode of winter climate variability in the region, strongly influencing weather in Europe and eastern North America.

Phases

In the NAO's positive phase, both the Icelandic Low and the Azores High are stronger than usual; the increased pressure gradient drives stronger westerlies, bringing mild, wet, stormy winters to northern Europe and the eastern United States, cold dry conditions to Greenland and northeastern Canada, and dry weather to the Mediterranean. In the negative phase, both centres are weak, the westerlies slacken and shift south, and northern Europe experiences cold, dry winters — as in the severe European winters of 2009–10 and 2010–11 — while the Mediterranean becomes wetter. The NAO index is commonly defined from the normalised pressure difference between stations in Iceland (e.g. Reykjavík or Stykkishólmur) and the Azores, Gibraltar, or Lisbon.

Discovery and variability

The phenomenon was described in the 1920s by Sir Gilbert Walker, who identified the "Northern Oscillation" among global pressure seesaws. The NAO varies from week to week and decade to decade without a fixed period; it trended positive from the 1970s to the mid-1990s, contributing measurably to European winter warming in those decades. Its behaviour interacts with the Arctic oscillation (to which it is closely related), stratospheric polar vortex events, and Atlantic sea-surface temperatures, and seasonal forecasting of the NAO has become a major goal of climate prediction because of its large impacts on energy demand, transport, agriculture, and water resources.

Ecological effects

Because it governs winter temperature, precipitation, and ocean mixing, the NAO synchronises ecological changes across the North Atlantic basin — affecting plankton abundance, fish recruitment (notably cod), bird breeding, and plant phenology on both sides of the ocean.
