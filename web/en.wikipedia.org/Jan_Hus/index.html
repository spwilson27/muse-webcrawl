Jan Hus (Czech: [jan ˈɦus]; c. 1369 – 6 July 1415), sometimes anglicized as John Hus or John Huss, was a Czech theologian, Catholic priest, philosopher and master and rector at Charles University in Prague. He became a church reformer and an inspirer of the Hussite movement, a key forerunner of the Protestant Reformation. After John Wycliffe, the theorist of ecclesiastical reform, Hus is considered the first church reformer.

Life and teaching

Hus was born of peasant stock at Husinec in southern Bohemia. He studied at the University of Prague, becoming dean of the philosophical faculty (1401) and rector (1402–03 and 1409–10). Ordained a priest in 1400, he preached in Czech at the Bethlehem Chapel in Prague, attacking clerical corruption, simony and the sale of indulgences, and promoting communion and preaching accessible to laypeople. Influenced by the writings of John Wycliffe, which were circulating in Bohemia, Hus argued that the Church is the community of the predestined and that Christ, not the pope, is its head.

Conflict and trial

Excommunicated by the Curia in 1411 and forced to leave Prague in 1412, Hus continued writing, including his major Latin work De ecclesia ("On the Church", 1413). In 1414 he travelled to the Council of Constance under a safe-conduct from Emperor Sigismund, but was arrested on arrival, imprisoned and tried for heresy. Refusing to recant opinions he said he had never held, he was condemned, defrocked and burned at the stake on 6 July 1415; his ashes were thrown into the Rhine.

Legacy

Hus's execution ignited outrage in Bohemia and led to the Hussite Wars (1419–1434), in which Hussite armies under leaders such as Jan Žižka repeatedly defeated crusades sent against them. Hus became a Czech national hero; his feast day, 6 July, is a Czech public holiday, and the Jan Hus Memorial (1915) stands in Prague's Old Town Square. In 1999 Pope John Paul II expressed "deep regret" for Hus's death. Hus is also credited with reforming Czech spelling by introducing diacritical marks.
