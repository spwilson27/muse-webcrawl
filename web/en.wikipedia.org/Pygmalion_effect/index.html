Pygmalion effect

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Pygmalion effect is a psychological phenomenon in which high expectations lead to improved performance in a given area and low expectations lead to worse performance. It is a form of self-fulfilling prophecy: the greater the expectation placed upon people, the better they perform. The effect is named after the Greek myth of Pygmalion, the sculptor whose statue came to life, and was illustrated in George Bernard Shaw's play Pygmalion and its adaptation My Fair Lady.

The Rosenthal–Jacobson study

The effect was described by social psychologist Robert Rosenthal, who with Lenore Jacobson reported a 1968 study (Pygmalion in the Classroom) in which teachers were told that randomly selected students had been identified by a test as likely to show dramatic intellectual growth. At the end of the school year, those students showed significantly greater gains in IQ than the control group, especially in the youngest grades. Rosenthal attributed the difference to teachers' subtly different behaviour toward the designated students — in climate, feedback, input, and opportunities to respond.

Mechanisms and evidence

Expectations are thought to operate through differential treatment: warmer socio-emotional climate, more and richer feedback, teaching more material, and giving more opportunities for the expected-to-succeed person to respond. Research has extended the effect to workplaces, the military, and management (where it is sometimes applied as the "Pygmalion in management" effect), and meta-analyses generally find small-to-moderate effects, stronger in some contexts than others. The opposite phenomenon — low expectations depressing performance — is called the Golem effect.

Criticism

The original study drew methodological criticism, including concerns about the IQ test used, outliers driving the results, and replicability; later replications have produced mixed but generally supportive findings. Critics also note that teacher expectations in real classrooms are often largely accurate rather than arbitrary, limiting the room for expectancy effects to operate.
