QWERTY

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

QWERTY ( /ˈkwɜːrti/ KWUR-tee) is a keyboard layout for Latin-script alphabets. The name comes from the order of the first six keys on the top letter row of the keyboard: Q W E R T Y. The QWERTY design is based on a layout included in the Sholes and Glidden typewriter sold by E. Remington and Sons from 1874, and has remained the dominant layout for typewriters and computer keyboards in the English-speaking world.

History

The layout was developed by Christopher Latham Sholes, a Milwaukee newspaper editor and printer, in the late 1860s and early 1870s. Successive revisions separated commonly paired letters to reduce the jamming of typebars in early machines — a popular (though contested) explanation of the arrangement — and placed all the letters of the word "typewriter" on the top row, said to have been convenient for sales demonstrations. Remington's commercial success and the spread of touch-typing training entrenched QWERTY by the early twentieth century.

Alternatives and criticism

QWERTY has often been cited, notably by economist Paul David, as an example of path dependence and market lock-in to an allegedly suboptimal standard. The Dvorak layout (1936) and Colemak (2006) claim greater typing efficiency and comfort, but neither has displaced QWERTY; studies of relative efficiency are disputed. Regional variants include QWERTZ in Central Europe and AZERTY in France and Belgium.

Modern use

QWERTY is implemented in software on virtually all computers and smartphones, where physical typebar constraints no longer apply. National variants add accented letters and currency symbols while keeping the QWERTY letter arrangement for English-language use.
