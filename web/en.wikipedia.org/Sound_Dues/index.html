Sound Dues

Levied byKingdom of Denmark
LocationØresund (the Sound), at Helsingør
Introduced1429, by Eric of Pomerania
Abolished1857, Treaty of Copenhagen

The Sound Dues (Danish: Øresundstolden) were a toll levied by Denmark on foreign ships passing through the Øresund, the strait between Denmark and Sweden connecting the North Sea to the Baltic Sea. Introduced in 1429 by King Eric of Pomerania, they were collected at Helsingør (Elsinore), under the guns of Kronborg Castle, and remained a mainstay of Danish state revenue for more than four centuries.

History

Every foreign vessel entering or leaving the Baltic had to stop at Helsingør, declare its cargo, and pay a duty based on its value; false declarations risked confiscation, and Denmark reserved the right to buy cargo at its declared value, discouraging under-reporting. The Sound Toll Registers, kept almost continuously from 1497 to 1857, are among the most complete maritime trade records in the world and are now a key source for economic historians.

Abolition

Under pressure from maritime powers — especially the United States, which refused to renew its treaty obligations — Denmark agreed to abolish the dues in the Treaty of Copenhagen of 1857, in return for a one-time compensation payment of some 30 million Danish rigsdaler shared among the trading nations.
