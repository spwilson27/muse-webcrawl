Pataliputra

Pataliputra, adjacent to modern-day Patna, was an ancient Indian city originally built by the Magadha ruler Ajatashatru in 490 BCE as a small fort near the Ganges river. It became the capital of successive Indian empires — the Nanda, Maurya, Shunga, and Gupta dynasties — and for centuries was among the largest cities in the world.

Excavated remains of the ancient capital at Kumhrar, Patna.

History

Situated at the confluence of the Ganges and Son rivers, Pataliputra owed its prominence to its command of river trade and its defensible position. Under Chandragupta Maurya around 320 BCE it became the capital of the first great Indian empire; the Greek ambassador Megasthenes described a vast city enclosed by a massive wooden palisade with 570 towers and 64 gates, and the emperor Ashoka later ruled from there, building palaces and convening a Buddhist council. The city flourished again as the Gupta capital in the 4th–6th centuries CE, when the Chinese pilgrim Faxian described its prosperity, before declining after invasions and floods. Excavations at Kumhrar and Agam Kuan have revealed pillared halls, palisade timbers, and other remains of the ancient city.

Legacy

Modern Patna, capital of Bihar, stands on and around the ancient site — one of the oldest continuously inhabited places in the world. A planned modern township nearby took the name Pataliputra, and the ancient name remains a symbol of India's imperial and scholarly past.
