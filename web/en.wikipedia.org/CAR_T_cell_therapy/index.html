CAR T cell therapy

Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cell therapy is a type of cancer immunotherapy in which a patient's own T cells are collected, genetically engineered to express a synthetic receptor that targets cancer cells, expanded in the laboratory, and reinfused into the patient.

How it works

T cells are obtained by leukapheresis and modified, typically using a viral vector, to express a chimeric antigen receptor combining an antibody-derived antigen-binding domain with T-cell signalling domains. Most approved therapies target CD19, a protein on B cells, or BCMA in multiple myeloma.

Approvals and uses

The first CAR T therapy, tisagenlecleucel (Kymriah), was approved by the US FDA in 2017 for certain B-cell leukaemias; axicabtagene ciloleucel (Yescarta) followed for large B-cell lymphoma. Several products are now approved for leukaemias, lymphomas and multiple myeloma.

Side effects

Serious adverse effects include cytokine release syndrome, in which massive immune activation causes fever, low blood pressure and organ dysfunction, and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS). These are managed with drugs such as tocilizumab and corticosteroids.
