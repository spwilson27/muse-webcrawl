Intellectual disability

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Intellectual disability (ID), also known as general learning disability in the United Kingdom and formerly as mental retardation, is a generalized neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by significant impairment in intellectual and adaptive functioning. It is defined by an IQ under 70, in addition to deficits in two or more adaptive behaviours, that negatively affect everyday life and manifest before the age of 18.

Signs and symptoms

Intellectual functions include reasoning, problem solving, abstract thinking, judgment, and learning from experience. Adaptive functioning covers conceptual skills (language, literacy, money and time concepts), social skills (interpersonal skills, responsibility, following rules), and practical skills (personal care, occupational skills, travel, and safety). Severity is commonly classified as mild, moderate, severe, or profound, based primarily on adaptive functioning rather than IQ alone.

Causes

Among the commonest identifiable causes are genetic conditions such as Down syndrome, fragile X syndrome, and phenylketonuria; prenatal exposure to alcohol (fetal alcohol spectrum disorders) and certain infections; problems during birth such as oxygen deprivation; and later causes including traumatic brain injury, infections such as meningitis, and severe malnutrition. In many cases no single cause is identified.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on standardized intelligence testing together with assessments of adaptive behaviour, such as the Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scales. There is no cure; management focuses on education, behavioural and occupational therapy, speech and language support, and treatment of co-occurring conditions such as epilepsy, anxiety, or ADHD. Early intervention and inclusive schooling improve outcomes.

Epidemiology and society

Intellectual disability affects an estimated 1–3 percent of the population worldwide, with males diagnosed more often than females. Historically, people with intellectual disabilities were institutionalized; since the late 20th century the emphasis has shifted toward community living, supported employment, and legal capacity, reflected in the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities. Terminology has repeatedly changed as older terms acquired stigma; Rosa's Law (2010) replaced "mental retardation" in U.S. federal law.
