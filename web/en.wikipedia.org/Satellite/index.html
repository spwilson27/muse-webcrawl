Satellite

A satellite is an object that orbits a larger body. In astronomy the term usually refers to natural satellites — moons, such as Earth's Moon or the moons of Jupiter. In the context of spaceflight, a satellite (or artificial satellite) is an object intentionally placed into orbit, where thousands now serve purposes from communications and navigation to Earth observation and science.

Natural satellites

The Solar System contains hundreds of known moons. They range from tiny irregular captured asteroids to Ganymede, larger than the planet Mercury. Moons formed from accretion disks around young planets or, in the case of Earth's Moon, most likely from the debris of a giant impact.

Artificial satellites

The first artificial satellite, Sputnik 1, was launched by the Soviet Union on 4 October 1957, inaugurating the Space Age. Artificial satellites occupy orbits classified by altitude — low Earth orbit (LEO), medium Earth orbit (MEO), and geostationary orbit (GEO), the last remaining fixed over one point on the equator at about 35,786 km. Major applications include satellite television and broadband (including megaconstellations such as Starlink), GPS and other navigation systems, weather monitoring, remote sensing, and space telescopes. Retired satellites become space debris, a growing hazard managed through tracking and de-orbiting guidelines.
