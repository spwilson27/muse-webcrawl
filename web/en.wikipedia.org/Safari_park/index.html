Safari park

Visitors drive through large enclosures to view free-roaming animals.

A safari park, sometimes called a drive-through zoo, is a zoo-like tourist attraction where visitors can drive their own vehicles or ride park vehicles to observe animals roaming freely in large enclosures, rather than viewing them in small cages. Safari parks are typically larger than conventional zoos and aim to mimic aspects of a wildlife safari.

History

The first modern safari park was Longleat Safari Park in Wiltshire, England, opened in 1966 on the estate of the Marquess of Bath, with lions roaming a drive-through reserve — a novelty that drew enormous publicity and queues. Woburn Safari Park followed in 1970, and the concept spread to North America, Asia, and Australia, with parks such as Lion Country Safari in Florida (1967), the San Diego Zoo Safari Park (1972, as the Wild Animal Park), and Werribee Open Range Zoo near Melbourne.

Features and criticism

Safari parks usually combine drive-through sections for large herbivores and carnivores with walking areas, shows, and amusement rides. Many participate in coordinated captive breeding programs for endangered species. Criticisms have included surplus-animal management, the 2014 cull of a healthy giraffe at Copenhagen Zoo (a conventional zoo, but a controversy that sharpened debate about captivity generally), escapes, and attacks when visitors leave vehicles or open windows. Supporters argue the format gives animals far more space and naturalistic social grouping than traditional cages.
