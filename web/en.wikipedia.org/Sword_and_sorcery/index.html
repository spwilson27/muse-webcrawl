Sword and sorcery

Image placeholder: sword-and-sorcery artwork in the style of Frank Frazetta.

Sword and sorcery (S&S) is a subgenre of fantasy characterised by sword-wielding heroes engaged in exciting and violent adventures, usually involving supernatural elements — sorcery, monsters and evil gods — and personal stakes rather than world-endangering quests. It is distinguished from "high fantasy" by its gritty, fast-paced tone and its morally ambiguous protagonists.

History

The genre originated in the pulp magazines of the 1920s and 1930s, above all in Weird Tales. Its founding figure is Robert E. Howard, whose Conan the Barbarian, Kull of Atlantis and Solomon Kane stories defined its style; other early contributors included Clark Ashton Smith and C. L. Moore. The term "sword and sorcery" itself was coined by Fritz Leiber in 1961, whose Fafhrd and the Gray Mouser stories became classics of the form. A paperback revival in the 1960s and 1970s, edited in part by Lin Carter and L. Sprague de Camp, brought Howard back into print and inspired writers such as Michael Moorcock (Elric of Melniboné) and Karl Edward Wagner (Kane).

Influence

Sword and sorcery strongly influenced role-playing games such as Dungeons & Dragons, heavy metal imagery, and films including Conan the Barbarian (1982). Its planetary cousin is sword and planet.
