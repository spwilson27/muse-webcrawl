Schooner

A gaff-rigged two-masted schooner.

A schooner is a type of sailing vessel defined by its rig: it is fore-and-aft rigged on all of its two or more masts, and the foremost mast (foremast) is no taller than the rear masts. Schooners first evolved in the Netherlands in the 17th century and were developed extensively in North America from the early 18th century, becoming one of the most important vessel types for coastal trade, fishing, and privateering.

Rig description

The classic schooner carries gaff sails behind each mast, often topped by triangular topsails. Because the rig is fore-and-aft, a schooner can be sailed by a much smaller crew than a comparable square-rigged vessel, though it cannot match a square rigger running before strong trade winds. Schooners were built with two to seven masts; the only seven-masted schooner ever built, the steel-hulled Thomas W. Lawson (1902), was also one of the largest sailing ships in history.

History

Fast, weatherly schooners were prized as privateers and blockade runners during the American Revolutionary War and the War of 1812, leading to the clipper-like Baltimore schooners. In the 19th century, fleets of schooners carried fish from the Grand Banks, lumber, coal, and general cargo along the North American coasts and across oceans. The racing fishing schooners of Canada and the United States, such as Bluenose, became national symbols.

Modern use

Steam and motor power displaced commercial schooners in the early 20th century, but the rig survives on yachts, sail-training ships, and traditional "windjammer" cruise vessels. Modern schooner yachts may use gaff or Bermudian (Marconi) rigs.
