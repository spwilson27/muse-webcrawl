Coherentism

Coherentism is a theory of epistemic justification in philosophy. It holds that beliefs are justified not by resting on basic, self-evident foundations, but by belonging to a coherent system of beliefs in which each belief is supported by its relations to the others. Coherentism is the main rival to foundationalism, and is illustrated by Neurath's metaphor of sailors rebuilding their ship at sea, plank by plank, without ever returning to dry dock.

Coherence

Coherence involves more than mere logical consistency: a coherent set of beliefs should be mutually explanatory, comprehensive, and free of unexplained anomalies. Different theorists spell out coherence in terms of consistency, entailment relations, probabilistic support, or explanatory integration. Prominent coherentists have included Laurence BonJour, Donald Davidson, and Keith Lehrer; W. V. O. Quine's image of a "web of belief" is a related picture, although Quine's view is a form of holism rather than a pure justification theory.

Objections

The best-known objection is the isolation objection: a perfectly coherent system of beliefs might be entirely detached from the world, like a well-constructed work of fiction, so coherence alone cannot guarantee truth or justification. BonJour responded by arguing that a coherent system must include cognitively spontaneous (observational) beliefs and that, over the long run, the best explanation of a system's stability is that it corresponds to reality. The alternative systems objection asks how one should choose between two equally coherent but incompatible systems. Some philosophers have proposed hybrid views, such as Susan Haack's "foundherentism", that combine elements of both foundationalism and coherentism.
