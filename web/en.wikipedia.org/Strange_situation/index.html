Strange situation

The Strange Situation is a procedure devised by developmental psychologist Mary Ainsworth in the 1970s to study attachment in children. The procedure involves a series of eight episodes, lasting roughly three minutes each, in which a child aged between 12 and 18 months is observed during separations from and reunions with their caregiver in an unfamiliar playroom, in the presence and absence of a stranger.

Procedure

The infant, caregiver, and a stranger move through a standard sequence: the caregiver and infant enter the room; the stranger enters and interacts; the caregiver leaves, then returns; the caregiver leaves the infant alone; the stranger returns; and finally the caregiver returns. The child's behaviour at reunion — whether they seek comfort, avoid the caregiver, or resist contact — is the key basis for classification.

Attachment classifications

  • Secure attachment (B) — the infant is distressed by separation, seeks contact at reunion, is readily comforted, and returns to play. About 60–65% of middle-class American samples.
  • Insecure-avoidant (A) — the infant shows little distress at separation and avoids or ignores the caregiver on reunion.
  • Insecure-resistant/ambivalent (C) — the infant is highly distressed by separation and seeks contact at reunion but resists comforting and cannot easily return to play.
  • Disorganized/disoriented (D) — added later by Mary Main; the infant shows contradictory, fearful, or disoriented behaviour, often associated with frightened or frightening caregiving.

Significance and criticism

The Strange Situation became the most widely used laboratory measure of infant attachment and generated a large body of research linking early attachment security to later social and emotional development. Critics have noted that classifications vary across cultures — for example, higher rates of avoidant classification in some northern European samples and resistant classification in Japanese samples — and that the procedure captures one relationship at one moment rather than a fixed trait of the child.
