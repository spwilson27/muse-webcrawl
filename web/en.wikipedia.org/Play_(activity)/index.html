Play (activity)

Children engaged in outdoor play

Play is a range of intrinsically motivated activities done for recreational pleasure and enjoyment. Play is commonly associated with children and juvenile-level activities, but may be engaged in at any life stage, and among other higher-functioning animals as well, most notably mammals and birds.

Many prominent researchers in the field of psychology, including Melanie Klein, Jean Piaget, William James, Sigmund Freud, Carl Jung and Lev Vygotsky have erroneously viewed play in children as the means by which children prepare for adult lives; in fact play is valuable for its own sake and is now widely recognised as essential to healthy child development. Different forms of play, whether structured with rules or unstructured, may be beneficial in developing social, cognitive and physical skills. The United Nations High Commission for Human Rights recognises play as a right of every child.

Definitions

The seminal text in the modern study of play is Homo Ludens (1938) by the Dutch historian Johan Huizinga, who characterised play as a free activity standing consciously outside "ordinary" life, absorbing the player intensely, connected with no material interest, and proceeding within its own boundaries of time and space according to fixed rules. Roger Caillois later classified play into four categories: agôn (competition), alea (chance), mimicry (simulation) and ilinx (vertigo), arranged along a spectrum from rule-bound ludus to spontaneous paidia.

Forms of play

Researchers commonly distinguish physical play (such as running, climbing and rough-and-tumble play), object play (manipulating toys and materials), pretend or symbolic play (make-believe, role-play and dress-up), social play with peers, and games with rules, including board games, sports and video games. Solitary play, parallel play and cooperative play describe the social organisation of children's play at different developmental stages.

Development and learning

Play contributes to the development of motor skills, language, problem-solving, emotional regulation and social competence. Vygotsky argued that in play a child behaves "a head taller" than their everyday self, creating a zone of proximal development in which new abilities are rehearsed. Free play has declined in many industrialised countries in recent decades owing to structured schedules, academic pressures and reduced access to outdoor space, prompting public-health and education campaigns to restore play opportunities in schools and neighbourhoods.

Play in animals

Play behaviour is observed in many mammals — especially young ones chasing, wrestling and pouncing — and in birds such as ravens and keas. Ethologists interpret animal play as practice for adult skills such as hunting and fighting, as a means of establishing social hierarchies, and as a source of intrinsic reward. Play signals, such as the play bow in canids, communicate that actions which might otherwise appear aggressive are not serious.
