Shamanism

Shamanism is a range of traditional beliefs and practices that involve a practitioner — a shaman — reaching altered states of consciousness in order to perceive and interact with a spirit world and channel energies into the physical world, typically for healing, divination, and guidance of the community. The word derives from the Tungusic languages of Siberia, where šamán became known to Europeans through Russian explorers in the 17th century.

Practices

Shamans typically enter trance through drumming, dancing, chanting, fasting, or, in some cultures, psychoactive substances. In the shamanic worldview, illness may be attributed to soul loss or intrusion by harmful spirits, which the shaman treats through soul retrieval, extraction, or negotiation with spirits. Shamans also conduct ceremonies marking life transitions and maintain relations with ancestral and nature spirits.

Distribution and study

Practices described as shamanic are found historically in Siberia, Central Asia, Korea, the Americas, and parts of Africa, Europe, and Southeast Asia. Scholars debate whether "shamanism" names a single cross-cultural phenomenon or a collection of locally distinct traditions; anthropologists since Mircea Eliade's Shamanism: Archaic Techniques of Ecstasy (1951) have argued both ways. Contemporary "neoshamanism" adapts shamanic motifs into modern spiritual practice outside their original cultural settings.
