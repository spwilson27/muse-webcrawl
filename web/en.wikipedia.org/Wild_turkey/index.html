Wild turkey

The wild turkey (Meleagris gallopavo) is an upland game bird native to North America, one of the two extant species of turkey and the heaviest member of the order Galliformes. It is the ancestor to the domestic turkey.

Description

Adult males, called toms or gobblers, have dark, iridescent plumage, a fan-shaped tail, a bare red-and-blue head, and a distinctive beard of modified feathers on the chest. Females, or hens, are smaller and duller. Wild turkeys can weigh up to about 11 kg and, despite their size, are capable of short, fast flights.

Behaviour

Turkeys are omnivorous ground feeders, eating acorns, nuts, seeds, insects and small reptiles. They roost in trees at night. During the spring mating season, males gobble and display to attract hens; the species is polygynous.

Subspecies and range

Five subspecies are generally recognised, including the Eastern, Osceola, Rio Grande, Merriam's and Gould's wild turkeys, distributed across the United States, Canada and Mexico.

Conservation

Overhunting and habitat loss reduced wild turkeys to perhaps 100,000 birds by the early 20th century; trap-and-transplant restoration by wildlife agencies raised the population to several millions, making it a celebrated conservation success. The bird is famously served at Thanksgiving dinners in the United States.
