Magret

Magret (French: magret de canard) is the breast of a duck — specifically a mulard or Muscovy duck raised for foie gras production — prepared and served like a steak. The word comes from the Gascon magret, a diminutive of magre, "lean". Magret is a speciality of southwestern France, particularly the Gers and Landes departments, and is prized for its dark red, beef-like meat and thick layer of fat beneath the skin.

History

Although duck breast had long been eaten in Gascony, the modern dish was created and popularised in the 1960s by chef André Daguin of the Hôtel de France in Auch, who had the idea of cooking the fatty breast of the foie-gras duck like a beef steak — seared quickly, skin crisped, flesh left pink. Previously the breast had generally been used for confit or roasted whole. The dish quickly became a signature of nouvelle cuisine and of southwestern French regional cooking.

Preparation

The skin is scored in a criss-cross pattern without cutting the flesh, seasoned, and placed skin-side down in a cold pan; the heat is raised so the fat renders slowly and the skin becomes crisp. The breast is then turned briefly to sear the flesh side, rested, and sliced diagonally. It is served rosé (pink inside) with the rendered fat often used to cook accompanying Sarladaise potatoes. Classic accompaniments include orange sauce (as in canard à l'orange), green peppercorn sauce, or honey and balsamic glazes. A 2013 poll of French diners ranked magret among the nation's favourite dishes.
