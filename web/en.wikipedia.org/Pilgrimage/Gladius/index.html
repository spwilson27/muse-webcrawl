Gladius (Latin: [ˈɡɫadiʊs]) is a Latin word meaning "sword", used primarily to describe the famous short sword that was the primary weapon of the Roman legionary. The Romans adopted the design from the peoples of the Iberian Peninsula during the Punic Wars; the type is therefore sometimes called the gladius hispaniensis, "Spanish sword".

Design

Early gladii were wasp-waisted with a long tapering point, effective for both cutting and thrusting. Later types, such as the Mainz and Pompeii patterns, had shorter points and parallel edges. A typical example weighed about 0.7–1 kg (1.5–2.2 lb) and was carried on the right hip in a scabbard suspended from a belt or baldric.

Use in battle

Legionaries fought in close formation behind the large scutum shield, first throwing heavy pila (javelins) and then closing to stab with the gladius in short, controlled thrusts at the abdomen and thighs. The word itself passed into modern languages, giving rise to terms such as "gladiator" — literally, "swordsman".
