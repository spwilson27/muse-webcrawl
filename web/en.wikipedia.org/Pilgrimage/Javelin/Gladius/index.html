Gladius

The gladius was the short sword that was the primary sidearm of the Roman legionary for several centuries. Derived from the Celtiberian gladius Hispaniensis, it was a double-edged weapon, typically 45–68 cm long, optimised for close-quarters thrusting and cutting behind the shield wall.

Use in battle

After throwing his javelins (pila), the legionary closed with the enemy, using the large scutum to unbalance his opponent and thrusting with the gladius at the abdomen and thighs. The classic Roman sequence – javelin volley, then sword work at close range – made the gladius the emblematic weapon of the legions and gave Latin the word for sword itself.

Types

Recognised types include the Mainz, Fulham and Pompeii patterns, the last with a shorter, parallel-edged blade and triangular point. From the later 2nd century AD the longer spatha gradually replaced the gladius among infantry.
