Henry VIII (28 June 1491 – 28 January 1547) was King of England from 22 April 1509 until his death in 1547. He is best known for his six marriages and for his efforts to annul his first marriage to Catherine of Aragon, which led to the separation of the Church of England from papal authority and the start of the English Reformation.

Break with Rome

When Pope Clement VII refused to annul Henry's marriage, the king, advised by Thomas Cromwell and Thomas Cranmer, had Parliament pass a series of statutes culminating in the Act of Supremacy (1534), which declared him Supreme Head of the Church of England. Between 1536 and 1541 he ordered the dissolution of the monasteries, seizing their wealth and lands.

Marriages and succession

Henry married six times: Catherine of Aragon, Anne Boleyn, Jane Seymour, Anne of Cleves, Catherine Howard and Catherine Parr. His desire for a male heir drove much of his religious and political policy. He was succeeded by his son Edward VI, then by his daughters Mary I and Elizabeth I.

Legacy

Henry's reign transformed England's religion, government and military — he is regarded as a founder of the Royal Navy — but he was also authoritarian, executing tens of thousands, including two of his queens and his former ministers Thomas More and Thomas Cromwell.
