Pilum

TypeHeavy javelin
Used byRoman legionaries
Lengthc. 2 m (6 ft 7 in)
In servicec. 315 BC – AD 350

The pilum (plural pila) was a heavy javelin carried by Roman legionaries, about two metres long, consisting of a wooden shaft joined to a long, slender iron shank topped by a small pyramidal point. Each legionary typically carried two pila, which were thrown in a volley just before the legion closed with the enemy with swords.

Design and effect

The pilum's long iron shank—roughly 60 cm—gave it great penetrating power against shields and armour. Once it struck a shield, the weight of the shaft tended to bend or drag the point down, encumbering the enemy's shield and often forcing him to discard it just before the Roman charge. Ancient authors and modern experiments suggest the shank sometimes bent on impact, which also prevented the weapon being thrown back at the Romans. Whether bending was a deliberate design feature remains debated; the soft iron of the shank and the stresses of impact made it a frequent outcome.

History

The pilum was adopted by the Romans probably in the fourth century BC, possibly influenced by Samnite or Iberian weapons, and remained the legion's standard heavy missile weapon for more than six centuries. Designs varied over time: earlier pila had the shank tanged or socketed into the shaft, later ones were riveted, and in the late empire the pilum was gradually replaced by other throwing weapons such as the spiculum and lighter javelins.
