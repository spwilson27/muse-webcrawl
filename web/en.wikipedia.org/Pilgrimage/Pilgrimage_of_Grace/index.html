Pilgrimage of Grace

DateOctober 1536 – February 1537
LocationNorthern England (Lincolnshire, Yorkshire, Lancashire, Cumberland)
LeaderRobert Aske
ResultRebellion dispersed; leaders executed

The Pilgrimage of Grace was a popular uprising in northern England in 1536–37 against the religious and political policies of King Henry VIII, in particular the dissolution of the monasteries and the activities of his chief minister Thomas Cromwell. Beginning in Lincolnshire in October 1536 and spreading rapidly across Yorkshire and the north, it brought together gentry, clergy and commoners—perhaps 30,000 to 40,000 people at its height—under the leadership of the lawyer Robert Aske, who styled the rising a "pilgrimage" and marched his followers under the banner of the Five Wounds of Christ.

Course of the rising

The rebels occupied York and Pontefract Castle and presented a list of articles demanding, among other things, the restoration of the dissolved monasteries, the removal of Cromwell and reforming bishops, and a halt to enclosures and new taxes. Unable to defeat the pilgrims in the field, the king's commander the Duke of Norfolk negotiated a truce at Doncaster in December 1536 and promised a pardon and a parliament at York to consider the grievances.

Suppression

When fresh disturbances broke out in early 1537, Henry used them as a pretext to abandon his promises. Aske and other leaders were arrested, tried and executed, and roughly 200 people were put to death in the repression that followed. The rising was the most serious rebellion of Henry VIII's reign; its failure cleared the way for the completion of the dissolution and the extension of royal control over the north through the Council of the North.
