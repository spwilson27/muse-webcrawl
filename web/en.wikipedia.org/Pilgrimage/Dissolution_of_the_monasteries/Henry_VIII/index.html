Henry VIII — the Dissolution and the Pilgrimage of Grace

Under Henry VIII, the English crown carried out the Dissolution of the Monasteries (1536–1541), the process by which some 800 religious houses in England and Wales were disbanded and their lands, wealth, and buildings seized by the Crown. Administered by Thomas Cromwell following the break with Rome and the Act of Supremacy (1534), the dissolution followed the Valor Ecclesiasticus survey of church wealth and proceeded through acts of Parliament suppressing first the smaller and then the greater houses.

The Pilgrimage of Grace

Opposition to religious change and the dissolution erupted in the Pilgrimage of Grace (1536–1537), the most serious Tudor rebellion, beginning in Lincolnshire and spreading through Yorkshire under the leadership of the lawyer Robert Aske. Tens of thousands of "pilgrims" demanded the restoration of the monasteries and the removal of Cromwell and heretical bishops. Henry stalled with pardons and promises, then, after a smaller rising at Bigod's rebellion gave a pretext, arrested and executed the leaders, including Aske.

Consequences

The dissolution transferred immense wealth to the Crown, much of it sold to gentry and courtiers, permanently reshaping landholding, education, charity, and the English landscape, and it removed the last institutional counterweight to the royal supremacy in religion.
