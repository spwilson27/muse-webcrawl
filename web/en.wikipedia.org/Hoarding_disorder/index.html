Hoarding disorder

Hoarding disorder is a mental disorder characterised by a persistent difficulty discarding or parting with possessions, regardless of their actual value, together with strong urges to save items and distress associated with discarding them. The resulting clutter congests living areas and substantially compromises their intended use. Hoarding disorder was recognised as a distinct diagnosis in the DSM-5 in 2013, having previously been considered a symptom of obsessive–compulsive disorder (OCD) or obsessive–compulsive personality disorder; it is also listed in ICD-11.

Signs and symptoms

People with hoarding disorder experience an intense perceived need to save items and marked distress at the thought of discarding them. Commonly accumulated items include newspapers, magazines, clothing, bags, books, mail, and paperwork, though almost anything can be hoarded; a related presentation involves acquiring and failing to control large numbers of animals. Clutter progressively fills rooms, hallways, and surfaces until cooking, cleaning, sleeping, and even moving through the home become difficult or unsafe. Consequences include fire and fall hazards, sanitation problems, social isolation, family conflict, eviction risk, and substantial occupational impairment. Insight varies widely: some individuals recognise the problem, while others firmly believe their behaviour is reasonable — a feature that complicates treatment.

Causes

The causes are not fully understood. Hoarding runs in families, and twin and linkage studies suggest a substantial genetic component, sometimes associated with chromosome 14. Neuropsychological research links hoarding to deficits in attention, categorisation, and decision-making, and to abnormal activity in the anterior cingulate cortex and insula — regions involved in salience and error monitoring. Many patients report that symptoms began or worsened after stressful or traumatic events such as bereavement. Hoarding frequently co-occurs with major depression, anxiety disorders, ADHD, and OCD, but is now regarded as largely independent of OCD.

Diagnosis

DSM-5 criteria require: (A) persistent difficulty discarding possessions regardless of value; (B) a perceived need to save items with distress at discarding; (C) accumulation that congests and clutters active living areas, unless others intervene; (D) clinically significant distress or functional impairment; and exclusion of medical causes (e.g. brain injury, Prader–Willi syndrome) and other mental disorders. Specifiers note excessive acquisition (present in most cases) and level of insight. Assessment tools include the Saving Inventory–Revised and the pictorial Clutter Image Rating.

Treatment

The best-supported treatment is a specialised form of cognitive behavioural therapy developed by Steketee, Frost, and colleagues, combining motivational interviewing, skills training in organising and decision-making, graded exposure to discarding and non-acquiring, and cognitive restructuring of beliefs about possessions; it is delivered in individual, group, and peer-facilitated formats (such as the "Buried in Treasures" workshops). No medication is approved specifically for hoarding disorder; antidepressants (SSRIs) may help comorbid conditions, and results for hoarding itself have been mixed. Forced cleanouts without treatment are generally ineffective and can be traumatising.

Epidemiology and history

Community surveys estimate prevalence between roughly 2% and 6% of adults, with symptoms typically emerging in the teens, remaining subclinical for years, and worsening each decade — average treatment-seeking age is around 50. Famous historical cases include the Collyer brothers of New York (found dead amid tons of accumulated material in 1947) and the reclusive "Grey Gardens" Beales. Scholarly study began in earnest in the 1990s with the work of Randy Frost and Tamara Hartl, whose 1996 cognitive-behavioural model reframed hoarding as a disorder of information processing and emotional attachment to possessions.
