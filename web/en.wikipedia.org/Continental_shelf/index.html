Continental shelf

A continental shelf is a portion of a continent that is submerged under an area of relatively shallow water known as a shelf sea. Much of the shelf was exposed as dry land during glacial periods such as the Last Glacial Period. The shelf surrounding an island is known as an insular shelf.

Geography

The continental shelf extends from the coastline seaward to the shelf break, where the continental slope descends steeply toward the deep ocean floor. Shelves average about 65 km in width but vary enormously: the Siberian shelf in the Arctic Ocean extends some 1,500 km, while shelves off mountainous Pacific coasts may be only a few kilometres wide. Shelf waters are usually less than 200 m deep and are among the most biologically productive parts of the ocean, supporting major fisheries.

Geology

Shelves are underlain by continental crust and are typically covered with sediments derived from erosion of the adjacent land. During low sea-level stands, rivers cut valleys across exposed shelves; many submarine canyons on the slope continue these systems. Below the slope lies the continental rise, a wedge of sediment, before the abyssal plain.

Legal definition

Under the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea, a coastal state has sovereign rights over the continental shelf for exploring and exploiting its natural resources. The legal shelf extends to at least 200 nautical miles from the coast, and further where the geological continental margin extends beyond that distance, subject to limits and to submissions reviewed by the Commission on the Limits of the Continental Shelf. These rights underpin offshore oil and gas development.
