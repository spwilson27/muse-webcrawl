Rita Levi-Montalcini (22 April 1909 – 30 December 2012) was an Italian neurobiologist who shared the 1986 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Stanley Cohen for the discovery of nerve growth factor (NGF). She was the first Nobel laureate to reach the age of 100.

Early life and wartime research

Levi-Montalcini studied medicine at the University of Turin under the histologist Giuseppe Levi. Mussolini's 1938 racial laws barred Jews from academic careers, so during the Second World War she built a small laboratory in her bedroom in Turin and continued experiments on the development of nerve fibres in chick embryos, working also from a cottage in the Piedmont countryside while fleeing the German invasion.

Career

In 1947 she accepted a position at Washington University in St. Louis, where she remained for some thirty years. There, with biochemist Stanley Cohen, she isolated nerve growth factor, the first identified growth factor, a protein that directs the growth and survival of neurons. The discovery transformed developmental neurobiology and opened lines of research into conditions including Alzheimer's disease and cancer.

From the 1960s she divided her time between St. Louis and Rome, directing research units of the Italian National Research Council. In 2001 she was appointed a senator for life of Italy. She remained scientifically active into her second century and died in Rome at the age of 103.
