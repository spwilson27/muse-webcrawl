Population exchange between Greece and Turkey

The 1923 population exchange between Greece and Turkey was a compulsory transfer of populations agreed in the Convention Concerning the Exchange of Greek and Turkish Populations, signed at Lausanne on 30 January 1923. Under its terms, roughly 1.2 to 1.5 million Orthodox Christians living in Turkey were required to move to Greece, and some 350,000 to 500,000 Muslims living in Greece were required to move to Turkey. Religion, not language or ethnicity, was the criterion of transfer.

Background

The exchange followed the Greco-Turkish War of 1919–1922, which ended in Greek defeat in Anatolia, the burning of Smyrna, and the flight or expulsion of most of Anatolia's Greek Orthodox population before the convention was even signed. For many refugees the convention merely ratified a fait accompli. Exemptions were granted to the Greeks of Constantinople and the islands of Imbros and Tenedos, and to the Muslims of Western Thrace.

Implementation and consequences

A Mixed Commission supervised by the League of Nations, with Fridtjof Nansen as High Commissioner for Refugees, oversaw the transfer and the liquidation of property. Greece, a country of under five million people, had to absorb a refugee population equal to more than a quarter of its inhabitants, transforming its cities, agriculture — refugees expanded tobacco cultivation in Macedonia — and politics for generations. Turkey lost much of its commercial and artisan class. Although brutal in its human cost, the exchange was later cited, approvingly and critically, as a precedent for resolving ethnic conflict by partition and transfer; it stabilised Greek–Turkish relations sufficiently for the rapprochement of the 1930s under Atatürk and Venizelos.
