Emissivity

Emissivity
Symbolε
Range0 (perfect reflector) to 1 (perfect blackbody)
UnitsDimensionless
Governing lawStefan–Boltzmann law

The emissivity of the surface of a material is its effectiveness in emitting energy as thermal radiation. It is defined as the ratio of the thermal radiation emitted by the surface to the radiation emitted by an ideal black surface (blackbody) at the same temperature. Emissivity, denoted ε, has values between 0 and 1; a true blackbody has ε = 1, while polished metals may have values below 0.1.

Definitions and dependence

Emissivity depends on the material, its surface finish, temperature, the wavelength of the radiation, and the direction of emission. The total hemispherical emissivity integrates over all wavelengths and directions; spectral emissivity and directional emissivity are more detailed quantities. Kirchhoff's law of thermal radiation states that, for a surface in thermal equilibrium, emissivity at a given wavelength and direction equals absorptivity at the same wavelength and direction — so good absorbers are good emitters.

Typical values

Human skin has an emissivity near 0.97 in the thermal infrared, which is why infrared thermometers work well on skin. Water and ice are around 0.96, most paints and oxidized metals lie between 0.6 and 0.95, polished aluminium and silver are near 0.03–0.05, and oxidized steel is roughly 0.8. These variations are the source of errors in radiation thermometry: a pyrometer aimed at a shiny metal surface reads far below its true temperature unless emissivity is correctly set.

Applications

Emissivity is essential in infrared thermography, remote sensing of land and sea surface temperatures from satellites, spacecraft thermal control (where low-emissivity "low-E" coatings reduce radiative heat loss), building insulation, and climate science — Earth's surfaces and atmospheric gases must be characterized by emissivity in radiation-budget calculations.
