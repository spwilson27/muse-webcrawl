Star

A star is a luminous sphere of plasma held together by its own gravity and powered by nuclear fusion in its core. The nearest star to Earth is the Sun. Stars form inside collapsing clouds of interstellar gas, shine for millions to trillions of years depending on their mass, and end their lives as white dwarfs, neutron stars or black holes, in the most massive cases after a supernova explosion. Nearly all the chemical elements heavier than hydrogen and helium were synthesised inside stars or in their deaths.

Formation and life cycle

Stars are born in molecular clouds when regions of gas collapse under gravity, heating until hydrogen fusion ignites. They then spend most of their lives on the main sequence, fusing hydrogen into helium. When core hydrogen is exhausted, a star's fate depends on mass: Sun-like stars swell into red giants, shed their outer layers as planetary nebulae and leave white dwarfs; stars above about eight solar masses fuse progressively heavier elements and explode as core-collapse supernovae.

Classification

Stars are classified by spectral type — O, B, A, F, G, K, M from hottest to coolest — and plotted on the Hertzsprung–Russell diagram of luminosity against temperature. Their properties are governed largely by mass: mass determines luminosity, lifetime and eventual remnant. Many stars are in binary or multiple systems, and a large fraction host planetary systems; over 5,000 exoplanets have been confirmed.

Observation and significance

There are an estimated 200 billion stars in the Milky Way and hundreds of billions of galaxies in the observable universe. Stars have served humanity as navigational aids, calendar markers and objects of myth since prehistory, and their study founded modern astrophysics.
