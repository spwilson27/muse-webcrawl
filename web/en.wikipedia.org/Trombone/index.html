Trombone

A tenor trombone

ClassificationBrass, aerophone
RangeE2–F5 (tenor, typical)
Related instrumentsTrumpet, sackbut, bass trombone

The trombone (German: Posaune, Italian/French: trombone) is a musical instrument in the brass family. As with all brass instruments, sound is produced when the player's vibrating lips cause the air column inside the instrument to vibrate. Nearly all trombones use a telescoping slide mechanism to change the instrument's length and thus its pitch, instead of valves, which are used by most other brass instruments. The trombone derives from the Renaissance sackbut.

Construction

The trombone consists of a cylindrical tube bent into an elongated "S" shape, a cup-shaped mouthpiece, and a large flared bell. The slide has seven positions, each lengthening the tube to lower the pitch by a semitone. Common types include the tenor trombone in B♭, the bass trombone, and the smaller alto trombone; many tenor instruments have an F attachment, a rotary valve adding extra tubing for lower notes and alternate positions.

History

The trombone emerged in the 15th century from the slide trumpet and became a mainstay of civic bands, churches, and courts across Europe. Beethoven was among the first major composers to use trombones in symphonies; by the 19th century the trio of alto, tenor, and bass trombones was a standard part of the orchestra. In the 20th century, trombonists such as Tommy Dorsey, Glenn Miller, and J. J. Johnson made the instrument central to big-band jazz, while virtuoso soloists have expanded its technique with glissandi, multiphonics, and growls.

Repertoire and technique

Because the slide moves continuously, the trombone can execute a true glissando — a smooth slide between notes — an effect exploited in jazz and in comic "sad trombone" cues. Important solo repertoire includes works by Ferdinand David, Launy Grøndahl, and Kazimierz Serocki, and orchestral excerpts by Wagner, Mahler, and Ravel.
