A deal with the Devil (also called a Faustian bargain) is a cultural motif exemplified by the legend of Faust and the figure of Mephistopheles, as well as being elemental to many Christian traditions. In the archetypical tale, a person trades their soul to the Devil in exchange for diabolical favours such as youth, knowledge, wealth, or power. Some traditions hold that the pact is signed in blood.

Origins

The motif draws on the Christian belief in a cosmic struggle between good and evil and appears in medieval exempla long before Faust. A notable early version is the sixth-century legend of Theophilus of Adana, a cleric who, having lost his bishop's favour, sold his soul to regain his position, then repented and was saved by the Virgin Mary. Similar stories attach to saints and sinners throughout the Middle Ages, and the theme coloured the witchcraft accusations of the early modern period, in which an alleged pact with Satan was a standard charge.

The Faust legend

The story of Johann Georg Faust, an itinerant German astrologer and alchemist, was codified in the anonymous Faustbuch of 1587 and dramatised by Christopher Marlowe in The Tragical History of the Life and Death of Doctor Faustus (c. 1592). In Marlowe's play, Faustus signs a twenty-four-year pact with Mephistopheles and is damned despite moments of repentance. Johann Wolfgang von Goethe's two-part Faust (1808, 1832) transformed the tale: his Faust strives perpetually and is ultimately redeemed, and the bargain becomes a wager on whether Mephistopheles can make him content.

Variations in folklore and art

Folktales frequently subvert the bargain: bridges, churches, and castles across Europe are said to have been built by the Devil in a night, with the builder cheated of the promised soul by a trick — a cock crowing before dawn, or an animal sent across first as the "soul" owed. Musicians from Niccolò Paganini and Giuseppe Tartini to bluesman Robert Johnson have been rumoured in legend to owe their virtuosity to infernal pacts, and the motif recurs in works by Thomas Mann, Stephen King, and in the film The Devil's Advocate and the musical Damn Yankees.

Theological views

Historically, the Roman Catholic Church treated belief in diabolical pacts as spiritually dangerous superstition while prosecuting alleged pacts in witch trials; modern scholarship reads the motif as a parable about ambition, short-term gain, and moral compromise, and "Faustian bargain" survives as a common idiom for any trade in which something of great moral value is exchanged for worldly benefit.
