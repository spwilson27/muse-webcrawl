Pancreatic duct

The pancreatic duct, or duct of Wirsung (after its discoverer, the German anatomist Johann Georg Wirsung), is a duct joining the pancreas to the common bile duct. It carries pancreatic juice — containing digestive enzymes and bicarbonate produced by the exocrine pancreas — into the duodenum, where it aids digestion.

Anatomy

The duct begins in the tail of the pancreas and runs through the body of the gland toward the head, receiving smaller tributary ducts along its course and increasing in calibre. In the head of the pancreas it usually joins the common bile duct to form the hepatopancreatic ampulla (ampulla of Vater), which opens into the descending part of the duodenum at the major duodenal papilla. The flow is controlled by the sphincter of Oddi.

Accessory duct

Many people also have an accessory pancreatic duct (duct of Santorini), a remnant of the embryonic dorsal pancreatic bud, which drains part of the head of the pancreas into the duodenum at the minor duodenal papilla. In the congenital variant known as pancreas divisum, the main drainage occurs through this smaller duct, which can predispose to pancreatitis.

Clinical significance

Obstruction of the pancreatic duct by gallstones, tumours, or strictures can cause acute or chronic pancreatitis. The duct can be imaged by endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) and magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP), and dilated ducts are an important diagnostic sign of pancreatic disease, including pancreatic cancer.
