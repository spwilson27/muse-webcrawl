Civil law

Civil law is a term with two connected meanings. In one sense it denotes the legal tradition, derived from Roman law, that is dominant in continental Europe, Latin America, and much of Asia and Africa; in another it denotes the body of private law governing relationships between individuals, as opposed to criminal law.

The civil law tradition

Civil law systems rely primarily on comprehensive written codes, above all a civil code covering obligations, property, contracts, and family law. The tradition descends from the Roman Corpus Juris Civilis of Justinian, revived by medieval scholars, and from the great codifications of the modern era: the French Napoleonic Code of 1804 and the German Bürgerliches Gesetzbuch of 1900. Unlike common law systems, precedent plays a formally limited role, and judges apply codes and statutes, though in practice case law is influential.

Civil (private) law

As a branch of law, civil law regulates disputes between private parties—contracts, torts (delicts), property, inheritance, and family matters—where the usual remedy is compensation rather than punishment. Cases are typically brought by the injured party, decided on a balance of probabilities, and resolved by damages or injunctions.

Distribution

Some 150 countries operate principally civil law systems, including France, Germany, Japan, Brazil, and China, making it the most widespread legal tradition in the world. Many jurisdictions, such as Louisiana, Quebec, Scotland, and South Africa, are mixed systems combining civil and common law elements.
