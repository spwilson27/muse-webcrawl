Dike (construction)

A dike or dyke, in geological usage, is a sheet of rock that is formed in a fracture in a pre-existing rock body. In engineering and everyday usage, however, a dike is an embankment — a long wall or bank built to prevent flooding from the sea or a river, or to hold back water. This article concerns the construction: a dike is a long low crest or embankment constructed of earth, clay, sand or stone, often reinforced, that regulates water levels. It is similar to a levee, though the term levee is more common in American English for river embankments, while dike is entrenched in the Netherlands, where a large part of the country lies below sea level.

Purpose and use

Dikes protect low-lying land (including polders) from inundation by the sea, lakes and rivers. Primary dikes face open water directly and must withstand storm surges and wave attack; secondary or summer dikes provide additional lines of defence. Dike systems are central to flood protection in the Netherlands, northern Germany, Belgium, the Po delta in Italy, Bangladesh, Vietnam's Mekong delta, and parts of China and the United States.

Construction

A typical dike has a core of sand or clay, an impermeable clay cover, and a grassed or armoured slope on the waterside, often protected at the toe by stone revetment or riprap. The landward slope is usually gentler. Design crest heights combine the design water level, wave run-up allowances, settlement and a freeboard margin; Dutch practice after the catastrophic North Sea flood of 1953 led to the Delta Works and statutory safety standards expressed as annual exceedance probabilities (e.g. 1 in 10,000 years for the most critical areas).

Failure

Dikes fail by overtopping, which erodes the inner slope; by piping, in which seepage water carries soil particles out from beneath the dike; by slope instability and sliding; and by erosion of the outer slope. Notable breaches include the 1953 North Sea flood, the 1927 and post-Katrina levee failures around New Orleans (2005), and repeated dike failures on the Yellow River in China historically.

Maintenance and governance

In the Netherlands, regional water boards (waterschappen) — among the oldest democratic institutions in the country — are responsible for dike inspection, maintenance and reinforcement. Modern programmes such as the Dutch Flood Protection Programme continuously strengthen dikes in response to sea-level rise and updated hydraulic statistics.
