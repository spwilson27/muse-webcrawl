Syrinx

The syrinx (from Greek σύριγξ, a panpipe) is the vocal organ of birds. Located at the base of a bird's trachea, where it divides into the bronchi, it produces sounds without the vocal folds of mammals. Sound is produced by vibrations of some or all of the walls of the syrinx caused by air flowing through it, and a system of muscles can modify the tension of the membranes and bronchial openings, allowing many birds — especially songbirds — an extraordinary degree of control over pitch and timbre.

Structure

The syrinx sits in an air chamber surrounded by an interclavicular air sac. In songbirds (oscines), intrinsic syringeal muscles allow the two sides of the syrinx to be controlled independently, so that some birds can produce two unrelated pitches simultaneously. Parrots and songbirds use this ability in complex vocal learning.

Evolution

Fossil evidence, including a mineralised syrinx from the Antarctic bird Vegavis iaai of the Late Cretaceous, indicates the organ evolved in dinosaurs before the origin of birds themselves. The syrinx probably evolved from the laryngeal structures at the top of the trachea found in other reptiles.

In mythology

The name also refers to the nymph Syrinx of Greek myth, who was transformed into river reeds from which Pan fashioned his panpipes.
