The Siouan languages (also Siouan–Catawban) are a language family of North America, spoken historically across a large area of the central continent, from the Great Plains to the Ohio and Mississippi valleys, with an outlying branch (Catawban) in the eastern United States. The family takes its name from the Sioux peoples, whose languages — Lakota, Dakota, and Nakota — are its best known members.

Classification

The Siouan family proper is usually divided into four branches: the Missouri River branch (Crow and Hidatsa), Mandan (often treated as an isolate branch), the Mississippi Valley branch (including the Chiwere languages such as Winnebago and Iowa-Oto, the Dhegiha languages such as Omaha, Ponca, Osage, Kansa and Quapaw, and the Dakotan languages), and the Ohio Valley branch (Tutelo, Saponi and related, now extinct). The Catawban branch in the east (Catawba and Woccon, both extinct) is generally classed as a sister family within Siouan–Catawban.

Features

Siouan languages are typically agglutinative, with complex verb morphology including pronominal affixes marking subject and object. Many have a stative–active alignment in their pronominal systems. Dakotan languages are known for their three-way division corresponding to the historical dialects of the Sioux. Several Siouan languages are severely endangered, though revitalisation programmes are under way, particularly for Lakota and Dakota.

Proto-language

Scholars have reconstructed aspects of Proto-Siouan phonology and grammar through the comparative method. The original homeland of the family is debated, with proposals placing it in the Ohio Valley or the upper Mississippi region.
