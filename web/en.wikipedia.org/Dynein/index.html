Dynein

Dyneins are a family of cytoskeletal motor proteins that move along microtubules within cells, converting the chemical energy of ATP hydrolysis into mechanical work. Dyneins are large protein complexes — cytoplasmic dynein has a mass of about 1.5 megadaltons — and are responsible for a wide range of cellular movements. The name comes from "dyne", a unit of force.

Types

There are two main classes. Cytoplasmic dynein (dynein-1) is found in nearly all eukaryotic cells and drives transport toward the minus ends of microtubules, which are usually anchored near the cell centre; it positions organelles, moves vesicles and chromosomes, and powers retrograde transport in axons. A second specialised form, dynein-2, drives intraflagellar transport toward the base of cilia. Axonemal dyneins form the inner and outer arms of the axoneme and power the sliding of microtubules that bends cilia and flagella, enabling cell swimming and fluid flow over epithelia.

Structure and mechanism

The dynein heavy chain folds into a ring of six AAA+ ATPase domains, from which projects a long stalk ending in a microtubule-binding domain, and a linker element that swings during the ATPase cycle to produce the power stroke. In cells, cytoplasmic dynein is activated by the adaptor complex dynactin together with cargo-specific activating adaptors; alone, mammalian cytoplasmic dynein adopts an autoinhibited "phi" conformation.

Medical relevance

Defects in axonemal dyneins cause primary ciliary dyskinesia, including Kartagener syndrome (with situs inversus), while mutations in cytoplasmic dynein heavy chain (DYNC1H1) are associated with forms of Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease, spinal muscular atrophy and neurodevelopmental disorders. The lissencephaly protein LIS1 is a key regulator of dynein.
