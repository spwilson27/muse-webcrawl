Republic of Genoa

The Republic of Genoa (Italian: Repubblica di Genova; Ligurian: Repúbrica de Zêna) was a medieval and early modern maritime republic from the years 1005 to 1797 in Liguria on the northwestern Italian coast. During the late Middle Ages it was one of the principal commercial powers of the Mediterranean, rivalling Venice, with colonies and trading posts from the Black Sea to Spain.

Rise

Genoa grew prosperous through trade, shipbuilding and piracy suppression alongside Pisa, helping to expel Muslim raiders from Sardinia and Corsica. Genoese fleets supported the First Crusade and were rewarded with quarters in Levantine ports. Victories over Pisa at Meloria (1284) and over Venice at Curzola (1298) — the battle in which Marco Polo was captured and dictated his travels — made Genoa briefly supreme, but the ruinous War of Chioggia (1378–1381) ended in Venetian ascendancy and internal factional strife between noble houses such as the Doria, Spinola, Grimaldi and Fieschi.

Bank of Saint George and empire

The Bank of Saint George (Banco di San Giorgio, founded 1407), one of Europe's first public banks, administered much of the republic's debt and even governed colonies such as Corsica and the Black Sea outpost of Caffa. Genoa controlled Liguria, Corsica (until its sale to France in 1768), and at various times Sardinian, Sicilian, Aegean (Chios, Lesbos) and Crimean possessions. Genoese bankers later financed the Spanish monarchy, prompting the saying that the silver of Potosí enriched Genoa.

Government and fall

After 1528, Andrea Doria's constitutional reforms created an oligarchic republic of noble families with doges elected for two-year terms. The republic survived as a minor but wealthy state until 1797, when Napoleon Bonaparte transformed it into the French client Ligurian Republic; in 1815 the Congress of Vienna annexed Genoa to the Kingdom of Sardinia.
