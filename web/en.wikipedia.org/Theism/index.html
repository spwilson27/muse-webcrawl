Theism is broadly defined as the belief in the existence of at least one deity. In common usage, the term often describes the classical conception of God found in monotheistic religions — an omniscient, omnipotent, omnipresent, and supremely good creator who remains involved with the universe — as in Christianity, Judaism, and Islam. Theism contrasts with atheism (no deities exist), agnosticism (belief is suspended or unknowable), and deism (a creator God who does not intervene).

Types of theism

Monotheism holds that exactly one God exists. Polytheism accepts many gods. Pantheism identifies God with the universe itself; panentheism holds that the universe is in God while God exceeds it. Deism affirms a creator known by reason and nature who does not interfere. Henotheism worships one god without denying others; monolatry worships one god exclusively while acknowledging others exist.

Arguments

Classical philosophy of religion debates arguments for God's existence — the cosmological argument (a first cause), the teleological argument (design), the ontological argument (Anselm), and arguments from religious experience and morality — and arguments against, above all the problem of evil and divine hiddenness. Philosophers such as Aquinas, Descartes, and Kant shaped the terms of this debate.

History of the term

The words "theist" and "theism" entered English in the 17th century (notably in the writings of Ralph Cudworth), originally as a contrast to "atheist." The term has since broadened to cover any belief in god(s), while "classical theism" specifically denotes the philosophical doctrine of God as ipsum esse — being itself — developed in medieval scholasticism.
