Lazarus taxon

In paleontology, a Lazarus taxon (plural Lazarus taxa) is a taxon that disappears for one or more periods from the fossil record, only to appear again later. The term refers to the biblical story of the raising of Lazarus from the dead. The phenomenon is usually explained by gaps in fossilization (the taxon lived in environments or regions with poor fossil records, or in very low numbers), by refugia where a small population survived, or simply by insufficient sampling — not by literal resurrection.

The coelacanth, absent from the fossil record for ~66 million years before a living specimen was found in 1938.

Explanation

Lazarus taxa are a consequence of the incompleteness of the fossil record (the Signor–Lipps effect and related sampling effects). During episodes of mass extinction, Lazarus taxa characteristically vanish from the record at the crisis and reappear in the recovery interval — this pattern helps paleontologists distinguish true extinction from temporary absence. The concept was developed in studies of post-Paleozoic marine faunas by David Jablonski and others in the 1980s.

Related concepts

An Elvis taxon is a look-alike that has evolved convergently to mimic an extinct taxon, and is mistaken for its descendant or survivor. A zombie taxon is a fossil that has been reworked from older sediments into younger deposits, so that it appears to have lived later than it really did. In conservation biology, the term Lazarus species is used for living species rediscovered after having been presumed extinct.

Examples

  • Coelacanths — known from fossils spanning the Devonian to the end of the Cretaceous, they were thought extinct for 66 million years until a living specimen was trawled off South Africa in 1938.
  • Laotian rock rat (Laonastes aenigmamus) — described as a new species in 2005, then recognized as the sole survivor of the family Diatomyidae, thought extinct for 11 million years.
  • Wollemi pine — a conifer genus known only from fossils until living trees were found in an Australian canyon in 1994.
  • Takahe — a flightless New Zealand rail presumed extinct, rediscovered in the Murchison Mountains in 1948.
  • Chacoan peccary — known first as a fossil, found alive in Paraguay in the 1970s.
  • Monoplacophorans — molluscs known only from Cambrian–Devonian fossils until live specimens were dredged in 1952.

Significance

Lazarus taxa complicate estimates of extinction severity and recovery times after mass extinctions, since a group may be recorded as extinct at a boundary only because its representatives were living in unsampled refuges. Their existence also underscores how rarely fossilization occurs for most species.
