History of the Jews in Hungary

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Dohány Street Synagogue in Budapest, the largest synagogue in Europe, built in 1859.

The history of the Jews in Hungary dates from at least the Kingdom of Hungary in the eleventh century, although Jewish communities may have lived in the region under Roman rule. Medieval Hungarian Jews worked as merchants and financiers; their position fluctuated under different kings, with periods of toleration alternating with restrictions and expulsions, as from Buda in 1349 during the Black Death.

Ottoman and Habsburg periods

After the Ottoman conquest of much of Hungary in the sixteenth century, Jews under Turkish rule often enjoyed comparatively favourable conditions. When the Habsburgs reconquered Hungary in the late seventeenth century, many Jews were expelled or massacred, but new immigration followed from Moravia, Bohemia and Poland in the eighteenth century. The 1783 Systematica gentis Judaicae regulatio of Joseph II began a process of gradual legal improvement.

Emancipation and the golden age

Jews supported the Hungarian Revolution of 1848, and full emancipation was enacted in 1867, the year of the Austro-Hungarian Compromise. The following decades were a golden age: Hungarian Jews prospered in commerce, industry, the professions and culture, and Budapest became one of Europe's great Jewish centres, with Jews making up nearly a quarter of the capital's population by 1910. The Neolog and Orthodox movements divided Hungarian Judaism, and the Dohány Street Synagogue (1859) became a symbol of the acculturated Neolog community.

The Holocaust

After the First World War, the numerus clausus law of 1920 restricted Jewish university admission, and under the regency of Miklós Horthy Hungary passed anti-Jewish laws in 1938, 1939 and 1941. Following the German occupation of 19 March 1944, Hungarian authorities collaborated in ghettoization and the deportation of roughly 437,000 Jews from the countryside to Auschwitz in a matter of weeks; most were murdered. In Budapest, tens of thousands were killed by the Arrow Cross in late 1944 and early 1945, while rescue efforts by diplomats such as Raoul Wallenberg saved many. In total, more than 560,000 Hungarian Jews perished.

Postwar period

About 100,000 Jews remained in Budapest after liberation. Under communism religious life was restricted; some Jews emigrated after the 1956 revolution. Since 1989, Jewish religious and cultural life in Hungary has revived, with Budapest home to the largest Jewish community in East-Central Europe, estimated at around 100,000 people.
