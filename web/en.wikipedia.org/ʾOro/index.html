ʾOro

ʾOro is a major deity of traditional Polynesian religion in the Society Islands of French Polynesia. Regarded in many traditions as a god of war and a son of the creator god Taʻaroa, ʾOro rose to prominence through the cult centred on the great marae of Taputapuātea on the island of Raiatea, which became a hub of voyaging, ritual exchange, and religious influence across eastern Polynesia.

The cult of ʾOro

The cult spread from Raiatea to Tahiti and other islands, carried in part by the arioi — a religious society of performers and priests associated with ʾOro who travelled between islands. Sacred objects representing the god, including feathered girdles and images wrapped in sennit, were kept at marae and presented during ceremonies. Traditions differ between islands about his genealogy, consorts, and offspring, reflecting the localised character of Polynesian theology.

History

By the time of sustained European contact in the late 18th century, the worship of ʾOro was politically significant on Tahiti, where rival chiefly factions contested control of his sacred regalia. The cult declined rapidly in the early 19th century as Tahitian rulers adopted Christianity and the traditional marae ceremonies were abandoned, though ʾOro remains an important figure in Polynesian cultural memory and scholarship.
