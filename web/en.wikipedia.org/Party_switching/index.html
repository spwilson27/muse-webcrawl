Party switching

Party switching is the changing of one's political party affiliation, usually by an elected official, from the party under whose banner they were elected to another party or to independent status. In Westminster systems it is often called crossing the floor, after the physical act of moving across the chamber to sit with another party. Party switches can alter parliamentary majorities and are among the most dramatic events in representative politics.

Causes

Switches arise from ideological realignment, personal conflict with party leadership, scandal, ambition for office or reselection, shifts in a politician's electorate, or a party's own policy changes — a classic justification being "I didn't leave the party; the party left me." Some switches are coordinated defections that found new parties or merge factions.

By country

In the United States, party switching has punctuated realignments: Southern conservatives migrated from the Democratic to the Republican Party across the later 20th century. Notable individual cases include Senator Strom Thurmond (1964), Senator Jim Jeffords, whose 2001 switch to independent status flipped control of the US Senate, and Senator Arlen Specter (2009). In the United Kingdom, Winston Churchill famously crossed the floor twice — to the Liberals in 1904 and back to the Conservatives in 1924 ("ratting and re-ratting"). In India, frequent defections prompted the Anti-Defection Law (Tenth Schedule, 1985), disqualifying legislators who voluntarily give up party membership or defy the whip. South Africa briefly legalised floor-crossing windows in the 2000s before abolishing them. In several countries — including New Zealand, whose "waka-jumping" law can expel switchers from Parliament — legislation constrains the practice.

Effects and debate

Supporters of restricting switches argue voters elect a party programme, not merely a person; defenders of the right to switch argue legislators owe constituents their conscience and judgment, and that party discipline should not be absolute. Empirically, many switchers lose the next election, though well-timed switches can secure long careers under new colours.
