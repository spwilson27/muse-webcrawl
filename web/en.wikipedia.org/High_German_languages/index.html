High German languages

The High German languages (German: hochdeutsche Mundarten) comprise the varieties of German in the upland and southern parts of the German-speaking area of central Europe. They are distinguished from Low German by the High German consonant shift, a series of sound changes that affected the southern dialects in the early Middle Ages. "High" refers to the higher elevation of the region where the languages are spoken, not to social status; the standard German language developed primarily from High German varieties.

The consonant shift

The High German consonant shift changed the voiceless stops /p, t, k/ into fricatives or affricates (compare Low German/English maken with High German machen, and English apple with German Apfel). The bundle of isoglosses marking the shift's northern limit includes the famous Benrath line (the maken/machen line) and the Uerdingen line (the ik/ich line). The shift spread furthest in the southernmost (Upper German) dialects and faded progressively northward.

Subdivisions

Central German

Central German includes West Central German (Riparian, Moselle Franconian — including Luxembourgish — and Rhine Franconian, including Palatine and Hessian varieties), and East Central German (Thuringian, Upper Saxon, and Silesian German). These dialects underwent the shift only partially and form a transition zone to Low German.

Upper German

Upper German includes Alemannic (Swiss German, Swabian, Alsatian), Bavarian (spoken in Bavaria, Austria, and South Tyrol), and East Franconian. These dialects applied the consonant shift most completely.

Standard German and Yiddish

Modern Standard German is based mainly on East Central German written usage, standardized through Luther's Bible translation and chancellery practice, combined with features from across the High German area. Yiddish, the historic language of Ashkenazi Jews, also descends from medieval High German varieties, enriched with Hebrew-Aramaic and Slavic elements.
