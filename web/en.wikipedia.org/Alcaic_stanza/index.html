Alcaic stanza

The Alcaic stanza is a Greek lyric verse form named after the poet Alcaeus of Mytilene (c. 625–c. 580 BC), who is credited with its invention or perfection. It was adopted and naturalised in Latin by Horace, who used it in many of his Odes, and it became one of the most prestigious stanza forms of classical poetry.

Structure

The stanza consists of four lines: two Alcaic hendecasyllables, followed by an Alcaic enneasyllable and an Alcaic decasyllable. The characteristic rhythm rises through the first lines and resolves in the shorter, swift final line — a shape Horace exploited for effects of surge and release.

Later use

Imitated in many European languages, the Alcaic stanza appears in German poetry (Klopstock, Hölderlin), in Carducci's Italian Odi barbare, and in English in poems by Tennyson and others, though quantitative metre sits awkwardly in stress-based languages. The French national anthem's poetic ancestry and Swinburne's experiments are also sometimes linked to the form.
