MaterialAmber (single piece)
CreatedEarly Bronze Age, c. 1750–1550 BC
Discovered1856, Hove, East Sussex

The Hove amber cup is a Bronze Age drinking vessel carved from a single piece of amber, found in 1856 in a burial mound at Hove, East Sussex, England. It is one of a very small number of such cups known from northern Europe and is considered one of the most remarkable prehistoric amber objects found in Britain.

Discovery

Workmen digging for a housing development opened a large burial barrow — part of a group sometimes associated with the name Palmeira — and found an oak coffin containing skeletal remains, a dagger, a whetstone and the cup. Amber-working tools appear to have been included in the burial. The find was reported by the antiquary Barclay Phillips, and the cup passed to Hove's museum.

Description and significance

The cup is about 90 mm high and 84 mm across, with a rounded body, an imperforate lug-shaped handle and a slightly flattened base, hollowed and turned from one block of amber, presumably Baltic amber traded into Britain. It belongs to the Wessex Culture of the Early Bronze Age; related amber and shale cups are known from sites such as Clandon in Dorset. Such vessels were likely prestige goods associated with elite burials and long-distance exchange networks.
