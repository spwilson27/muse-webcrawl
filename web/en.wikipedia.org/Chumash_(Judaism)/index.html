Chumash (Judaism)

A Chumash (also Ḥumash; Hebrew: חומש, plural חומשים chumashim, from חמש chamesh, "five") is a printed edition of the Torah — the Five Books of Moses — as opposed to the handwritten Sefer Torah scroll used for ritual reading in synagogue. In modern usage the term usually refers to a codex (bound book) containing the Hebrew text of the Pentateuch together with accompanying material such as the Haftarah readings and commentaries.

Contents

A typical Chumash contains the five books of the Torah — Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, and Deuteronomy — divided into the weekly Torah portions (parashot). Most editions also include the Haftarot (selections from the Prophets read after the Torah portion on Shabbat and festivals) and classical commentaries, most famously that of Rashi. Many editions add Targum Onkelos, an Aramaic translation, and modern translations into the vernacular.

Notable editions

The Soncino Chumash, the Hertz Chumash compiled by Chief Rabbi Joseph Hertz, and the Etz Hayim Humash used in Conservative congregations are widely used in the English-speaking world. ArtScroll's Chumash Mesoras Harav and the Stone Edition are common in Orthodox communities. Gushee and Stone editions present the text in Hebrew with facing English translation and extensive notes.

Etymology

The word derives from chamesh, the Hebrew word for "five," referring to the five books of Moses. In medieval usage, chumash could denote any one of the five books individually copied in codex form.
