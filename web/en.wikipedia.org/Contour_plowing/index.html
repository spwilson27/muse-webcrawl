Contour plowing

Contour plowing
PracticeSoil conservation
PurposeReduce soil erosion and runoff on slopes
RelatedTerracing, strip cropping, keyline design

Contour plowing (also contour ploughing or contour farming) is the practice of plowing and planting across a slope following its elevation contour lines. These contour lines create a water break which reduces the formation of rills and gullies during times of heavy water run-off, giving the water more time to settle into the soil. In contour plowing, the ruts made by the plow run perpendicular rather than parallel to the slope, generally forming roughly level curves around the land, akin to shallow terracing.

Effectiveness

On gentle slopes (roughly 2–8 per cent) contouring can cut erosion substantially compared with up-and-down-slope cultivation, especially when combined with strip cropping — alternating strips of close-growing and row crops along the same contours. Alone it is less effective on steep or very long slopes, where it may be supplemented by terraces or grassed waterways. A similar method is land forming, and the practice was widely promoted by the U.S. Soil Conservation Service in response to the Dust Bowl of the 1930s.

History

Contour cultivation is ancient — practised by Phoenician farmers and in traditional systems worldwide — but was systematized in modern agriculture by conservationists such as Hugh Hammond Bennett in the United States and P. A. Yeomans, whose keyline system refines contour planning to the shape of the whole landscape.
