Coefficient of friction

The coefficient of friction, usually symbolised by the Greek letter μ, is a dimensionless scalar value describing the ratio of the force of friction between two bodies to the force pressing them together.

Static and kinetic friction

The coefficient of static friction μs applies to surfaces at rest: the maximum static friction force is f = μs·N, where N is the normal force. The coefficient of kinetic (sliding) friction μk applies once surfaces are moving, and is usually lower than μs — which is why a heavy object is harder to start moving than to keep moving.

Typical values

Values depend on both materials and surface conditions: rubber on dry asphalt may approach or exceed 0.7, steel on steel is around 0.5–0.8 dry and much lower when lubricated, and steel on ice can be as low as 0.03. Under special conditions coefficients can exceed 1. The laws of dry friction are associated with the work of Leonardo da Vinci, Guillaume Amontons, and Charles-Augustin de Coulomb.
