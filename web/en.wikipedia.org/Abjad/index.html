Abjad

An abjad is a type of writing system where each symbol or glyph stands for a consonant, in effect leaving it to the reader to infer or otherwise supply the appropriate vowel. The term was coined in 1990 by the linguist Peter T. Daniels, who derived it from the first four letters of the Arabic alphabet (ʾa-b-j-d) in one of its traditional orderings, in analogy to the word "alphabet" itself. Abjads contrast with alphabets proper, in which vowels have full status as independent letters, and with abugidas, in which vowels are indicated by diacritics.

Impure abjads

Most historical and modern abjads are "impure": some vowels are indicated by letters with a secondary function (the matres lectionis, such as Arabic alif, wāw and yāʾ, and Hebrew he, vav and yud), or by optional diacritical marks such as Arabic ḥarakāt and Hebrew niqqud. Truly "pure" abjads, with no vowel indication at all, include early Phoenician.

Examples

  • The Arabic alphabet, used for Arabic, Persian, Urdu and many other languages
  • The Hebrew alphabet
  • The Syriac and Aramaic alphabets
  • The ancient Phoenician and Pahlavi scripts

Semitic languages, with their consonantal root morphology, are well suited to abjad writing: the consonantal skeleton of a word usually identifies it, and readers restore vowels from context.
