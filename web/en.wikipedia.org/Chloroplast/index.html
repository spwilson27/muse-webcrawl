Chloroplast
TypeOrganelle (plastid)
FunctionPhotosynthesis
SizeTypically 5–10 µm long
GenomeSmall circular DNA (~120–160 kb)

A chloroplast is a type of organelle known as a plastid that conducts photosynthesis mostly in plant and algal cells. Chloroplasts capture light energy and convert it to chemical energy stored in sugars. They are lens-shaped, typically 5–10 micrometres across, and bounded by a double membrane.

Structure

Inside the inner membrane lies the stroma, a fluid containing enzymes of the Calvin cycle and the chloroplast's own circular DNA and ribosomes. Suspended in the stroma is the thylakoid system: flattened membrane sacs, stacked into grana, whose membranes house chlorophyll, the photosystems, and the ATP synthase of the light-dependent reactions.

Origin

Chloroplasts are descended from free-living cyanobacteria taken up by an ancestral eukaryotic cell in a primary endosymbiosis more than a billion years ago — evidence includes their double membrane, bacterial-type ribosomes, and circular genome. Lineages of algae acquired plastids second-hand by engulfing other algae (secondary endosymbiosis).

Other functions

Beyond photosynthesis, chloroplasts synthesise fatty acids, amino acids and plant hormones, participate in nitrogen and sulfur assimilation, and act as sensors in plant immunity and stress responses. They develop from proplastids and can convert to other plastid types, such as the pigment-storing chromoplasts of ripe fruit.
