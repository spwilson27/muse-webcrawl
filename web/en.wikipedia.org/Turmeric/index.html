Turmeric

Turmeric rhizomes and ground turmeric powder.

Turmeric (Curcuma longa) is a flowering plant in the ginger family, Zingiberaceae, whose rhizomes are widely used as a spice and colouring agent. It is native to the Indian subcontinent and Southeast Asia, and requires temperatures between 20 and 30 °C and a considerable amount of annual rainfall to thrive. The plants are gathered each year for their rhizomes, some of which are replanted for the next season.

The rhizomes are boiled, dried, and ground into a deep orange-yellow powder commonly used as a spice in many Asian cuisines, especially for curries, and as a dye. The principal active constituent is curcumin, a polyphenol that gives turmeric its characteristic colour and has been investigated for possible medicinal properties.

History

Turmeric has been used in India for thousands of years, in cooking, in religious ceremonies, as a cosmetic, and in traditional medicine systems such as Ayurveda. Trade carried it across Asia and to East Africa; Marco Polo remarked on it in the 13th century, comparing it to saffron. It reached Europe through the spice trade but remained less prominent there than in Asia. The name likely derives from the Latin terra merita ("meritorious earth"), referring to the colour of the ground spice.

Culinary use

Turmeric is a foundational ingredient in curry powders and masalas throughout South Asia, and is used in dishes such as dals, rice preparations (giving them a yellow colour), and pickles. In Southeast Asia the fresh rhizome and leaves are also used. Outside Asia it appears in mustard, in some cheese and butter colourings, and in drinks such as "golden milk" or turmeric lattes. It is approved as a food colouring (E100 in the European Union).

Composition and research

Ground turmeric contains roughly 3–6% curcuminoids, chiefly curcumin, along with volatile oils such as turmerone. Curcumin has been extensively studied in laboratory settings for antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity; however, it is poorly absorbed from the digestive tract, is rapidly metabolised, and has not been shown to be an effective treatment for any disease in rigorous clinical trials. Health authorities have warned against marketing claims for curcumin supplements, and researchers have noted that curcumin's chemical instability and assay-interfering behaviour make it a problematic subject of study.

Traditional and cultural uses

In India, turmeric paste is applied in pre-wedding ceremonies such as the haldi ritual, and turmeric-dyed cloth has religious significance in Hindu practice. It has been used historically as a dye for fabrics, though it fades readily, and as a component of traditional cosmetics in parts of Asia. Turmeric solutions have also been used as an acid–base indicator, turning reddish-brown in alkaline conditions.

Production

India is by far the world's largest producer and exporter of turmeric, accounting for the great majority of global output; the city of Erode in Tamil Nadu is a major trading centre, sometimes called "Turmeric City". Other producers include Bangladesh, Pakistan, China, and Myanmar.
