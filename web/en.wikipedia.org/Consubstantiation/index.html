Consubstantiation

Consubstantiation is a Christian theological doctrine that (like transubstantiation) describes the real presence of Christ in the Eucharist. It holds that during the sacrament, the substance of the body and blood of Christ are present alongside the substance of the bread and wine, which remain present. The term is most commonly associated — sometimes inaccurately — with the Eucharistic teaching of Martin Luther and Lutheran churches.

Doctrine

According to the doctrine, the bread and wine do not cease to be bread and wine; rather, Christ's body and blood are "in, with and under" the forms of bread and wine, a phrase used in Lutheran theology to express the sacramental union. Luther illustrated the idea with the analogy of iron in fire: as heated iron remains iron while being united with heat, so the consecrated elements remain while being united with Christ's body and blood.

Relationship to Lutheran teaching

Many Lutheran theologians prefer the term "sacramental union" and reject "consubstantiation" because it suggests a philosophical explanation (a mixing or local inclusion of substances) that the Lutheran confessions avoid. The Formula of Concord (1577) affirmed the real presence while declining to define the mode in Aristotelian terms. Nonetheless, outside Lutheranism the word consubstantiation remains the usual label for the position.

Contrast with other doctrines

Consubstantiation differs from the Roman Catholic doctrine of transubstantiation, in which the substance of bread and wine is converted into the substance of Christ's body and blood while the accidents remain. It also differs from memorialist views, such as Zwingli's, in which the Eucharist is chiefly a commemoration, and from Calvin's doctrine of a spiritual real presence.

History

Ideas resembling consubstantiation can be traced to medieval theologians who taught an "impanation" of Christ in the bread. The terminology became standard in the polemics of the Reformation era and in later comparative theology.
