Samuel Hearne (February 1745 – November 1792) was an English explorer, fur-trader, author and naturalist in the service of the Hudson's Bay Company. He is best known as the first European to make an overland journey from Hudson Bay to the Arctic Ocean, reaching the mouth of the Coppermine River in 1771.

Early life

Hearne was born in London in 1745. His father, a secretary to the Bridge House, died when Hearne was three years old. After a modest education he joined the Royal Navy in 1756 as a captain's servant, and later became a midshipman. He served during the Seven Years' War, including at the bombardment of Le Havre in 1759. In 1766 he left the navy and joined the Hudson's Bay Company as a mate aboard the sloop Churchill.

Overland journeys

Based at Prince of Wales Fort on Hudson Bay, Hearne made three attempts to travel overland to the north-west. His first two expeditions (1769 and 1770) failed because of broken instruments, the loss of his quadrant, and the desertion or incapacity of his guides. On his third attempt he was guided by the Chipewyan leader Matonabbee, whose knowledge of the country proved decisive.

The party set out in December 1770 and travelled across the Barren Lands. In July 1771 Hearne witnessed the attack by his Dene companions on an Inuit encampment at Bloody Falls on the Coppermine River — an event he recorded with evident horror. On 17–18 July 1771 he reached the river's mouth and took formal possession of the shore, becoming the first European known to have reached the Arctic Ocean overland from the east. His survey also showed that no navigable Northwest Passage existed at that latitude.

Later career

Hearne founded Cumberland House in present-day Saskatchewan in 1774, the Hudson's Bay Company's first inland post. He later became governor of Prince of Wales Fort, which he surrendered to the French commander Jean-François de La Pérouse in 1782 without a shot. He retired to England in 1787 in poor health.

Legacy

Hearne's journal, A Journey from Prince of Wales's Fort in Hudson's Bay to the Northern Ocean, was published posthumously in 1795 and remains a classic of exploration literature, valued for its observations of wildlife — including early descriptions of the muskox — and of Chipewyan life. Though his longitude calculations were later criticised, his journey confirmed the geography of the northern coastline and refuted hopes of a strait through the continent in that region.
