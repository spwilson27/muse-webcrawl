Sudeten Germans

Sudeten Germans (German: Sudetendeutsche) were the ethnic Germans living in the Sudetenland, the German-speaking border regions of Bohemia, Moravia, and Czech Silesia, in what became Czechoslovakia. German settlement in these regions dated from the Middle Ages, and by the early twentieth century ethnic Germans made up nearly a quarter of the population of the Bohemian lands.

History

After the First World War, the Sudetenland was incorporated into the new state of Czechoslovakia despite the wishes of many of its German inhabitants to join German Austria. In the 1930s, amid the Great Depression and rising nationalism, the Sudeten German Party under Konrad Henlein gained mass support and became a vehicle for Nazi German pressure on Prague.

Munich Agreement and war

Under the Munich Agreement of September 1938, the Sudetenland was annexed by Nazi Germany. Following Germany's defeat in 1945, the Czechoslovak government, under the Beneš decrees, expelled the overwhelming majority of the Sudeten German population — roughly three million people — to Germany and Austria, in a transfer accompanied by considerable violence and loss of life.

Legacy

Expellee organizations in West Germany long campaigned over the expulsions, and the issue remained a sensitive point in Czech-German relations well into the post-Cold War period.
