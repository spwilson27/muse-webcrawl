In Buddhism, the term anattā (Pali) or anātman (Sanskrit) — "non-self" or "no-soul" — refers to the doctrine that no unchanging, permanent self or essence can be found in any phenomenon. According to the doctrine, since no permanent essence exists, there is no self that underlies a person's experiences; what is conventionally called a "person" is a changing composite of physical and mental constituents. Anattā is the third of the three marks of existence, together with anicca (impermanence) and dukkha (suffering/unsatisfactoriness), and realizing it is considered essential for liberation from saṃsāra.

The chariot: a label for an assembly of parts, with no separate chariot-essence

Doctrine

The Buddha taught, most famously in the Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta — traditionally his second discourse, delivered to the five ascetics at Sarnath — that each of the five aggregates (skandhas) that make up a person — form (body), feeling, perception, mental formations, and consciousness — is not-self: if form (for example) were the self, it would not lead to affliction, and one could command it, "let my form be thus". Since the aggregates are impermanent and arise dependently, none of them, nor any combination, nor anything apart from them, can be a permanent self. The conclusion is drawn for all phenomena: "all dhammas are not-self".

Anattā is a mark to be realized rather than merely an abstract thesis. Buddhist texts treat clinging to a self — the conceit "I am", identity view (sakkāya-diṭṭhi, a fetter abandoned at stream-entry) — as a root of craving and suffering. The doctrine is also not a simple denial that persons exist conventionally: teachings distinguish conventional truth (persons, chariots, and selves as useful designations) from ultimate analysis, a distinction developed systematically in later Abhidhamma and Madhyamaka thought.

Relation to other traditions

The doctrine directly contrasts with the Hindu concept of ātman — an eternal, unchanging self identified in the Upanishads with Brahman — prevalent in the religious milieu in which the Buddha taught. The dialogue Milindapañha gives the classic illustration: the monk Nāgasena explains to King Milinda that "chariot" is merely a name for the arrangement of axle, wheels, pole and other parts; no chariot-essence exists apart from them, and "Nāgasena" is likewise a designation for the aggregates. In Mahayana philosophy, Nāgārjuna extended non-self into the doctrine of śūnyatā (emptiness): all phenomena are empty of intrinsic existence (svabhāva), which is presented as a middle way between eternalism and annihilationism.

Interpretation debates

Scholars and practitioners debate how radically to read anattā: whether the early texts deny any self whatsoever or only deny that the aggregates are the self, leaving the question of a transcendent self undeclared — one of the Buddha's famous silences on the "undeclared questions". Certain later movements, notably the Mahayana tathāgatagarbha (Buddha-nature) literature and the Tibetan shentong school, speak positively of a true nature in terms uncomfortably close to self-language, and their relation to anattā remains contested. In modern times, anattā has been compared with bundle theories of the self in Western philosophy, such as David Hume's, and with contemporary neuroscience findings on the constructed nature of self-experience.
