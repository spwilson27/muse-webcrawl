Harmonic series (music)

The harmonic series is the sequence of harmonics, musical tones, or partials whose frequencies are integer multiples of a fundamental frequency. Pitched musical instruments are usually based on some sort of harmonic oscillator, such as a string or a column of air, which oscillates at numerous frequencies simultaneously. At these resonant frequencies, waves travel in both directions along the string or air column, reinforcing and cancelling each other to form standing waves.

Harmonics and timbre

The relative amplitudes of the harmonics determine the timbre or tone colour of an instrument: a clarinet emphasises odd harmonics, while a flute produces a nearly pure fundamental. The harmonic series explains why notes an octave apart (frequency ratio 2:1) sound so similar, and why the fifth (3:2) and major third (5:4) form consonant intervals.

The series in practice

Starting from a fundamental C, the series ascends: C (fundamental), C (octave), G (perfect fifth), C, E (major third), G, B-flat (a flat seventh), C, D, E, F-sharp, and so on, with the higher partials drifting increasingly out of tune with equal temperament. Natural brass instruments such as the hunting horn play melodies using only these harmonics, selected by the player's embouchure.

Terminology

The first harmonic is the fundamental itself; the second harmonic is the first overtone. Musicians sometimes count "partials" from the fundamental as the first partial, while acousticians may use "overtone" for any partial above the fundamental, whether or not it is an exact integer multiple (in real strings, slight inharmonicity stretches the partials sharp).
