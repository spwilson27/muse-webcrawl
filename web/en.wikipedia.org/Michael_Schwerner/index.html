Michael Schwerner

Michael Henry Schwerner (November 6, 1939 – June 21, 1964) was an American civil rights activist and social worker. He was murdered in Mississippi in 1964, along with fellow activists James Chaney and Andrew Goodman, by members of the Ku Klux Klan during the Freedom Summer voter registration campaign — a crime that became known as the Mississippi Burning murders and helped galvanize support for civil rights legislation.

Early life

Schwerner was born in New York City and raised in a liberal Jewish family. He studied at Michigan State University and Cornell University, then earned a master's degree in social work from Columbia University. He worked in a housing project on Manhattan's Lower East Side before joining the Congress of Racial Equality (CORE).

Freedom Summer

In early 1964 Schwerner and his wife Rita moved to Meridian, Mississippi, where he ran a CORE field office and organized a community center. His effectiveness made him a target: the Klan knew him by the codename "Goatee." During the Freedom Summer of 1964 he helped organize voter registration drives among African Americans in Mississippi.

Murder

On June 21, 1964, Schwerner, Chaney and Goodman were arrested by Neshoba County deputy Cecil Price, released late at night, and then stopped again on a road near Philadelphia, Mississippi, where Klansmen shot all three. Their bodies were found on August 4, 1964, buried in an earthen dam. The national outrage contributed to the passage of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965.

Trials and legacy

In 1967, seven conspirators were convicted of federal civil rights violations in United States v. Price. In 2005, Edgar Ray Killen was convicted of manslaughter in state court. The case inspired the 1988 film Mississippi Burning. In 2014, Schwerner was posthumously awarded the Presidential Medal of Freedom along with Chaney and Goodman.
