Capillary

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A capillary is a small blood vessel, from 5 to 10 micrometres in diameter, and is part of the microcirculation. Capillaries form networks called capillary beds, which connect arterioles and venules and enable the exchange of water, oxygen, carbon dioxide, and nutrients between blood and surrounding tissues.

Structure

The wall of a capillary consists of a single layer of endothelial cells resting on a basement membrane, thin enough to allow substances to pass by diffusion, filtration, and transcytosis. Three structural types are recognised: continuous capillaries, fenestrated capillaries (with small pores, found in the kidneys and intestines), and discontinuous or sinusoidal capillaries (found in the liver, spleen, and bone marrow).

Function

Exchange across capillary walls is governed by hydrostatic and osmotic pressures described by the Starling equation. Capillaries also play roles in thermoregulation and, through pericytes and endothelium, in regulating blood flow to tissues. There are an estimated 10 billion capillaries in the human body, with a total length of tens of thousands of kilometres.
