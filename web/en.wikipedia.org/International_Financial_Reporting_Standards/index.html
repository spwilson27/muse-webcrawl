International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS) are accounting standards issued by the International Accounting Standards Board (IASB), an independent body of the IFRS Foundation based in London. Their stated objective is to bring transparency, accountability and efficiency to financial markets around the world by providing a common global language for company accounts, so that investors and other participants can make economic decisions on an informed basis.

History

The IASB's predecessor, the International Accounting Standards Committee (IASC), was founded in 1973 and issued International Accounting Standards (IAS), many of which remain in force. When the IASB took over in 2001 it began issuing new standards under the IFRS name. The European Union required listed companies to use IFRS from 2005, and adoption spread across most major economies; the United States remains the largest jurisdiction retaining its own GAAP for domestic issuers.

Characteristics

IFRS is described as principles-based rather than rules-based: standards set out objectives and principles, supported by a Conceptual Framework, leaving room for professional judgement. Important standards include IFRS 9 (financial instruments), IFRS 15 (revenue from contracts with customers), IFRS 16 (leases, which brought most leases onto the balance sheet), and IFRS 17 (insurance contracts). A reduced-disclosure version, the IFRS for SMEs standard, serves smaller entities.

Sustainability standards

In 2021 the IFRS Foundation created the International Sustainability Standards Board (ISSB), which issued IFRS S1 and IFRS S2 in 2023, establishing a global baseline for sustainability and climate-related financial disclosures.
