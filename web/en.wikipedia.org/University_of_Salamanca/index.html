The University of Salamanca (Spanish: Universidad de Salamanca) is a Spanish university in the city of Salamanca, in the autonomous community of Castile and León. Founded in 1218 by King Alfonso IX of León, it is the oldest university in Spain and one of the oldest in continuous operation in the world. Pope Alexander IV recognised it in 1255 as a studium generale.

History

The university began as a cathedral school, whose existence can be traced to 1130. Its plateresque main facade, built in the early sixteenth century, is a masterpiece of Spanish Renaissance sculpture; tradition holds that finding the small carved frog on a skull on the facade brings good luck to students. In the sixteenth century the School of Salamanca, led by Francisco de Vitoria, pioneered theories of international law and human rights, and debated the legitimacy of the Spanish conquest of the Americas.

Notable figures

Members of the university included Antonio de Nebrija, author of the first Castilian grammar (1492), the theologian Francisco de Vitoria, the writer Miguel de Unamuno — rector in the early twentieth century — and, as a student, possibly Christopher Columbus consulted its scholars on his proposed voyage. Hernán Cortés and the poet Fray Luis de León were also associated with the institution.

Today

The university is particularly known for teaching Spanish to foreign students, drawing tens of thousands annually. It celebrated its 800th anniversary in 2018. Its library, founded in 1254, holds some 280,000 volumes including medieval manuscripts.
