Élie Metchnikoff (French: [el i mɛtʃnikɔf]; born Ilya Ilyich Mechnikov, 15 May 1845 – 15 July 1916) was a Russian and French zoologist and immunologist, best known for his discovery of phagocytosis and his pioneering work on cellular immunity, for which he shared the 1908 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Paul Ehrlich. He is also regarded as a founder of gerontology and an early advocate of probiotics.

Life

Mechnikov was born near Kharkiv, in present-day Ukraine, studied natural sciences at Kharkiv University, and worked in Germany and Italy before becoming a professor at the University of Odessa. Political turmoil and personal tragedy — including the death of his first wife — drove him from Russia; in 1888 Louis Pasteur gave him a laboratory at the Pasteur Institute in Paris, where he remained for the rest of his life, becoming sub-director in 1895 and taking French nationality. He died in Paris in 1916; his ashes were placed in the Pasteur Institute library.

Phagocytosis and immunity

In Messina, Sicily, in 1882, Metchnikoff observed mobile cells in transparent starfish larvae surrounding a rose thorn he had inserted, and concluded that specialised cells engulf and digest invaders — the process he named phagocytosis. His cellular theory of immunity, developed over the following decades in works such as Immunity in Infectious Diseases (1901), initially rivalled the humoral theory of Ehrlich and others; the two approaches were later recognised as complementary, and the rivals shared the 1908 Nobel Prize.

Ageing and probiotics

In The Nature of Man (1903) and The Prolongation of Life (1907), Metchnikoff coined the term "gerontology" and argued that ageing is driven by toxic bacteria in the gut and chronic phagocyte attack on the body's own tissues. Noting the reputed longevity of Bulgarian peasants who consumed soured milk, he promoted diets of fermented milk containing lactic acid bacteria — the origin of the modern probiotic concept.
