Equivalent dose

This article is about the quantity used in radiological protection. For the related concept of dose measured over a whole body, see effective dose.

Equivalent dose is a dose quantity H representing the stochastic health effects of low levels of ionizing radiation on the human body which represents the probability of radiation-induced cancer and genetic damage. It is derived from the physical quantity absorbed dose, but also takes into account the biological effectiveness of the radiation, which depends on the radiation type and energy. In the international system of units (SI), its unit of measure is the sievert (Sv).

Definition

The equivalent dose HT in a tissue or organ T is defined as the absorbed dose DT,R delivered by radiation type R, multiplied by a radiation weighting factor WR:

HT = WR × DT,R

Where several types of radiation contribute, the equivalent doses from each are summed. The radiation weighting factors are dimensionless numbers selected by the International Commission on Radiological Protection (ICRP) to reflect the relative biological effectiveness of each radiation type. Photons and electrons have a weighting factor of 1, alpha particles a factor of 20, and neutrons a factor that varies continuously with neutron energy, peaking around 20.

History

The concept of equivalent dose evolved from earlier dose concepts. It replaced the older "dose equivalent" defined by the International Commission on Radiation Units and Measurements (ICRU), which used a quality factor based on the linear energy transfer of the radiation in water. The ICRP introduced radiation weighting factors in its 1990 recommendations (Publication 60) and revised the values for neutrons and protons in its 2007 recommendations (Publication 103).

Use

Equivalent dose is used in radiological protection to place limits on exposure of individual tissues and organs. It is an intermediate quantity: by weighting each organ's equivalent dose by a tissue weighting factor and summing, one obtains the effective dose, which expresses the overall detriment to the whole body from a non-uniform exposure. Because the sievert is a large unit for everyday exposures, equivalent doses are often expressed in millisieverts (mSv) or microsieverts (µSv).
