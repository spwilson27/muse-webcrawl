Na+/K+-ATPase


Labelled image: the sodium–potassium pump in the plasma membrane

The Na+/K+-ATPase, commonly known as the sodium–potassium pump (or Na+/K+ pump), is an enzyme (a transmembrane ATPase) found in the membrane of all animal cells. For each molecule of ATP hydrolyzed, it pumps three sodium ions out of the cell and two potassium ions in, against their concentration gradients. It is a P-type ATPase and a classic example of primary active transport.

Discovery and mechanism

The pump was discovered in 1957 by Danish scientist Jens Christian Skou while working on nerves of the shore crab; he shared the 1997 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the discovery. The pump cycles between conformations (the Post–Albers scheme): in the E1 state it binds three intracellular Na+ and ATP, is phosphorylated, and shifts to E2, releasing Na+ outside; it then binds two extracellular K+, dephosphorylates, and returns to E1, releasing K+ inside.

Function

The gradients the pump maintains underlie cell volume regulation, the resting membrane potential, the excitability of neurons and muscle, and secondary active transport of glucose, amino acids and other solutes driven by the sodium gradient. The pump consumes a large fraction of cellular ATP — roughly a third in many cells and up to two-thirds in neurons.

Pharmacology

The pump is specifically inhibited by ouabain and by cardiac glycosides such as digoxin, long used to treat heart failure: inhibition raises intracellular Na+, which slows the sodium–calcium exchanger and raises Ca2+ in cardiac myocytes, increasing contractility. Mutations in its α subunits (ATP1A1–ATP1A3) cause human diseases including rapid-onset dystonia-parkinsonism and alternating hemiplegia of childhood.
