Membrane transport

Membrane transport is the collection of mechanisms that regulate the passage of solutes such as ions and small molecules through biological membranes — lipid bilayers equipped with specific transport proteins. It is essential to homeostasis: cells must take up nutrients, expel wastes, maintain ion gradients and cell volume, and generate electrical signals.

Transport may be passive, following an electrochemical gradient, or active, driven against the gradient by an energy source.

Passive transport

In simple diffusion, small nonpolar molecules such as oxygen and carbon dioxide cross the bilayer directly. Facilitated diffusion uses channel proteins, which form selective aqueous pores (for example aquaporins for water and voltage-gated channels for Na+, K+ and Ca2+), or carrier proteins that bind and shuttle their cargo. Osmosis, the diffusion of water across a selectively permeable membrane, is driven by solute concentration differences.

Active transport

Primary active transport uses ATP directly, as in the sodium–potassium pump (Na+/K+-ATPase), calcium ATPases and the ABC transporter family. Secondary active transport couples the movement of one species down its gradient to another moving up its gradient, through symporters and antiporters such as the Na+/Ca2+ exchanger and sodium–glucose cotransporters.

Vesicular transport

Large particles and bulk fluid cross membranes by vesicular transport: endocytosis (including phagocytosis and pinocytosis) brings material into the cell, and exocytosis releases material, as in neurotransmitter secretion.
