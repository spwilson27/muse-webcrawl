Cotransporter

A cotransporter is an integral membrane protein that moves two or more different molecules or ions across a cell membrane in the same direction. Cotransport is a form of secondary active transport: the energetically favourable movement of one species (typically a sodium ion moving down its electrochemical gradient) drives the movement of another against its gradient.

A cotransporter that moves its substrates in the same direction is also called a symporter; one that moves them in opposite directions is an antiporter (or exchanger).

Mechanism

Cotransporters work by an alternating-access mechanism, exposing binding sites to one side of the membrane at a time. Unlike ion channels, they bind their substrates stoichiometrically and undergo conformational changes, transporting far fewer molecules per second than an open channel.

The driving ion is usually Na+ in animal cells and H+ in plants, fungi and many bacteria.

Examples

Well-known cotransporters include the sodium–glucose linked transporters (SGLT1 and SGLT2) that absorb glucose in the intestine and kidney and are targets of the gliflozin class of diabetes drugs; the Na+/K+/2Cl− cotransporter (NKCC), blocked by loop diuretics such as furosemide; and the Na+–Cl− cotransporter (NCC) of the kidney's distal tubule, the target of thiazide diuretics. The sodium–iodide symporter concentrates iodide in the thyroid gland.
