Sodium–potassium pump

The sodium–potassium pump (also sodium–potassium ATPase or Na+/K+-ATPase) is an enzyme found in the plasma membrane of all animal cells. It uses energy from ATP hydrolysis to export three sodium ions out of the cell and import two potassium ions into it, against both ions' concentration gradients.

By maintaining the resting membrane potential and the sodium gradient, the pump underlies nerve impulses, muscle contraction, nutrient uptake and the control of cell volume. It consumes a large share of a cell's ATP — up to roughly a third in many cells, and more in neurons.

History and mechanism

The pump was identified in 1957 by the Danish scientist Jens Christian Skou, who shared the 1997 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the discovery of this first ion-transporting enzyme. It is a P-type ATPase, phosphorylated during its reaction cycle, alternating between E1 and E2 conformations that expose ion-binding sites to opposite sides of the membrane.

Regulation and pharmacology

Cardiac glycosides such as digoxin and ouabain bind and inhibit the pump from outside the cell; digoxin, derived from foxglove, is still used to increase the force of contraction in heart failure by raising intracellular calcium indirectly through the sodium–calcium exchanger. The pump is also stimulated by hormones including insulin and thyroid hormone, and its activity helps generate body heat in some tissues.
