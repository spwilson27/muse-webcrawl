Active transport

Active transport
FieldCell biology / membrane transport
Energy sourceATP hydrolysis (primary) or ion gradients (secondary)
DirectionAgainst the electrochemical gradient
Key proteinsPumps, carriers, ABC transporters

Active transport is the movement of molecules or ions across a cell membrane from a region of lower concentration to a region of higher concentration — against the concentration or electrochemical gradient. Because this process is thermodynamically unfavourable on its own, it requires an input of energy, usually supplied directly or indirectly by ATP.

Primary active transport

In primary active transport, energy from ATP hydrolysis drives the transporter directly. The classic example is the sodium–potassium pump (Na+/K+-ATPase), which exports three sodium ions and imports two potassium ions per ATP consumed. Other examples include the calcium ATPases of the sarcoplasmic reticulum and plasma membrane, the gastric H+/K+-ATPase targeted by proton-pump inhibitors, and the ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporter family.

Secondary active transport

Secondary active transport uses the electrochemical gradient established by primary transport as its energy source. Symporters move both substances in the same direction — as in the intestinal sodium–glucose cotransporter (SGLT) and the Na+/K+/2Cl− cotransporter of the kidney — while antiporters exchange one substance for another, as in the sodium–calcium exchanger. These mechanisms allow cells to accumulate nutrients and regulate ions and pH without spending ATP at the point of transport.

Significance

Active transport underlies nutrient absorption in the gut, neurotransmitter reuptake, kidney function, and the resting membrane potential, and its transporters are major drug targets.
