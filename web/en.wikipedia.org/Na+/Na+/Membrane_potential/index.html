Membrane potential

Membrane potential (also called transmembrane potential or membrane voltage) is the difference in electric potential between the interior and the exterior of a biological cell. With respect to the exterior of the cell, typical values of membrane potential, normally measured in millivolts, range from about −40 mV to −80 mV, depending on the type of cell.

All animal cells are surrounded by a plasma membrane composed of a lipid bilayer with embedded proteins. The membrane potential arises because ions are distributed unequally across the membrane: the cell interior contains high concentrations of potassium (K+) and negatively charged organic molecules, while the exterior is rich in sodium (Na+) and chloride (Cl−). Selective ion channels allow some ions to cross more readily than others, and ion pumps such as the sodium–potassium pump (Na+/K+-ATPase) actively maintain the gradients by exchanging three sodium ions out of the cell for two potassium ions in, at the cost of ATP.

Resting potential

In a neuron at rest, the membrane is much more permeable to potassium than to sodium, so the resting potential lies close to the potassium equilibrium potential, around −70 mV. The equilibrium potential for a single ion can be predicted from the Nernst equation, while the combined effect of several permeant ions is described by the Goldman–Hodgkin–Katz voltage equation.

Action potentials

When a neuron or muscle cell is stimulated, voltage-gated sodium channels open and Na+ rushes in, depolarising the membrane; the resulting action potential propagates along the cell as a wave of depolarisation followed by repolarisation as potassium channels open. These rapid, transient changes in membrane potential underlie nerve impulses, muscle contraction and hormone secretion.
