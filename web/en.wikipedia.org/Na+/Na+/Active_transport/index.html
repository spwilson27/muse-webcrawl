Active transport

Active transport
TypeCellular membrane transport
Energy sourceATP (primary) or ion gradients (secondary)
Key exampleNa+/K+-ATPase (sodium–potassium pump)

Active transport is the movement of molecules or ions across a cell membrane from a region of lower concentration to a region of higher concentration — against the concentration gradient. Unlike passive transport, active transport requires cellular energy. In primary active transport, energy is derived directly from the breakdown of adenosine triphosphate (ATP); in secondary active transport, the energy stored in an electrochemical gradient set up by primary transport drives the movement of another substance.

The sodium–potassium pump

The best-known primary active transporter is the Na+/K+-ATPase, discovered by Jens Christian Skou in 1957. In each cycle the pump exports three sodium ions and imports two potassium ions at the cost of one ATP molecule, maintaining steep Na+ and K+ gradients across the plasma membrane of animal cells. These gradients are essential for the resting membrane potential, nerve impulse conduction, and nutrient uptake. The cardiac glycosides ouabain and digoxin inhibit the pump.

Secondary active transport

Secondary transporters couple the downhill movement of Na+ (or H+) to the uphill movement of another solute. Symporters move both species in the same direction — for example, the sodium–glucose cotransporter of the intestine and kidney — while antiporters move them in opposite directions, as in the Na+/Ca2+ exchanger.
