Digoxin

Digoxin
Trade names: Lanoxin, others
Class: Cardiac glycoside
Formula: C41H64O14
Elimination half-life: ~36 hours (normal renal function)

Digoxin (also called digitalin) is a purified cardiac glycoside obtained from the leaves of the foxglove plant, Digitalis lanata. It is used in the treatment of heart failure and certain types of irregular heartbeat, particularly atrial fibrillation, and is sold under the brand name Lanoxin among others. It is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines.

Mechanism of action

Digoxin works by reversibly inhibiting the sodium–potassium ATPase pump (Na+/K+-ATPase) in the membranes of heart muscle cells. Inhibition of the pump raises intracellular sodium, which reduces the activity of the sodium–calcium exchanger and thereby increases intracellular calcium. The extra calcium strengthens the force of contraction (a positive inotropic effect). Digoxin also increases vagal tone, slowing conduction through the atrioventricular node, which helps control ventricular rate in atrial fibrillation.

Medical uses

Digoxin is used for symptomatic heart failure in patients who remain symptomatic despite other therapy, and for rate control in chronic atrial fibrillation. Its use declined after trials such as the DIG trial (1997) showed it reduced hospitalisations but did not reduce overall mortality.

Adverse effects and toxicity

Digoxin has a narrow therapeutic index. Toxicity — more likely with hypokalaemia, hypomagnesaemia, hypercalcaemia or renal impairment — can cause nausea, vomiting, visual disturbances (including yellow-green halos), confusion and potentially fatal arrhythmias. Severe poisoning is treated with digoxin-specific antibody fragments (DigiFab). Blood levels and electrolytes are monitored during therapy.

History

The medicinal use of foxglove was described by William Withering in 1785 for dropsy (oedema). Digoxin itself was isolated in 1930 from Digitalis lanata by Sydney Smith at Burroughs Wellcome in London.
