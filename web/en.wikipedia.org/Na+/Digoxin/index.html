Digoxin is a medication used to treat heart failure and certain irregular heart rhythms, particularly for controlling the ventricular rate in atrial fibrillation. It is a purified cardiac glycoside extracted from the woolly foxglove plant, Digitalis lanata, and is among the oldest drugs still in cardiovascular use, with foxglove preparations described for dropsy by William Withering in 1785.

Mechanism of action

Digoxin binds to and inhibits the sodium–potassium pump (Na+/K+-ATPase) in cardiac cell membranes. The resulting increase in intracellular sodium reduces the activity of the sodium–calcium exchanger, raising intracellular calcium and thereby increasing the force of contraction (a positive inotropic effect). Digoxin also increases vagal tone, slowing conduction through the atrioventricular node.

Clinical use and risks

Large trials, notably the DIG trial of 1997, found that digoxin reduces hospitalisations for heart failure but does not reduce mortality. The drug has a narrow therapeutic index: toxicity produces nausea, visual disturbances such as yellow-tinted vision, and potentially fatal arrhythmias, and is worsened by low potassium. Blood levels, kidney function, and electrolytes are monitored during treatment, and severe poisoning can be treated with digoxin-specific antibody fragments.
