Membrane potential

Membrane potential
DefinitionElectric potential difference across a cell membrane (inside relative to outside)
Typical resting valueAbout −30 to −90 mV (neuron ≈ −70 mV)
Main determinantsIon gradients (Na+, K+, Cl−), selective permeability, Na+/K+-ATPase
Key equationsNernst equation; Goldman–Hodgkin–Katz equation

Membrane potential (or transmembrane potential) is the difference in electric potential between the interior and the exterior of a biological cell. All cells maintain such a voltage across their plasma membrane; in excitable cells such as neurons and muscle fibres it changes rapidly during signalling, while in other cells it regulates transport and volume.

Origins

The potential arises from unequal distributions of ions — sodium and chloride concentrated outside, potassium and organic anions inside — maintained by the sodium–potassium pump, together with the membrane's selective permeability through ion channels. At rest the membrane is most permeable to potassium, so the resting potential lies near potassium's equilibrium (Nernst) potential but is pulled slightly toward sodium's by a small sodium leak. The multi-ion steady state is described by the Goldman–Hodgkin–Katz voltage equation.

Resting and action potentials

A typical neuron rests near −70 mV. When voltage-gated sodium channels open, sodium rushes in and the membrane depolarises, generating an action potential; potassium efflux and channel inactivation then restore the resting value. Graded potentials, threshold, and refractory periods all follow from these dynamics, first quantified by Hodgkin and Huxley in the squid giant axon.

Measurement

Membrane potential is measured with intracellular microelectrodes, patch-clamp techniques, and voltage-sensitive dyes or genetically encoded indicators.
