The Gospel of Wealth

"The Gospel of Wealth" is an article written by Andrew Carnegie in June 1889, published in the North American Review, which describes the responsibility of philanthropy by the new upper class of self-made millionaires. It became the founding text of modern large-scale philanthropy.

Argument

Carnegie, the Scottish-American steel magnate, accepted the growing inequality of industrial capitalism as the price of progress but argued that surplus wealth is a trust to be administered by its possessor for the community. "The man who dies thus rich dies disgraced," he wrote. The rich man should live modestly, provide for dependents, and devote his fortune during his lifetime to public purposes — libraries, parks, universities, concert halls — that give the striving poor a "ladder upon which the aspiring can rise", rather than indiscriminate charity or posthumous bequests.

Carnegie's giving

Carnegie practised his doctrine on an unprecedented scale, giving away some $350 million (the bulk of his fortune), funding more than 2,500 public libraries in the English-speaking world, Carnegie Hall, the Carnegie Institution for Science, and the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace. His example influenced later philanthropists from John D. Rockefeller to the signatories of the modern Giving Pledge.

Criticism

Contemporaries and historians have noted the tension between the gospel and Carnegie's labour practices, including the violent Homestead Strike of 1892, and questioned philanthropy as a substitute for fair wages and taxation.
