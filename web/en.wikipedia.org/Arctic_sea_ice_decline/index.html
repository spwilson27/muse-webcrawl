Arctic sea ice decline is the sea ice loss of the Arctic Ocean and its neighbouring seas in recent decades, driven by human-caused global warming. Satellite observations since 1979 show a decline in every month of the year, with the strongest losses at the end of the melt season in September. The Arctic has lost much of its thick, multi-year ice, and the remaining ice cover is younger and thinner.

Observations

September Arctic sea ice extent has declined at a rate of roughly 13% per decade relative to the 1981–2010 average. The lowest extent in the satellite record was measured in September 2012, at 3.39 million km². Ice volume has declined even faster than extent, by an estimated 70% or more since 1979, because thick multi-year ice has been replaced by seasonal first-year ice.

Causes

The decline is caused by Arctic amplification of global warming: the Arctic warms several times faster than the global average, partly through the ice–albedo feedback, in which darker open water absorbs more solar radiation than reflective ice, causing further warming. Changes in atmospheric and ocean circulation also contribute in individual years.

Consequences

Sea ice loss affects Arctic ecosystems and species such as polar bears and walruses that depend on the ice, disrupts Indigenous hunting cultures, and opens the Arctic Ocean to shipping and resource extraction. Loss of reflective ice also amplifies global warming, though melting sea ice itself does not raise sea level because it already floats.

Projections

Climate models project that the Arctic Ocean will experience practically ice-free Septembers (extent below 1 million km²) by mid-century under most emissions scenarios, with the timing depending on future greenhouse gas emissions.
