A sex-determination system is a biological system that determines the development of sexual characteristics in an organism. Most organisms that reproduce sexually have two sexes. The mechanisms vary remarkably across life: in some species sex is fixed by chromosomes at fertilization, in others by the environment during development, and in some by age or social context.

Chromosomal systems

In the XY system, used by most mammals including humans, females have two X chromosomes and males one X and one Y; in humans the SRY gene on the Y chromosome triggers male development. Birds, butterflies, and some reptiles use the ZW system, in which females are the heterogametic sex (ZW) and males are ZZ. In the XO system, found in many insects, females have two X chromosomes and males only one. Haplodiploidy, characteristic of bees, ants, and wasps, produces males from unfertilized (haploid) eggs and females from fertilized (diploid) ones.

Environmental systems

In temperature-dependent sex determination, seen in many reptiles such as turtles and crocodilians, the temperature at which eggs incubate during a critical period sets the sex ratio — a mechanism of concern under climate change. Some fish change sex during life (sequential hermaphroditism): clownfish are protandrous, becoming female with age and rank, while many wrasses are protogynous. Social environment, parasites such as Wolbachia, and even photoperiod can also determine sex in various taxa.

Evolution

Sex-determination mechanisms evolve rapidly, and transitions between genetic and environmental systems have occurred repeatedly. Understanding them illuminates broader questions in evolutionary genetics, development, and conservation biology. See also sex chromosome and sexual dimorphism.
