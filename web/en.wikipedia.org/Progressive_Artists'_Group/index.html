Progressive Artists' Group

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Members of the Progressive Artists' Group, Bombay, c. 1948

The Progressive Artists' Group (PAG) was a group of modern artists in India, formed in Bombay (now Mumbai) in 1947, shortly after Indian independence. Founded by the painter Francis Newton Souza together with S. H. Raza, M. F. Husain, K. H. Ara, H. A. Gade and S. K. Bakre, the group sought to break with the revivalist nationalism of the Bengal School of Art and to establish a distinctly Indian avant-garde engaged with international modernism.

The Progressives are widely credited with founding modern art in India. Their first exhibition, held in Bombay in July 1949, announced a new cosmopolitan idiom that drew on European movements such as Expressionism, Cubism and Surrealism while engaging with Indian subjects, city life and mythology.

History

The group coalesced in the ferment of 1947, the year of Partition and independence. Souza, who wrote the group's manifesto, argued that Indian art had to free itself both from colonial academic realism and from sentimental revivalism. The manifesto declared that the group had no fixed dogma but sought "to paint with absolute freedom for content and technique".

Early exhibitions in Bombay and later in Europe introduced the artists to international audiences. Raza moved to Paris in 1950, and Souza to London the following year; Husain remained in India and became the group's most famous member. Other artists associated with the group included Akbar Padamsee, Tyeb Mehta, Ram Kumar, V. S. Gaitonde and Krishen Khanna, some of whom joined its later circle of influence.

The group effectively dissolved by 1956 as its members dispersed, but its impact on Indian art was lasting: it set the terms for post-independence modernism and launched the careers of artists whose works now command record prices at auction.

Style and themes

Although stylistically diverse, the Progressives shared a commitment to expressive form and an engagement with the realities of modern Indian life. Souza's work featured distorted figures and erotic and religious imagery; Raza moved from expressionist cityscapes toward geometric abstraction rooted in Indian philosophy; Husain developed a modernist vocabulary derived from Indian folk art, classical sculpture and cinema.

Legacy

The Progressive Artists' Group is regarded as the most influential artists' collective in twentieth-century India. Major retrospectives, including exhibitions at the National Gallery of Modern Art in New Delhi and Mumbai, have surveyed its work. The group's members were among the first Indian artists to receive sustained international attention, and their paintings anchor museum collections of South Asian modern art worldwide.
