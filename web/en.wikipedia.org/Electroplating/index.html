Electroplating

Electroplating is a process that uses an electric current to deposit a thin layer of metal onto the surface of an object. The item to be coated is made the cathode of an electrolytic cell; the plating metal, present as dissolved ions in the bath or as a sacrificial anode, is reduced and deposited on its surface. Coatings range from a fraction of a micrometre (gold on electrical contacts) to hundreds of micrometres for hard industrial chrome.

Process

The workpiece is cleaned and often pre-treated, then immersed in a plating bath containing metal salts, acids or alkalis, and additives that control brightness and levelling. Direct current reduces metal cations at the cathode surface, building the coating atom by atom; thickness is governed by current density and time (Faraday's laws of electrolysis). Common plating metals include copper, nickel, chromium, zinc, tin, silver and gold, along with alloys such as brass.

Applications

Electroplating serves decorative purposes (chrome trim, silverware, jewellery), corrosion protection (galvanising complements zinc plating of steel parts), wear resistance (hard chrome on machine components), electrical conductivity and solderability (gold and tin on connectors and circuit boards), and manufacturing — electroforming produces moulds, records stampers and micro-components. The process was commercialised in the 1840s following the work of Luigi Brugnatelli (1805) and the Elkington brothers' patents in Birmingham; some effluents (notably hexavalent chromium and cyanide baths) require strict environmental control.
