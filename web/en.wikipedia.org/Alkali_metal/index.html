Alkali metal

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Group: 1 of the periodic table

Members: Lithium, sodium, potassium, rubidium, caesium, francium

Electron configuration: ns1

The alkali metals consist of the chemical elements lithium (Li), sodium (Na), potassium (K), rubidium (Rb), caesium (Cs) and francium (Fr). Together with hydrogen they constitute group 1 of the periodic table. All are shiny, soft, highly reactive metals with a single electron in their outermost shell.

Properties

Alkali metals are silvery (caesium has a golden tinge), soft enough to cut with a knife, and of low density — lithium, sodium and potassium float on water. They react vigorously, increasingly so down the group, with water to form alkaline hydroxides and hydrogen gas; rubidium and caesium react explosively. Because they tarnish instantly in air and react with moisture, they are stored under oil or in inert atmospheres. They form +1 cations, impart characteristic colours to flames (crimson for lithium, yellow for sodium, lilac for potassium), and show smoothly varying trends in melting point, atomic radius and ionisation energy that make them a textbook example of periodicity.

Occurrence and uses

Being so reactive, the alkali metals never occur free in nature but are abundant as salts: sodium and potassium are the seventh and eighth most abundant elements in the Earth's crust, occurring in seawater, rock salt and minerals such as feldspar. Lithium is essential for rechargeable batteries; sodium is used in street lighting, as liquid coolant in some reactors, and in the production of compounds such as caustic soda; potassium compounds are major fertilisers; caesium defines the second through its atomic-clock transition. Francium is extremely rare and radioactive, with no commercial use.
