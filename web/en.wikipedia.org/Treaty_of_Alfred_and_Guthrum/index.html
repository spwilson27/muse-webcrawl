Treaty of Alfred and Guthrum

[Image: Map of England showing the Danelaw boundary]

The Treaty of Alfred and Guthrum is an agreement between Alfred the Great, King of Wessex, and Guthrum, the Danish king of East Anglia, traditionally dated to the late 880s (around 886–890). The treaty, preserved in a single Old English manuscript, defines the boundary between Wessex and the Danish-controlled territory that became known as the Danelaw, and regulates wergilds (compensation payments) and legal relations between English and Danes.

Background

After Alfred defeated Guthrum at the Battle of Edington in 878, Guthrum withdrew to East Anglia, was baptised (taking the name Æthelstan), and settled his army. The peace that followed formalised the division of England: Wessex and western Mercia remained under English rule, while the Danes held the east and north. Alfred's recovery of London in 886 likely prompted the written treaty.

Provisions

The treaty traces a boundary "up on the Thames, and then up on the Lea, and along the Lea unto its source, then right to Bedford, then up on the Ouse unto Watling Street" — roughly dividing England north-west/south-east and defining the extent of the Danelaw. It sets the wergild of an English thegn and of a Danish hold as equal (eight half-marks of gold), and prescribes procedures for disputes between the two peoples, including oaths and the use of advocates.

Significance

The treaty is one of the earliest surviving English legal texts and a foundational document of Anglo-Saxon law. Its boundary line shaped the administrative geography of England for centuries and marks the formal establishment of the Danelaw. Some scholars debate its exact date and whether it was a formal treaty or a memorandum, but its authenticity is generally accepted.
