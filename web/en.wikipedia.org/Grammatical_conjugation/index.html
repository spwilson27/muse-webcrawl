Grammatical conjugation

In linguistics, conjugation is the creation of derived forms of a verb from its principal parts by inflection. It may be affected by person, number, gender, tense, aspect, mood, or voice. The full set of inflected forms of a verb is called its conjugation paradigm; verbs that follow the normal pattern of their language are regular, while those that deviate are irregular.

Examples

English has relatively sparse conjugation: most verbs distinguish only the third-person singular present (she walks) from the base form, plus past and participial forms. Romance languages are far richer: a Spanish verb such as hablar ("to speak") has dozens of forms across tenses, moods, persons, and numbers. Latin verbs fall into four conjugation classes, each with its own set of endings; Ancient Greek adds a dual number and an optative mood.

Auxiliary verbs and periphrasis

Many languages form some tenses periphrastically, combining an auxiliary verb with a participle or infinitive — English "has walked" and "will walk", French passé composé with avoir or être. The boundary between conjugation proper and periphrastic construction is a matter of analysis, but both belong to the verb's paradigm.

Irregularity

The most frequent verbs in a language tend to be the most irregular, preserving archaic patterns such as English be/am/is/was (suppletion, where forms come from historically different roots), Germanic strong verbs with vowel alternation (sing/sang/sung), and the irregular Latin verb esse "to be".
