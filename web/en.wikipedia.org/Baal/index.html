Baal, from a Northwest Semitic word meaning "lord" or "owner", was a title and honorific applied to various gods in the ancient Near East, and in particular the name of the storm god Hadad in his role as chief deity of the Canaanite city of Ugarit. Baal was regarded as a fertility god responsible for rain and storms, whose annual death and return mirrored the cycle of the seasons.

The Baal Cycle

The principal source for Baal's mythology is the Baal Cycle, a series of Ugaritic poems on clay tablets from the thirteenth century BC. The cycle narrates Baal's conflict with Yam, god of the sea, whom he defeats to win kingship of the gods; the building of his palace on Mount Zaphon by the craftsman god Kothar-wa-Khasis; and his descent into the underworld at the hands of Mot, god of death, followed by his eventual return — interpreted as a myth of seasonal drought and rain.

In the Hebrew Bible

In the Hebrew Bible, Baal appears chiefly as the rival god whose worship by Israelites is condemned by the prophets. The best-known episode is Elijah's contest with the prophets of Baal on Mount Carmel (1 Kings 18), in which the rival priests fail to call down fire while Elijah succeeds. Baal worship is associated especially with the reign of Ahab and his Phoenician queen Jezebel.

Later forms

Local manifestations — Baal Peor, Baal Hermon, Baal Zephon — were worshipped across the Levant. In Carthage, Baal Hammon was the chief god of the Punic pantheon. The name also survived in demonology: Beelzebub derives from Baal-zebub, a Philistine god of Ekron.
