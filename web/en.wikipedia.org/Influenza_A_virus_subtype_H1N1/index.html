Influenza A virus subtype H1N1

ClassificationInfluenza A virus, family Orthomyxoviridae
Surface proteinsHemagglutinin H1, Neuraminidase N1
Notable pandemics1918 "Spanish flu", 2009 "swine flu"

Influenza A virus subtype H1N1 is a subtype of influenza A virus distinguished by its type-1 hemagglutinin (H) and type-1 neuraminidase (N) surface glycoproteins. Strains of H1N1 caused two pandemics — the catastrophic 1918 pandemic and the 2009 swine flu pandemic — and descendants of the 2009 virus continue to circulate as a seasonal influenza strain.

1918 pandemic

The 1918 H1N1 pandemic infected roughly a third of the world's population and killed an estimated 50 million people, with unusually high mortality among healthy young adults. The virus, reconstructed from preserved tissue by researchers in 2005, was an avian-origin H1N1. It circulated seasonally until 1957, when it was displaced by H2N2, and reappeared in 1977, possibly after a laboratory-related release.

2009 pandemic

In 2009 a novel H1N1 of swine origin — a reassortment of human, avian and swine influenza segments — emerged in Mexico and North America and was declared a pandemic by the WHO in June 2009. It caused an estimated 151,000–575,000 deaths in its first year, disproportionately affecting younger people, and replaced the previous seasonal H1N1.

Virology and prevention

Like other influenza A viruses, H1N1 has a segmented, negative-sense RNA genome prone to antigenic drift and shift. Annual influenza vaccines include an H1N1 component modelled on the circulating A(H1N1)pdm09 lineage; antiviral drugs such as oseltamivir are effective against most strains.
