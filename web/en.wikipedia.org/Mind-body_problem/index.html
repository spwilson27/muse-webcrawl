Mind–body problem

The mind–body problem is a longstanding debate in philosophy of mind concerning the relationship between thought and consciousness in the human mind and the body as a physical organism. It asks how mental states — beliefs, desires, sensations, experiences — can exist in, arise from, or interact with a material body and brain. The problem became sharply defined in the work of René Descartes, who held that mind and body are two fundamentally different substances, but versions of it appear in ancient Greek, Indian and Chinese philosophy and it remains central to philosophy, psychology and neuroscience.

Descartes proposed that mind and body interact through the pineal gland.

Dualism

Substance dualism, classically defended by Descartes in the Meditations (1641), holds that the mind is a non-physical, thinking substance (res cogitans) distinct from extended matter (res extensa). Its chief difficulty is the interaction problem, pressed by Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia: how can an immaterial mind cause physical events, and vice versa, without violating the causal closure of physics? Responses by Descartes's successors included Malebranche's occasionalism and Leibniz's pre-established harmony. Property dualism holds instead that there is one substance but two kinds of properties, physical and irreducibly mental; David Chalmers's work and his "hard problem of consciousness" (1995) are modern heirs. Frank Jackson's "Mary's room" and Thomas Nagel's "What is it like to be a bat?" (1974) argue that physical knowledge leaves out subjective experience (qualia).

Monist responses

  • Behaviourism (Gilbert Ryle's The Concept of Mind, 1949) analyses mental states as behavioural dispositions and dismisses the "ghost in the machine".
  • Identity theory (U. T. Place, J. J. C. Smart) identifies mental states with brain states.
  • Functionalism defines mental states by their causal roles, allowing "multiple realizability" in brains or machines — influential in cognitive science and artificial intelligence.
  • Eliminative materialism (Paul and Patricia Churchland) predicts that folk-psychological categories will be replaced by mature neuroscience.
  • Idealism, neutral monism and panpsychism take the mental to be fundamental, or posit a single underlying reality of which mind and matter are aspects.

Contemporary debate

Neuroscience has deepened correlations between brain processes and experience — through lesions, stimulation and imaging — without settling the metaphysical question of why any physical process should be accompanied by experience at all (the "explanatory gap", Joseph Levine). The problem connects to debates about free will, personal identity, animal and machine consciousness, and the nature of causation. Positions such as Donald Davidson's anomalous monism, John Searle's biological naturalism, Daniel Dennett's multiple-drafts model and integrated information theory all stake out territory between the dualist and reductionist poles.
