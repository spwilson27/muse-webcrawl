Socioeconomic status

Socioeconomic status (SES) is an economic and sociological combined total measure of a person's work experience and of an individual's or family's access to economic resources and social position in relation to others. In common use, SES is typically measured as a combination of education, income, and occupation, and is used to study inequalities in health, education, and life chances.

Measurement

No single indicator captures SES. Researchers use composite indices such as the Hollingshead Four-Factor Index or, in Britain, occupational schemes like the NS-SEC. Family SES for children is usually based on parental education, occupation, and household income; area-level deprivation indices aggregate census data for neighbourhoods.

Effects

SES is a strong predictor of outcomes. Higher SES is associated with better physical and mental health (the "status syndrome" documented in the Whitehall studies of British civil servants), higher educational attainment, and lower mortality. Mechanisms include material resources, health behaviors, chronic stress, and access to services. Psychologist Robert Sapolsky and epidemiologist Michael Marmot have emphasized the role of perceived rank itself.

Related concepts

SES overlaps with social class but is a graded, measurable construct rather than a categorical one. It differs from wealth (accumulated assets) and from cultural and social capital in the sense developed by Pierre Bourdieu.
