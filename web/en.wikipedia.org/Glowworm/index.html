Glowworm

Glowworm or glow-worm is the common name for various groups of insect larvae and adult females that glow through bioluminescence. They include the females of lampyrid fireflies (such as the common European glow-worm, Lampyris noctiluca) and the larvae of fungus gnats of the genera Arachnocampa and Orfelia, as well as some click beetles and railroad worms.

Bioluminescence

The glow is produced by a chemical reaction in which the pigment luciferin is oxidised in the presence of the enzyme luciferase, adenosine triphosphate (ATP) and oxygen, releasing light with very little heat. In the common glow-worm, the flightless female climbs onto grass stems on summer nights and glows bright green from organs at the tip of her abdomen to attract the flying males.

Fungus gnat glowworms

The name is especially associated in New Zealand and Australia with the larvae of Arachnocampa luminosa and related species. These larvae live in caves, grottos and sheltered banks, where they build nests of silk tubes and hang down curtains of sticky silk threads. Their blue-green glow attracts flying insects, which become entangled in the threads and are hauled up and eaten. The Waitomo Glowworm Caves on New Zealand's North Island, where thousands of Arachnocampa larvae cover the cave ceiling like a living starry sky, are a famous tourist attraction.

Other glowworms

In North America, the larvae of the fungus gnat Orfelia fultoni — sometimes called "dismalites" — glow with blue light in moist Appalachian ravines. The South American railroad worm (Phrixothrix) is a beetle larva with a row of green lights along its body and red lights on its head, resembling a miniature train at night.
