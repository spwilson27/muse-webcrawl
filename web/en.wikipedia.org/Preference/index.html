Preference

In psychology, economics and philosophy, a preference is a technical term usually used in relation to choosing between alternatives: someone has a preference for A over B if they would rather choose A than B. Preferences are central to decision theory and microeconomics.

In economics

Consumer theory models a consumer as having a preference ordering over bundles of goods, usually assumed to be complete (any two bundles can be compared), transitive (if A is preferred to B and B to C, then A is preferred to C) and reflexive. Where preferences satisfy continuity and other regularity conditions they can be represented by a utility function, and can be drawn as a family of indifference curves. Monotonicity ("more is better") and convexity (preference for balanced bundles) are commonly assumed. Kenneth Arrow and Gérard Debreu developed the axiomatic treatment of preferences, while revealed preference theory, introduced by Paul Samuelson, infers preferences from observed choices.

In psychology and decision theory

Empirical research has shown that human preferences often violate the axioms of rational choice: they can be incomplete, intransitive, framing-dependent and constructed on the fly. Phenomena such as preference reversals, the endowment effect and loss aversion, documented by Amos Tversky, Daniel Kahneman and others, motivated behavioural economics. In decision theory, preferences over outcomes under uncertainty are governed by the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms.

Other uses

The term is used in voting theory (preference ballots and preference aggregation), in philosophy of action and ethics (desire satisfaction theories of welfare), and in animal behaviour studies, where preference tests measure which resources or environments an organism chooses.
