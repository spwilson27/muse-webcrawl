Nenets people

The Nenets (Nenets: ненэй ненэче, nenəj nenəče; Russian: ненцы, nentsy), also known in older literature as Samoyeds, are a Samoyedic ethnic group native to Siberia, in northern Russia. According to the 2021 Russian census, about 49,000 Nenets live in Russia, concentrated in the Yamalo-Nenets Autonomous Okrug and Nenets Autonomous Okrug of the Russian Arctic, with smaller populations in the Taymyr Peninsula and the Komi Republic. They speak the Nenets languages (Tundra Nenets and Forest Nenets), belonging to the Samoyedic branch of the Uralic language family. The Nenets are the largest of the Samoyedic peoples and among the largest Indigenous small-numbered peoples of the Russian North.

History

The Samoyedic peoples originated in southern Siberia, around the Sayan Mountains, and migrated northward along the great rivers — the Ob and the Yenisei — in the first millennium AD. The Nenets came into contact with the Russian state from the sixteenth century onward, following the conquest of Siberia, paying tribute (yasak) in furs. Russian colonization brought trade, taxation, epidemics, and missionary activity; nominal conversion to Orthodox Christianity overlay older shamanic beliefs, which in many places persisted.

Under the Soviet Union, the Nenets were subjected to collectivization of their reindeer herds in the 1930s, the closure of many nomadic schools and trading posts, and boarding-school education for children, which weakened language transmission. At the same time, the Soviet state created the autonomous okrugs that bear the Nenets name and supported literacy in the Nenets language; a written standard based on the Cyrillic alphabet was established in the 1930s. From the 1960s, and especially since the 1980s, large-scale oil and natural gas development on the Yamal and Gydan peninsulas — including the Bovanenkovo gas field and the Yamal LNG project — has transformed the region, bringing wage labour, infrastructure, and conflict over pasture land.

Culture and livelihood

The Nenets are traditionally nomadic reindeer herders, undertaking some of the longest pastoral migrations in the world: brigades on the Yamal Peninsula travel more than a thousand kilometres annually between winter pastures in the forest-tundra near the Ob and summer pastures on the Kara Sea coast, crossing the frozen Ob River in spring and autumn. Reindeer provide transport (in sledges, called narty), meat, hides for clothing and tent covers, sinew for thread, and antlers for tools. The conical tent, the chum, covered with reindeer hides in winter and bark or canvas in summer, remains the mobile dwelling of herding families.

Traditional clothing is sewn from reindeer skin: the men's hooded malitsa and women's yagushka, worn with fur boots (pimy) and decorated with cloth ornament. Fishing — for species such as muksun and nelma — and hunting supplement herding, especially among the Forest Nenets of the forest zone around the Pur and Taz rivers, whose dialect, economy, and way of life differ from those of the Tundra Nenets majority.

Nenets traditional religion is animist and shamanic, centred on a supreme sky god, Num, and on spirits of places, animals, and ancestors; sacred sites, often on hills or islands, held carved wooden idols and received offerings. Epic songs (yarabts) and sung tales (syudbabts) preserved history and law.

Contemporary issues

Today the Nenets are divided between wage employment in towns and the energy industry, settled village life, and commercial reindeer herding, which on Yamal — with herds numbering in the hundreds of thousands — remains one of the world's largest surviving nomadic pastoral systems. Challenges include the impact of industrial development and climate change on pastures, overgrazing and a major anthrax outbreak in 2016 linked to thawing permafrost, and the declining transmission of the Nenets language to younger generations, particularly in settlements, where Russian dominates schooling and media.
