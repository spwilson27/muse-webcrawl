Asbestos is a group of naturally occurring fibrous silicate minerals that have been widely used in construction and industry because of their resistance to heat, fire, chemicals and electricity, and their high tensile strength. The term covers six regulated minerals: chrysotile (white asbestos), of the serpentine family, and five amphibole minerals — amosite (brown asbestos), crocidolite (blue asbestos), tremolite, anthophyllite and actinolite.

Prolonged inhalation of asbestos fibres is now known to cause serious and often fatal diseases, including asbestosis, lung cancer and mesothelioma. Because of these hazards, asbestos use has been banned in more than 60 countries, and its removal from older buildings is a major industry in many others.

History

Asbestos has been used since the Stone Age; Finnish pottery dating to about 2500 BC was strengthened with asbestos fibres. The ancient Greeks and Romans valued its flame-resistant qualities for lamp wicks and burial shrouds — the word derives from the Greek asbestos, meaning "inextinguishable". Industrial mining began in the nineteenth century in Canada, Russia and South Africa, and asbestos became a ubiquitous building material in the twentieth century, used in insulation, roofing, cement, brake linings, floor tiles and shipbuilding.

Health effects

When asbestos-containing material is disturbed, microscopic fibres can become airborne and be inhaled. The fibres penetrate deep into the lungs and can remain there for decades. Diseases typically have latency periods of 20 to 50 years. Asbestosis is a chronic scarring of the lungs; mesothelioma is a cancer of the pleural and peritoneal linings strongly associated with asbestos exposure; asbestos also increases the risk of lung cancer, particularly among smokers.

Regulation and removal

The United States Environmental Protection Agency began restricting asbestos in the 1970s; the European Union banned all forms by 2005. Because so much asbestos remains in buildings, demolition and renovation are strictly controlled, and removal ("abatement") must be carried out by licensed contractors using wet methods, protective equipment and sealed containment. Chrysotile remains in use in some countries, and global production continues, mainly in Russia, Kazakhstan and China.
