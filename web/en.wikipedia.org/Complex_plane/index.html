Complex plane

In mathematics, the complex plane is the plane formed by the complex numbers, with a Cartesian coordinate system such that the horizontal x-axis, called the real axis, is formed by the real numbers, and the vertical y-axis, called the imaginary axis, is formed by the imaginary numbers. A complex number z = x + iy corresponds to the point (x, y).

History

The geometric interpretation of complex numbers was described by Caspar Wessel in 1799, Jean-Robert Argand in 1806, and Carl Friedrich Gauss, whose name is sometimes attached to the plane ("Gaussian plane"). Diagrams of complex numbers in this form are also called Argand diagrams.

Polar coordinates

Each point in the complex plane can also be specified by its distance from the origin — the modulus or absolute value |z| — and the angle it makes with the positive real axis — the argument. This yields the polar form z = r(cos θ + i sin θ) = reiθ, the latter by Euler's formula. Multiplication of complex numbers corresponds to multiplying moduli and adding arguments, giving an elegant geometric picture of rotation and scaling.

Uses

The complex plane is fundamental in complex analysis, control theory, electrical engineering (phasor analysis), signal processing, and fluid dynamics. Adding a point at infinity gives the Riemann sphere, the model of the extended complex plane used in projective geometry and complex dynamics.
