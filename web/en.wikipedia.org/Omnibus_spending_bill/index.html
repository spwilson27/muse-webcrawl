Omnibus spending bill

TypeAppropriations legislation (United States)
CombinesMultiple regular appropriations bills
RelatedContinuing resolution; minibus

An omnibus spending bill is a type of bill in the United States Congress that packages together several of the regular annual appropriations bills into one large measure, often running to thousands of pages, that funds much of the federal government for a fiscal year.

How it arises

Congress is meant to pass twelve separate appropriations bills through the regular order. When it fails to do so by the start of the fiscal year on October 1, it may fund the government temporarily with continuing resolutions and eventually consolidate the unfinished bills into an omnibus. Because such bills are typically "must-pass" to avert a government shutdown, they frequently attract unrelated policy provisions ("riders") and earmarks, and are often negotiated by leadership and released shortly before the vote.

Criticism and alternatives

Critics argue omnibus bills reduce transparency and deliberation, presenting members with an all-or-nothing choice; defenders note they are a pragmatic way to complete the budget process amid gridlock. A smaller package combining only some appropriations bills is nicknamed a "minibus". Notable examples include the Consolidated Appropriations Acts enacted for most recent fiscal years, some exceeding 5,000 pages with additions.
