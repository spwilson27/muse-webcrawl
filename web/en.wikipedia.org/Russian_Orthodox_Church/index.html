The Russian Orthodox Church (Moscow Patriarchate) is the largest autocephalous church of Eastern Orthodox Christianity, claiming jurisdiction over Orthodox Christians in Russia, Ukraine, Belarus, Moldova and much of the post-Soviet space and diaspora. Its history begins with the baptism of Kievan Rus' in 988 under Prince Vladimir of Kiev.

History

Christianity spread among the Rus' from Byzantium; after the Mongol invasions the metropolitan see moved from Kiev to Vladimir and then Moscow. The Russian Church declared de facto independence in 1448, and in 1589 the first Patriarch of Moscow, Job, was enthroned. In the 17th century, Patriarch Nikon's liturgical reforms provoked the schism of the Old Believers (Raskol). Peter the Great abolished the patriarchate in 1721, governing the church through a Holy Synod under state oversight — an arrangement that lasted until 1917.

Under Soviet rule the church suffered severe persecution: clergy were executed or imprisoned, and the number of functioning parishes collapsed by the late 1930s. A limited revival was permitted during the Second World War. After 1991 the church experienced a major resurgence, rebuilding thousands of churches, including Moscow's Cathedral of Christ the Saviour, demolished in 1931 and reconstructed in the 1990s.

Recent controversies

In 2019 the Ecumenical Patriarchate granted autocephaly to the Orthodox Church of Ukraine, prompting Moscow to break communion with Constantinople. Patriarch Kirill's support for Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine strained relations with other Orthodox churches and with many Ukrainian parishes, which moved to distance themselves from Moscow.

Organisation

The church is governed by the Patriarch together with the Holy Synod and periodic councils of bishops. It is organised into dioceses, deaneries and parishes, with monasticism — centred on lavras such as the Trinity Lavra of St. Sergius — playing a central spiritual role. It operates seminaries, academies and extensive publishing and social-service work.
