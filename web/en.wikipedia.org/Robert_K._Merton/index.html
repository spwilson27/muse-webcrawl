Robert King Merton (born Meyer Robert Schkolnick; 4 July 1910 – 23 February 2003) was an American sociologist who is considered a founding figure in modern sociology, and a major contributor to the subfield of criminology. He served as the Giddings Professor of Sociology at Columbia University, where he spent most of his career, and in 1994 became the first sociologist to receive the U.S. National Medal of Science.

Contributions

Merton coined or popularized many concepts now standard in social science and everyday language, including self-fulfilling prophecy, role model, unintended consequences, and reference group. His essay "Social Structure and Anomie" (1938) founded strain theory, explaining deviance as a product of the disjunction between culturally approved goals and legitimate means of achieving them. His advocacy of "theories of the middle range" shaped empirical sociological research.

In the sociology of science, Merton formulated the norms of science (the "Mertonian norms" or CUDOS: communalism, universalism, disinterestedness, organized scepticism) and described the Matthew effect, whereby eminent scientists receive disproportionate credit for joint work. His collected studies appeared in Social Theory and Social Structure (1949, expanded 1968). His son, Robert C. Merton, is the Nobel laureate economist.
