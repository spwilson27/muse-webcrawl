Orrery

An orrery is a mechanical model of the Solar System that illustrates or predicts the relative positions and motions of the planets and moons, usually according to the heliocentric model. Driven by clockwork gearing, an orrery's arms rotate model planets around a central sun at rates proportional to their orbital periods. A device showing only the Earth, Moon, and Sun is called a tellurian; one showing Earth and Moon alone is a lunarium.

History

The Antikythera mechanism of ancient Greece was an early geared astronomical calculator. The modern orrery emerged in the early 18th century: around 1704–1709 George Graham and Thomas Tompion built an early model, and the instrument was named after Charles Boyle, 4th Earl of Orrery, for whom a copy was made. John Rowley produced an orrery around 1712, and examples became fashionable teaching and display pieces. Eise Eisinga built a famous ceiling-mounted planetarium in Franeker, Netherlands, between 1774 and 1781, still operating today.

Construction

Orreries sacrifice accurate scale (both of size and distance) for legibility; gear ratios approximate orbital periods, such as Saturn's roughly 29.5 Earth years. Grand orreries may include all planets known at their construction and elaborate gilt decoration. Human orreries, in which people stand on marked disks and walk the orbits, have been built at several museums.
