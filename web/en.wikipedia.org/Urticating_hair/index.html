Urticating hair

Urticating hairs or urticating bristles are one of the primary defense mechanisms used by numerous plants, almost all New World tarantulas, and various lepidopteran caterpillars. Urtica is Latin for "nettle" (stinging nettles are in the genus Urtica), and bristles that urticate are characteristic of this type of plant, and many other plants in several families.

In tarantulas

Most New World tarantulas possess urticating hairs (setae) on the abdomen. When threatened, the spider kicks or brushes these barbed hairs into the air toward a predator using its hind legs. The hairs embed in skin or mucous membranes, causing itching, rashes, and irritation; if inhaled or lodged in the eyes they can cause serious injury, including a condition called ophthalmia nodosa in humans. Old World tarantulas lack these hairs and rely more on biting and venom. Scientists have classified tarantula urticating setae into seven types (I–VII), differing in size, shape, and mode of release — some are kicked loose, while others transfer on contact.

In plants and caterpillars

In plants, urticating hairs are hollow, needle-like trichomes that inject irritating chemicals such as histamine, acetylcholine, and formic acid — most famously in stinging nettles. Certain caterpillars, such as those of the brown-tail moth, bear detachable barbed hairs that can become airborne and cause dermatitis and respiratory symptoms in people nearby.

Human effects

Skin reactions to urticating hairs are typically a mechanical and inflammatory dermatitis treated by removing embedded hairs (for example with adhesive tape) and symptomatic care. Tarantula keepers are advised to use gloves and eye protection when handling species known to flick hairs.
