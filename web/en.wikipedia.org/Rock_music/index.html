Rock music

Stylistic originsRock and roll, rhythm and blues, blues, country, folk
Cultural origins1950s, United States and United Kingdom
Typical instrumentsElectric guitar, bass guitar, drums, vocals, keyboards
SubgenresHard rock, punk rock, heavy metal, alternative rock, progressive rock, indie rock, glam rock, psychedelic rock

Rock music is a broad genre of popular music that originated as "rock and roll" in the United States in the late 1940s and early 1950s, developing into a range of different styles by the mid-1960s, particularly in the United States and the United Kingdom. It has its roots in rock and roll, a style that drew directly from the black musical genres of blues, rhythm and blues, and from country music. Typically centred on the electric guitar — often as part of a group with electric bass, drums, and one or more singers — rock has been the dominant form of popular music worldwide for much of the period since the 1960s.

Origins: rock and roll

Rock and roll emerged from the fusion of African-American rhythm and blues with country and gospel, amplified by the spread of the electric guitar and the 45 rpm single. Artists such as Chuck Berry, Little Richard, Elvis Presley, Buddy Holly, and Bo Diddley established its sound in the 1950s: a strong backbeat, twelve-bar blues structures, and lyrics aimed at a newly affluent teenage audience. By the end of the decade the first wave had faltered, but the music had transformed youth culture on both sides of the Atlantic.

The 1960s: the British Invasion and expansion

In 1964 the Beatles' arrival in the United States launched the "British Invasion," followed by the Rolling Stones, the Who, the Kinks, and others who had absorbed American blues and R&B and sold it back transformed. Rock rapidly diversified: Bob Dylan and the folk-rock movement brought literary ambition; the Beach Boys and the Beatles pushed studio experimentation toward the album-as-artwork, exemplified by Pet Sounds and Sgt. Pepper's Lonely Hearts Club Band (both 1966–67). Psychedelic rock, blues rock, and the beginnings of hard rock and heavy metal — with Jimi Hendrix, Cream, Led Zeppelin, and Black Sabbath — closed out the decade.

The 1970s: fragmentation and spectacle

The 1970s saw rock split into subgenres: the virtuosity and concept albums of progressive rock (Pink Floyd, Genesis, Yes); the theatre of glam rock (David Bowie, T. Rex); the stadium scale of arena rock; the soft rock of singer-songwriters; and Southern and country rock. In the later 1970s, punk rock — the Ramones, the Sex Pistols, and the Clash — reacted against perceived excess with short, fast, stripped-down songs and a do-it-yourself ethic, spawning post-punk and new wave in its wake.

The 1980s and 1990s: metal, alternative, and grunge

Heavy metal splintered into glam metal, thrash, and death metal during the 1980s, while MTV made image central to rock stardom. College radio and independent labels nurtured alternative rock (R.E.M., the Smiths, Sonic Youth), which broke into the mainstream at the start of the 1990s with Nirvana's Nevermind and the grunge movement, alongside Pearl Jam and Soundgarden. In Britain, Britpop (Oasis, Blur) dominated the mid-1990s, while Radiohead pushed alternative rock toward experimental territory.

2000s to present

The 2000s brought a garage-rock and post-punk revival (the Strokes, the White Stripes, Arctic Monkeys) and the indie rock boom, even as hip hop and electronic music displaced rock as the commercial centre of popular music — by the late 2010s hip hop had surpassed rock in US consumption. Rock persists as a major live draw and in numerous subcultures, and its canonical albums of the 1960s and 1970s continue to anchor critical lists of the greatest records ever made.

Social and cultural impact

Rock music has been a vehicle for political protest, countercultural identity, and generational self-definition, from the civil-rights era and anti-Vietnam War movements through punk and Live Aid. Its fashions, iconography, and mythology of rebellion have permeated global popular culture, while critics continue to debate its artistic canon and its roots in Black American music.
