Etrog

The etrog (Hebrew: אֶתְרוֹג, plural etrogim) is the yellow citron (Citrus medica) used by Jews during the week-long holiday of Sukkot as one of the Four Species. Together with the lulav (palm branch), hadass (myrtle) and aravah (willow), it is waved in ceremonies recalling the harvest and prayers for rain.

Requirements

Traditional law (halakha) requires an etrog to be unblemished, of good shape and colour, ungrafted, and ideally with its pitom (style remnant) intact. A verse in Leviticus 23:40 commands taking the "fruit of a beautiful tree," interpreted by rabbinic tradition as the citron. Markets before Sukkot inspect and grade etrogim, with prized specimens selling for hundreds of dollars.

Cultivation and history

Preferred varieties include the Yemenite, Moroccan, Corsican and Calabrian (Diamante citron) types; Calabria's rabbis have supplied etrogim for centuries. Historical controversies erupted in the 19th century over possibly grafted Greek fruit from Corfu, leading to price wars and boycotts known as the Etrog controversies.

Symbolism

Midrash compares the etrog, which has both taste and fragrance, to Jews who possess both learning and good deeds. After the holiday, etrogim are sometimes made into jam or used in customs for fertility and protection in childbirth. See also Sukkot, Four Species and Citron.
