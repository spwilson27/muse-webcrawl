Kuru (disease)

Kuru is a rare, incurable, fatal neurodegenerative disorder that was formerly common among the Fore people of Papua New Guinea. It is a transmissible spongiform encephalopathy caused by an infectious protein called a prion, spread through ritualistic funerary cannibalism, in which relatives consumed the bodies — including the brain — of the dead.

Signs and course

Kuru means "to shiver" or "trembling" in the Fore language, referring to the body tremors that are a classic symptom. The disease progresses through ambulant, sedentary and terminal stages, with loss of coordination, difficulty walking and speaking, and emotional changes including pathological laughter — hence its occasional name "laughing sickness". Death typically follows within one to two years of symptom onset.

History

Identified in the 1950s, kuru was studied by researchers including Daniel Carleton Gajdusek, who showed its transmissibility and won the Nobel Prize. As the practice of endocannibalism ceased in the late 1950s, the epidemic declined, though long incubation periods — sometimes exceeding 50 years — meant cases continued to appear into the 21st century. Kuru was important in establishing the prion concept and is related to Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease.
