Apostille

An apostille is a standardised certificate that authenticates the origin of a public document — such as a birth certificate, court order, or notarised paper — so that it is recognised in another country. The system was established by the Hague Convention of 5 October 1961 Abolishing the Requirement of Legalisation for Foreign Public Documents.

How it works

An apostille is issued by a competent authority designated by the country where the document originated (for example, a secretary of state in the US or the Foreign Office's Legalisation Office in the UK). It certifies the authenticity of the signature, the capacity of the signer, and the identity of any seal or stamp — but not the truth of the document's contents. The certificate follows a prescribed format, is titled "Apostille (Convention de La Haye du 5 octobre 1961)", and includes French wording as required by the Convention.

Scope

More than 120 countries are parties to the Apostille Convention. Between member states, the apostille replaces the older chain-legalisation process involving multiple consular certifications. Electronic apostilles (e-Apostilles) are increasingly issued under the Hague Conference's e-APP programme.
