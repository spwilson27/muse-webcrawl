Heat

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In thermodynamics, heat is the thermal energy transferred between systems due to a temperature difference. In colloquial use, heat sometimes refers to thermal energy itself. Thermal energy is the kinetic energy of vibrating and colliding atoms in a substance. Heat flows spontaneously from a hotter body or system to a cooler one, though it can be forced in the opposite direction by doing work, as in refrigerators and heat pumps.

As a form of energy, heat has the unit joule (J) in the International System of Units (SI). In many applied fields of engineering the British thermal unit (BTU) and the calorie are also used. The standard unit for the rate of heat transferred is the watt (W), defined as one joule per second. The use of the symbol Q for the total amount of energy transferred as heat is due to Rudolf Clausius in 1850.

Heat transfer

Heat can be transferred by three fundamental mechanisms: conduction, convection and radiation. Conduction is the transfer of energy between neighbouring atoms or molecules through collisions and lattice vibrations, without bulk motion of matter. Convection involves the bulk movement of a fluid carrying thermal energy. Radiation is the transfer of energy by electromagnetic waves, which requires no medium at all — the way the Sun warms the Earth.

Heat and thermodynamics

In thermodynamics, heat is distinguished from work, the other way of transferring energy across a system boundary. The first law of thermodynamics states that the change in the internal energy of a system equals the heat added to the system minus the work done by the system. Historically, the nature of heat was debated between the caloric theory, which treated heat as a weightless fluid, and the kinetic theory; experiments by Joule and others established the mechanical equivalent of heat and the modern energy-based view.
