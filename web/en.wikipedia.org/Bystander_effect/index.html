Bystander effect

Bystander effect
FieldSocial psychology
Key researchersJohn Darley; Bibb Latané
Related conceptsDiffusion of responsibility; pluralistic ignorance

The bystander effect, or bystander apathy, is a social psychological theory stating that individuals are less likely to offer help to a victim in the presence of other people. The greater the number of bystanders, the less likely it is that any one of them will help; a person is more likely to act when alone or in a small group.

Research

Research began after the 1964 murder of Kitty Genovese in New York City, widely (though partly inaccurately) reported as witnessed by dozens of neighbours who failed to intervene. In experiments by Darley and Latané, participants who believed they were alone reported smoke entering a room or helped a person having a seizure far more often and quickly than those who believed others were also present. The main mechanisms are diffusion of responsibility — each bystander assumes someone else will act — and pluralistic ignorance, in which people look to others' calm reactions to judge whether an event is an emergency.

Five-step model

Latané and Darley proposed that helping requires noticing the event, interpreting it as an emergency, assuming personal responsibility, knowing how to help, and deciding to act; the bystander effect can block any of these steps.

Qualifications

Later work, including studies of real CCTV footage of public conflicts, shows that bystanders intervene more often than the classic theory suggests, and that larger groups can increase the chance that at least someone helps in violent confrontations. Knowing about the effect, and having relevant skills or a personal connection, counteracts it; experts advise addressing a specific person directly ("you, in the red jacket, call an ambulance") to break diffusion of responsibility.
