Operation Frequent Wind was the final phase in the evacuation of American civilians and "at-risk" Vietnamese from Saigon, South Vietnam, before the takeover of the city by the North Vietnamese People's Army of Vietnam on 30 April 1975. Carried out on 29–30 April, it was the largest helicopter evacuation in history and became the defining image of the end of the Vietnam War.

A helicopter being pushed overboard to make room for more evacuees.

Background

North Vietnam's 1975 Spring Offensive rapidly overran South Vietnam. As the PAVN closed on Saigon in late April, fixed-wing evacuations from Tan Son Nhut Air Base became impossible after rocket and artillery attacks on 28–29 April destroyed aircraft and closed the runways. US Ambassador Graham Martin finally authorised the helicopter option, signalled by the playing of "White Christmas" on Armed Forces Radio.

The evacuation

US Marine and Air America helicopters flew from ships of Task Force 76 offshore to landing zones at the Defense Attaché Office compound and the US Embassy. Over about 18 hours, more than 80 helicopters evacuated roughly 1,400 Americans and 5,600 third-country nationals and South Vietnamese. South Vietnamese pilots flew additional thousands out to the fleet on their own initiative; many helicopters were ditched at sea or pushed off decks to make space. The last Marines left the embassy roof on the morning of 30 April, hours before North Vietnamese tanks reached the presidential palace.

Legacy

The photograph of a helicopter on a Saigon rooftop (actually an Air America helicopter atop an apartment building at 22 Gia Long Street) became an iconic image of the war's end. Combined with fixed-wing flights and the earlier evacuation of orphans (Operation Babylift), the final operations brought out well over 100,000 Vietnamese during 1975.
