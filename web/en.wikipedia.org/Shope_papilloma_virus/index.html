Shope papilloma virus


A cottontail rabbit bearing the horn-like warts caused by the Shope papilloma virus.

The Shope papilloma virus (SPV), also known as the cottontail rabbit papillomavirus (CRPV) or Kappapapillomavirus 2, is a papillomavirus that causes horn-like warts, resembling keratinous carcinomas, on the heads of cottontail rabbits, particularly around the ears and eyelids. It was discovered in the 1930s by Richard Edwin Shope, who demonstrated that the growths could be transmitted by a filterable agent — one of the first demonstrations that a virus could cause tumours in a mammal.

Biology

Like other papillomaviruses, SPV is a small, non-enveloped virus with a circular double-stranded DNA genome of about 8 kilobases, including the early genes E6 and E7 whose protein products interfere with tumour suppressor pathways in infected keratinocytes. In its natural host, the Eastern cottontail (Sylvilagus floridanus), the virus produces benign papillomas that usually regress, but in domestic rabbits (Oryctolagus cuniculus) the lesions frequently progress to squamous cell carcinoma.

Research significance

The virus became a foundational model system for studying viral carcinogenesis: work by Shope and by Peyton Rous showed that virus-induced warts could become malignant and that chemical carcinogens could cooperate with the virus, foreshadowing the multistep model of cancer. The rabbit system informed later research on human papillomaviruses (HPV) and the development of HPV vaccines.

Jackalope folklore

The grotesque horn-like growths on infected rabbits are considered a likely source of the American folktale of the jackalope, a mythical horned or antlered jackrabbit of the western United States.
