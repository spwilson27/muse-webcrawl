HeLa

HeLa (/ˈhiːlɑː/) is an immortalised human cell line used worldwide in scientific research. Derived from cervical cancer cells taken in 1951 from Henrietta Lacks, a patient at Johns Hopkins Hospital in Baltimore, HeLa was the first human cell line to be grown successfully in culture for extended periods. Its name comes from the first two letters of Lacks's first and last names. HeLa cells have contributed to tens of thousands of studies and major advances including the polio vaccine, cancer research, and the mapping of the human genome.

Origin

In February 1951, tissue from Lacks's cervical tumour was given to researcher George Gey, whose laboratory had long tried and failed to culture human cells. Unlike previous samples, Lacks's cells — infected with human papillomavirus 18, which had integrated into her genome — doubled roughly every 24 hours and did not die off. Gey propagated and freely distributed the line; a dedicated production facility at Tuskegee scaled up HeLa culture to supply Jonas Salk's polio vaccine testing programme in the 1950s.

Characteristics

HeLa cells are remarkably durable and prolific, with active telomerase that prevents the shortening of chromosome ends, making them effectively immortal. They are heavily aneuploid, carrying far more than the normal 46 chromosomes, and grow so aggressively that they have contaminated and displaced many other cell lines — a persistent problem documented by Stanley Gartler in the 1960s and catalogued by the International Cell Line Authentication Committee.

Ethics and the Lacks family

The cells were taken and used without Lacks's knowledge or consent, standard practice at the time but the subject of enduring ethical debate. Her family learned of the line's existence only in the 1970s. Rebecca Skloot's 2010 book The Immortal Life of Henrietta Lacks brought the story to wide attention. In 2013, after the HeLa genome was published without family consultation, the U.S. National Institutes of Health reached an agreement giving Lacks family members a role in governing access to the genome data; in 2023 her estate settled a lawsuit with a biotechnology company over profits from the cells.
