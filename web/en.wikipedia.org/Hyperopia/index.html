Hyperopia

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hyperopia is a defect of vision caused by an imperfection in the eye (often when the eyeball is too short or the lens cannot become round enough), causing difficulty seeing near objects, and in extreme cases causing a sufferer to be unable to focus on objects at any distance. As an object moves toward the eye, the eye must increase its optical power to keep the image in focus on the retina. If the power of the cornea and lens is insufficient, as in hyperopia, the image will appear blurred.

Hyperopia is often confused with presbyopia, another condition that frequently causes blurry near vision. Presbyopia sufferers, however, usually do not have difficulty focusing on distant objects. Hyperopia is also known as long-sightedness, far-sightedness, and hypermetropia. Young people with mild hyperopia can often compensate using accommodation; as they age, or in cases of significant hyperopia, the ciliary muscle effort causes eye strain (asthenopia), headaches, and intermittent blur.

Signs and symptoms

The common symptoms of hyperopia are blurry vision for near objects, eye strain, headaches after close work such as reading, aching or burning eyes, and squinting. In children, uncorrected moderate-to-high hyperopia is associated with accommodative esotropia (crossed eyes), because the effort of accommodation is linked to convergence, and with an increased risk of amblyopia (lazy eye). Children may show no complaints because their accommodative amplitude is large, so screening is important.

Causes

Hyperopia can commonly be caused by:

  • Axial hyperopia: the eyeball is too short from front to back (the most common cause).
  • Refractive hyperopia: the cornea is too flat or the crystalline lens has insufficient refractive power.
  • Positional causes: displacement or absence of the lens (aphakia, as after cataract surgery without an intraocular lens) produces high hyperopia.

Hyperopia has a substantial genetic component; most infants are born mildly hyperopic, and the eye normally grows toward emmetropia during childhood (emmetropization). Failure of this process, or excessive eye growth producing the opposite error (myopia), leaves a residual refractive error.

Diagnosis

A diagnosis is made by an eye-care professional using refraction — measurement of the eye's refractive error with a retinoscope or automated refractor, refined by trial lenses in a phoropter. In children and young adults, cycloplegic refraction (using drops to paralyse accommodation) may be needed to reveal the full "latent" hyperopia that accommodation otherwise masks. Severity is measured in dioptres of positive (+) lens power required for correction.

Treatment

The simplest treatment is corrective lenses: spectacles or contact lenses with convex (plus) lenses, which add converging power so the image focuses on the retina. Refractive surgery options include LASIK, PRK, and other procedures that steepen the cornea, though the correctable range is smaller and results less predictable than for myopia. Intraocular options (phakic lenses or refractive lens exchange) exist for high hyperopia. Treatment in children focuses on preventing amblyopia and strabismus; adults may need separate near corrections as presbyopia develops and accommodative reserve declines.

Epidemiology

The prevalence of hyperopia varies with age and population: it is common in infancy, declines through school age as eyes elongate, and is estimated to affect roughly 10 percent of adults in the United States to a clinically significant degree, with higher rates in some populations. It is less prevalent than myopia in East Asian populations, where myopia has reached epidemic proportions, and relatively more common among some Indigenous and rural populations.
