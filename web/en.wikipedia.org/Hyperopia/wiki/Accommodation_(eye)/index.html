Accommodation (eye)

Accommodation is the process by which the vertebrate eye changes optical power to maintain a clear image, or focus, on an object as its distance varies. In this process the ciliary body adjusts the tension of the zonular fibres, changing the curvature of the crystalline lens so that near objects can be focused on the retina.

Mechanism

According to the Helmholtz theory, contraction of the ciliary muscle releases zonular tension, allowing the elastic lens to become more spherical and increase its refractive power for near vision. Relaxation of the muscle restores tension and flattens the lens for distance vision. The near response combines accommodation with convergence of the eyes and constriction of the pupil.

Amplitude and ageing

The amplitude of accommodation — the maximum change in lens power, measured in dioptres — is greatest in childhood, at around 14 dioptres, and declines steadily with age as the lens stiffens. When the amplitude falls below what is needed for comfortable reading, typically around the age of 40–45, the resulting condition is known as presbyopia and is corrected with reading glasses or bifocals.
