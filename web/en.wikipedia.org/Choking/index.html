Choking

Choking is the mechanical obstruction of the flow of air from the environment into the lungs. It occurs when a foreign object blocks the trachea (windpipe) or when the airway is compressed externally. Choking prevents breathing and, if not relieved, can rapidly cause unconsciousness, brain damage, and death.

Causes

In adults, choking most often happens while eating, when food — commonly meat — lodges in the airway. In young children, small objects such as coins, buttons, and pieces of toys are frequent causes, along with foods such as grapes and nuts. Alcohol, poor dentition, and neurological conditions that impair swallowing increase the risk.

Signs and first aid

A person who is choking may clutch at the throat (the universal distress signal), be unable to speak or cough effectively, and develop a blue tinge to the lips. First aid typically combines back blows with abdominal thrusts (the Heimlich manoeuvre) to expel the obstruction; emergency services should be called immediately.

Prevention

  • Cut food into small pieces for children
  • Chew slowly and avoid talking while eating
  • Keep small objects away from infants and toddlers
