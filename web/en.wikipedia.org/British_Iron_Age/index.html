British Iron Age

The British Iron Age is the period of British prehistory from about 800 BC to the Roman conquest in AD 43 (and later in unconquered northern regions), during which ironworking spread through the islands and society was organised in tribal groups speaking Celtic languages. It is characterised by hillforts, roundhouses, sophisticated metalwork in iron, bronze and gold, coinage in the south-east, and growing contact with the Roman world before the conquest. The term "Iron Age" comes from the three-age system of Thomsen; modern archaeologists emphasise that there was no single "Celtic invasion" but gradual change and exchange with the Continent.

Society and settlement

Iron Age Britons lived mostly in farming settlements of thatched roundhouses, organised regionally into groups later recorded by Roman writers as tribes — among them the Iceni, Brigantes, Catuvellauni, Atrebates, Brigantes and Silures. Hundreds of hillforts, from Danebury in Hampshire to the vast ramparts of Maiden Castle in Dorset, were built and remodelled, serving as defended refuges, communal centres and symbols of power, though how continuously they were occupied is debated. In Atlantic Scotland, communities built brochs — tall drystone towers such as Mousa in Shetland — and crannogs (artificial island dwellings) dot Scottish and Irish lakes.

Economy and craft

Agriculture rested on spelt, barley, cattle and sheep, with grain stored in pits and four-post granaries. Iron transformed tools and weapons: socketed axes, ploughshares, and long slashing swords. British metalwork reached extraordinary sophistication in bronze and gold — the Battersea Shield's red-enamelled La Tène ornament, the gold torcs of the Snettisham Hoard, and the decorated mirrors buried with women. Chariot burials in East Yorkshire (the Arras culture) echo Continental practice. From about 100 BC, lowland tribes minted coins inscribed with rulers' names, and large nucleated settlements (oppida) such as Camulodunum (Colchester) and Verulamium (St Albans) grew up, trading wine, pottery and slaves with Roman Gaul.

Religion and ritual

Religion is glimpsed through Roman accounts of the Druids — condemned by Roman authors for alleged human sacrifice — and through archaeology: offerings deposited in rivers and bogs, including the sacrificed "bog bodies" such as Lindow Man, ritual shafts, and shrines at sites like Hayling Island. Defleshing and the curation of human remains within settlements suggest complex attitudes to death quite unlike Roman practice.

Roman contact and conquest

Julius Caesar's expeditions of 55 and 54 BC imposed tribute and client kings, inaugurating a century of Romanisation in the south-east — rulers like Cunobelinus and Verica issued Roman-style coins and imported Roman luxuries. The emperor Claudius's invasion of AD 43, prompted partly by dynastic appeals from British kings, ended the Iron Age in the lowland zone, though unconquered Caledonia and Ireland (Hibernia, which shared the wider Iron Age culture) retained Iron Age lifeways for centuries longer.
