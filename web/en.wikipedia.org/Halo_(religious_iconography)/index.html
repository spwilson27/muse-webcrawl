Halo (religious iconography)

Also calledNimbus, aureole, gloriole
MediumPainting, sculpture, mosaic
PeriodsAncient to early modern art

A halo (also known as a nimbus, aureole, or gloriole) is a crown of light rays, circle, or disc surrounding a person's head in a work of art. Halos indicate sacred or holy figures and have been used in the iconography of many religions to denote divinity, sanctity, or spiritual illumination.

History

Radiant crowns appear in ancient Egyptian, Hellenistic, and Roman art, where they could denote gods, deified emperors, or the sun god Helios and Sol Invictus. Early Christian art adopted the device from the 4th century onward, applying the round halo to Christ, angels, and saints. In Byzantine and medieval Western art, halos were often rendered in gold leaf; distinct forms, such as triangular halos for God the Father or square halos for living donors, carried specific meanings.

Forms

An aureole is a radiance surrounding the whole body, often almond-shaped (a mandorla when almond-shaped). A halo may be plain, rayed, or inscribed with a cross (a cruciform nimbus, reserved for Christ). In Buddhist and Hindu art, similar head and body halos appear around Buddhas, bodhisattvas, and deities.

Decline

During the Renaissance, artists seeking naturalism, such as those using linear perspective, increasingly shrank, foreshortened, or omitted halos, and by the later Renaissance the convention had largely faded from Western painting, surviving in traditional icon painting and popular religious imagery.
