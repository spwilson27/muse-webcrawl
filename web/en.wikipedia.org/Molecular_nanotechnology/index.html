Molecular nanotechnology

A hypothetical molecular assembler, the emblem of molecular nanotechnology

Molecular nanotechnology (MNT), also called molecular manufacturing, is a proposed technology based on the ability to build structures to complex, atomic specifications by means of mechanosynthesis — the positioning of individual atoms and molecules by machine. It is distinguished from the broader field of nanotechnology, which largely concerns materials with nanoscale features, by its goal of precise, programmable control over molecular building blocks.

History

The concept traces to Richard Feynman's 1959 talk "There's Plenty of Room at the Bottom". K. Eric Drexler developed it in the 1980s in Engines of Creation (1986) and his technical work Nanosystems (1992), envisioning general-purpose "assemblers" and, ultimately, desktop factories able to manufacture atomically precise products — and raising the spectre of self-replicating machines running out of control (the "grey goo" scenario). Drexler debated chemist Richard Smalley, who argued that proposed assembler arms would fail in practice (the "fat fingers" and "sticky fingers" problems); mainstream nanoscience pursued bottom-up chemistry and self-assembly instead.

Current status

No general-purpose molecular assembler exists. Progress has come from adjacent fields: scanning probe manipulation of single atoms, DNA nanotechnology and DNA origami, protein engineering, and synthetic molecular motors. Roadmap efforts have catalogued steps toward atomically precise manufacturing, but the field remains largely theoretical and controversial.
