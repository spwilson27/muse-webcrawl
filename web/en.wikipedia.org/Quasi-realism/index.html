Quasi-realism

Quasi-realism is a meta-ethical theory developed by Simon Blackburn which holds that although moral sentences do not express genuine propositions about objective moral facts, we may legitimately talk as if they do. It seeks to explain why moral discourse has the surface features of realism while resting on an expressivist foundation.

Theory

Starting from projectivism — the idea that we project our attitudes onto the world — quasi-realism aims to "earn the right" to truth, knowledge, and even talk of moral facts and properties, using minimalist accounts of those notions. Blackburn argues this solves the Frege–Geach problem for expressivism by explaining how moral sentences function in embedded contexts such as conditionals and negation.

Criticism

Critics such as John McDowell have argued that quasi-realism either collapses into genuine realism or cannot fully explain objective-seeming features of moral thought; the dispute centres on whether the earned realist idiom is merely "as if".
