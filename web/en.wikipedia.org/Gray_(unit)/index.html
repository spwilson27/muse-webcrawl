The gray (symbol: Gy) is the unit of ionizing radiation dose in the International System of Units (SI), defined as the absorption of one joule of radiation energy per kilogram of matter. It is used as the unit of the radiation quantity absorbed dose, which measures the energy deposited by ionizing radiation in a unit mass of absorbing material, and also for the related quantities of specific energy and kerma.

History and definition

In 1975 the unit was named in honour of the British physicist Louis Harold Gray, a pioneer in the study of radiation effects on biological tissue. The gray replaced the earlier unit the rad: 1 Gy = 100 rad. In SI base units, 1 Gy = 1 J/kg = 1 m²·s⁻².

Use

Absorbed dose in grays is a purely physical measure of energy deposition. The biological effect of a given dose depends on the type of radiation and the tissue exposed, so dose equivalent is instead measured in sieverts, applying weighting factors to the absorbed dose. In radiotherapy, tumour doses are prescribed in grays, commonly delivered in fractions of about 1.8–2 Gy. Whole-body acute doses above roughly 4–5 Gy can be lethal to humans without treatment.

Materials science uses the gray to express doses in irradiation processing, and radiation-hardened electronics are rated by the total ionizing dose they can withstand, often in kilograys.
