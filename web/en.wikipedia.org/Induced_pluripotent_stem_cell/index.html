Induced pluripotent stem cell

First reported2006 (mouse), 2007 (human)
Key researcherShinya Yamanaka
Reprogramming factorsOct4, Sox2, Klf4, c-Myc (Yamanaka factors)
RecognitionNobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, 2012

Induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs or iPS cells) are pluripotent stem cells artificially derived from ordinary adult cells, such as skin fibroblasts, by reprogramming them with a defined set of transcription factors. Like embryonic stem cells, they can differentiate into virtually any cell type of the body.

Discovery

In 2006 Shinya Yamanaka's team in Kyoto showed that introducing four genes — Oct3/4, Sox2, Klf4 and c-Myc — into mouse fibroblasts produced cells resembling embryonic stem cells. Human iPSCs followed in 2007 from both Yamanaka's group and James Thomson's. Yamanaka shared the 2012 Nobel Prize with John Gurdon for the discovery that mature cells can be reprogrammed.

Methods

Original methods used integrating retroviruses, raising safety concerns; later techniques include Sendai virus, episomal plasmids, mRNA and small-molecule approaches that avoid genomic integration. Reprogramming is inefficient and slow, and iPSCs can retain epigenetic memory of their tissue of origin.

Applications

Patient-specific iPSCs enable disease modelling in a dish, drug screening and toxicity testing, and are the basis of regenerative-medicine trials for conditions including Parkinson's disease, retinal degeneration and heart failure. Because they can be made from the patient's own cells, they promise personalised therapies, though tumour risk and manufacturing cost remain challenges.
