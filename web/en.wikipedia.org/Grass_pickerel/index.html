Grass pickerel

Scientific name: Esox americanus vermiculatus

Family: Esocidae

Conservation status: Least Concern (IUCN); listed as Special Concern in Canada

The grass pickerel (Esox americanus vermiculatus) is a subspecies of the American pickerel (Esox americanus), a small freshwater fish of the pike family Esocidae. It is one of the smallest members of the pike family and inhabits slow-moving, heavily vegetated waters of eastern North America.

Description

The grass pickerel is a small ambush predator, typically reaching 20–30 cm (8–12 in) in length and rarely exceeding about 0.4 kg. Like other pikes it has an elongated body, a flattened duckbill-shaped snout and sharp teeth. Its colouring is olive to brown with dark, wavy, vermiculated (worm-like) markings along the sides, which provide camouflage among submerged vegetation. It can be distinguished from its close relative, the redfin pickerel (E. a. americanus), chiefly by the pattern of markings and fin colouration.

Distribution and habitat

The grass pickerel is found from the Great Lakes and St. Lawrence River drainages south through the Mississippi River basin to the Gulf Coast, and west into Texas. It prefers shallow, clear, slow-flowing streams, marshes, swamps, pond margins and the vegetated edges of lakes. In Canada its range is limited to parts of southern Ontario and Quebec, where loss of wetland habitat has led to it being designated a species of Special Concern.

Behaviour and diet

Grass pickerel are ambush predators that lie motionless among plants and dart out to seize small fish, crayfish, aquatic insects and other invertebrates. They spawn in early spring in flooded vegetation. Females scatter adhesive eggs over submerged plants, and no parental care is given.
