Cognitive psychology

Cognitive psychology is the scientific study of mental processes such as attention, language use, memory, perception, problem solving, creativity, and reasoning. It emerged as a distinct field in the 1950s and 1960s, during the "cognitive revolution" that reacted against behaviourism's refusal to study internal mental states.

History

Although questions about the mind go back to ancient philosophy, and to early experimentalists such as Wilhelm Wundt and Hermann Ebbinghaus, behaviourism dominated American psychology in the first half of the 20th century. Key turning points included Noam Chomsky's 1959 critique of B. F. Skinner's account of language, George A. Miller's work on short-term memory limits, and Ulric Neisser's 1967 book Cognitive Psychology, which gave the field its name.

Main areas

Cognitive psychologists study how people perceive and attend to information, how it is stored and retrieved in memory (sensory, short-term/working, and long-term systems), how language is comprehended and produced, and how people reason, judge, decide, and solve problems. Research methods include controlled laboratory experiments, reaction-time measures, eye tracking, computational modelling, and neuroimaging in collaboration with cognitive neuroscience.

Applications

The field informs education, human–computer interaction, eyewitness-testimony research, and clinical practice; cognitive behavioural therapy draws on cognitive models of distorted thinking in depression and anxiety. Findings such as the heuristics and biases catalogued by Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman reshaped economics and decision science.
