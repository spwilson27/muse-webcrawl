Negative concord

Negative concord, popularly known as double negation, is a phenomenon of a language in which two or more negative words or elements occur within a single clause or sentence to express a single negation. Rather than cancelling each other out, as in standard logic, the negative elements reinforce one another.

Examples

In many varieties of English, such as African American Vernacular English, a sentence like "I didn't do nothing" uses two negatives but expresses one negation. Similar constructions are standard in Spanish (No vi nada, literally "I didn't see nothing"), Italian, French, Polish, Russian, and many other languages.

History

Negative concord was common in Old and Middle English — Chaucer could stack several negatives for emphasis. In the 18th century, prescriptive grammarians such as Robert Lowth argued that two negatives make a positive, and the construction was stigmatized in standard English, though it persisted in nonstandard dialects.

Analysis

Linguists treat negative concord as agreement: a single negative operator licenses multiple negative indefinites, much as a verb agrees with its subject in number. Languages differ in whether the negative marker must precede the verb (strict concord, as in Slavic languages) or may be flexible (non-strict concord, as in Spanish and Italian).
