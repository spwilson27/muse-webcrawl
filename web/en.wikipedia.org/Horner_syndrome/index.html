Horner syndrome

Horner syndrome results from damage to the sympathetic nerve supply to the eye and face.
SpecialtyNeurology, ophthalmology
SignsMiosis, ptosis, anhidrosis, enophthalmos appearance
CausesDamage to the sympathetic trunk: stroke, tumor, carotid dissection, trauma
Named afterJohann Friedrich Horner

Horner syndrome, also known as oculosympathetic paresis, is a combination of symptoms that arises when a group of nerves known as the sympathetic trunk is damaged. The signs occur on the same side as the lesion of the sympathetic trunk and include a constricted pupil (miosis), a drooping upper eyelid (ptosis), reduced sweating on the affected side of the face (anhidrosis), and the appearance of a sunken eye.

The syndrome is named after Swiss ophthalmologist Johann Friedrich Horner, who described it in 1869, although earlier descriptions existed.

Causes

Horner syndrome can be congenital or acquired at any point along the three-neuron sympathetic pathway from the hypothalamus to the eye. Causes include brainstem strokes such as the lateral medullary syndrome, apical lung tumors (Pancoast tumors), internal carotid artery dissection, cluster headaches, trauma, and syringomyelia. In children, it may signal a neuroblastoma.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is suggested by the clinical triad of miosis, ptosis, and anhidrosis. Pharmacologic testing with cocaine or apraclonidine eye drops can confirm the diagnosis, and hydroxyamphetamine drops can help localize the lesion. Imaging of the brain, neck, and chest is used to find the underlying cause.

Treatment

There is no specific treatment for Horner syndrome itself; management targets the underlying cause. The cosmetic eyelid droop is usually mild.
