Translational symmetry

Translational symmetry of an object means that the object looks the same after a particular translation. In geometry, a translation "slides" an object by a vector a so that every point of the object moves in the same direction and by the same distance, with no rotation or reflection. If the object is unchanged by the translation, it possesses translational symmetry for that vector.

In geometry

For a given object, the translations for which it is invariant form a group, the symmetry group of the object. Patterns on an infinite frieze or wallpaper can be invariant under translations in one or two independent directions. In two dimensions, lattices describe the possible translational symmetries of infinite periodic patterns, and crystallography classifies crystals by their translational symmetry combined with rotations and reflections.

In physics

Translational symmetry in physical systems has deep consequences. By Noether's theorem, invariance of a system under spatial translation corresponds to conservation of linear momentum. A system that is invariant under arbitrary translations in time likewise conserves energy. In solid-state physics, the periodicity of a crystal lattice gives rise to Bloch's theorem and the band structure of solids.

Discrete and continuous symmetry

Translational symmetry may be discrete, holding only for specific translation vectors (as in a crystal lattice), or continuous, holding for every translation (as in a homogeneous fluid or empty space). Fractals may exhibit translational symmetry only approximately or over a limited range of scales.
