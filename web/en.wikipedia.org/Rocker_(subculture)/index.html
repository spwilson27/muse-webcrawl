Rocker (subculture)

Rockers, leather boys, Ton-up boys, or caff racers are members of a biker subculture that originated in the United Kingdom during the 1950s and was primarily centred on fast motorcycles, British motorcycles, and rock and roll music. The rockers were a young and rebellious rock and roll subculture, contrasting strongly with the scooter-riding mods.

Origins

The rocker subculture grew out of post-war British motorcycling clubs and the ton-up boys who raced between transport cafés, most famously the Ace Cafe on London's North Circular Road. The aim was to reach 100 miles per hour—"the ton"—before a record on the jukebox finished playing. Rockers wore black leather jackets (often studded and painted), jeans, and motorcycle boots, and styled their hair in quiffs influenced by American rock and roll stars.

Music and lifestyle

Rockers favoured 1950s rock and roll and rockabilly by artists such as Elvis Presley, Gene Vincent, Eddie Cochran, Chuck Berry, and Bo Diddley. Their motorcycles were typically standard machines stripped down and modified in the café racer style, with low clip-on handlebars and rear-set footrests.

Mods and rockers

The rockers' rivalry with the mods reached its peak in the clashes at British seaside resorts—Brighton, Clacton, and Margate—in 1964, which were heavily reported in the press and were later analyzed by sociologist Stanley Cohen in his study of moral panic, Folk Devils and Moral Panics.

Legacy

The rocker style influenced later subcultures including heavy metal fashion and the Japanese bōsōzoku. The Ace Cafe reopened in 1997 and remains a centre of revival rocker culture.
