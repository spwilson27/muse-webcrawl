Warmia (Polish: Warmia; German: Ermland) is a historical region in northern Poland, on the south-eastern shore of the Baltic Sea. Originally inhabited by the Old Prussian Warmians, it became the territory of the Prince-Bishopric of Warmia, a theocratic state whose bishops and cathedral chapter ruled from the 13th century until the late 18th century. Together with neighbouring Masuria, it forms the modern Warmian–Masurian Voivodeship, with Olsztyn as its capital and largest city.

History

The region was conquered and Christianized during the Prussian Crusade of the Teutonic Order, and in 1243 the Diocese of Ermland was founded as one of four Prussian dioceses. After the Second Peace of Thorn (1466), Warmia passed under the Polish Crown as part of Royal Prussia, while retaining the prince-bishop's temporal rule — an arrangement that set Catholic Polish-oriented Warmia apart from the surrounding Duchy of Prussia, which later became Lutheran and German. Nicolaus Copernicus spent most of his working life in Warmia, as a canon of the Frombork chapter, and wrote much of De revolutionibus there; he also administered and defended Olsztyn Castle during the Polish–Teutonic war of 1519–21.

Warmia was annexed by Prussia in the First Partition of Poland (1772) and became part of East Prussia. After World War II it was transferred to Poland under the Potsdam Agreement, its German population was expelled and replaced by Poles, including many resettled from Poland's former eastern territories. Warmia remains a distinctly Catholic region with its own dialect, customs, and Gothic brick architecture, including the cathedrals of Frombork and Olsztyn and the bishops' castle at Lidzbark Warmiński, known as the "Wawel of the North".
