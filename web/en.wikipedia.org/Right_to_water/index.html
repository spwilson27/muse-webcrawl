Right to water

The human right to water and sanitation is a principle stating that clean drinking water and sanitation are a human right because they are essential to people's health, dignity and prosperity. It was recognised as a human right by the United Nations General Assembly on 28 July 2010 (Resolution 64/292), which called upon states and international organisations to provide financial resources and technology to provide safe, clean, accessible and affordable drinking water and sanitation for all.

Legal foundations

Before 2010 the right was derived from other instruments: the Committee on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights' General Comment No. 15 (2002) read the right to water into Articles 11 and 12 of the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights. Explicit mentions also appear in CEDAW, the Convention on the Rights of the Child, and international humanitarian law. The UN Human Rights Council affirmed the right in 2010, and a Special Rapporteur on the human rights to safe drinking water and sanitation oversees its implementation.

Content of the right

The right entitles everyone to sufficient, safe, acceptable, physically accessible and affordable water for personal and domestic uses. The World Health Organization's guidelines suggest roughly 50–100 litres per person per day to meet basic needs. States bear obligations to respect, protect and fulfil the right, moving progressively toward universal access without discrimination.

Implementation

Several countries, including South Africa, have constitutionalised the right; South African courts applied it in the Mazibuko case concerning Johannesburg's free basic water policy. Globally, Sustainable Development Goal 6 seeks clean water and sanitation for all by 2030, though billions still lack safely managed services.
