Local Autonomy Law

The Local Autonomy Law (地方自治法, Chihō jichi hō), Law No. 67 of 1947, is a Japanese law that lays out the framework of local self-government in Japan. Enacted under the Allied occupation alongside the post-war Constitution of Japan, which guarantees local autonomy in Articles 92 to 95, it defines the organisation, powers and finances of Japan's prefectures and municipalities.

Framework

The law classifies Japan's local public entities into ordinary bodies — the 47 prefectures (to, dō, fu and ken) and municipalities (cities, towns and villages) — and special bodies such as the special wards of Tokyo and local public cooperatives. Each prefecture and municipality has a directly elected chief executive (governor or mayor) and a directly elected assembly, creating a presidential-style dual representation at local level.

Local governments administer affairs close to residents' daily lives, such as education (through boards of education), public health, local roads, water and sewerage, and resident registration, alongside functions delegated from the national government. The law also provides for special cities with delegated prefectural powers — designated cities, core cities and (formerly) special cities.

Reforms

The system established in 1947 was paired with a local finance framework intended to balance autonomy with national redistribution, notably the local allocation tax. Major decentralisation reforms in 2000 abolished the system of agency-delegated functions, under which local executives had acted as agents of the central ministries, and sought to place central–local relations on a more equal footing.
