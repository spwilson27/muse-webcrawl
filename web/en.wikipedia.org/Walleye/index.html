Walleye

The walleye (Sander vitreus, synonym Stizostedion vitreum) is a freshwater perciform fish native to most of Canada and to the Northern United States. It is a North American close relative of the European zander, also known as the pikeperch. The walleye is sometimes called the "yellow walleye" to distinguish it from the blue walleye, which was a subspecies that was once found in the southern Ontario and Quebec regions, but is now presumed extinct. However, physiochemical and morphological analyses have shown the blue walleye to be a distinct color morph and not a subspecies.

Physical description

Walleyes are largely olive and gold in color (hence the French common name: doré—golden). A walleye is distinguished by a dorsal fin that is separated into two sections and a large, dark spot at the base of the pectoral fin. The name "walleye" comes from their pearlescent eyes, which glow due to a reflective layer of pigment, the tapetum lucidum, that enables them to see and feed in low-light conditions. They grow to about 80 cm (31 in) in length and weigh up to about 9 kg (20 lb). The maximum recorded size for the species is 107 cm (42 in) in length and 13 kg (29 lb) in weight.

Ecology

Walleyes are found across Canada and the northern United States, inhabiting large lakes, reservoirs, and rivers. They are carnivorous, feeding on smaller fish such as yellow perch, as well as crayfish, snails, frogs, mudpuppies, and small mammals when other food is scarce. Walleyes spawn in early spring over gravel shoals in lakes or in tributary rivers.

Fishing

The walleye is one of the most sought-after freshwater game fish in North America, prized for its delicate, flaky white flesh. It is the state fish of Minnesota, Ohio, and South Dakota. Major walleye fisheries include Lake Erie, Lake of the Woods, and the Missouri River reservoirs.
