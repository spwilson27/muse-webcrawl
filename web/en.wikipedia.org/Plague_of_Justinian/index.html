The Plague of Justinian or Justinianic Plague (541–549 AD) was an epidemic of bubonic plague that afflicted the entire Mediterranean Basin, Europe, and the Near East, severely affecting the Byzantine Empire and Sasanian Empire. It was the first known pandemic of plague, and is named for the Byzantine emperor Justinian I, who reigned at the time and was himself infected but survived. Recurrences of the plague continued for roughly two centuries, until around 750.

Outbreak and spread

The historian Procopius, the principal eyewitness source, reported that the plague began at Pelusium on the eastern edge of the Nile Delta in 541 and spread along trade routes. It reached Constantinople in the spring of 542, probably carried in grain shipments from Egypt. At its peak in the capital, Procopius claimed, deaths reached 5,000–10,000 per day, and mass graves and towers filled with corpses were used to dispose of the dead.

Pathogen

Ancient DNA studies of skeletons from sixth-century cemeteries in Bavaria and elsewhere have confirmed the causative agent as Yersinia pestis, the same bacterium responsible for the later Black Death. Genomic analysis suggests the Justinianic strain was an early, now-extinct lineage of the bacterium, distinct from the lineage that caused the second pandemic.

Mortality debate

Traditional accounts, following literary sources, held that the plague killed a quarter to a half of the population of Constantinople and devastated the empire demographically and fiscally. More recent scholarship has questioned the scale of mortality, arguing from archaeological, documentary, and polling evidence that the demographic impact may have been considerably smaller and regionally uneven. The question remains contested.

Consequences

The plague struck while Justinian was engaged in wars of reconquest in Italy and the West and in conflict with Persia. It is frequently cited as a factor in the failure to permanently restore the empire's western provinces, in labour shortages and tax shortfalls, and in social and religious upheavals, including processions and heightened apocalyptic expectation. Its waves coincided with the climatic anomalies of 536 onwards (the Late Antique Little Ice Age), which some researchers link to the outbreak's severity.

Later recurrences

Further outbreaks recurred across the Mediterranean and Near East at intervals of roughly a decade or more—in 558, 573–574, 580, 588, 599, and through the seventh and eighth centuries—with the last major wave in Naples and the Levant in 746–747. After about 750 the first plague pandemic disappears from the historical record.
