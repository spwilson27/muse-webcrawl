Mise-en-scène

Mise-en-scène (French for "placing on stage") is an expression used to describe the design aspects of a theatre or film production: everything that appears within the frame and its arrangement — sets, props, costumes, makeup, lighting, and the positioning and movement of actors. Borrowed from theatre criticism, the term was taken up by film critics, notably those of Cahiers du Cinéma, to discuss how directors express meaning visually rather than through dialogue or editing alone.

Elements

Film scholars typically break mise-en-scène into four areas: setting (location and set design), costume and makeup, lighting (including high-key and low-key schemes), and staging — the movement and performance of figures within the frame. Depth of staging, the balance of the composition, and the use of foreground and background planes all shape how a scene reads. Directors celebrated for their mise-en-scène, such as Jean Renoir, Max Ophüls, Kenji Mizoguchi and Orson Welles, often used long takes and deep focus so that these arranged elements, rather than cutting, carry the drama.

In film studies

The concept is central to film analysis because it draws attention to visual storytelling: a change in a character's costume, the placement of an object, or a pool of shadow can carry narrative and thematic weight. Debates about mise-en-scène versus montage formed one of the classic fault lines of film theory in the mid-twentieth century.
