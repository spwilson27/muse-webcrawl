Solidarity (Polish: Solidarność) is a Polish trade union founded in September 1980 at the Lenin Shipyard in Gdańsk under the leadership of Lech Wałęsa. It was the first independent trade union legally recognized in a Soviet-bloc country, and it grew into a broad social movement of some ten million members that played a central role in the fall of communism in Poland and, by example, across Eastern Europe.

Origins

In August 1980, strikes triggered by food price rises spread from the Gdańsk shipyard, where workers demanded the reinstatement of crane operator Anna Walentynowicz and free trade unions. The resulting Gdańsk Agreement of 31 August 1980 conceded the right to strike and to form independent unions. Solidarity's 21 demands also included freedom of speech and release of political prisoners.

Martial law and underground

On 13 December 1981 General Wojciech Jaruzelski imposed martial law; Solidarity was suspended and then outlawed, its leaders interned. The movement survived underground with support from the Catholic Church and Western labour movements. Wałęsa received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1983.

Triumph in 1989

Economic crisis forced the regime into Round Table talks in early 1989. Solidarity was relegalized and won the partly free elections of 4 June 1989 overwhelmingly; in August, Tadeusz Mazowiecki became the first non-communist prime minister in the Soviet bloc, and Wałęsa was elected President of Poland in 1990.

Later history

As a political force Solidarity fragmented, though the union survives as one of Poland's major labour federations. Its red logo, drawn by Jerzy Janiszewski, remains an international symbol of peaceful resistance.
