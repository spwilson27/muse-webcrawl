The Chilean War of Independence was a military and political event that allowed Chile to emancipate itself from Spanish rule and begin its life as an independent republic. It developed in the context of the Spanish American wars of independence, in which patriot forces across South America fought against the Spanish Empire. The process is traditionally divided into three stages: the Patria Vieja (Old Homeland, 1810–1814), the Reconquista (Reconquest, 1814–1817), and the Patria Nueva (New Homeland, 1817–1823 and beyond).

Background

In 1808 Napoleon Bonaparte invaded Spain and placed his brother Joseph on the Spanish throne, precipitating a crisis of legitimacy throughout the Spanish Empire. In the Captaincy General of Chile, criollo elites debated whether to remain loyal to the imprisoned Ferdinand VII or to establish a local governing junta. On 18 September 1810, an open cabildo in Santiago established the First Government Junta of Chile, an event now celebrated as the beginning of Chilean independence.

Patria Vieja (1810–1814)

During the first stage, figures such as José Miguel Carrera pushed the patriotic cause forward, creating national symbols including a flag and coat of arms, and founding the Instituto Nacional and the National Library. Divisions among the patriots, however, weakened their position. Royalist forces dispatched from the Viceroyalty of Peru defeated the patriots at the Battle of Rancagua on 1–2 October 1814, ending the Patria Vieja and driving many patriot leaders into exile in Mendoza, Argentina.

Reconquista (1814–1817)

Under governors Mariano Osorio and Casimiro Marcó del Pont, royalist authorities attempted to restore absolutist rule, persecuting patriots through measures such as exile to the Juan Fernández Islands. Meanwhile, in Mendoza, exiled Chileans joined the Army of the Andes being organized by José de San Martín, with Bernardo O'Higgins as one of its principal officers. Manuel Rodríguez waged guerrilla warfare inside Chile to distract the royalists.

Patria Nueva (1817–1826)

In January–February 1817 the Army of the Andes crossed the Andes mountains in one of the great military feats of the era and defeated the royalists at the Battle of Chacabuco on 12 February 1817. O'Higgins became Supreme Director of Chile. Although the patriots were surprised and defeated at Cancha Rayada in March 1818, San Martín's forces won a decisive victory at the Battle of Maipú on 5 April 1818, securing Chilean independence, which had been formally declared on 12 February 1818.

The naval campaign led by Lord Thomas Cochrane, including the capture of the Spanish stronghold of Valdivia in 1820, weakened royalist power further. Royalist resistance continued in the south in the so-called Guerra a Muerte (War to the Death) and on the island of Chiloé, which was not incorporated into Chile until the Treaty of Tantauco in 1826.

Legacy

The war established the Republic of Chile and set the institutional foundations of the new state, though political instability continued until the conservative settlement of the 1830s. The principal dates of the independence process — 18 September 1810 and the victories of Chacabuco and Maipú — remain central to Chilean national commemoration.
