Grafting

Grafting or graftage is a horticultural technique in which tissues of plants are joined so as to continue their growth together. The upper part of the combined plant is called the scion, and the lower part is called the rootstock. The technique is used to propagate desirable cultivars asexually, because seedlings grown from seed do not reliably reproduce the parent variety's fruit characteristics.

Reasons for grafting

  • Preserving cultivars: apple, grape, citrus, and many ornamental varieties are maintained by grafting rather than seed.
  • Disease and soil adaptation: rootstocks can confer resistance to soil-borne pests and diseases; European wine grapes (Vitis vinifera) are grafted onto American rootstocks resistant to the phylloxera louse that devastated vineyards in the 19th century.
  • Size control: dwarfing rootstocks produce small trees suited to orchards and gardens.
  • Faster fruiting: a scion from a mature tree can bear fruit years sooner than a seedling.
  • Repair and novelty: damaged trees can be bridge-grafted, and several varieties can be grown on a single tree.

Techniques

Successful grafting requires aligning the vascular cambium layers of scion and stock. Common methods include whip and tongue grafting, cleft grafting, bark grafting, approach grafting, and bud grafting (budding), in which a single bud—such as in T-budding or chip budding—is inserted under the stock's bark. The union is usually wrapped or waxed to prevent drying while callus tissue forms and the vascular tissues reconnect. Compatibility is generally greatest between closely related plants, usually within a genus, though intergeneric grafts are sometimes possible within a family.

Natural and scientific grafting

Natural grafting occurs when roots or branches of adjacent trees fuse (inosculation). In research, grafting is used to study long-distance signalling in plants; in medicine, the analogous concept includes skin grafts and other tissue transplants.
