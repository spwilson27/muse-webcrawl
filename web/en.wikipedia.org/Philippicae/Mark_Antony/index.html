Mark Antony (Latin: Marcus Antonius; January 14, 83 BC – August 1, 30 BC), commonly known in English as Mark Antony, was a Roman politician and general who played a critical role in the transformation of the Roman Republic from a constitutional republic into the autocratic Roman Empire. A supporter of Julius Caesar, he formed an official political alliance, the Second Triumvirate, with Octavian and Lepidus after Caesar's assassination. Their triumvirate collapsed after Lepidus's exile, and Antony — governing the East and allied with Cleopatra of Egypt — was defeated by Octavian at the Battle of Actium in 31 BC and died by suicide the following year.

Early career and Caesar

Antony was born in Rome to Marcus Antonius Creticus and Julia, a cousin of Julius Caesar. After a famously dissolute youth, he distinguished himself as a cavalry commander in the East under Gabinius, fighting in Judaea and Egypt. Joining Caesar in Gaul, he served as quaestor, legate and military tribune; as tribune of the plebs in 49 BC he championed Caesar's cause in Rome before fleeing to join him, precipitating the Civil War. He fought at Pharsalus (48 BC), administered Italy chaotically in Caesar's absence, and as Caesar's co-consul in 44 BC was at his side in the final months of his life.

After the Ides of March

Following Caesar's murder on March 15, 44 BC, Antony seized the political initiative, securing an amnesty for the assassins while obtaining public honours for Caesar. At Caesar's funeral he delivered the famous oration — dramatised by Shakespeare — that turned the Roman crowd against the conspirators. His increasingly autocratic conduct brought him into conflict with the Senate and with Cicero, whose fourteen Philippics — a sustained exercise in political invective modelled on Demosthenes — attacked Antony's character and ambition and urged war against him. Defeated at Mutina in 43 BC, Antony regrouped in Gaul and, in November 43 BC, formed the Second Triumvirate with Octavian and Lepidus, legalised by the Lex Titia. The triumvirs' proscriptions killed thousands, including Cicero, whose head and hands Antony had displayed in the Forum.

Triumvir in the East

After defeating Brutus and Cassius at Philippi (42 BC), the triumvirs divided the Roman world: Antony took the wealthy East. From Ephesus and Athens he reorganised the eastern provinces and client kingdoms, summoned Cleopatra to Tarsus in 41 BC — their meeting inaugurated the alliance and love affair central to his later legend — and campaigned, disastrously, against Parthia in 36 BC. His political marriage to Octavian's sister Octavia (40 BC) briefly stabilised the triumvirate, but he returned to Cleopatra, celebrated a triumph in Alexandria, and in the Donations of Alexandria (34 BC) granted Roman-claimed territories to Cleopatra and their children.

War with Octavian, Actium, death

Octavian waged a brilliant propaganda campaign against Antony in Rome, publishing his will and portraying him as bewitched by a foreign queen. The Senate declared war on Cleopatra in 32 BC. At the naval Battle of Actium on September 2, 31 BC, Antony and Cleopatra's fleet was routed; they fled to Egypt with the remnant of their forces. When Octavian invaded in 30 BC and Antony's troops deserted, Antony, falsely told that Cleopatra was dead, fell on his sword. He died in her arms in Alexandria on August 1, 30 BC, and was buried beside her after her own suicide days later.

Legacy

Antony's historical image was shaped by the victors' propaganda and by Plutarch's Life of Antony, the main source for Shakespeare's Antony and Cleopatra, which fixed the portrait of the great Roman soldier undone by love, luxury and fortune. Modern historians give a more balanced account of an able general and administrator whose eastern settlement largely endured, while recognising the political misjudgements that cost him the Roman world.
