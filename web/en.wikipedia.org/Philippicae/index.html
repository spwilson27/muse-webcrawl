Philippicae

Cicero denouncing Mark Antony before the Senate.

The Philippicae or Philippics are a series of 14 speeches composed by the Roman statesman and orator Marcus Tullius Cicero in 44 and 43 BC, denouncing Mark Antony. Cicero likened these speeches to those of Demosthenes against Philip II of Macedon; both Demosthenes's speeches and Cicero's are known as Philippics. Cicero's Second Philippic is famous as an example of sustained political invective.

Background

After the assassination of Julius Caesar on the Ides of March 44 BC, Cicero initially supported an amnesty but turned against Antony as Antony consolidated power, seized Caesar's papers and funds, and maneuvered against the liberators. In September 44 BC, Cicero began delivering a series of speeches in the Senate and before the people, attacking Antony's conduct and urging the Senate to support the young Octavian and declare Antony a public enemy.

Content

The First Philippic (2 September 44 BC) was a relatively moderate critique of Antony's legislation. Antony replied with threats, and Cicero answered with the Second Philippic — never actually delivered, but circulated as a pamphlet — a blistering personal attack accusing Antony of drunkenness, debauchery, cowardice and theft. The remaining twelve speeches (December 44 – April 43 BC) dealt with events as the crisis deepened: the siege of Mutina, embassies to Antony, and honours for his opponents.

Consequences

The speeches helped bring about war between the Senate and Antony, but their success was brief. Octavian reconciled with Antony and formed the Second Triumvirate with Lepidus; Cicero was proscribed, and on 7 December 43 BC he was executed. According to tradition, Antony had Cicero's head and hands — the hands that wrote the Philippics — nailed to the Rostra in the Forum.

Legacy

The word philippic entered English (via Latin and French) as a general term for a fiery denunciatory speech. The Philippics are regarded as the summit of Cicero's oratory and a classic of political rhetoric.
