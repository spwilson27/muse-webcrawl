Invective (from Late Latin invectiva, "a reproachful speech") is abusive, denunciatory or highly critical language used to express blame or to attack a person or institution. As a recognised rhetorical genre in classical antiquity (Greek psogos, blame, the counterpart of encomium, praise), invective followed conventional topics — mocking an opponent's birth, education, appearance, character, private vices and public failures — while serving purposes that ranged from courtroom character assassination to political warfare and literary entertainment.

Classical invective

Greek oratory and Old Comedy supplied early models: Aristophanes pilloried politicians on the comic stage, and Demosthenes' Philippics against Philip II of Macedon (4th century BC) became the archetype of the political denunciation speech, though his exchanges with Aeschines show invective aimed at a personal rival — ancestry, cowardice and venality are all stock charges. In Rome, Cicero raised the genre to an art: the In Pisonem, In Verrem and above all the fourteen Philippics (44–43 BC) against Mark Antony deploy irony, rhetorical questions, vivid character sketches and systematic attacks on drunkenness, lust, greed and treason. Catullus's obscene epigrams, Horace's and Juvenal's satires, and Claudian's late-antique In Rufinum and In Eutropium extended the tradition in verse.

Stock topics

Rhetorical handbooks codified the topoi of blame. Typical lines of attack included low or foreign birth; physical deformity; servile or disreputable occupations; poverty or debt; sexual misconduct; drunkenness; cowardice in war; cruelty; impiety; and betrayal of friends or country. Because the charges were conventional, ancient audiences did not necessarily take them as factual; their purpose was to destroy an opponent's ethos — his credibility and standing — before judges or assemblies.

Later tradition

Invective flourished again in the Renaissance, when humanists such as Poggio Bracciolini and Lorenzo Valla exchanged learned invectivae in Latin, and in Reformation pamphlet wars, where Luther and his opponents rivalled each other in vituperation. The English tradition produced lampoons and "flyting" contests, the formalised exchange of abuse in verse. In modern usage, "invective" denotes any forceful, insulting denunciation — in parliamentary clashes, pamphlets, polemics and journalism — and the "philippic" remains a synonym for a bitter verbal onslaught.

In rhetoric and law

Within rhetoric, invective belongs to the strategies of ethos and pathos: it discredits the opponent and rouses the audience's contempt or indignation. Its excesses are policed by the laws of defamation and by rules of parliamentary and courtroom decorum, and rhetoricians from Quintilian onward debated its propriety — Quintilian warned that abuse, though sometimes effective, could rebound on the speaker. Modern rhetorical criticism studies invective as a strategy of polarisation and as a boundary-marking device by which communities define insiders and outsiders.
