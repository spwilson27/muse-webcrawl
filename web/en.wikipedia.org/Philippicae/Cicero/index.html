Marcus Tullius Cicero (January 3, 106 BC – December 7, 43 BC) was a Roman statesman, lawyer, scholar, philosopher, writer and Academic sceptic who tried, in the last years of the Roman Republic, to uphold optimate principles during the political crises that led to the establishment of the Roman Empire. He is widely considered Rome's greatest orator and prose stylist, and his extensive writings — speeches, treatises and more than 900 surviving letters — are a primary source for the history of the late Republic.

Early life and career

Cicero was born in Arpinum, a hill town in Latium, into a wealthy equestrian family. Educated in Rome and Greece in law, rhetoric and philosophy, he made his name as an advocate, notably defending Sextus Roscius in 80 BC. A novus homo ("new man") without noble ancestors, he climbed the cursus honorum by talent: quaestor in Sicily (75 BC), where he prosecuted the corrupt governor Verres in the In Verrem speeches; praetor (66 BC); and consul in 63 BC, when he suppressed the conspiracy of Catiline, delivering the four Catilinarian Orations and having the conspirators executed — an act for which he was later exiled (58–57 BC).

Statesman in a collapsing republic

Recalled from exile, Cicero struggled against the ascendancy of Caesar, Pompey and Crassus. During Caesar's civil war he reluctantly joined Pompey; pardoned by Caesar, he withdrew from politics and, in the personal crisis after his daughter Tullia's death, produced his great philosophical works. After Caesar's assassination in March 44 BC, Cicero emerged as the Senate's leading voice against Mark Antony. His fourteen Philippics against Antony — modelled on, and named after, Demosthenes' speeches against Philip of Macedon — are among the supreme examples of Roman oratory and invective. The gamble failed: when Octavian, Antony and Lepidus formed the Second Triumvirate, Cicero was proscribed, overtaken near Formiae on December 7, 43 BC, and murdered; his head and hands were displayed on the Rostra in the Forum.

Works and thought

Cicero's speeches (58 survive in whole or part), rhetorical treatises (De Oratore, Brutus, Orator) and philosophical dialogues transmitted Greek philosophy to Latin readers, coining much of Latin's philosophical vocabulary. In De Re Publica and De Legibus he defended the mixed republican constitution and natural law; De Officiis (On Duties), addressed to his son, became a staple of Western moral education; the Tusculan Disputations, De Natura Deorum, De Senectute and De Amicitia popularised Stoic, Epicurean and Academic arguments. His personal letters to Atticus, Quintus and others offer an unmatched insider's record of Roman politics.

Legacy

Cicero's Latin set the standard for classical prose and was central to the Renaissance humanism of Petrarch (who rediscovered his letters) and to early-modern republican and natural-law thought, influencing Locke, Montesquieu and the American Founders. His reputation has oscillated between admiration as a defender of the republic and criticism of his vanity and vacillation, but his corpus remains foundational for the study of Roman law, rhetoric and philosophy.
