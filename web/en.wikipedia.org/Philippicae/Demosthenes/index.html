Demosthenes (384 – October 12, 322 BC) was a Greek statesman and orator of ancient Athens. His orations constitute the last significant expression of Athenian intellectual and political life and provide a thorough insight into the politics and culture of ancient Greece during the 4th century BC. The Alexandrian canon compiled by Aristophanes of Byzantium and Aristarchus of Samothrace ranked him among the ten greatest Attic orators and authors, and Cicero considered him the perfect orator.

Early life and training

Demosthenes was born in Athens to a wealthy sword-maker of the same name. Orphaned at seven, he was cheated of his inheritance by his guardians and, on reaching adulthood, sued them in speeches that marked the start of his career as a professional speechwriter (logographer). He studied under Isaeus and, according to tradition, overcame natural disadvantages — a weak voice and a speech defect — by rigorous exercises: declaiming with pebbles in his mouth, reciting verses while running uphill, and practising before a mirror. He also served at the Battle of Chaeronea and first entered public life as a legal advocate.

Career and the struggle against Macedon

From the 350s BC Demosthenes became Athens' leading voice warning against the expansion of Philip II of Macedon. His Philippics (351–340 BC) and Olynthiacs (349 BC) urged the Athenians to fund fleets, use the theoric fund for defence and resist Philip before it was too late; the speeches gave their name to the genre of fierce political denunciation — the "philippic." He helped build the coalition that fought Philip at Chaeronea in 338 BC, where Macedon crushed the Greek allies; Demosthenes delivered the funeral oration for the Athenian dead. In 330 BC he crowned his career by defending, in On the Crown, his whole anti-Macedonian policy against his rival Aeschines, in what is often judged the greatest speech of antiquity.

Downfall and death

In 324 BC Demosthenes was implicated in the Harpalus affair — the disappearance of money brought to Athens by Alexander the Great's fugitive treasurer — fined and driven into exile. Recalled after Alexander's death in 323 BC, he roused Athens for the Lamian War against Macedon. When that cause failed, the Macedonian regent Antipater demanded his surrender; Demosthenes fled to the temple of Poseidon on the island of Calauria and took poison on October 12, 322 BC, as Antipater's men arrived.

Speeches and legacy

Sixty-one speeches transmitted under Demosthenes' name survive (some probably by other hands), along with letters and proems. They include public deliberative orations, forensic speeches for private clients, and display pieces. His style — carefully structured argument, periodic sentences and controlled passion — became the standard of Greek prose for later rhetoric. Cicero modelled his own Philippics against Mark Antony on Demosthenes' example, and Quintilian and Dionysius of Halicarnassus canonised him as the supreme orator, a judgement repeated by critics ever since.
