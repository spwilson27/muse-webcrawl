Sequence alignment

In bioinformatics, a sequence alignment is a way of arranging the sequences of DNA, RNA, or protein to identify regions of similarity that may be a consequence of functional, structural, or evolutionary relationships between the sequences. Aligned sequences of nucleotide or amino acid residues are typically represented as rows within a matrix, with gaps inserted between residues so that identical or similar characters are aligned in successive columns.

Pairwise alignment

For two sequences, an alignment is scored using substitution scores (for example the PAM or BLOSUM matrices for proteins) and gap penalties. The Needleman–Wunsch algorithm (1970) finds the optimal global alignment of two sequences by dynamic programming, while the Smith–Waterman algorithm (1981) finds optimal local alignments of similar substrings. Because exact dynamic programming is quadratic in sequence length, heuristic search tools such as FASTA and BLAST are used to find similar sequences in large databases rapidly.

Multiple sequence alignment

Aligning more than two sequences — a multiple sequence alignment (MSA) — is computationally hard, so practical tools such as Clustal, MUSCLE, and MAFFT use progressive or iterative heuristics: pairwise distances guide a guide tree, and sequences are aligned in order of relatedness. MSAs are fundamental for phylogenetic analysis, predicting protein structure and function, and identifying conserved motifs and active sites.

Interpretation

If two sequences in an alignment share a sufficiently large region of similarity, they are inferred to be homologous — descended from a common ancestor — since convergent similarity of long sequences is improbable. Statistical measures such as BLAST's E-value quantify this. Alignment is also the basis of genome assembly, read mapping in sequencing (with aligners such as BWA and Bowtie), and variant detection.
