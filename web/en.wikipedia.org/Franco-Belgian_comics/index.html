Franco-Belgian comics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Franco-Belgian comics (French: bande dessinée franco-belge) are comics that are created for a Francophone European market, principally France and Belgium. Together with American comics and Japanese manga, they form one of the three great traditions of the world comics industry. The field is often described in French simply as la BD and was recognized as the "ninth art".

Format

The characteristic format is the hardcover album: a full-color book, typically 48 to 64 pages in A4 size, collecting a complete story or episode of a series. Stories were traditionally serialized first in weekly magazines such as Spirou, Tintin and Pilote before album publication.

History

Belgian comics flowered after the Second World War around Journal de Tintin and Spirou. Hergé's The Adventures of Tintin, drawn in his clear-line (ligne claire) style, and the Marcinelle school of Spirou artists such as Jijé, Franquin (Spirou et Fantasio, Gaston), Peyo (The Smurfs) and Morris (Lucky Luke) defined the classic era. In France, René Goscinny and Albert Uderzo created Asterix in 1959, which became the best-selling European comics series. From the 1970s, magazines such as Métal Hurlant pushed the medium toward adult science fiction with artists like Mœbius, while publishers Casterman, Dupuis, Le Lombard and Dargaud anchored the industry. The Angoulême International Comics Festival, founded in 1974, became the field's premier event.

Notable series

  • The Adventures of Tintin (Hergé)
  • Asterix (Goscinny and Uderzo)
  • Lucky Luke (Morris and Goscinny)
  • The Smurfs (Peyo)
  • Spirou et Fantasio (Franquin and others)
  • Blueberry (Charlier and Giraud)
  • Thorgal (Van Hamme and Rosiński)

Cultural status

Comics enjoy unusual institutional prestige in France and Belgium: Brussels hosts the Belgian Comic Strip Center and a Comic Strip Route of murals, leading characters such as Tintin are national icons, and the state officially supports the medium as an art form.
