Agency cost

An agency cost is an economic concept that refers to the cost incurred by a principal when an agent makes decisions on their behalf that diverge from the principal's interests. The concept is central to agency theory and corporate finance, notably in the work of Jensen and Meckling (1976), who analyzed the conflicts between shareholders (principals) and managers (agents) in the modern corporation.

Components

Jensen and Meckling decomposed agency costs into three parts: monitoring expenditures by the principal (audits, boards, reporting systems); bonding expenditures by the agent (contractual guarantees, deferred compensation, non-compete clauses); and the residual loss — the remaining divergence in outcomes even after monitoring and bonding. Agency costs of debt also arise when shareholders and creditors conflict, for example through incentives to take excessive risk or underinvest.

Governance responses

Mechanisms to reduce agency costs include independent boards, performance-linked pay and equity ownership by managers, takeover markets, debt discipline, disclosure, and shareholder activism. The separation of ownership and control, analyzed by Berle and Means in 1932, remains the classic setting in which agency costs arise.
