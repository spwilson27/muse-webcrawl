Conversion (law)

In law, conversion is an intentional tort to personal property where the defendant's intentional exercise of dominion or control over the plaintiff's property so seriously interferes with the plaintiff's rights that the defendant may be required to pay the full value of the property as damages. Conversion applies to tangible personal property (chattels), not to real property (land).

AreaTort law
Applies toPersonal property
Typical damagesFull value of the property

Elements

A claimant must generally show that they owned or had an immediate right to possession of the property, that the defendant intentionally exercised dominion or control over it, and that this seriously interfered with the claimant's rights. Unlike the tort of trespass to chattels, good faith is usually no defence: a person who innocently buys stolen goods can still be liable in conversion.

Acts of conversion

Examples include taking property without authority, wrongfully transferring or selling it, seriously damaging or destroying it, and refusing to return it after a lawful demand by the owner. Because damages are measured by the full value of the goods, a successful claim effectively forces a sale of the property to the defendant.

Equitable conversion

The term is also used in equity: equitable conversion is the doctrine under which, once a contract for the sale of land is signed, equity treats the buyer as the owner of the land and the seller as owner of the purchase money. See also trover and tort.
