Umayyad Mosque

The Umayyad Mosque (Arabic: ٱلْجَامِع ٱلْأُمَوِي), also known as the Great Mosque of Damascus, is one of the largest and oldest mosques in the world, located in the old city of Damascus, Syria. It was built between 705 and 715 by the Umayyad caliph al-Walid I on the site of a Roman temple to Jupiter and a Byzantine cathedral dedicated to John the Baptist, whose relics the mosque still houses. It is regarded by many Muslims as the fourth holiest site in Islam.

History

The site has been sacred for millennia: an Aramaean temple to Hadad stood here in the Iron Age, followed by a Roman temple of Jupiter under the empire and, from the late 4th century, a Christian cathedral. After the Muslim conquest of Damascus in 634, the building was shared for a time between Christians and Muslims until al-Walid I purchased the site and built the mosque, incorporating the Roman temenos walls and towers. Its mosaics of gold and glass, depicting lush landscapes and the Barada river, remain masterpieces of Umayyad art.

Religious significance

The mosque contains a shrine traditionally identified as holding the head of John the Baptist (Yahya in Islam), discovered during construction and revered by both Muslims and Christians; Pope John Paul II visited it in 2001, the first pope to enter a mosque. According to Islamic tradition, the southeastern Minaret of Jesus (Isa) is where Jesus will descend at the end of time. The mosque also includes a shrine marking the place where the head of Husayn ibn Ali was kept after the Battle of Karbala.

Architecture

The mosque's vast courtyard (sahn) is paved with marble and surrounded by arcades. The prayer hall along the southern side has three aisles divided by classical columns and is surmounted by the Dome of the Eagle. Notable elements include the Dome of the Treasury, an octagonal pavilion on columns in the courtyard, and extensive later restorations after fires in 1069, 1401 (under Timur) and 1893.
