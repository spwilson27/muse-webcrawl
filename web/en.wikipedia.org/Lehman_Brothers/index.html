Lehman Brothers Holdings Inc. was an American global financial services firm founded in 1850. Before filing for bankruptcy in 2008, Lehman was the fourth-largest investment bank in the United States, behind Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley, and Merrill Lynch, with about 25,000 employees worldwide. Its bankruptcy filing on September 15, 2008, listing $639 billion in assets, remains the largest in United States history and is widely regarded as the pivotal event of the financial crisis of 2007–2008.

History

The firm began in 1844 when Henry Lehman, a German Jewish immigrant from Rimpar, Bavaria, opened a dry-goods store in Montgomery, Alabama. His brothers Emanuel and Mayer joined him, and by 1850 the business traded as Lehman Brothers, dealing in cotton and other commodities. After the American Civil War the firm moved its headquarters to New York City, where it became a founding member of the New York Cotton Exchange and later a member of the New York Stock Exchange.

Through the twentieth century Lehman grew into a major underwriter of securities, advising on landmark transactions and financing railroads, retailers, and entertainment companies. In 1969 it merged with Kuhn, Loeb & Co. to form Lehman Brothers, Kuhn, Loeb Inc. The firm was acquired by American Express in 1984 and spun off as an independent public company in 1994.

Collapse

Under chief executive Richard Fuld, Lehman aggressively expanded into mortgage origination and securitization in the 2000s, acquiring subprime lenders such as BNC Mortgage. By 2007 the firm held enormous positions in mortgage-backed securities. As housing prices fell and defaults rose, Lehman's leverage — roughly 30 dollars of assets for every dollar of equity — left it acutely vulnerable.

In September 2008, after takeover talks with Bank of America and Barclays failed and the United States government declined to rescue the firm, Lehman filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection in the early hours of September 15. The failure triggered panic across global markets, froze money-market funds, and pushed the world economy into the Great Recession. Barclays acquired Lehman's North American investment banking business, and Nomura Holdings bought its Asian and European operations; roughly 10,000 employees kept their jobs under the new owners.

Legacy

The Lehman collapse prompted sweeping regulatory reform, including the Dodd–Frank Act of 2010, and remains a central case study in systemic risk, moral hazard, and the "too big to fail" debate. The bankruptcy estate spent more than a decade winding down, ultimately returning over $100 billion to creditors.
