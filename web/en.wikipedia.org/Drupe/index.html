Drupe

In botany, a drupe (or stone fruit) is a fruit in which an outer fleshy part — the exocarp (skin) and mesocarp (flesh) — surrounds a single hardened endocarp, the "stone" or "pit", which contains the seed. Drupes develop from a single carpel and are typically indehiscent, meaning they do not split open to release the seed.

Examples

Familiar drupes include the stone fruits of the genus Prunus — peach, plum, cherry, apricot, almond and nectarine — as well as olive, mango, date, and coffee (whose "beans" are the seeds of a drupe). The coconut is a fibrous drupe whose mesocarp is dry and husky rather than fleshy. Aggregates of small drupes, called drupelets, form fruits such as the raspberry and blackberry.

Structure and dispersal

The three pericarp layers — thin exocarp, fleshy mesocarp, and lignified endocarp — protect the seed and attract animal dispersers, which eat the flesh and void or discard the stone. Some endocarps are themselves the valued product: the almond is the seed within the pit, and walnut and pecan are drupaceous nuts whose husk is removed before sale.
