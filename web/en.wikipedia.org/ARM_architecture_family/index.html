ARM architecture family

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

ARM designs power the vast majority of the world's mobile devices.

ARM (stylised in lowercase as arm, and previously an acronym for Advanced RISC Machines, originally Acorn RISC Machine) is a family of reduced instruction set computer (RISC) instruction set architectures (ISAs) for computer processors, configured for various environments. Arm Ltd. develops the ISAs and licenses them to other companies, who build products that use them. Arm Ltd. also designs processor cores that implement these ISAs.

Due to their low cost, modest power consumption, and low heat generation, ARM processors are useful for light, portable, battery-powered devices, including smartphones, laptops, and tablet computers, as well as embedded systems. By the 2020s, ARM-based chips had also become prominent in servers and supercomputers; the Fugaku supercomputer, built with ARM processors, topped the TOP500 list in 2020. ARM processors have been shipped in the hundreds of billions, making ARM the most widely used ISA family in the world.

History

The ARM architecture was originally developed at Acorn Computers in Cambridge, England. The first ARM processor, designed principally by Sophie Wilson and Steve Furber, was simulated in 1983 and first produced in silicon on 26 April 1985. It powered the Acorn Archimedes range. In 1990 the ARM division was spun out as Advanced RISC Machines Ltd, a joint venture between Acorn, Apple, and VLSI Technology, formed to develop the processor for Apple's Newton handheld device.

ARM's licensing model — selling intellectual property rather than manufacturing chips — proved highly successful during the mobile phone boom. In 2016, SoftBank Group acquired Arm for about £24.3 billion (US$32 billion). A planned acquisition by Nvidia, announced in 2020 at US$40 billion, was abandoned in 2022 following regulatory opposition. Arm returned to the public markets with an IPO on Nasdaq in September 2023.

Architecture

ARM architectures emphasise energy efficiency and a clean load-store design. The 32-bit ARMv7 generation introduced the Thumb-2 mixed 16/32-bit instruction encoding for code density. The 64-bit architecture, AArch64, arrived with ARMv8-A in 2011 and underpins modern smartphone processors as well as Apple silicon for Mac computers. Processor families are organised into profiles: Cortex-A for applications processors, Cortex-R for real-time systems, and Cortex-M for microcontrollers.

Licensing

Companies license ARM in two main ways: by licensing ready-made Cortex core designs, or by taking an architecture licence that permits designing custom cores implementing the ARM ISA, as Apple, Qualcomm, and Amazon have done. Licensees include most major semiconductor companies, and ARM-based systems on a chip are produced by firms such as Samsung, MediaTek, Broadcom, and NXP.

Uses

ARM processors are found in nearly all smartphones and tablets, most smart televisions and wearables, networking equipment, automotive systems, and an increasing share of cloud servers, including Amazon's Graviton processors. Apple's transition of the Mac line to ARM-based Apple silicon, beginning with the M1 chip in 2020, brought the architecture to mainstream personal computers.
