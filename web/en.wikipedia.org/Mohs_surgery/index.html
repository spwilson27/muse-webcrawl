Mohs surgery


Developer: Frederic E. Mohs (1930s)
Used for: Basal cell carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma
Cure rates: up to 99% for primary basal cell carcinoma

Mohs surgery, developed in 1938 by general surgeon Frederic E. Mohs, is a microscopically controlled surgery used to treat both common and rare types of skin cancer. During the surgery, after each removal of tissue, the surgeon examines it under a microscope; tissue removal continues until no cancerous cells remain, allowing complete removal of the cancer while sparing as much healthy tissue as possible.

Procedure

The procedure is performed in stages under local anaesthesia, usually in an outpatient setting. A thin layer of tissue around and beneath the visible tumour is excised, mapped and colour-coded, frozen, sectioned horizontally, and examined. Because the entire margin is examined — unlike the "bread-loaf" sampling of standard excision — any remaining tumour can be precisely located and another layer removed only where needed. The process is repeated until the margins are clear, after which the wound is repaired.

Indications and outcomes

Mohs surgery is preferred for cancers on cosmetically or functionally sensitive areas such as the face, ears, and hands; for recurrent cancers; and for aggressive or poorly defined tumours. Reported five-year cure rates are as high as 99% for primary basal cell carcinoma and around 97% for recurrent tumours, the highest of any skin cancer treatment.
