Hellenistic period

In classical antiquity, the Hellenistic period covers the time in Mediterranean and West Asian history following Classical Greece, between the death of Alexander the Great in 323 BC and the death of Cleopatra VII of Egypt in 30 BC (or the Battle of Actium in 31 BC), which saw the annexation of the last major Hellenistic kingdom by the Roman Republic.

The term "Hellenistic" (from the Greek Hellēnistḗs, "one who uses Greek language and customs") was coined by the historian Johann Gustav Droysen in the 19th century. The period saw Greek culture spread across an enormous area — from Greece and Egypt to Mesopotamia, Persia and Central Asia — blending with local traditions.

The Diadochi and the kingdoms

After Alexander's death, his generals (the Diadochi, "Successors") fought a series of wars over his empire. By the early 3rd century BC three great dynasties had stabilized: the Ptolemies in Egypt, the Seleucids in Asia (from Syria to Bactria), and the Antigonids in Macedon and Greece. Smaller kingdoms such as Pergamon under the Attalids, Bactria, and later Pontus and the Maccabean kingdom in Judea also flourished.

Culture and learning

Hellenistic cities — above all Alexandria in Egypt, with its Great Library and Museum — became centers of scholarship, science and art. This era produced Euclid's geometry, Archimedes' mathematics and engineering, Eratosthenes' measurement of the Earth's circumference, Aristarchus's heliocentric hypothesis, and advances in medicine by Herophilus and Erasistratus. Koine Greek served as the lingua franca of the eastern Mediterranean. Philosophy flourished in the new schools of Stoicism, Epicureanism, Cynicism and Skepticism, while art favored drama, realism and emotion, as in the Winged Victory of Samothrace and the Laocoön group.

Economy and society

Kings founded dozens of new cities, which acted as engines of trade and Greek settlement. Long-distance commerce linked the Mediterranean with Arabia, India and even China along routes later incorporated into the Silk Road. Ruler cults, mystery religions, and syncretic deities such as Serapis reflected the era's cosmopolitan religious mixing.

Decline and Roman conquest

From the 2nd century BC, the expanding Roman Republic progressively absorbed the Hellenistic states: Macedon fell in 168 BC, Greece followed, the Seleucid realm shrank before Rome and Parthia, and finally Ptolemaic Egypt — the last Hellenistic kingdom — was annexed after Octavian's victory at Actium in 31 BC and Cleopatra's death in 30 BC. Greek culture, however, continued to dominate the eastern Roman world for centuries.
