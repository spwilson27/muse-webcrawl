Social Security


A United States Social Security card

Social security is the principle of a government-administered safety net providing financial protection to people against the economic risks of old age, disability, unemployment, sickness, and poverty. The term may refer broadly to social welfare and social insurance programmes in general, or specifically to the federal Social Security programme of the United States, created by the Social Security Act of 1935, which provides retirement, disability, and survivors benefits funded by payroll taxes.

United States programme

In the United States, Social Security is primarily the Old-Age, Survivors, and Disability Insurance (OASDI) programme administered by the Social Security Administration. Workers and employers each pay a payroll tax under the Federal Insurance Contributions Act (FICA) on earnings up to a taxable maximum. Benefits are paid monthly to retired and disabled workers, their dependents, and survivors of deceased workers. In 2024, roughly 68 million people received benefits, and the programme is the largest single item of federal government spending, at about $1.5 trillion annually.

Eligibility for retirement benefits generally requires 40 credits (about ten years of covered work); full retirement age ranges from 66 to 67 depending on year of birth, with reduced benefits available from age 62 and increased benefits for delaying to age 70. Long-running demographic trends—lower birth rates and longer retirements—have led the trustees to project that the combined trust funds will be depleted in the mid-2030s, after which payroll tax revenue would cover roughly four-fifths of scheduled benefits unless Congress acts.

International usage

Internationally, "social security" describes a wide range of systems. Bismarckian systems, originating in 1880s Germany, tie benefits to employment contributions; Beveridge-style systems, following the United Kingdom's 1942 report, provide flat-rate benefits funded from general taxation. The International Labour Organization's Social Security (Minimum Standards) Convention, 1952 (No. 102) sets benchmarks covering medical care, sickness, unemployment, old age, employment injury, family, maternity, invalidity, and survivors' benefits.
