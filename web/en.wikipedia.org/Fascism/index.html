Fascism

Fascism is a far-right, authoritarian and ultranationalist political ideology and movement, characterised by a dictatorial leader, centralised autocracy, militarism, forcible suppression of opposition, belief in a natural social hierarchy, subordination of individual interests for the perceived good of the nation or race, and strong regimentation of society and the economy.

Origins

Fascism rose to prominence in early-20th-century Europe in the aftermath of the First World War. The first fascist movements emerged in Italy, where Benito Mussolini founded the Fasci Italiani di Combattimento in 1919, drawing the name from the fasces, a bundle of rods symbolising strength through unity. Mussolini's National Fascist Party seized power in 1922 following the March on Rome and established a one-party dictatorship.

Characteristics

Scholars disagree on a precise definition, but common features identified in the literature include palingenetic ultranationalism (a myth of national rebirth), rejection of both liberal democracy and Marxist socialism, a cult of the leader, glorification of violence and war, corporatist economic organisation, and the subordination of the individual to the state. Fascist regimes in Italy and elsewhere suppressed independent trade unions, opposition parties and a free press.

Spread and Second World War

Fascist and fascist-influenced movements appeared across Europe in the interwar period. Nazi Germany under Adolf Hitler is often classified as a form of fascism, though Nazism's racial ideology gave it distinctive features. Fascist Italy and Nazi Germany formed the Axis alliance and were defeated in the Second World War (1939–1945), after which fascism was widely discredited; movements describing themselves as fascist have remained marginal, though scholars continue to debate the term's application to later movements.
