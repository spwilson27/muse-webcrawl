Double bass

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The double bass, also known simply as the bass (or, among other names, the contrabass, string bass and upright bass), is the largest and lowest-pitched bowed string instrument in the modern orchestra. It has four strings (sometimes five) tuned in fourths — E1, A1, D2, G2 — and sounds an octave lower than written.

History and construction

The instrument evolved in 16th- and 17th-century Europe, combining features of the viol and violin families: sloping shoulders betray its viol ancestry, while its carved back and f-holes resemble the violin. Players use either the French (overhand) or German (underhand) bow grip. Because of its size, fractional instruments (3/4, 1/2, 1/4) are used by students and many professionals.

Use

In the orchestra the bass section typically doubles the cellos an octave below, anchoring the harmony, though composers from Haydn and Mozart to Mahler and Stravinsky wrote independent parts, and concertos exist by Bottesini, Koussevitzky and others. In jazz, the pizzicato "walking bass" line is foundational; the bass is also central to bluegrass, rockabilly, blues, tango and early rock and roll, before the electric bass guitar largely took over popular music.
