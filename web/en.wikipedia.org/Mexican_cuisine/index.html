Mexican cuisine

Mexican cuisine is the set of culinary traditions of modern Mexico, blending Indigenous Mesoamerican cooking with European — especially Spanish — elements introduced after the conquest of the Aztec Empire in 1521, along with later African, Asian and Middle Eastern influences. Its core ingredients remain native staples: corn (maize), beans, squash, tomatoes, avocados and chili peppers, complemented by rice, wheat, pork, beef and dairy brought from the Old World. In 2010, traditional Mexican cuisine was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

Elements

Maize, treated with lime in nixtamalization, is made into tortillas, tamales and atoles. Beans and squash complete the ancient "three sisters" agriculture. Sauces are central: raw and cooked salsas and the complex moles — most famously mole poblano — built from chilies, spices, nuts and sometimes chocolate. Herbs such as epazote and hoja santa, and cheeses such as Oaxaca and cotija, vary by region.

Regional cuisines

Oaxaca is famed for its seven moles and tlayudas; Puebla for chiles en nogada and cemitas; Yucatán for cochinita pibil and achiote-based recados; the north for wheat flour tortillas and grilled beef; and the Pacific and Gulf coasts for seafood preparations such as aguachile and pescado a la veracruzana. Street food — tacos in dozens of forms, elotes, quesadillas — is a defining feature of urban eating.

Influence abroad

Mexican restaurants worldwide range from faithful regional cooking to adaptations such as Tex-Mex in the United States, and ingredients including tomatoes, chocolate, vanilla and chilies — all Mesoamerican in origin — transformed cuisines globally after the Columbian exchange.
