Fluvial landforms

Fluvial landforms are the shapes created on the Earth's surface by the action of running water, principally rivers and streams. The study of these features forms a central part of fluvial geomorphology. Running water shapes the landscape through three linked processes: erosion, transportation of sediment, and deposition where the water loses energy.

Erosional landforms

In upland reaches, where gradients and stream energy are high, rivers cut downward to form V-shaped valleys, interlocking spurs, gorges, waterfalls and plunge pools, rapids, and potholes drilled into the channel bed. Lateral erosion on the outside of bends produces river cliffs. Headward erosion extends valleys at their heads, sometimes capturing the drainage of neighbouring streams (river capture).

Depositional and mixed landforms

In lower reaches, reduced gradient encourages deposition and lateral migration. Characteristic features include meanders and the oxbow lakes left when they are cut off; floodplains built from overbank sediment, with raised natural levees along the channel; alluvial fans where streams emerge from mountains; braided channels where sediment supply exceeds transport capacity; terraces recording past floodplain levels abandoned by incision; and deltas where rivers deposit their loads into standing bodies of water, as in the Mississippi, Nile and Ganges–Brahmaputra deltas.

Processes

Fluvial erosion operates by hydraulic action, abrasion (corrasion), attrition and solution (corrosion). Transport occurs as traction (rolling and sliding bedload), saltation, suspension and solution. The balance between erosion and deposition along a river's long profile — described by the concept of the graded river — changes with climate, tectonics, base level and human intervention such as dams and channelization.
