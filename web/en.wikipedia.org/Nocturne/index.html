Nocturne

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A nocturne (from French nocturne, "of the night") is a musical composition that is inspired by, or evocative of, the night. The form was developed principally for the solo piano by the Irish composer John Field in the early nineteenth century and brought to its highest expression by Frédéric Chopin, whose twenty-one nocturnes are among the most beloved works of the Romantic piano repertoire. The term is also applied to night scenes in painting and, in the Roman Catholic liturgy, to a part of the office of Matins.

Musical nocturnes

John Field published his first nocturnes in 1812, creating a character piece in which a lyrical, song-like melody floats over a widely spread arpeggiated accompaniment — a style indebted to Italian opera aria. Chopin, who admired Field's playing, transformed the genre between 1827 and 1846, adding dramatic central sections, operatic ornamentation, and profound emotional ambiguity; famous examples include the Nocturnes Op. 9, the C-sharp minor Nocturne (published posthumously), and the two Nocturnes Op. 27. Later composers of piano nocturnes include Gabriel Fauré, Erik Satie, Alexander Scriabin, and Karol Szymanowski. In orchestral music, the genre extends to the third movement ("Sirènes") of Debussy's Nocturnes (1899) and to night-music movements in works by Bartók and others, characterised by eerie, quiet evocations of natural night sounds.

In painting

In art, "nocturne" denotes a night scene; James McNeill Whistler adopted the musical term for paintings such as Nocturne in Black and Gold — The Falling Rocket (c. 1875), the work at the centre of his famous libel suit against the critic John Ruskin. Rembrandt's The Night Watch is an earlier celebrated example of the night-piece tradition.

In liturgy

In the traditional Roman Breviary, the office of Matins on Sundays and feasts is divided into nocturns — one, two, or three sections of psalms and readings, originally prayed during the night.
