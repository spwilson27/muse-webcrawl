Wood preservation

Wood preservation covers the methods used to protect timber and wood products from decay fungi, wood-boring insects, marine borers and weathering, extending their service life from a few years to several decades. Untreated wood in contact with the ground may fail quickly, whereas properly treated timber is used for railway sleepers, utility poles, fencing, decking and marine piling.

Preservatives

Historically, creosote — a coal-tar distillate — was the dominant industrial preservative, followed by chromated copper arsenate (CCA), whose use in residential settings was phased out in many countries because of arsenic concerns. Modern alternatives include alkaline copper quaternary (ACQ), copper azole, borates for interior use, and light organic solvent preservatives. Oil-borne and water-borne treatments are applied by pressure impregnation in large cylinders, by dipping or brushing, or by diffusion.

Non-chemical methods

Thermal modification heats wood to alter its chemistry and reduce moisture uptake; acetylation reacts wood with acetic anhydride to make it dimensionally stable and decay-resistant. Good design — keeping wood dry, ventilated and off the ground — remains a fundamental preservation strategy, along with selecting naturally durable species such as teak or western red cedar.

Environmental concerns

Leaching of preservative chemicals and disposal of treated wood are regulated in many jurisdictions, and treated timber is generally excluded from ordinary burning or landfill. See also wood warping.
