Palaeontology

Palaeontology (also spelled paleontology) is the scientific study of life that existed prior to the Holocene epoch, roughly 11,700 years ago and earlier, based on fossils — the preserved remains, impressions and traces of ancient organisms. It uses evidence from fossilised bodies, footprints, burrows, eggs and even chemical traces to reconstruct how organisms evolved and how ecosystems changed over geological time.

Scope and methods

Palaeontology sits at the boundary between biology and geology. Palaeontologists date fossils using stratigraphy — the relative ordering of rock layers — together with radiometric dating of surrounding strata. Subfields include vertebrate palaeontology (the study of dinosaurs, early mammals and other backboned animals), invertebrate palaeontology, palaeobotany (fossil plants), micropalaeontology (microscopic fossils, vital to the petroleum industry), and taphonomy, the study of how organisms decay and fossilise.

History

Fossils were noticed and wondered at in antiquity, but the science crystallised around 1800. Georges Cuvier demonstrated that distinct fossil faunas represented extinct forms of life, establishing extinction as fact. Mary Anning's discoveries of ichthyosaurs, plesiosaurs and pterosaurs along the Dorset coast transformed understanding of Mesozoic seas. William Smith showed that rock layers could be ordered by their fossil content, and the work of Charles Lyell and Charles Darwin placed the fossil record within the frameworks of deep time and evolution by natural selection. The late nineteenth-century "Bone Wars" in North America and twentieth-century discoveries in China, Africa and elsewhere greatly expanded the record.

Significance

The fossil record documents the major transitions of life — the Cambrian explosion, the conquest of land, the rise and fall of the dinosaurs, and the origins of humans — and records the great mass extinctions. Palaeontological evidence also informs studies of past climates and present-day biodiversity change.
