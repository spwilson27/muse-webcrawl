Direct speech

Direct speech is conventionally marked with quotation marks.

Direct speech, also called quoted speech or direct discourse, is a sentence or utterance in which the exact words spoken are reproduced, typically within quotation marks, rather than being reported in the speaker's own paraphrase. It contrasts with indirect speech (reported speech), in which the content is embedded in the reporter's sentence with shifts in pronouns, tense, and deictic expressions.

Features

In written English, direct speech is set off by quotation marks and often introduced by a reporting clause: She said, "I will be late." The quoted portion preserves the original speaker's wording, pronouns, tense, and viewpoint, and forms a syntactically independent unit — the main clause expresses only the act of saying. Punctuation conventions differ between British and American usage in the placement of commas and full stops relative to the closing quotation mark.

Conversion to indirect speech

When direct speech is converted to indirect speech, the reporting verb often shifts to a past tense ("backshift"), pronouns are adjusted to the reporter's perspective, and time and place expressions change: "I will finish it tomorrow," he said becomes He said that he would finish it the next day. Questions, commands, and exclamations require additional structural adjustments in reported form.

Uses

Direct speech is a central device of narrative fiction and drama, where dialogue reveals character and advances plot, and of journalism and legal transcription, where exact wording is evidentially important. Free indirect speech is an intermediate technique, common in the novel, that blends the narrator's voice with a character's words and thoughts without quotation marks.
