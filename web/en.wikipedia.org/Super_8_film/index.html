Super 8 film

Super 8 is a motion picture film format released in 1965 by Eastman Kodak as an improvement over the older "Double" or "Regular" 8 mm home movie format. The film is nominally 8 mm wide, the same as standard 8 mm film, but the dimensions of the rectangular perforations along one edge are smaller, allowing a larger image area and therefore better picture quality.

Format

Super 8 film is supplied in plastic cartridges containing 50 feet (about 15 metres) of film, which runs for approximately 3 minutes 20 seconds at 18 frames per second, or 2 minutes 30 seconds at 24 frames per second. The cartridge design made loading simple: the user merely inserted the cartridge into the camera, eliminating the need to thread film or flip the reel halfway through, as regular 8 mm required. Kodak discontinued the sound cartridge in 1997, but silent cartridges remain in production.

History

Super 8 was introduced at the 1964/65 World's Fair and quickly became the dominant home movie format. Fuji followed with a competing single-8 system using the same film dimensions but a different cartridge. The format was also widely used by students, experimental filmmakers and, later, music video and television productions seeking a distinctive grainy aesthetic. The advent of consumer video cameras in the 1980s displaced Super 8 in the home market.

Modern use

Despite its decline, Super 8 survives as an artistic and educational medium. Kodak continues to manufacture several Super 8 film stocks, including colour reversal, colour negative and black-and-white films. Professional features such as Super 8 (2011), directed by J. J. Abrams, celebrated the format, and some directors shoot sequences on Super 8 for its texture. Film festivals dedicated to small-gauge filmmaking continue to screen new Super 8 work.
