Kakadu National Park is a protected area in the Northern Territory of Australia, about 171 kilometres (106 mi) southeast of Darwin. It is Australia's biggest national park, covering almost 20,000 square kilometres — an area roughly the size of Wales or nearly half the size of Switzerland. The park includes the region of the South Alligator River and is jointly managed by Parks Australia and the Aboriginal traditional owners of the land.

History

Aboriginal people have occupied the Kakadu region continuously for tens of thousands of years; archaeological evidence suggests habitation dating back at least 20,000 years and possibly far longer. The park contains one of the highest concentrated areas of rock art sites in the world, including the celebrated galleries at Ubirr and Nourlangie (Burrunggui), which record hunting scenes, ancestral beings and contact with Europeans. The park was proclaimed in stages beginning in 1979, largely in response to concern about uranium mining at Ranger and Jabiluka within enclaves surrounded by the park. Kakadu was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in three stages — 1981, 1987 and 1992 — for both its cultural and natural values.

Geography and ecology

The park extends from the coast and estuaries in the north to the rocky escarpment country of the Arnhem Land plateau in the south. Its habitats include tidal flats, floodplains, billabongs, lowlands and stony country. Kakadu protects a huge diversity of wildlife: about one-third of Australia's bird species, around a quarter of its freshwater and estuarine fish species, and large populations of saltwater crocodiles, water buffalo and wallabies. The Yellow Water Billabong and the Jim Jim and Twin Falls are among its best-known natural features.

Climate

The climate is tropical monsoonal, with a wet season from roughly November to April and a dry season from May to October. Aboriginal owners recognise six distinct seasons in the Kakadu region, from the stormy Gudjewg to the cooler Wurrgeng.

Tourism

Kakadu receives around 200,000 visitors a year. Attractions include rock art sites, waterfalls, cruises on Yellow Water, and the Bowali Visitor Centre near Jabiru. Access during the wet season can be limited by flooding.
