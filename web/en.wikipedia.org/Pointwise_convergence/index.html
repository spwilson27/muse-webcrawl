Pointwise convergence

In mathematics, pointwise convergence is one of several senses in which a sequence of functions can converge to a particular function. It is the natural notion of convergence in the topology of pointwise convergence (the product topology) on a space of functions.

Definition

Suppose { fn } is a sequence of functions defined on a set X with values in a metric space (usually ℝ or ℂ). The sequence converges pointwise to a function f if, for every x in X, limn→∞ fn(x) = f(x). Equivalently: for every point x and every ε > 0 there exists an N (which may depend on both ε and x) such that |fn(x) − f(x)| < ε for all n ≥ N.

Properties

Pointwise convergence is weaker than uniform convergence: a sequence converging uniformly must converge pointwise to the same limit, but not conversely. For example, the functions fn(x) = xn on [0, 1] converge pointwise to a limit function that is 0 for x < 1 and 1 at x = 1 — showing that the pointwise limit of continuous functions need not be continuous. The standard counterexample obstacles to interchanging limits with integrals and derivatives under pointwise convergence motivated the development of uniform convergence and, later, the dominated convergence theorem of measure theory, which gives conditions under which a pointwise limit can be integrated term by term.

Topology

The topology of pointwise convergence makes the set of all functions from X into a topological space Y into the product space YX. By Tychonoff's theorem this space is compact when Y is. In functional analysis the weak and weak-* topologies are important instances of pointwise-type convergence.
