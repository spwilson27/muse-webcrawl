The Slave River is a Canadian river that flows from the Peace–Athabasca Delta in northeastern Alberta to Great Slave Lake in the Northwest Territories. About 434 km (270 mi) long, it carries the combined waters of the Peace and Athabasca river systems northward and forms part of the vast Mackenzie River drainage basin.

Course

The river begins where the outflows of Lake Athabasca and the Peace River combine in the Peace–Athabasca Delta, one of the world's largest inland freshwater deltas and a Ramsar-listed wetland within and around Wood Buffalo National Park. Flowing north, the Slave River passes Fort Smith, where a series of four sets of rapids — Cassette, Pelican, Mountain Portage and the Rapids of the Drowned — drop the river some 33 metres and historically forced a portage that made Fort Smith the gateway to the North.

History and name

The river's name derives from the Slavey people (Deh Gah Got'ine), an Athabaskan First Nation, whose name was translated by the Cree as "slave" — later applied by Europeans to the river and to Great Slave Lake. The river was a key fur trade route; trading posts were established in the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, and steamboats later navigated between the Athabasca country and the Mackenzie.

Recreation and environment

The Slave River rapids are internationally known for whitewater kayaking and rafting, and the river hosts an annual paddle festival at Fort Smith. The surrounding boreal forest and delta support wood bison, whooping cranes (which nest in nearby Wood Buffalo National Park) and major migratory bird habitat.
