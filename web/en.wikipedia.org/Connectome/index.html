Connectome

Connectome
FieldNeuroscience
DefinitionA comprehensive map of neural connections in the brain
First complete exampleCaenorhabditis elegans (1986)

A connectome is a comprehensive map of neural connections in the brain, expressible as a network whose nodes are neurons (or brain regions) and whose edges are synapses (or white-matter tracts). The term was proposed independently in 2005 by Olaf Sporns and by Patric Hagmann, by analogy with the genome. The field of study is sometimes called connectomics.

Scales

Connectomes are studied at several scales. At the microscale, individual neurons and their synaptic connections are mapped, generally using electron microscopy of thin tissue sections. At the mesoscale, connections between populations of neurons are traced. At the macroscale — the level of most human research — connections between brain regions are estimated non-invasively using diffusion MRI tractography for structural connectivity and correlated activity in functional MRI for functional connectivity.

History

The first, and for decades only, complete connectome was that of the nematode worm Caenorhabditis elegans, whose 302 neurons and roughly 7,000 connections were mapped by John White and colleagues in 1986. In the 2010s and 2020s, electron-microscopy projects mapped large volumes of fruit fly brain, culminating in a complete adult fly brain connectome, and cubic-millimetre volumes of mouse and human cortex (the MICrONS project). The US-funded Human Connectome Project (launched 2009) mapped macroscale connectivity in over a thousand living human subjects.

Significance and challenges

Connectomics aims to explain how network structure gives rise to perception, cognition and behaviour, and how its disruption contributes to psychiatric and neurological disease. Critics note that wiring alone omits synaptic strengths, neuromodulation, glia and dynamics; a connectome is a scaffold, not a complete simulation, of the brain.
