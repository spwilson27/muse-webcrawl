Edward Said

Edward Wadie Said (1 November 1935 – 25 September 2003) was a Palestinian-American professor of literature at Columbia University and the founder of the academic field of postcolonial studies.

Edward Wadie Said (Arabic: إدوارد وديع سعيد, 1 November 1935 – 25 September 2003) was a Palestinian-American academic, literary critic and political activist. A professor of literature at Columbia University, he was among the founders of postcolonial studies. As a cultural critic, Said is best known for his book Orientalism (1978), a foundational text which critiques the cultural representations that are the bases of Orientalism — how the Western world perceives the Orient.

Life and career

Said was born in Jerusalem, then part of the British Mandate of Palestine, to a wealthy Christian Arab family. He was educated at Victoria College in Cairo and at Princeton and Harvard universities in the United States. He joined the faculty of Columbia University in 1963 and taught there for the rest of his career, becoming University Professor of English and Comparative Literature.

Said was also an accomplished pianist and music critic, writing a regular column for The Nation magazine and several books on music, including Musical Elaborations (1991) and Parallels and Paradoxes (2002), the latter co-written with conductor Daniel Barenboim, with whom he founded the West–Eastern Divan Orchestra.

Orientalism

In Orientalism (1978), Said argued that Western scholarship about the Middle East and Asia was inextricably tied to imperial power, producing a distorted image of the "Orient" as exotic, backward and dangerous in order to justify colonial domination. Drawing on Michel Foucault's concept of discourse, the book reshaped the humanities and remains widely debated.

Palestinian advocacy

Said was a prominent advocate for Palestinian rights, serving on the Palestinian National Council from 1977 to 1991. His works on Palestine include The Question of Palestine (1979) and Covering Islam (1981). He later became critical of the Oslo Accords, which he described as an instrument of Palestinian surrender.

Death and legacy

Said died in New York City in 2003 after a decade-long battle with chronic lymphocytic leukemia. His memoir Out of Place (1999) received the New Yorker Book Award. The Edward Said Chair at Columbia and numerous lecture series commemorate his influence on literary and cultural studies.
