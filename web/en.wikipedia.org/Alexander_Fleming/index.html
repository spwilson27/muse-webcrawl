Sir Alexander Fleming (6 August 1881 – 11 March 1955) was a Scottish physician and microbiologist, best known for discovering the world's first broadly effective antibiotic substance, which he named penicillin. His discovery in 1928 of what he called "mould juice" from the fungus Penicillium rubens has been described as the greatest single victory ever achieved over disease. He shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1945 with Howard Florey and Ernst Boris Chain.

Early research

Fleming studied medicine at St Mary's Hospital Medical School in London and spent his career in its Inoculation Department under Almroth Wright. His experience treating wounded soldiers in the First World War convinced him that antiseptics often did more harm than good in deep wounds. In 1922 he discovered lysozyme, a mildly antibacterial enzyme present in mucus and tears, after mucus from his own nose dripped onto a culture plate and dissolved the bacteria.

Discovery of penicillin

In September 1928, returning from holiday, Fleming noticed that a culture of Staphylococcus bacteria had been contaminated by a Penicillium mould, and that the bacteria surrounding the mould had been killed while distant colonies grew normally. He identified the mould's active secretion as "penicillin" and published his findings in 1929, noting its therapeutic potential, but penicillin proved difficult to produce in quantity. Only in 1940–41 did Florey and Chain's Oxford team develop methods to purify and mass-produce it, transforming medicine during the Second World War and ushering in the antibiotic era. In a famous 1945 warning, Fleming foresaw that underdosing could breed antibiotic resistance.
