Simón José Antonio de la Santísima Trinidad Bolívar y Ponte Palacios y Blanco (24 July 1783 – 17 December 1830) was a Venezuelan statesman, military officer and revolutionary who led what are currently the countries of Bolivia, Colombia, Ecuador, Panama, Peru, and Venezuela to independence from the Spanish Empire. He is known as El Libertador ("the Liberator") in Spanish America.

Early life

Bolívar was born in Caracas into a wealthy criollo family of Basque descent. Orphaned young, he was educated in part in Spain and travelled in Europe, where in 1805, on the Monte Sacro in Rome, he famously swore an oath not to rest until Spanish rule in America was broken. He returned to Venezuela and joined the independence movement as the colonial order collapsed following Napoleon's invasion of Spain.

The wars of independence

After early defeats and exiles, Bolívar issued the Decree of War to the Death in 1813 and led the Admirable Campaign that briefly liberated Caracas. Driven out again, he wrote the Jamaica Letter (1815), setting out his vision of Spanish American unity. In 1819 he crossed the flooded llanos and the Andes in one of the most celebrated marches in military history, defeating the Spanish at the Battle of Boyacá and liberating New Granada. Victory at Carabobo (1821) secured Venezuela, and his armies, together with those of Antonio José de Sucre, went on to win at Pichincha, Junín and Ayacucho (1824), ending Spanish power in South America.

Gran Colombia and later years

Bolívar became president of the republic of Gran Colombia, uniting Venezuela, Colombia, Panama and Ecuador, and for a time also governed Peru; the country of Bolivia was named in his honour. His project of continental unity foundered on regional rivalries, and after surviving an assassination attempt in 1828 he resigned in 1830, dying of tuberculosis near Santa Marta on his way into exile.

Legacy

Bolívar remains the pre-eminent hero of South American independence, commemorated in place names, currencies and monuments across the continent, and his political writings continue to shape debates about Latin American unity.
