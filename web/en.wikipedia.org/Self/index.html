Self

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Self-awareness — the capacity to recognize oneself as a distinct individual — is central to the concept of self.

In philosophy and psychology, the self is the subject of one's own experience: the individual person as the object of their own reflective consciousness. The self encompasses one's identity, self-concept, self-image, self-esteem, and the sense of continuity that makes a person the same individual over time. Questions about the self — what it is, whether it is unified or constructed, and how it relates to the body and to others — run through philosophy of mind, phenomenology, cognitive science, and the contemplative traditions of world religions.

Philosophy

Modern philosophy of the self begins with Descartes' cogito: the thinking subject certain of its own existence. Locke grounded personal identity in continuity of consciousness and memory. Hume, by contrast, argued that introspection finds only a bundle of perceptions, never a simple self. Kant distinguished the empirical self from the transcendental unity of apperception that makes experience coherent. Buddhist philosophy's doctrine of anatta (no-self) denies any permanent, independent self, seeing the person as a changing aggregate of processes — a view echoed in some contemporary cognitive science.

Psychology

William James distinguished the "I" (the knower) from the "Me" (the known self). Symbolic interactionists such as George Herbert Mead and Charles Horton Cooley argued the self arises socially, through the "looking-glass" of others' responses. Developmental psychology studies the emergence of self-recognition in infancy, often tested with the mirror test. Carl Rogers placed the self-concept at the center of personality, while social psychologists study self-schemas, self-esteem, self-efficacy, and self-regulation.

Neuroscience

Research on the bodily self, agency, and the default mode network explores how the brain constructs a first-person perspective, and how it can fragment in depersonalization, schizophrenia, or after brain injury — evidence many take to show that the self is a model generated by the brain rather than a single indivisible entity.
