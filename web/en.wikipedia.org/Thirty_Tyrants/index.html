The Thirty Tyrants were a pro-Spartan oligarchy installed in Athens after its defeat in the Peloponnesian War in 404 BC. Chosen to draft a new constitution, the thirty men — among them Critias and Theramenes — instead seized power and ruled Athens in a reign of terror for about eight months.

Rule

With the support of a Spartan garrison, the Thirty purged the democratic leadership, restricted citizenship to a list of three thousand, disarmed the rest of the population, and executed an estimated 1,500 people — about five percent of the citizen body — often confiscating their property. Metics (resident foreigners) were also targeted; the orator Lysias escaped, but his brother Polemarchus was executed. Theramenes, who objected to the extremism, was himself forced to drink hemlock by Critias.

Fall

Democratic exiles led by Thrasybulus seized the border fortress of Phyle and then defeated the oligarchs' forces at the Battle of Munychia in 403 BC, where Critias was killed. A Spartan-brokered reconciliation (the Amnesty of 403 BC) restored democracy and famously forbade "remembering wrongs" against most of the Thirty's collaborators.

Legacy

The episode became a byword for oligarchic brutality in classical literature, discussed by Xenophon, Aristotle's school and later political writers; the term "Thirty Tyrants" has since been applied to other ruling juntas.
