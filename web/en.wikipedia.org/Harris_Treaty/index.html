Harris Treaty

The Harris Treaty, formally the Treaty of Amity and Commerce between the United States and Japan, was signed on July 29, 1858, at Shimoda. Negotiated by the American consul Townsend Harris, it opened Japanese ports to American trade and is counted among the "unequal treaties" imposed on Japan in the late Edo period.

Harris Treaty
Signed: July 29, 1858
Parties: United States and Tokugawa Japan
Negotiator: Townsend Harris
Effect: Opened ports including Kanagawa, Nagasaki, Niigata, and Hyōgo to US trade

Terms

The treaty provided for the opening of additional ports to American commerce, residence of Americans in Edo and Osaka, fixed low tariff rates under foreign control, and extraterritoriality for American citizens in Japan. It built on the 1854 Convention of Kanagawa negotiated by Commodore Matthew Perry.

Consequences

Similar treaties with European powers followed, collectively known as the Ansei Treaties. The loss of tariff autonomy and extraterritorial privileges became major grievances; the unequal provisions were only revised in the 1890s, with full tariff autonomy regained in 1911. The treaty's signing also intensified political conflict within Japan in the Bakumatsu era.
