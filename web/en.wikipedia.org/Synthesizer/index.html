Synthesizer

A synthesizer (also synthesiser, or simply synth) is an electronic musical instrument that generates audio signals. Synthesizers typically create sounds by generating waveforms through methods including subtractive synthesis, additive synthesis and frequency modulation synthesis. These sounds may be altered by components such as filters, which cut or boost frequencies; envelope generators, which articulate the loudness of a note; and low-frequency oscillators, which modulate parameters such as pitch, volume or filter characteristics affecting the sound's timbre. Synthesizers are typically played with keyboards or controlled by sequencers, other instruments or software.


A modular synthesizer, in which individual modules are connected with patch cables.

The first mass-produced synthesizer, the Moog synthesizer, was released in 1964 and popularized by artists such as Wendy Carlos and the Beatles. In the 1970s, synthesizers were incorporated into progressive rock, disco, and film soundtracks. Synthesizers developed rapidly in the 1980s with digital synthesis and sampling, exemplified by the Yamaha DX7 (1983), the first commercially successful digital synthesizer. In the 1990s, software synthesizers running on general-purpose computers became common, and in the 2010s modular synthesis enjoyed a revival with the Eurorack format.

Synthesizers were initially seen as commercial and academic tools, largely produced by institutions and hobbyist builders, but they became central to popular music genres including electronic dance music, synth-pop, hip hop and contemporary classical composition. Today synthesizers are used in nearly every genre of recorded music.

History

Precursors

Early electric instruments that anticipated the synthesizer include the Telharmonium (1897) of Thaddeus Cahill, which generated tones with rotating dynamos, and the Theremin (1920) of Léon Theremin, which was played without physical contact. The Ondes Martenot (1928) and the Trautonium (1930) introduced expressive control methods later echoed in synthesizer design. In 1928, RCA's engineers developed early electronic sound synthesis based on vacuum tubes, and the RCA Mark II Sound Synthesizer (1957), installed at the Columbia-Princeton Electronic Music Center, was one of the first programmable synthesizers, using punched paper tape to control its modules.

The Moog synthesizer

In 1964, American engineer Robert Moog introduced the Moog synthesizer, a modular system whose voltage-controlled oscillators, filters and amplifiers could be connected with patch cords. Moog's keyboard interface made the instrument accessible to performing musicians. In 1968, Wendy Carlos released the album Switched-On Bach, recorded entirely on a Moog synthesizer; it became one of the best-selling classical albums of its era and demonstrated the synthesizer's musical potential to a broad audience. Keith Emerson, of Emerson, Lake & Palmer, was among the first rock musicians to perform with a Moog modular system on stage.

Commercial expansion

In 1970, Moog introduced the Minimoog, a compact, pre-wired monophonic synthesizer with a built-in keyboard. It became the best-selling synthesizer of its time and a fixture of progressive rock, funk and jazz fusion. ARP Instruments produced the widely used ARP 2600 (1971) and ARP Odyssey (1972). In Japan, Yamaha, Roland and Korg developed synthesizers through the 1970s, and Sequential Circuits released the Prophet-5 in 1978, the first synthesizer with a microprocessor and fully programmable memory, enabling musicians to store and recall sounds.

Digital synthesis and sampling

The 1980s saw the arrival of digital synthesizers. The Yamaha DX7 (1983), using frequency modulation synthesis developed by John Chowning at Stanford University, sold more than 200,000 units and defined the bright, bell-like sounds of mid-1980s pop. Digital samplers such as the Fairlight CMI (1979), E-mu Emulator (1981) and Akai MPC series (from 1988) allowed any recorded sound to be played from a keyboard, transforming hip hop and dance music production. The MIDI standard, agreed in 1983, allowed synthesizers, sequencers and computers from different manufacturers to interoperate.

Software and revival

By the late 1990s, personal computers were fast enough to synthesize sound in real time, and software synthesizers ("softsynths") distributed as plug-ins became standard in digital audio workstations. Affordable analogue reissues and the Eurorack modular format, introduced by Doepfer in the mid-1990s, drove a revival of hardware synthesis in the 2000s and 2010s.

Components and synthesis methods

A typical synthesizer voice consists of one or more oscillators producing raw waveforms (sine, sawtooth, square, triangle and noise), a filter that shapes the spectrum, and an amplifier whose level is controlled over time by an envelope generator, commonly with attack, decay, sustain and release (ADSR) stages. Low-frequency oscillators (LFOs) modulate parameters cyclically to produce vibrato, tremolo and filter sweeps. In modular synthesizers, modules are interconnected by the user; in most other designs, the signal path is fixed or semi-fixed.

Subtractive synthesis starts from harmonically rich waveforms and removes energy with filters; it is the basis of most analogue synthesizers. Additive synthesis sums sine partials to build timbres directly. Frequency modulation (FM) synthesis modulates the frequency of one oscillator with another at audio rates, producing complex spectra from simple controls. Wavetable synthesis scans through stored single-cycle waveforms. Physical modelling synthesis simulates the acoustics of strings, reeds and bodies with mathematical models. Sampling, while not synthesis in the strict sense, is frequently integrated into synthesizer architectures.

Types

Synthesizers are classified by their sound generation (analogue, digital, software, hybrid), by their voice count (monophonic, playing one note at a time, versus polyphonic), and by their interface: keyboard synthesizers, desktop modules, rack units, modular systems and semi-modular instruments. Workstation synthesizers combine synthesis with sequencing and sampling for complete music production.

Impact on popular music

Synthesizers reshaped popular music repeatedly: the psychedelic and progressive rock of the late 1960s and 1970s, the electronic dance music pioneered by Kraftwerk and Giorgio Moroder, the synth-pop of the early 1980s, and the sample- and synth-driven production of hip hop and modern pop. Their falling cost and increasing portability placed electronic sound design in the hands of independent musicians worldwide.
