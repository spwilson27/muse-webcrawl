Thermionic emission

Thermionic emission is the liberation of charged particles from a hot surface, usually electrons from a heated metal or metal oxide surface. The phenomenon occurs because the thermal energy given to the charge carriers overcomes the work function of the material. The charge carriers can be electrons or ions, and in older literature are sometimes called "thermions". After emission, a charge that is equal in magnitude and opposite in sign to the total charge emitted will initially be left behind in the emitting region; if the emitter is connected to a battery, the charge left behind is neutralised by charge supplied by the battery.

Thermionic emission is the basis of the vacuum tube: a heated cathode emits electrons that flow through a vacuum to an anode. It is also used in electron microscopes, X-ray tubes, cathode-ray tubes and thermionic converters that turn heat directly into electricity.

History

The effect was first reported in 1873 by Frederick Guthrie, who observed that a red-hot iron sphere would lose a positive charge but not a negative one. Thomas Edison rediscovered the effect in 1880 while investigating why light-bulb filaments failed, and patented it in 1884 (the "Edison effect"), though he did not develop a practical application. John Ambrose Fleming applied the effect in the first vacuum diode (1904), and Lee de Forest's triode (1906) made amplification possible. Owen Willans Richardson worked out the theoretical basis of the emission law and received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1928 "for his work on the thermionic phenomenon and especially for the discovery of the law named after him".

Richardson's law

The current density J of emitted electrons is described by the Richardson–Dushman equation:

J = AG T2 e−W/(kT)

where T is the temperature in kelvin, W is the work function of the metal, k is the Boltzmann constant, and AG is a constant depending on the material. The exponential dependence on temperature and work function explains why practical cathodes use low-work-function materials such as thoriated tungsten or barium oxide coatings, allowing useful emission at moderate temperatures.

Schottky emission and field emission

An applied electric field lowers the effective barrier at the surface and increases emission, an effect known as Schottky emission. At very high field strengths electrons can tunnel directly through the barrier, giving field emission, which is exploited in field-emission electron sources and displays.
