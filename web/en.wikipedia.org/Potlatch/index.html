Potlatch

A potlatch is a gift-giving feast practised by Indigenous peoples of the Pacific Northwest Coast of Canada and the United States, among whom it is traditionally the primary governmental institution, legislative body, and economic system. This includes the Haida, Heiltsuk, Nuxalk, Tlingit, Makah, Tsimshian, Nuu-chah-nulth, Kwakiutl, and Coast Salish cultures. Potlatches are also a common feature of the cultures of the Interior and Subarctic peoples, as well as the Athapaskan, Tlingit, and Ahtna cultures.

Overview

A potlatch involves giving away or destroying wealth or valuable items in order to demonstrate a leader's wealth and power. Potlatches are also focused on the reaffirmation of family, clan, and international connections, and the human connection with the supernatural world. Potlatch also serves as a strict resource management regime. The word comes from the Chinook Jargon, meaning "to give away" or "a gift"; originally from the Nuu-chah-nulth word paɬaˑč, to make a ceremonial gift in a potlatch.

Legal and economic functions include the payment of debts, the confirmation of titles and privileges, the witnessing of marriages, births, deaths, and the assumption of names, and the settling of disputes. Feasting, dancing, singing, and storytelling accompany the distribution of goods, which historically included food, blankets, canoes, slaves, and copper objects of great value.

History and prohibition

The practice was outlawed in Canada in 1885 under an amendment to the Indian Act, as missionaries and government agents considered it wasteful, unproductive, and contrary to "civilized" values of accumulation. The ban remained in force until 1951, though enforcement varied. The most famous enforcement action was the 1921 potlatch of Dan Cranmer at Village Island, after which many participants were arrested and hundreds of ceremonial objects confiscated and sold to museums and collectors. Since the ban was lifted, potlatching has undergone a revival and remains a vital institution among Northwest Coast nations.

Anthropological significance

The potlatch has been extensively studied by anthropologists, including Franz Boas, who documented Kwakwaka'wakw potlatches in detail. Marcel Mauss used the potlatch as a central example in his theory of gift exchange in The Gift (1925). Georges Bataille later took it as a paradigm of "non-productive expenditure" in his concept of the general economy.
