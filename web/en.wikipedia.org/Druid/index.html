A druid was a member of the high-ranking priestly class in ancient Celtic cultures. Druids were religious leaders as well as legal authorities, adjudicators, lorekeepers, medical professionals, and political advisors. The earliest detailed accounts come from Greco-Roman writers — above all Julius Caesar's Commentarii de Bello Gallico — supplemented by later Irish and Welsh literature, since the druids themselves transmitted their learning orally and committed nothing to writing.

Classical accounts

Caesar reports that druids in Gaul presided over sacrifices, judged disputes and crimes, taught the young, and were exempt from taxation and military service; their training could last twenty years of memorised verse. He and writers such as Tacitus and Pliny the Elder also describe human sacrifice — Caesar's famous "wicker man" — and rituals involving mistletoe cut with a golden sickle, though Roman authors had political motives to exaggerate Celtic barbarity. Caesar states that the discipline was believed to have originated in Britain; in 60–61 AD the Romans attacked the druid stronghold on Anglesey (Mona), described by Tacitus in his account of Suetonius Paulinus's campaign.

Decline and revival

In Gaul and Britain, Roman rule suppressed druidry, and in Ireland it faded with Christianisation, its functions passing partly to the filid poets. From the 17th and 18th centuries, antiquarians such as John Aubrey and William Stukeley romantically linked druids with Stonehenge and Avebury — monuments far older than the druids — sparking a Druidic revival that produced fraternal orders and, in Wales, the druidic Gorsedd of the National Eisteddfod. Modern neo-Druidry, including orders such as OBOD, treats druidry as a living spiritual path, and in 2010 the Druid Network was recognised as a charity in England, making druidry an officially recognised religion there.
