Right-to-work law

In the context of labor law in the United States, a right-to-work law is a statute that prohibits agreements between employers and labor unions that make membership in a union, or payment of union fees, a condition of employment. Under such laws, employees in unionised workplaces may not be required to join the union or pay for its services, even though the union remains obliged to represent all workers in the bargaining unit.

As of the mid-2020s, more than half of US states have right-to-work statutes.

History

The legal basis for state right-to-work laws is section 14(b) of the Taft–Hartley Act of 1947, which permits states to ban "union security agreements" more broadly than federal law. Florida and Arkansas adopted the first such laws in the mid-1940s; the policy spread mainly through the South and Mountain West, and in the 2010s several industrial Midwestern states, including Indiana, Michigan and Wisconsin, enacted right-to-work statutes. In Janus v. AFSCME (2018), the US Supreme Court extended a similar rule to all public-sector employment under the First Amendment.

Debate

Supporters argue the laws protect freedom of association, attract business investment, and make unions more accountable to workers. Opponents, including most unions, argue they create a free-rider problem—workers receive union representation without contributing—and weaken organised labour, lowering wages. Empirical studies of effects on employment, wages and union density remain contested.
