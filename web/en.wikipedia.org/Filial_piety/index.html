Filial piety

In Confucian philosophy, filial piety (Chinese: 孝, xiào) is the virtue of exhibiting respect, love, and support for one's parents and ancestors, and more generally for the elderly and one's superiors. Regarded in Confucian texts such as the Classic of Filial Piety (Xiaojing) and the Analects as the root of all virtue, it has profoundly shaped family and social life in China, Korea, Vietnam, and other East Asian societies.

Doctrine

Confucius taught that filial piety begins with serving one's parents in daily life, continues with bringing them honour and not disgrace, and extends after their death to ancestral rites. It is not mere obedience: the Analects insist that dutiful conduct without reverence is little different from feeding animals, and classical commentators allowed respectful remonstrance with a parent who errs. Mencius considered producing descendants and preserving the family line part of filial duty.

Exemplars and law

The Yuan-dynasty collection The Twenty-four Filial Exemplars popularised stories of extraordinary devotion, some of which — such as burying a child so a parent might eat — later drew criticism for extremity. In imperial China, unfilial conduct (buxiao) was one of the Ten Abominations in law. Modern legal codes in China still oblige adult children to support and visit their parents.

Modern interpretations

Scholars and ethicists debate filial piety's relevance today, distinguishing authoritarian readings emphasising obedience from relational readings emphasising gratitude, care, and reciprocity. Analogous duties toward parents appear in many traditions, making filial piety a frequent subject of comparative ethics and of empirical field research on family life.
