Mischief rule

The mischief rule is one of the traditional rules of statutory interpretation used by judges in common-law countries to determine the intention of a legislature when the wording of a statute is ambiguous. It derives from Heydon's Case (1584), in which the Barons of the Exchequer set out four questions for courts to consider.

Heydon's Case

Under the approach in Heydon's Case, a court asks: (1) what the common law was before the statute was passed; (2) what mischief or defect the common law did not provide for; (3) what remedy Parliament intended to provide; and (4) the true reason for the remedy. The court then interprets the statute so as to suppress the mischief and advance the remedy.

Examples

A classic application is Smith v Hughes (1960), where prostitutes soliciting from windows and balconies were held to be "in a street or public place" under the Street Offences Act 1959, because the mischief Parliament aimed to prevent was the molestation of passers-by. In Royal College of Nursing v DHSS (1981), the House of Lords used the mischief rule to hold that nurses could lawfully take part in abortion procedures under the Abortion Act 1967.

Comparison with other rules

The mischief rule contrasts with the literal rule, which gives statutory words their plain ordinary meaning, and the golden rule, which modifies the literal meaning to avoid absurdity. Modern courts more often adopt a purposive approach, of which the mischief rule is an early ancestor.
