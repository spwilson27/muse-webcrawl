Cracking (chemistry)

In petrochemistry, petroleum geology and organic chemistry, cracking is the process whereby complex organic molecules such as kerogens or long-chain hydrocarbons are broken down into simpler molecules such as light hydrocarbons, by the breaking of carbon–carbon bonds in the precursors. The rate of cracking and the end products are strongly dependent on the temperature and presence of catalysts.

Cracking is the breakdown of large alkanes into smaller, more useful alkenes. Simply, hydrocarbon cracking is the process of breaking a longer chain of hydrocarbons into shorter ones. This process requires high temperatures.

History and patenting

Among several variants of thermal cracking methods (variously known as the “Shukhov cracking process”, “Burton process”, “Burton–Humphreys process”, and “Dubbs process”), the Shukhov cracking process was invented and patented by Russian engineer Vladimir Shukhov in 1891. William Merriam Burton developed his process in the United States in 1912, greatly increasing gasoline yield from crude oil. In 1936, Eugène Houdry developed the first successful catalytic cracking process.

Cracking methodologies

Thermal cracking

Modern high-pressure thermal cracking operates at absolute pressures of roughly 7,000 kPa. Overall, a hydrogen-rich product and a carbon-rich solid residue (coke) result. Steam cracking, conducted at very high temperatures (above 800°C) with short residence times, is the principal industrial route to ethene (ethylene) and propene.

Fluid catalytic cracking

Fluid catalytic cracking (FCC) is the most widely used cracking process in refineries. It employs a zeolite catalyst at around 500°C and modest pressure, with the catalyst continuously regenerated by burning off deposited coke. FCC converts heavy gas oils mainly into gasoline and liquefied petroleum gas.

Hydrocracking

Hydrocracking is a catalytic cracking process assisted by hydrogen, carried out at elevated pressure. It produces saturated products such as jet fuel and diesel with low sulphur content.
