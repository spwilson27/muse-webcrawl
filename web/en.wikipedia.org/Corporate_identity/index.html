Corporate identity

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A corporate identity is the manner in which a corporation, firm, or business enterprise presents itself to the public, such as customers and investors, as well as to its own employees. The corporate identity is typically visualised by branding and trademarks, but it can also include things like product design, advertising, public relations, corporate philosophy and organisational culture. In general, a corporate identity is the "persona" of a corporation which is designed to accord with and facilitate the attainment of the company's business objectives.

Components

Corporate identity work usually divides into several elements:

  • Corporate design — the visual identity: logo, corporate colours, typography, uniforms, vehicle livery, signage and other graphic standards, usually codified in a style guide or brand book.
  • Corporate communication — advertising, public relations, internal communication and information policy, which determine the company's "voice".
  • Corporate behaviour — the conduct of the company and its staff toward customers, partners and the public, including leadership style and service standards.
  • Corporate philosophy and culture — the mission, values, and self-image that underlie the other elements.

Identity, image and reputation

Scholars distinguish identity (how the organisation sees and presents itself), image (how stakeholders currently perceive it), and reputation (the accumulated judgement of its conduct over time). A central task of identity management is to align these — for example through consistent design and communication programmes — while recognising that actual behaviour ultimately determines reputation.

History

Early examples of systematic corporate identity include the work of Peter Behrens for the German electrical company AEG after 1907, often described as the first comprehensive corporate design programme, and Frank Pick's development of the London Underground's visual identity in the early twentieth century, which gave the network its roundel logo and Johnston typeface. The field professionalised after the Second World War with consultancies such as Lippincott and Wolff Olins, and with programmes like Paul Rand's identity for IBM.

Legal aspects

Elements of a corporate identity may be protected as trademarks and trade dress. Companies police unauthorised use of their logos and get-up to prevent consumer confusion and dilution of the brand.
