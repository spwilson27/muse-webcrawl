Ein deutsches Requiem, Op. 45 — in English A German Requiem — is a large-scale work for soprano and baritone soloists, chorus, and orchestra by Johannes Brahms, composed between 1865 and 1868. Unlike the Latin Requiem Mass, whose texts focus on the dead and the Day of Judgment, Brahms assembled his libretto from the German Lutheran Bible with an emphasis on consolation for the living who mourn. Brahms himself remarked that he might happily have called the work "A Human Requiem".

History

The work grew out of personal grief. Brahms's mentor Robert Schumann died in 1856, and the death of Brahms's mother in 1865 is often cited as a direct impetus. An early version of six movements was premiered on Good Friday, 10 April 1868, in Bremen Cathedral conducted by the composer to great acclaim. The fifth movement, the soprano solo "Ihr habt nun Traurigkeit" ("Ye now have sorrow"), was added afterwards; the complete seven-movement work was first performed in Leipzig in February 1869.

Structure

The seven movements are symmetrically arranged around the fourth, the idyllic chorus "Wie lieblich sind deine Wohnungen" ("How lovely are thy dwellings"). The first movement ("Selig sind, die da Leid tragen" — "Blessed are they that mourn") and the last ("Selig sind die Toten" — "Blessed are the dead") frame the work with blessings. The second movement features the famous funeral-march chorus "Denn alles Fleisch, es ist wie Gras" ("For all flesh is as grass"), while the sixth movement includes a setting of words from the Book of Revelation and a powerful fugue.

Reception

The German Requiem established Brahms's reputation across Europe and remains one of the most frequently performed choral works in the repertoire. Clara Schumann wrote that it was "a truly tremendous piece of art" that seized her "through and through".
