Universal grammar

FieldLinguistics, cognitive science
Key figureNoam Chomsky
Introduced1950s–1960s
Related conceptsPrinciples and parameters, language acquisition device, poverty of the stimulus

In modern linguistics, universal grammar (UG) is the theory of the innate biological component of the language faculty. The fundamental claim of the theory is that all human beings, by virtue of their species, inherit a rich structure of mind that includes specifically linguistic components — a claim most famously developed by Noam Chomsky from the 1950s onward.

The argument

The theory is motivated by the "poverty of the stimulus" argument: children acquire the intricate grammatical system of their native language rapidly, uniformly, and from limited and noisy evidence, which suggests they bring substantial innate knowledge to the task. On the principles-and-parameters account, UG specifies a set of universal principles together with parameters (such as the head-directionality or pro-drop parameter) whose settings are fixed by exposure to a particular language, yielding the observed diversity of grammars from a shared blueprint.

Development of the theory

Chomsky's Syntactic Structures (1957) and Aspects of the Theory of Syntax (1965) established generative grammar and the notion of an innate language acquisition device. Later frameworks — government and binding theory and the minimalist program — progressively reduced the content attributed to UG, with the minimalist proposal centring on a single recursive operation, Merge, which builds hierarchical structure.

Criticism

Universal grammar has been vigorously contested. Usage-based linguists such as Michael Tomasello argue that general cognitive abilities plus rich social interaction explain acquisition without domain-specific innate grammar; field studies of languages such as Pirahã have been used to challenge claims about recursion; and statistical learning research shows children are sensitive to distributional patterns in input. The debate between nativist and empiricist accounts of language remains a central one in cognitive science.
