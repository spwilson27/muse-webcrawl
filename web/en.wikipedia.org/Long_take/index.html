Long take

A Steadicam long take being rehearsed

A long take is a continuous shot in film that lasts much longer than the conventional editing pace would suggest. Directors known for long takes include Andrei Tarkovsky, Theo Angelopoulos, Miklós Jancsó, Béla Tarr, Alfonso Cuarón and Alejandro González Iñárritu. A film composed of a single long take is called a one-shot film; some films, such as Russian Ark (2002) and Victoria (2015), consist of one genuine unbroken take, while others, such as Birdman (2014) and 1917 (2019), use hidden cuts to create the illusion of a single take.

Technique and effect

Long takes demand precise choreography of actors and camera, often using cranes, dollies, Steadicam or handheld movement, and careful blocking and lighting. The average shot length of mainstream cinema has shortened over time, so conspicuous long takes stand out as a stylistic statement: they can build tension, display virtuosity, immerse the viewer in real time, or emphasise spatial continuity. Famous examples include the opening of Orson Welles's Touch of Evil (1958), the Copa shot in Martin Scorsese's Goodfellas (1990), and the car-ambush sequence in Cuarón's Children of Men (2006).

History

Early films were necessarily long takes because editing conventions had not developed. Film theorist André Bazin championed the long take and deep focus in the 1950s as preserving the ambiguity and spatial unity of reality. Digital cameras, freed from the ten-minute limit of a film magazine, made extremely long takes practical in the 21st century.
