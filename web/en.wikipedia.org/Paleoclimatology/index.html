Paleoclimatology

Paleoclimatology (British spelling: palaeoclimatology) is the scientific study of the history and variability of Earth's climate before the widespread availability of instrumental records. It reconstructs past climates from natural archives — so-called proxy data — such as tree rings, ice cores, ocean and lake sediments, corals, cave deposits, and pollen, extending our knowledge of climate from the roughly 150 years of measurements back over hundreds of thousands to millions of years.

Proxy data

Because thermometers cover only recent centuries, paleoclimatologists use proxies whose properties vary with climate. Tree rings record annual growth conditions; isotopic ratios in the shells of foraminifera in marine sediments reflect ocean temperature and ice volume; air bubbles trapped in Antarctic and Greenland ice preserve samples of ancient atmospheres, including greenhouse-gas concentrations. Records can be dated by radiocarbon, uranium-series methods, layer counting, and correlation with dated volcanic ash layers.

Key findings

Paleoclimate research established the glacial–interglacial cycles of the Quaternary and their pacing by orbital (Milankovitch) variations; documented abrupt changes such as the Younger Dryas; characterised warm periods including the Medieval Climate Anomaly and cold intervals such as the Little Ice Age; and showed that atmospheric carbon dioxide and temperature have moved closely together over the last 800,000 years. Reconstructions of the past two millennia underpin assessments of how unusual recent warming is in a long-term context.

Relevance

Paleoclimatology tests climate models against real past states of the Earth system, constrains climate sensitivity, and provides context for current anthropogenic climate change, including rates of past sea-level and temperature change.
