French Revolution of 1848

The French Revolution of 1848, sometimes referred to as the February Revolution, was a wave of political upheaval in France and part of the wider Revolutions of 1848 across Europe. It ended the July Monarchy of King Louis-Philippe and created the French Second Republic. Fighting in Paris on 22–24 February 1848, sparked by the banning of a reform banquet and the killing of demonstrators on the Boulevard des Capucines, forced the king to abdicate and flee to England.

The Second Republic and the June Days

The provisional government, including the poet Lamartine and the socialist Louis Blanc, proclaimed universal male suffrage, abolished slavery in the colonies, and established the National Workshops to relieve unemployment. When the Workshops were closed in June 1848, Parisian workers rose in the June Days insurrection (23–26 June), which General Cavaignac suppressed with thousands killed and some 15,000 arrested or deported. In December 1848 Louis-Napoléon Bonaparte, nephew of Napoleon I, was elected President by landslide.

Outcome

The revolution's conservative turn, the "Party of Order" majority elected in May 1849, restrictions on the suffrage in 1850, and Louis-Napoléon's coup d'état of 2 December 1851 ended the republic and led to the Second Empire. Karl Marx analysed these events in The Class Struggles in France and The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte. Despite its failure, 1848 permanently established universal male suffrage in France and inspired democratic movements across Europe.
