Principle of least action

FieldClassical mechanics; variational principles
QuantityAction S = ∫ L dt, where L = T − V
Key figuresMaupertuis; Euler; Lagrange; Hamilton

The principle of least action — more precisely, the principle of stationary action — is a variational principle stating that the actual trajectory of a physical system between two configurations is the one for which the action functional is stationary (a minimum, maximum, or saddle point). The action is the time integral of the Lagrangian, the difference between kinetic and potential energy.

History

The idea traces to Pierre Louis Maupertuis in the 1740s, who proclaimed a metaphysical principle that nature acts with minimum effort. Leonhard Euler gave it a rigorous form for individual particles, Joseph-Louis Lagrange extended it within analytical mechanics, and William Rowan Hamilton formulated the modern version in 1834–35, from which the Euler–Lagrange equations of motion follow.

Significance

Stationary action unifies mechanics: Newton's laws, the behaviour of light rays (via Fermat's principle in optics), and the field equations of electromagnetism and general relativity can all be derived from suitable action principles. Symmetries of the action imply conservation laws via Noether's theorem. In quantum mechanics, Feynman's path integral formulation shows that the classical path of stationary action dominates the sum over all possible paths.
