An oak is a tree or shrub in the genus Quercus of the beech family, Fagaceae. There are approximately 500 extant species of oaks, native to the Northern Hemisphere across Asia, Europe, North Africa, and the Americas. The fruit of the oak is a nut called an acorn, borne in a cup-like structure known as a cupule.

Description

Oaks are generally large, long-lived trees. Their leaves are often lobed, though some species have serrated or entire leaves. Many species are deciduous, marcescent (retaining dead leaves through winter), while others are evergreen ("live oaks"). Oaks are monoecious: male flowers are borne in dangling catkins and female flowers are small and inconspicuous. Acorns mature in one or two years depending on the species group.

Ecology

Oaks are keystone species in many habitats, supporting more wildlife than most other trees — hundreds of insect species, fungi, birds, and mammals feed on their leaves, acorns, and dead wood. Jays and squirrels disperse acorns and are responsible for planting many new trees. Oaks hybridize readily, which complicates classification.

Uses

Oak wood is strong, hard, and durable, long used for shipbuilding, furniture, flooring, and barrels for ageing wine, whisky, and sherry, to which it imparts flavour. Cork comes from the bark of the cork oak (Quercus suber). Oak bark is rich in tannins, historically used for tanning leather.

Cultural significance

The oak symbolizes strength and endurance and is the national tree of several countries, including England, Germany, and the United States. Ancient oaks such as England's Major Oak are landmarks of great age.
