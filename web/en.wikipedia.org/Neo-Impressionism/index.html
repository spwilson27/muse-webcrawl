Neo-Impressionism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Neo-Impressionism is a term coined by the French art critic Félix Fénéon in 1886 to describe an art movement founded by Georges Seurat. Seurat's most famous work, A Sunday Afternoon on the Island of La Grande Jatte (1884–1886), marked the beginning of the movement when it appeared at the eighth and final Impressionist exhibition in Paris. Neo-Impressionist painting is characterised by the use of the Divisionist technique of Pointillism, in which small, distinct dots or strokes of pure colour are applied in patterns intended to fuse in the viewer's eye.

Origins and theory

The Neo-Impressionists drew on contemporary colour theory, particularly the writings of Michel Eugène Chevreul on simultaneous contrast and Ogden Rood's Modern Chromatics, as well as the aesthetic writings of Charles Henry. Unlike the spontaneous naturalism of Impressionism, Neo-Impressionism sought a scientific, rational method: colours were not mixed on the palette but juxtaposed on the canvas so that optical mixture produced greater luminosity. Paul Signac's treatise D'Eugène Delacroix au Néo-Impressionnisme (1899) became the movement's manifesto.

Artists

Besides Seurat and Signac, the group included Camille Pissarro (briefly), Maximilien Luce, Henri-Edmond Cross, Charles Angrand, Théo van Rysselberghe and the young Henri Matisse, who spent a summer with Signac in 1904, an encounter that helped precipitate Fauvism. Many Neo-Impressionists were also drawn to anarchist politics, and their serene, harmonious landscapes often carried a utopian social vision.

Legacy

Seurat died in 1891 at only 31, but the movement continued into the first decade of the twentieth century. Its systematic approach to colour influenced the Fauves, the Orphists, and later generations of painters interested in the science of perception. See also Post-Impressionism and Divisionism.
