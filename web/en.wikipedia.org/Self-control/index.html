Self-control

Self-control, an aspect of inhibitory control, is the ability to regulate one's emotions, thoughts, and behavior in the face of temptations and impulses. As an executive function, it is a cognitive process that is necessary for regulating one's behavior in order to achieve specific goals.

A related concept in psychology is emotional self-regulation. Self-control is thought to be like a muscle. According to studies, self-regulation, whether emotional or behavioral, was proven to be a limited resource which functions like energy. In the short term, overuse of self-control will lead to depletion. However, in the long term, the use of self-control can strengthen and improve over time.

Research

Delayed gratification

The classic experiments on delayed gratification were conducted by Walter Mischel at Stanford University, including the famous Stanford marshmallow experiment, in which children were offered a choice between one small reward immediately or two small rewards if they waited. Follow-up studies suggested that the ability to delay gratification correlated with later academic and life outcomes, though later replications found weaker effects once socioeconomic factors were controlled.

Ego depletion

Roy Baumeister and colleagues proposed that self-control draws on a limited resource, a phenomenon called ego depletion. The strength model suggests that acts of volition deplete this resource, making subsequent self-control harder, though this account has been debated in subsequent replication research.

Philosophy and religion

Self-control is one of the cardinal virtues in many traditions. In Stoicism, mastery of one's desires and emotions is central to the good life. The Greek concept of akrasia—acting against one's better judgment—poses the philosophical puzzle of weakness of will. Most major religions, including Christianity, Buddhism, and Islam, valorize self-discipline and restraint.

Applications

Self-control is implicated in outcomes ranging from academic achievement and health behaviour to criminal offending; the general theory of crime by Gottfredson and Hirschi identifies low self-control as a principal cause of criminal behaviour.
