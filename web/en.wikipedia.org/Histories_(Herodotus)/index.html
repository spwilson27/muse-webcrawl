Histories (Herodotus)

The Histories (Ancient Greek: Ἱστορίαι, Historíai, "inquiries") of Herodotus (c. 484 – c. 425 BCE) is the founding work of history in Western literature. Written in the Ionic dialect of Greek in the 5th century BCE, it is an inquiry into the origins of the Greco-Persian Wars, culminating in the Persian invasions of Greece in 490 and 480–479 BCE. It is at once a war narrative, a geography and ethnography of the known world, and a vast treasury of stories, customs and marvels gathered in Herodotus's travels.

Structure

Later editors divided the work into nine books, each named after one of the Muses. The opening books trace the growth of the Persian Empire under Cyrus, Cambyses, Darius and Xerxes through long digressions on Lydia, Egypt (Book 2, a famous account of Egyptian customs and history), Scythia (Book 4) and Libya. The final books narrate the Ionian Revolt, the Battle of Marathon (Book 6), and Xerxes' invasion — Thermopylae, Salamis and Plataea (Books 7–9). Herodotus states his aim in the first sentence: that human achievements may not be forgotten, and to explain the reason why Greeks and barbarians went to war.

Method and reliability

Herodotus worked from oral testimony, inscriptions and his own observation, often reporting variant accounts and flagging what he himself doubted ("I am obliged to report what is said, but I am not at all obliged to believe it"). Ancient critics were divided: Cicero called him the "Father of History", while others, including Plutarch in a hostile essay, branded him a "father of lies". Modern scholarship credits much of his ethnographic and geographical reporting while treating speeches and some numbers — notably the vast size of Xerxes' army — as literary constructions.

Legacy

The Histories shaped all later historiography through its successor Thucydides, and its set pieces — the meeting of Solon and Croesus, the 300 Spartans at Thermopylae, the bridge of boats across the Hellespont — remain canonical episodes of the classical tradition.
