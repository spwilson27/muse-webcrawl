Homestead Acts

The Homestead Acts were a series of laws in the United States that allowed adult citizens (or intended citizens) to claim surveyed government land — a homestead — and acquire title to it by residing on and improving it. The principal statute, the Homestead Act of 1862, signed by President Abraham Lincoln on 20 May 1862 during the Civil War, granted 160 acres (65 ha) of public land to claimants who lived on the land for five years and built a dwelling and cultivated it. Over the programme's life, roughly 1.6 million successful claims transferred about 270 million acres — some 10% of all US land — into private hands. The last claim under the general act was filed in Alaska in 1988.

Background

Free land for settlers had been a demand of labour and reform movements since the 1840s, championed by Horace Greeley and the Free Soil and Republican parties, but Southern senators blocked homestead bills because they feared new free states and opposed distributing land without revenue. A 1860 bill passed Congress but was vetoed by President Buchanan. Secession removed the obstruction: with Southern members gone, the Republican Congress passed the act alongside the Morrill land-grant college act and the Pacific Railway acts in 1862. The first claim under the act is generally credited to Daniel Freeman of Nebraska, filed minutes after midnight on 1 January 1863.

Provisions and related laws

Claimants had to be 21 or heads of household, citizens or immigrants who had filed for naturalisation — a category that included women, single or widowed, and, after the Fourteenth Amendment, African Americans; notably, it excluded Native Americans, whose land much of this was. After five years of residence and cultivation, and payment of a modest fee, the settler "proved up" and received a patent (deed). Land could also be "commuted" — purchased outright at $1.25 per acre after six months. Later statutes adapted the system: the Timber Culture Act (1873), the Desert Land Act (1877), the Enlarged Homestead Act (1909, 320 acres in dry country), and the Stock-Raising Homestead Act (1916, 640 acres). The Southern Homestead Act (1866) aimed, largely unsuccessfully, to open land in the South to freedpeople and loyal whites.

Impact and criticism

Homesteading peopled the Great Plains and West with farm families — ultimately claimants in 30 states — and fixed the 160-acre family farm in the American imagination. But the system was hard: drought, grasshoppers, and isolation defeated many entrants, and by some estimates a majority of claims were never proved up. Fraud was extensive — speculators and ranching and timber companies used dummy entrymen to assemble large holdings — and 160 acres was often unviable in the arid West. For Native nations the programme was a catastrophe, privatising lands taken through war, broken treaties, and coercive cessions; historians note the acts transferred vast Indigenous territories to non-Indian ownership. The programme wound down with the Taylor Grazing Act (1934), was repealed for the contiguous states by the Federal Land Policy and Management Act of 1976, and ended in Alaska in 1986; the final patent, to Kenneth Deardorff for land near the Stony River, was issued in 1988.
