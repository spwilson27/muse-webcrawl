Leonardo di ser Piero da Vinci (15 April 1452 – 2 May 1519) was an Italian polymath of the High Renaissance who worked as a painter, draughtsman, engineer, scientist, theorist, sculptor and architect. While his fame initially rested on his achievements as a painter, he has become known also for his notebooks, in which he made drawings and notes on a variety of subjects, including anatomy, astronomy, botany, cartography, painting and palaeontology. Leonardo is widely regarded as one of the greatest painters of all time and perhaps the most diversely talented person ever to have lived.

Early life and Florence

Leonardo was born out of wedlock to Piero da Vinci, a Florentine legal notary, and a peasant girl named Caterina, in the Tuscan hill town of Vinci. He received little formal education in Latin or mathematics but was apprenticed in his mid-teens to the leading Florentine workshop of Andrea del Verrocchio, where he learned painting, sculpture and the technical arts alongside Perugino and (slightly earlier) Botticelli. By 1478 he was an independent master. His early works include the Annunciation, the Ginevra de' Benci, and the unfinished Adoration of the Magi (1481).

Milan and The Last Supper

In about 1482 Leonardo entered the service of Ludovico Sforza, Duke of Milan, presenting himself principally as a military engineer while also designing festivals and stage sets. In Milan he painted the Virgin of the Rocks, the Lady with an Ermine (portrait of Ludovico's mistress Cecilia Gallerani), and, between about 1495 and 1498, The Last Supper on the refectory wall of Santa Maria delle Grazie — an experimental technique that began deteriorating within decades, yet one of the most famous and reproduced paintings in the world. His gigantic clay model for a bronze equestrian monument to Francesco Sforza was destroyed by invading French troops in 1499, and Leonardo left Milan.

Later career

After periods in Mantua, Venice and Florence — where he served Cesare Borgia as military engineer and cartographer, competed with Michelangelo on battle frescoes for the Palazzo Vecchio, and began the Mona Lisa — Leonardo returned to Milan under French rule, then spent 1513–1516 in Rome under Medici patronage. In 1516 King Francis I invited him to France, settling him at the manor of Clos Lucé near the royal château at Amboise, where he died in 1519, according to Vasari with the king at his side.

Paintings and technique

Fewer than twenty paintings are generally attributed to Leonardo in whole or large part. They are characterised by his mastery of sfumato (the smoky blending of tones), atmospheric perspective, and profound study of light, geology and expression. The Mona Lisa (Lisa del Giocondo) became the most famous portrait in art. The rediscovered Salvator Mundi, attributed wholly or partly to Leonardo, sold at auction in 2017 for $450.3 million, the highest price ever paid for a work of art.

Science and notebooks

Leonardo filled thousands of notebook pages — written in his characteristic left-handed mirror script — with studies of anatomy (based on some thirty dissections of human corpses), optics, hydraulics, flight, geology and mechanics. He designed flying machines, a helicopter-like aerial screw, a tank, concentrated solar power, a calculator and a double hull, most far ahead of the engineering of his day. Because the notebooks remained largely unpublished for centuries, his scientific influence on his own era was limited; their modern fame rests on their astonishing observational precision. His Vitruvian Man drawing has become an iconic image of the union of art and science.

Legacy

Leonardo has become the archetype of the "Renaissance man" — a figure of unquenchable curiosity and inventive imagination. Vasari's Lives already treated him as a wonder of nature, and modern scholars continue to debate his working methods, his unfinished projects, and the attribution of newly surfaced works.
