Rust Belt

The Rust Belt is a region of the United States, principally in the Northeast and Midwest, that was once the centre of American heavy industry — steel, automobiles, coal, and manufacturing — and that experienced severe industrial decline, population loss, and urban decay beginning in the late 20th century. It stretches roughly from upstate New York and Pennsylvania through Ohio, Michigan, Indiana, Illinois, and Wisconsin, including cities such as Buffalo, Cleveland, Pittsburgh, Detroit, Toledo, Gary, Milwaukee, and Flint.

Closed factories gave the region its name, evoking rusting industrial plant.

History

In the late 19th and early 20th centuries the region — then called the Manufacturing Belt — industrialised rapidly thanks to Great Lakes water transport, abundant coal and iron ore, immigrant labour, and access to eastern markets. It powered American growth through two world wars. From the 1970s onward, automation, foreign competition, the relocation of factories to the U.S. Sun Belt and overseas, and the decline of unionised industry destroyed hundreds of thousands of jobs. The term "Rust Belt" came into wide use in the early 1980s, popularised in part during Walter Mondale's 1984 presidential campaign.

Consequences and revival efforts

Many cities lost half or more of their populations from their mid-century peaks; Detroit's 2013 municipal bankruptcy was the largest in U.S. history. The region's economic distress became a major factor in national politics. Some cities have partially reinvented themselves around healthcare, education, logistics, and technology — Pittsburgh's shift from steel to "eds and meds" is often cited — while others continue to struggle with disinvestment, vacant housing, and the opioid epidemic.
