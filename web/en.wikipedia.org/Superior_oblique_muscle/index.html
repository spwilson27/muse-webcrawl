Superior oblique muscle

The superior oblique muscle is one of the six extraocular muscles that control movement of the eye. It originates from the body of the sphenoid bone, passes forward along the medial wall of the orbit, and runs through a fibrocartilaginous pulley, the trochlea, which changes its direction so that its tendon inserts on the posterolateral part of the eyeball behind the equator.

Actions and innervation

The superior oblique primarily intorts the eye (rotates its top toward the nose) and also depresses and abducts it; its depressing action is greatest when the eye is adducted. It is the longest and thinnest of the extraocular muscles and is supplied by the trochlear nerve (cranial nerve IV), the only cranial nerve to emerge from the dorsal brainstem and the only one supplying a single muscle.

Clinical significance

Palsy of the trochlear nerve or weakness of the superior oblique causes vertical double vision (diplopia) and a compensatory head tilt away from the affected side, often worsened by reading or descending stairs. Congenital superior oblique palsy is the commonest cause of vertical strabismus; acquired cases may follow head trauma, in which the nerve's long intracranial course makes it vulnerable.
