Mudpot

A mudpot — or mud pool — is a type of acidic hot spring, or fumarole, with limited water. It takes the form of a pool of bubbling mud, produced where rising steam and volcanic gases such as hydrogen sulfide are oxidised by micro-organisms to sulfuric acid, which dissolves the surrounding rock into clay. The result is a viscous, often brightly coloured slurry of mud that bubbles and plops as gases escape through it.

Formation

Mudpots form where the water table is low and steam condenses near the surface, creating highly acidic conditions (pH can be below 2). Because little liquid water is present, the dissolved rock cannot be carried away, so it accumulates as a thick mud. Colours range from white and grey to pink, red and brown, depending on the mineral content and the micro-organisms present.

Notable locations

Famous mudpots occur in Yellowstone National Park (notably the Fountain Paint Pot and Artist's Paint Pots), in Iceland's Hverir and Krýsuvík areas, in New Zealand's Rotorua and Wai-O-Tapu geothermal zones, and at the Salton Sea in California. Unlike mud volcanoes, which are driven by sedimentary gas rather than magmatic heat, mudpots are surface expressions of a hydrothermal system.
