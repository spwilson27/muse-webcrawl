Transitional justice

[Image: A truth commission hearing in session]
images/transitionaljustice.png

Transitional justice refers to the set of judicial and non-judicial measures societies adopt to address legacies of massive human rights violations when emerging from armed conflict, authoritarian rule or genocide. Typical instruments include criminal prosecutions, truth commissions, reparations programmes, institutional reforms (including vetting or "lustration"), and memorialisation. The field seeks to balance accountability, victims' rights, social reconciliation and political stability.

Instruments

  • Criminal prosecutions — domestic trials (e.g. Argentina's junta trials, Rwanda's gacaca adapted courts), ad hoc international tribunals for the former Yugoslavia and Rwanda, and the permanent International Criminal Court (est. 2002).
  • Truth commissions — official bodies documenting patterns of abuse; the best known is South Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission (1995–2002), which offered amnesty for full disclosure.
  • Reparations — compensation, rehabilitation and symbolic restitution to victims and communities.
  • Institutional reform — vetting security forces and judiciaries, disarmament programmes, and constitutional reform to prevent recurrence.

History and debates

Modern transitional justice traces roots to the Nuremberg and Tokyo trials after World War II, matured through Latin America's democratic transitions of the 1980s, and expanded after the Cold War with truth commissions in Chile, Guatemala, Sierra Leone and Timor-Leste. Ongoing debates concern the tension between peace and justice (whether prosecutions deter spoilers or destabilise settlements), the sequencing of measures, the balance between retributive and restorative approaches, and the risk of externally imposed "transplant" models ignoring local practices.
