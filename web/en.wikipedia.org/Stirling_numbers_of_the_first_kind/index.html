Stirling numbers of the first kind

In combinatorics, the Stirling numbers of the first kind count permutations according to their number of cycles. They are named after James Stirling, who introduced them in the 18th century. The unsigned Stirling numbers of the first kind, written c(n, k) or [n k], count the permutations of n elements with exactly k disjoint cycles; the signed numbers s(n, k) carry the sign (−1)n−k.

Definition and recurrence

The unsigned numbers satisfy the recurrence c(n + 1, k) = n·c(n, k) + c(n, k − 1), with c(0, 0) = 1: a new element either joins one of the n existing positions in cycles or forms a new cycle of its own. The first few rows are 1; 1, 1; 2, 3, 1; 6, 11, 6, 1.

Relation to factorials

The numbers arise in the expansion of the falling factorial: x(x − 1)(x − 2)…(x − n + 1) = Σ s(n, k) xk. Together with the Stirling numbers of the second kind — which count set partitions into blocks — they provide the change-of-basis matrices between ordinary powers and falling factorials.

Properties

Summing over k gives c summed to n!; the expected number of cycles in a random permutation of n elements is the harmonic number Hn. Stirling numbers of the first kind also appear in the asymptotic expansion Stirling's approximation contexts and in the theory of generating functions.
