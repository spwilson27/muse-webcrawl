Tula (Nahuatl: Tollan, "place of reeds") is a Mesoamerican archaeological site in the modern Mexican state of Hidalgo, about 75 kilometres north of Mexico City. It was the capital of the Toltec civilisation in the Early Postclassic period, and one of the largest cities of ancient Mesoamerica, with a population estimated at between 30,000 and 60,000 at its height.

History

Tula rose to prominence after the decline of Teotihuacan, reaching its peak between about 950 and 1150 CE. Later Aztec and colonial sources remembered Tollan as a legendary golden-age capital ruled by the priest-king Ce Ácatl Topiltzin Quetzalcóatl, whose exile is a central myth of later Mesoamerican tradition. The city appears to have been violently destroyed by fire around the mid-twelfth century, after which it was heavily looted.

Architecture

The ceremonial centre includes two pyramids. Pyramid B is famous for the four massive "Atlantean" statues—basalt warrior figures over 4.5 metres tall—that once supported its temple roof, and for carved reliefs of jaguars, coyotes and eagles eating human hearts. The adjacent Burned Palace (Palacio Quemado) contains colonnaded halls and benches with processional reliefs. The site also has two ballcourts and extensive residential zones.

Influence

Tula's art and architecture show notable parallels with the Maya city of Chichén Itzá in Yucatán, though the direction and nature of the influence between the two remains debated by archaeologists.
