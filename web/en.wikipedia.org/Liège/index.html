Liège (French: Liège; Dutch: Luik; German: Lüttich; Walloon: Lidje) is a city in eastern Belgium, the capital of the province of Liège in Wallonia, situated where the Meuse and Ourthe rivers meet. It is Wallonia's principal city and one of Belgium's major economic and cultural centers, long nicknamed "La Cité ardente" ("the fiery city").

History

Liège grew around the cult of Saint Lambert, martyred there in the early eighth century. From the tenth century it was the capital of a prince-bishopric — a state of the Holy Roman Empire ruled by its bishops — whose power peaked under Notker and whose territory lasted until the French Revolution. The city's medieval burghers repeatedly fought their prince-bishops for privileges, suffering a brutal sack by Charles the Bold of Burgundy in 1468. In August 1914, the Battle of Liège, resisting the German invasion around its ring of forts, was the first battle of the First World War.

Industry and transport

Coal, iron, and the skills of Walloon metalworkers made Liège one of the cradles of the continental Industrial Revolution; John Cockerill's integrated works at nearby Seraing exemplified its steel industry. The Port of Liège on the Meuse is one of Europe's largest inland ports, and Liège Airport is a major cargo hub. The city's modern station, Liège-Guillemins, designed by Santiago Calatrava, opened in 2009.

Culture

Liège is home to a French-speaking university (founded 1817), the Opéra Royal de Wallonie, and the Curtius Museum of archaeology and arms — the city being famous for centuries of gunmaking. Its folklore includes the marionette hero Tchantchès and the Sunday market of La Batte. In sport, the cycling classic Liège–Bastogne–Liège, first run in 1892, is one of the five Monuments of professional cycling.
