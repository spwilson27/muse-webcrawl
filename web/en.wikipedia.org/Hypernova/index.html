Hypernova

A hypernova is a type of stellar explosion, resembling a very energetic supernova, thought to result from the collapse of an extremely massive star directly into a black hole. The term was coined in the late 1990s to describe explosions like SN 1998bw, associated with the gamma-ray burst GRB 980425, which released far more kinetic energy than ordinary core-collapse supernovae — of the order of 1052 ergs or more.

Mechanism

Hypernovae are modelled as collapsars: a rapidly rotating massive star whose core collapses to a black hole, forming an accretion disk that drives powerful relativistic jets along the rotation axis. The jets and the energy released make the explosion highly asymmetric and much more luminous and energetic than a normal supernova. Viewed along the jet, the event may appear as a long gamma-ray burst; viewed off-axis, it presents as a broad-lined Type Ic supernova (Type Ic-BL).

Observations

Examples include SN 1998bw/GRB 980425, SN 2003dh/GRB 030329, and SN 2006aj. Because they are visible over cosmological distances and occur in low-metallicity dwarf galaxies, hypernovae and their associated bursts are used as probes of star formation in the early universe. Superluminous supernovae are a related class of extremely bright stellar explosions, though their power source may be different (a magnetar engine).
