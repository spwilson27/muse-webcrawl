Ancient Greek

Ancient Greek
Region: Greece, Aegean, western Asia Minor, Magna Graecia
Era: c. 1500 BC – c. AD 300 (into Koine)
Family: Indo-European → Hellenic
Scripts: Linear B, Greek alphabet

Ancient Greek is the stage of the Greek language attested from around the 16th century BC to the end of antiquity. It is the language of the epics of Homer, of classical Athenian literature and philosophy, and—in its Koine form—of the New Testament and the Hellenistic world. It is conventionally divided into Mycenaean, Archaic, Classical, Hellenistic (Koine), and late periods.

History

The earliest surviving Greek is Mycenaean, written in the Linear B syllabary on clay tablets from palaces such as Knossos and Pylos. After the Bronze Age collapse, Greek re-emerged written in an alphabet adapted from Phoenician, with vowels added. The Archaic and Classical periods saw a flowering of dialects—Attic, Ionic, Doric, Aeolic—and the Attic-based Koine spread across the eastern Mediterranean after the conquests of Alexander the Great.

Grammar

Ancient Greek is a highly inflected language. Nouns decline in three genders, three numbers (including a dual), and five cases; verbs conjugate for person, number, tense, mood, voice, and aspect, with a complex system of participles and infinitives. Word order is flexible, and meaning is carried largely by endings. A distinctive feature is the pitch accent, marked by the acute, grave, and circumflex.

Literature and legacy

The language preserves the foundational texts of Western literature: the Iliad and Odyssey, the tragedies of Aeschylus, Sophocles, and Euripides, the histories of Herodotus and Thucydides, and the philosophy of Plato and Aristotle. Greek supplied vast vocabulary to Latin and, through it and directly, to the modern languages of Europe, especially in science, medicine, and philosophy.
