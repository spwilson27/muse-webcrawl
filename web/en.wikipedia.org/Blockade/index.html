Blockade

A blockade is the act of actively preventing a country or region from receiving or sending out food, supplies, weapons, or communications, and sometimes people, by military force. A blockade differs from an embargo or sanction, which are legal barriers to trade rather than physical interdiction. Naval blockades are intended to cut off communications and shipping to an enemy coast.

Legal status

Under customary international law codified since the 1856 Paris Declaration, a blockade must be declared and notified to all states, must be effective (maintained by sufficient force), must be applied impartially to vessels of all nations, and must not bar access to neutral ports. Blockades that have the sole purpose of starving a civilian population are prohibited, and humanitarian relief must be allowed through under the San Remo Manual.

History

Britain's blockade of Napoleonic France and France's Continental System were economic warfare on a grand scale. In the American Civil War, the Union's Anaconda Plan blockaded Confederate ports, throttling the cotton trade. During the First World War, the Allied blockade of Germany contributed to severe food shortages, and Germany answered with unrestricted submarine warfare against Allied shipping. The Berlin Blockade of 1948–49, a land blockade, was defeated by the Allied airlift. During the Cuban Missile Crisis of 1962 the United States imposed a naval "quarantine" of Cuba, choosing the term to avoid the legal implications of "blockade".

Types

Blockades may be naval, aerial, or land-based; close or distant; and total or partial. A pacific blockade is imposed in peacetime as a coercive measure short of declared war.
