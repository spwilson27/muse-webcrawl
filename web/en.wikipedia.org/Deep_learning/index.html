Deep learning

A deep neural network: stacked layers of units transform inputs into increasingly abstract features.

Deep learning is a subset of machine learning that focuses on algorithms known as artificial neural networks, especially those with many layers ("deep" architectures). It is a data-driven approach that automatically learns hierarchical representations of data, with each layer transforming the representation at one level into a representation at a more abstract level. Deep learning underpins most modern artificial intelligence, including large language models, computer vision, speech recognition and game-playing systems.

History

Foundations run from the perceptron (Frank Rosenblatt, 1958) through backpropagation popularised in 1986 by Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams, and convolutional networks (LeNet, Yann LeCun, 1989–98). Progress stalled until the 2000s, when larger datasets, GPU computing and improved training methods (ReLU activations, dropout, better initialisation) converged. The 2012 ImageNet victory of AlexNet (Krizhevsky, Sutskever, Hinton) ignited the deep learning boom; the transformer architecture (Vaswani et al., "Attention Is All You Need", 2017) then enabled GPT-class language models and AlphaFold-class scientific breakthroughs. Training these models is fundamentally an optimisation problem solved with gradient descent — a direct application of calculus.

Architectures and methods

  • Feedforward and convolutional networks — images and general pattern recognition.
  • Recurrent networks and LSTMs — sequences (largely superseded by transformers).
  • Transformers — self-attention models behind BERT, GPT and modern multimodal systems.
  • Generative models — GANs, variational autoencoders and diffusion models, used for image and media synthesis including additive 3D printing design.
  • Reinforcement learning — DeepMind's DQN and AlphaGo combined deep nets with RL.

Applications and criticism

Applications span translation, protein-structure prediction, medical imaging, recommendation (including country music streaming), materials discovery relevant to band gap engineering, and cosmological inference about dark matter maps. Criticisms include huge energy and data demands, opacity ("black box" decisions), brittleness to adversarial examples, and bias inherited from training data.
