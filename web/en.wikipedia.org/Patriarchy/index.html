Patriarchy is a social system in which positions of dominance and privilege are held by men. The term is used in anthropology to describe a family or clan system governed by the eldest male or a council of males, and in feminist theory to describe a broader social structure in which men hold primary power in political leadership, moral authority, social privilege and control of property.

Definitions and usage

Literally "rule of the father" (from Greek pater, father, and arkhē, rule), patriarchy originally described societies organized around male household heads whose authority over women, children, juniors and property was institutionalized in law and custom. Extended uses in social theory analyse how gender hierarchy is reproduced through institutions such as the family, labour markets, the state and culture.

History and origins

The historian Gerda Lerner, in The Creation of Patriarchy (1986), argued that male dominance arose gradually in the ancient Near East over some 2,500 years, linked to the commodification of women's sexuality and reproduction, the rise of the archaic state, and the development of private property — a theme also present in Friedrich Engels's The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State (1884). Anthropologists debate how universal patriarchal organization is across societies and periods.

Feminist theory

Second-wave feminist writers such as Kate Millett (Sexual Politics, 1970) popularized patriarchy as an analytic term for systemic male domination. Sylvia Walby's Theorizing Patriarchy (1990) identified six structures — household production, paid work, the state, male violence, sexuality and cultural institutions — through which patriarchy operates, and distinguished private from public forms. Critics, including some feminists, have questioned the concept's universality and its capacity to account for differences among women by class, race and culture; intersectional approaches address patriarchy together with other systems of inequality.

Biology and evolution debates

Some evolutionary psychologists argue that aspects of male dominance reflect evolved sex differences, while many anthropologists and sociologists emphasize the variability of gender arrangements across cultures — from strongly patriarchal to relatively egalitarian — as evidence for social rather than purely biological determination.
