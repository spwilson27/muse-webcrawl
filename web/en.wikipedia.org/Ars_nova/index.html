Ars nova

Ars nova ("new art") is a term for the style of polyphonic music that flourished in the Kingdom of France and the Burgundian Low Countries in the 14th century, roughly from the publication of the treatise Ars nova (c. 1320), attributed to Philippe de Vitry, until the death of Guillaume de Machaut in 1377. It succeeded the ars antiqua of the Notre Dame era.

Innovations

The ars nova treatises legitimised duple as well as triple mensuration, smaller note values and greater rhythmic independence between voices. Composers developed the isorhythmic motet, in which a repeating rhythmic pattern (talea) organises the tenor, and refined the secular formes fixes — ballade, rondeau and virelai. Guillaume de Machaut, the style's leading figure, composed the first complete polyphonic setting of the Mass Ordinary attributable to a single composer, the Messe de Nostre Dame.

Geographical spread and late phase

In Italy the corresponding style is often called the Trecento, associated with composers such as Francesco Landini and its own notational system. In the later 14th century, musicians at Avignon and in southern France cultivated an extremely intricate rhythmic manner now termed ars subtilior. The ars nova's notational and structural advances laid the groundwork for Renaissance polyphony.
