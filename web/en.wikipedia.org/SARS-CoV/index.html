SARS-CoV

Severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus (SARS-CoV, now also called SARS-CoV-1) is a strain of coronavirus that causes severe acute respiratory syndrome (SARS), the disease responsible for the 2002–2004 SARS outbreak. It is an enveloped, positive-sense, single-stranded RNA virus of the genus Betacoronavirus, believed to have originated in bats and spread to humans through intermediate hosts such as masked palm civets.

The 2002–2004 outbreak

SARS emerged in Guangdong, China, in November 2002. It spread internationally in early 2003, notably through a hotel cluster in Hong Kong, reaching 29 countries. Carlo Urbani, the WHO physician who first recognised the new disease, died of it in March 2003. The outbreak was contained by July 2003 through case isolation and quarantine; a small number of cases recurred in late 2003 and a laboratory-acquired cluster in 2004, after which no natural cases have occurred.

Virology

Like other coronaviruses, SARS-CoV particles bear spike (S) glycoproteins that bind the human ACE2 receptor. Its genome encodes the replicase polyproteins, structural proteins S, E, M and N, and several accessory proteins. A closely related virus, SARS-CoV-2, emerged in 2019 and caused the COVID-19 pandemic; the two share about 79% genomic sequence identity.

Reservoir and origin

Horseshoe bats harbour many SARS-related coronaviruses and are considered the natural reservoir. Viruses nearly identical to human SARS-CoV were found in masked palm civets sold in Guangdong live-animal markets, suggesting civets served as an amplifying intermediate host after a bat-to-civet spillover.
