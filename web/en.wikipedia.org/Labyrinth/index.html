Labyrinth

In Greek mythology, the Labyrinth (Greek: Λαβύρινθος, labýrinthos) is an elaborate, confusing structure designed and built by the legendary artificer Daedalus for King Minos of Crete at Knossos. Its function was to hold the Minotaur, the monster eventually killed by the hero Theseus. Daedalus had so cunningly made the Labyrinth that he could barely escape it after he built it.

Although early Cretan coins occasionally exhibit branching (multicursal) patterns, the single-path (unicursal) seven-course "Classical" design without branching or dead ends became associated with the Labyrinth on coins as early as 430 BC, and similar non-branching patterns are widely used in modern labyrinth designs. Unicursal patterns were historically used both as a dancing ground and in religious ritual; many modern labyrinths aim to set a contemplative mood as a walking meditation or prayer.

Etymology

The word labyrinth comes from the Greek labýrinthos, a word of pre-Greek origin that some scholars connect with labrys, the double-headed axe used in Cretan ritual. A Linear B tablet from Knossos mentions "the mistress of the labyrinth," suggesting the term was associated with Crete in the Bronze Age.

The Cretan Labyrinth

According to myth, Pasiphaë, wife of Minos, gave birth to the Minotaur, a creature with the head of a bull and the body of a man. Minos ordered Daedalus to construct the Labyrinth to contain it. Every nine years (or annually, in some accounts), Athens was forced to send seven youths and seven maidens as tribute, to be devoured by the Minotaur. Theseus volunteered, and with the help of Minos's daughter Ariadne — who gave him a ball of thread — he slew the Minotaur and escaped by retracing his path.

Labyrinth versus maze

In modern usage, labyrinth and maze are often used interchangeably, but specialists distinguish a unicursal labyrinth, which has a single non-branching path to the centre, from a multicursal maze, which offers choices and dead ends, as in garden hedge mazes.

Historical and modern labyrinths

Famous examples include the turf labyrinths of England, the pavement labyrinth of Chartres Cathedral (c. 1200), Scandinavian Troy towns, and the hedge maze at Hampton Court Palace (planted c. 1690). Contemporary labyrinths are installed in hospitals, schools, parks and places of worship as tools for meditation.
