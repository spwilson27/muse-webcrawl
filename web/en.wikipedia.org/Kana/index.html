Kana (Japanese: 仮名, "provisional names/characters") are syllabaries used to write the Japanese language, in contrast to the logographic kanji borrowed from Chinese. There are two modern kana scripts — the cursive hiragana (ひらがな) and the angular katakana (カタカナ) — each representing the same set of sounds, together with the historical variant forms known as hentaigana.

Origin and development

Kana developed in the Heian period (ninth century onward) from man'yōgana, Chinese characters used for their sound values. Hiragana evolved from the highly cursive (sōsho) forms of whole characters, written chiefly by women of the court — hence its early name onnade, "women's hand" — and used for literature such as The Tale of Genji. Katakana was developed by Buddhist monks from fragments of characters, as a shorthand for annotating Chinese texts. The Meiji-era and post-1945 reforms standardized both syllabaries to 46 basic characters and fixed their modern orthography.

Structure

Each kana represents a mora — a vowel, a consonant–vowel combination, or the moraic nasal ん/ン — arranged traditionally in the gojūon ("fifty sounds") table. Voiced and palatalized sounds are written with diacritics (dakuten and handakuten) and small versions of the y-row characters. A small っ/ッ marks geminate consonants.

Usage

Hiragana is used for native Japanese words not written in kanji, grammatical particles and inflections (okurigana), and pronunciation guides (furigana) printed beside kanji. Katakana is used for foreign loanwords, foreign names, onomatopoeia, emphasis, and scientific names. Together with kanji and the Latin alphabet (rōmaji), kana form the mixed script of modern Japanese.
