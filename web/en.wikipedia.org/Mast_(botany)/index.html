Mast (botany)

Mast is the fruit of forest trees and shrubs, such as acorns and other nuts. The term derives from the Old English mæst, referring to the nuts of forest trees that accumulate on the ground and, historically, were used as fodder for swine taken into the woodland under the practice of pannage.

Types

Ecologists distinguish hard mast — acorns, hickory nuts, beechnuts and chestnuts — from soft mast, the fleshy fruits and berries of species such as blackberry, dogwood, grape and holly. Hard mast is a critical autumn and winter food for deer, bears, squirrels, turkeys, wood ducks and many other animals.

Mast years

Many species produce seeds synchronously and intermittently in "mast years" (masting or mast seeding), in which a population produces a bumper crop followed by years of little or no seed. Proposed explanations include predator satiation — overwhelming seed-eaters so that some seeds survive — pollination efficiency in wind-pollinated trees, and resource matching with favourable weather. Mast events can cascade through food webs, affecting rodent numbers, their predators, and even human disease risk such as Lyme disease following acorn-driven mouse booms.
