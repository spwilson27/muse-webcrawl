Biogeochemical cycle

A biogeochemical cycle is a pathway by which a chemical substance or element moves through the biotic (living) and abiotic (non-living) compartments of Earth — the biosphere, atmosphere, hydrosphere, and lithosphere — being repeatedly recycled. Well-known examples are the carbon cycle, nitrogen cycle, water cycle, oxygen cycle, phosphorus cycle, and sulfur cycle. The concept reflects that matter, unlike energy, is conserved and reused within the Earth system.

Reservoirs and fluxes

Each cycle is described in terms of reservoirs (or pools) where an element resides — such as carbon in ocean water, fossil fuels, or forest biomass — and fluxes, the rates of transfer between reservoirs. Residence times vary enormously: a carbon atom may spend days in the atmosphere as part of a fast biological loop, or hundreds of millions of years locked in sedimentary rock. Cycles are driven by biological processes (photosynthesis, respiration, decomposition, nitrogen fixation), geological processes (weathering, volcanism, sedimentation, uplift), and increasingly by human activity.

Major cycles

The water cycle moves water by evaporation, condensation, precipitation, and runoff. The carbon cycle exchanges carbon dioxide between atmosphere, oceans, soils, and living tissue, with a slow geological component through rocks. The nitrogen cycle depends on specialised microorganisms that fix atmospheric N₂, nitrify ammonium, and denitrify nitrates back to gas. The phosphorus cycle is unusual in lacking a significant atmospheric phase: phosphorus moves from rock weathering through soils and organisms into ocean sediments, often limiting ecosystem productivity along the way.

Human disruption

Human activities now rival or exceed natural fluxes in several cycles. Burning fossil fuels and deforestation have raised atmospheric CO₂ by roughly half since pre-industrial times. Industrial nitrogen fixation through the Haber–Bosch process has approximately doubled the input of reactive nitrogen to the biosphere, contributing to eutrophication of waterways. Mining of phosphate rock and fertiliser use have similarly accelerated the phosphorus cycle, and these perturbations interact, since carbon, nitrogen, and water cycles are tightly coupled in climate and ecosystems.
