Poaching (cooking)

Poaching is a cooking technique that involves gently heating food in a liquid, such as water, milk, stock or wine. Poaching is differentiated from the other "moist heat" cooking methods, such as simmering and boiling, in that it uses a relatively lower temperature (about 70–80 °C / 158–176 °F). This temperature range makes it suitable for delicate foods such as eggs, poultry, fish and fruit, which might easily fall apart or dry out using other cooking methods.

Technique

The liquid should barely tremble, with only occasional small bubbles rising; a full boil would batter delicate foods. Adding a little acid, such as vinegar, to the water helps egg whites set for poached eggs. Poaching liquids may be seasoned and are sometimes retained to form the base of a sauce.

Shallow and deep poaching

In shallow poaching, food is partially submerged in a small amount of liquid in a covered pan, combining poaching and steaming. Deep poaching fully submerges the food. Court-bouillon, a briefly cooked acidulated stock with aromatics, is the classic medium for fish.

Examples

Poached eggs are a hallmark of eggs Benedict; whole fish such as salmon are traditionally poached; pears poached in red wine are a classic dessert.
