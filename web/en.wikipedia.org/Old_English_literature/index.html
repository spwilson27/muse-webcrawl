Old English literature

Old English literature comprises the surviving literature written in Old English in Anglo-Saxon England from the earliest texts of the seventh century to the decades after the Norman Conquest of 1066. Though much has been lost, the corpus that remains — some 400 manuscripts — is the richest vernacular literature of early medieval Europe, encompassing heroic epic, elegy, saints' lives, biblical translation, riddles, homilies, and chronicles.

Poetry

Almost all Old English poetry survives in just four manuscripts: the Beowulf manuscript, the Exeter Book, the Vercelli Book, and the Junius manuscript. The towering work is Beowulf, a 3,182-line heroic epic. Other major poems include the elegies The Wanderer and The Seafarer, the dream-vision The Dream of the Rood, and the near-hundred Exeter Book riddles. Verse follows a Germanic alliterative metre with strong stress and caesura rather than rhyme.

Prose

King Alfred the Great's translation programme of the ninth century made Old English prose a vehicle for history, philosophy, and pastoral care; the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, begun under Alfred, was maintained for centuries. Later masters such as Ælfric and Wulfstan produced large bodies of homiletic prose distinguished by rhythmical style.
