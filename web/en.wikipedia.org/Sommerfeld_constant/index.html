Sommerfeld constant

The Sommerfeld constant is another name for the fine-structure constant, usually denoted by the Greek letter α. It is a dimensionless physical constant that quantifies the strength of the electromagnetic interaction between elementary charged particles. It was introduced in 1916 by Arnold Sommerfeld as part of his extension of the Bohr model of the atom, where it described the fine structure — the splitting of spectral lines — of hydrogen.

Its value is approximately:

α ≈ 7.2973525693 × 10⁻³ ≈ 1/137.036

In natural units the constant can be written as α = e²/(4πε₀ħc), combining the elementary charge e, the permittivity of free space ε₀, the reduced Planck constant ħ, and the speed of light c. Because α is dimensionless, its value is the same in any system of units, a fact that has attracted much commentary; Richard Feynman called 1/137 "one of the greatest damn mysteries of physics: a magic number that comes to us with no understanding by man".

Interpretation

In quantum electrodynamics, α is the coupling constant for the interaction of electrons and photons. It is also equal to the square of the ratio of the elementary charge to the Planck charge, and to the ratio of the velocity of the electron in the first circular orbit of the Bohr model to the speed of light. Although constant at low energies, its effective value "runs" — increasing slightly at higher interaction energies due to vacuum polarization.
