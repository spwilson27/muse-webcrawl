Orphan source

An orphan source is a sealed radioactive source that has escaped regulatory control.

An orphan source is a radioactive source that is no longer under proper regulatory control because it has been lost, stolen, abandoned, improperly disposed of, or otherwise transferred without authorisation. The term is used by the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) and national radiation-protection authorities.

Hazards

Orphan sources may pose serious health risks. People who find an unfamiliar metallic object — for example in scrap metal — may handle it without knowing it contains a powerful emitter such as cobalt-60, caesium-137, or iridium-192, suffering severe radiation burns or fatal exposure. Scrapyard monitors and border radiation detectors are deployed partly to intercept such sources.

Notable incidents

The 1987 Goiânia accident in Brazil, in which a caesium-137 source from an abandoned radiotherapy machine was broken open by scavengers, killed four people and contaminated hundreds. Other serious events include the 1962 Mexico City incident and the 2010 Mayapuri accident in Delhi, both involving orphan cobalt-60 sources.

Control

The IAEA promotes national registries of sealed sources, "cradle-to-grave" tracking, and search campaigns to recover orphan sources, which number in the thousands worldwide, especially in states where regulation weakened after the dissolution of the Soviet Union.
