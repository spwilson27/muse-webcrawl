The Medal of Honor is the United States government's highest and most prestigious military decoration, awarded to members of the armed forces who distinguish themselves "through conspicuous gallantry and intrepidity at the risk of his or her life above and beyond the call of duty." It is presented by the president in the name of the Congress and is often called the Congressional Medal of Honor.

History

The medal was created during the American Civil War: a Navy version was authorized in December 1861 and an Army version in July 1862; the first awards were made to members of the Andrews Raid ("Great Locomotive Chase"). Early criteria were loose — the medal was the only American military decoration for much of its early history, and 911 awards to the 27th Maine for re-enlisting were among those revoked by a 1917 review board that struck 911 names from the rolls. Standards were tightened after the First World War, and the Air Force adopted its own design in 1960.

Appearance and privileges

The current Army medal is a gold five-pointed star surrounded by a laurel wreath, suspended from a bar inscribed "VALOR" attached to a light blue ribbon dotted with thirteen white stars; Navy and Air Force variants have distinct designs. Recipients receive a monthly pension, burial honours at Arlington National Cemetery, and other privileges fixed by law; the medal is protected by federal statute against unauthorized sale, manufacture or wear. Mary Walker, a Civil War surgeon, remains the only woman recipient; nineteen men have received the medal twice.
