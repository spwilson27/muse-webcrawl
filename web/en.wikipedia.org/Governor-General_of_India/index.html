The Governor-General of India — from 1858 also styled Viceroy and Governor-General of India — was the representative of the British monarch in British India and the head of the British administration there. The office evolved from the governorship of Fort William in Bengal created by the Regulating Act of 1773, was extended to the whole of India by the Charter Act of 1833, and survived the transfer of power in 1947 as the ceremonial head of state of the Dominion of India until the republic was proclaimed in 1950.

History

Warren Hastings became the first Governor-General of Bengal in 1774, overseeing the East India Company's territories. Successive holders — Cornwallis, Wellesley, and others — expanded Company rule. The Charter Act of 1833 made William Bentinck the first Governor-General of India proper. After the Indian Rebellion of 1857, the Government of India Act 1858 transferred rule to the Crown; the Governor-General (first, Lord Canning) additionally bore the title Viceroy as the sovereign's personal representative, and dealt directly with the princely states.

Powers

The Governor-General governed with an Executive Council, controlled foreign policy and the army, and could legislate and veto legislation. After 1919 reforms (dyarchy) and the Government of India Act 1935, elected provincial ministries gained ground, but the Governor-General retained sweeping reserved powers. Notable holders include Lord Dalhousie (annexations and railways), Lord Curzon (partition of Bengal, 1905), Lord Irwin (of the Gandhi–Irwin Pact), and the last Viceroy, Lord Mountbatten (1947).

After independence

Mountbatten stayed on as Governor-General of the Dominion of India until June 1948, when Chakravarti Rajagopalachari became the first and last Indian to hold the post. On 26 January 1950 the office was replaced by the President of India.
