The Lipka Tatars are a Turkic ethnic group of Tatar origin who settled in the Grand Duchy of Lithuania from the late 14th century onward. "Lipka" derives from Lipka, a Crimean Tatar rendering of "Lithuania". Granted land in exchange for military service, they formed hereditary cavalry units of the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth for centuries while preserving their Sunni Muslim faith — making them one of the oldest continuously resident Muslim communities in Europe.

History

Tatar settlement in Lithuania began under Grand Duke Vytautas, who invited Tatar auxiliaries — including refugees from the Golden Horde and Crimean Khanate — to settle around Vilnius, Trakai, and in present-day Belarus and Podlachia. Lipka regiments fought at the Battle of Grunwald in 1410 and in most later Commonwealth wars. In 1672 some Lipka units defected to the Ottoman side in the so-called Lipka Revolt, but most were reconciled and their privileges (and the right to build mosques) confirmed by John III Sobieski, whom Tatar cavalry later served at the relief of Vienna in 1683.

Culture and religion

Over generations the Lipkas lost their Turkic language, adopting Ruthenian, then Polish, Lithuanian, or Belarusian, while writing religious manuscripts (kitabs) in Belarusian and Polish using Arabic script. Villages such as Bohoniki and Kruszyniany in Poland, and Nemėžis and Keturiasdešimt Totorių ("Forty Tatars") in Lithuania, preserve wooden mosques and Muslim cemeteries (mizars). Community numbers declined after border changes in 1945 divided them among Poland, Lithuania, and Soviet Belarus, but a cultural revival has followed since 1991.

Legacy

The Lipkas are celebrated as a model of long-standing religious coexistence: Muslim soldiers and citizens of a largely Catholic state for over six centuries. Tatar units served in the Polish army into the Second World War, and the community's history features prominently in Lithuanian and Polish heritage tourism.
