Daniel O'Connell

Born: 6 August 1775, Carhen, County Kerry, Ireland
Died: 15 May 1847, Genoa, Italy
Known for: Catholic Emancipation; Repeal of the Union

Daniel O'Connell (6 August 1775 – 15 May 1847), often referred to as the Liberator or the Emancipator, was an Irish political leader in the first half of the 19th century. He campaigned for Catholic emancipation—the right for Catholics to sit in the Westminster Parliament, denied for over 100 years—and repeal of the Acts of Union which combined Great Britain and Ireland into a single United Kingdom.

Early life

O'Connell was born in Carhen, near Cahersiveen, County Kerry, to the O'Connells of Derrynane, a wealthy Roman Catholic family. Under the patronage of his wealthy bachelor uncle Maurice "Hunting Cap" O'Connell, he was sent to be educated at the Irish colleges at St Omer and Douai in France, and was admitted as a barrister to Lincoln's Inn in 1794, transferring to the King's Inns in Dublin in 1796. He built a successful legal career in the south of Ireland.

Catholic Association and Emancipation

In 1823 O'Connell founded the Catholic Association, which aimed to secure Catholic emancipation through constitutional means, funded by a "Catholic rent" of a penny a month that drew in mass membership. In 1828 he won a by-election in County Clare but was unable to take his seat because of the anti-Catholic oath required of MPs. The resulting political crisis forced the government of the Duke of Wellington to pass the Roman Catholic Relief Act 1829, granting emancipation.

Repeal campaign and monster meetings

O'Connell then campaigned for repeal of the Act of Union 1800 and restoration of an Irish parliament. His Repeal Association organised enormous peaceful "monster meetings", including one at the Hill of Tara in 1843 attended by hundreds of thousands. When the government banned a planned meeting at Clontarf in October 1843, O'Connell called it off to avoid bloodshed. He was convicted of conspiracy in 1844, though the conviction was overturned on appeal by the House of Lords.

Death and legacy

Physically and politically weakened, O'Connell died in Genoa in 1847 while on a pilgrimage to Rome. His heart was buried in Rome and his body in Glasnevin Cemetery, Dublin, beneath a round tower. Dublin's main thoroughfare, O'Connell Street, and the O'Connell Bridge are named after him.
