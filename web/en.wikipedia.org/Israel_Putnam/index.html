Israel Putnam (January 7, 1718 – May 29, 1790), popularly known as "Old Put", was an American military officer and folk hero who served as a major general in the Continental Army during the American Revolutionary War. He is especially associated with the Battle of Bunker Hill.

Early life and French and Indian War

Putnam was born in Salem Village, Massachusetts (now Danvers), and moved to Pomfret, Connecticut, as a young farmer. Local legend credited him with killing one of the last wolves in Connecticut by crawling into its den — the famous "wolf den" episode. In the French and Indian War he served with distinction in Rogers' Rangers and regular provincial regiments, surviving capture by Native American forces in 1758 (he was famously bound to a tree and nearly burned before a French officer intervened), a shipwreck off Cuba during the 1762 Havana expedition, and the Pontiac campaign.

Revolutionary War

Hearing of the battles of Lexington and Concord while ploughing, Putnam — legend says he left his plough in the field — rode to Cambridge and was made one of the Continental Army's first major generals. At Bunker Hill (17 June 1775) he directed troops at the rail fence and is traditionally credited with the order "Don't fire until you see the whites of their eyes", though the attribution is disputed. He later commanded at New York in 1776, where his performance during the disastrous campaign was criticised, and then held the important Hudson Highlands post. A stroke in 1779 ended his field service.

Legacy

Putnam died in Brooklyn, Connecticut, in 1790. Numerous towns and counties — Putnam County, New York, among them — bear his name, and he remains a standard figure of American Revolutionary folklore.
