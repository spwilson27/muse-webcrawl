The dromedary (Camelus dromedarius), also called the dromedary camel, Arabian camel, or one-humped camel, is a large even-toed ungulate native to the Arabian Peninsula and North Africa. Distinguished from the two-humped Bactrian camel by its single hump, it accounts for about 94% of the world's camel population and exists almost entirely as a domesticated animal; its wild ancestor is extinct in the wild, though feral herds — most famously in Australia — number in the hundreds of thousands.

Adaptations

The dromedary is superbly adapted to desert life. Its hump stores fat, whose metabolism yields energy and water; it can lose 25–30% of its body water without harm, let its body temperature swing from about 34 °C to over 40 °C to reduce sweating, and drink 100 litres in minutes when water is found. Oval red blood cells keep flowing in dehydrated blood, closable nostrils and long double eyelashes exclude sand, and broad padded two-toed feet spread its weight on soft ground.

Domestication and uses

Dromedaries were domesticated in Arabia probably by 3000–2000 BC and transformed desert trade, carrying frankincense, silk, and salt along caravan routes across Arabia and the Sahara. They remain vital as pack and riding animals, sources of milk and meat, and racing animals in the Arabian Peninsula; camel milk is a staple in arid regions of Africa and Asia. Camel cavalry and corps served armies into the 20th century, and dromedaries carried explorers across the Australian interior, where escaped animals founded the large feral population.
