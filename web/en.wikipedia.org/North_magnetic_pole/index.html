North magnetic pole

North magnetic pole (2020)
Coordinatesc. 80.7° N, 72.7° W (2020)

The north magnetic pole is a point on the surface of the Northern Hemisphere of Earth at which the planet's magnetic field points vertically downward. It is the point where a compass needle's north end is drawn vertically down, and it is technically a "south" pole of the Earth's magnetic dipole. The pole wanders over time as the field, generated by the motion of molten iron in the outer core, changes.

History

The pole was first reached on 1 June 1831 by an expedition led by James Clark Ross, who located it on the Boothia Peninsula in what is now Nunavut, Canada. Roald Amundsen found it at a slightly different location in 1904, demonstrating that it moves. Canadian government scientists resurveyed it repeatedly through the twentieth century.

Movement

During the twentieth century the pole drifted northwest across the Canadian Arctic Archipelago at roughly 10–15 km per year. In the 1990s its motion accelerated dramatically, reaching speeds of 50–60 km per year as it left Canadian territory and moved across the Arctic Ocean toward Siberia. The acceleration forced an out-of-cycle update of the World Magnetic Model in 2019, which is used for navigation by ships, aircraft, and smartphone compasses.

Geomagnetic pole

The north magnetic pole should be distinguished from the north geomagnetic pole, the point where the axis of the best-fitting dipole — tilted about 9–10 degrees from Earth's rotation axis — intersects the surface. The geomagnetic pole lies near Qaanaaq (Thule) in northwestern Greenland. Because the field is not a perfect dipole, the actual magnetic dip pole differs from the geomagnetic pole by hundreds of kilometres.

Polar expeditions and reversal

Over geological time the Earth's magnetic field has reversed polarity many times, the most recent full reversal (the Brunhes–Matuyama reversal) occurring about 780,000 years ago. During a reversal the magnetic poles migrate and the field weakens temporarily, though the process takes thousands of years.
