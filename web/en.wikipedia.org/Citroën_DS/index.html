The Citroën DS is a front mid-engined, front-wheel-drive executive car manufactured and marketed by Citroën from 1955 to 1975. Noted for its aerodynamic, futuristic body design and innovative technology, the DS is widely regarded as one of the most influential automobiles of the twentieth century.

Debut and design

Unveiled at the 1955 Paris Motor Show, the DS reportedly received 743 orders in its first fifteen minutes and some 12,000 on the first day. Its sculptural styling was by Flaminio Bertoni, and its engineering by André Lefèbvre. The name is a pun: "DS" is pronounced like déesse, French for "goddess". The simpler ID model (a pun on idée, "idea") followed in 1957.

Technology

The DS pioneered a high-pressure hydropneumatic system powering self-levelling suspension, brakes, steering, and a semi-automatic clutch. It was the first mass-produced car with front disc brakes, and later models introduced swivelling directional headlights linked to the steering. The suspension's ability to maintain ride height and run on three wheels became legendary.

Legacy

The DS placed third in the 1999 Car of the Century competition. It famously saved President Charles de Gaulle during the 1962 Petit-Clamart assassination attempt, escaping on shredded tyres thanks to its suspension. Citroën revived the name in 2009 for a premium sub-brand, later spun off as DS Automobiles.
