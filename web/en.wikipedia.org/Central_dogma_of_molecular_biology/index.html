Central dogma of molecular biology

Overview
Proposed byFrancis Crick
Year1957 (published 1958; restated 1970)
Core flowDNA → RNA → Protein
FieldMolecular biology

The central dogma of molecular biology is an explanation of the flow of genetic information within a biological system. It is often stated as "DNA makes RNA, and RNA makes protein", although this is not its original meaning. It was first stated by Francis Crick in 1957, then published in 1958:

The Central Dogma. This states that once "information" has passed into protein it cannot get out again. In more detail, the transfer of information from nucleic acid to nucleic acid, or from nucleic acid to protein may be possible, but transfer from protein to protein, or from protein to nucleic acid is impossible.

General transfers

The dogma classifies the transfer of sequence information between biopolymers. The general transfers that occur in all cells are DNA replication (DNA → DNA), transcription (DNA → RNA), and translation (RNA → protein). Special transfers, known in some viruses or laboratory conditions, include RNA replication (RNA → RNA) and reverse transcription (RNA → DNA), the latter discovered in retroviruses and famously once thought to contradict the dogma — though Crick's formulation always permitted it.

Criticism

The term "dogma" has been criticized as misleading, since Crick used it loosely to mean a central guiding idea rather than an unquestionable belief. Later phenomena such as prions and epigenetic inheritance have prompted debate about the dogma's scope, though neither transfers sequence information from protein back into nucleic acid.
