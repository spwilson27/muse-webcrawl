F-number

DefinitionN = f / D (focal length ÷ entrance pupil diameter)
Standard scalef/1, f/1.4, f/2, f/2.8, f/4, f/5.6, f/8, f/11, f/16, f/22, f/32
Notationf/N, e.g. f/2.8
Each full stop halves the light admitted through the lens.

The f-number of an optical system such as a camera lens is the ratio of the system's focal length to the diameter of the entrance pupil ("clear aperture"). It is a dimensionless number that measures the speed of the lens and determines the amount of light reaching the film or sensor and the depth of field of the image.

Computation and scale

The f-number N is given by N = f/D, where f is focal length and D is the diameter of the entrance pupil. Standard full-stop values increase by a factor of √2 (about 1.414): f/1, f/1.4, f/2, f/2.8, and so on, each step halving the light intensity. A lens's maximum aperture — its smallest f-number — is commonly called its speed: an f/1.4 lens is "faster" than an f/2.8 lens.

Effects

A smaller f-number (larger aperture) admits more light, permitting faster shutter speeds, but yields a shallower depth of field and generally more visible optical aberrations; stopping down increases depth of field and sharpness up to the point where diffraction softens the image. In cinema, T-stops (transmission stops) are used instead, calibrated to actual light transmission. See also aperture, depth of field, and exposure value.
