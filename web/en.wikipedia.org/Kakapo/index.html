The kakapo (Māori: kākāpō, "night parrot"; Strigops habroptilus), also known as the owl parrot, is a species of large, flightless, nocturnal, ground-dwelling parrot endemic to New Zealand. It is the world's heaviest parrot, has a distinctive musty-sweet odour, and may be one of the world's longest-living birds, with a reported lifespan of up to 100 years.

Biology

Kakapo are herbivorous, feeding on leaves, shoots, fruit and seeds, and breed only in years when certain trees (notably rimu) mast heavily. Males gather at lek arenas and produce low-frequency "booming" calls to attract females, which alone raise the chicks. Because they evolved without mammalian predators, their defence is to freeze and rely on camouflage — ineffective against introduced stoats, rats and cats.

Conservation

Hunted by Māori and devastated by introduced predators after European colonisation, the species fell to a few dozen known individuals by the 1990s. Under the Department of Conservation's Kakapo Recovery Programme, all surviving birds were moved to predator-free islands (Codfish Island/Whenua Hou, Anchor Island and Little Barrier Island), where every individual is named, fitted with a transmitter and intensively managed. The population has since recovered to over 200 individuals, including the famous hand-reared bird Sirocco, but the species remains critically endangered with very low genetic diversity.
