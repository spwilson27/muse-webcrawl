The Senate of Canada (French: Sénat du Canada) is the upper house of the Parliament of Canada. Its 105 members — senators — are appointed by the governor general on the advice of the prime minister and serve until age 75. Modelled on the British House of Lords, the Senate was created in 1867 by the Constitution Act as a chamber of "sober second thought" that would also give the regions of the federation a voice in federal lawmaking.

Composition

Seats are distributed by region rather than strictly by population: Ontario and Quebec hold 24 each, the Maritime provinces 24 in total, the western provinces 24 in total, Newfoundland and Labrador 6, and each of the three territories 1. Senators must be at least 30 years old, hold property qualifications dating from 1867, and reside in the province or territory for which they are appointed.

Powers

The Senate may introduce bills (except money bills, which must originate in the House of Commons) and must pass all legislation before it becomes law. In practice it usually amends or reviews rather than blocks Commons bills, though it retains a formal veto it has used rarely. Senate committees are noted for long-form studies of public policy issues, including euthanasia, banking, and social affairs.

Reform debate

The appointed Senate has been controversial since Confederation. Alberta-led "Triple-E" proposals (elected, equal, effective) gained prominence in the 1980s and 1990s. A 2014 Supreme Court reference held that fundamental reform requires substantial provincial consent, and abolition unanimity. Since 2016, an independent advisory board has recommended non-partisan appointees, and most senators now sit in non-partisan groups rather than party caucuses.
