Blubber

Blubber is a thick layer of vascularised adipose tissue under the skin of most marine mammals, including whales, seals and walruses, and also found in penguins and polar bears. It insulates the animal in cold water, stores energy, and contributes to buoyancy and a streamlined body shape.

Structure and function

Unlike ordinary fat, blubber is rich in collagen fibres and blood vessels, forming a tough, structured layer that can exceed 30 centimetres in thickness in large whales. Blood flow to the blubber can be adjusted to conserve or shed heat. Because fat is less dense than water, thick blubber also helps marine mammals float, although it can hinder diving in some species.

Human use

For centuries, blubber was rendered into whale oil for lamps, soap and margarine, driving the industrial whaling industry. In Arctic indigenous cuisines, raw or fermented blubber — such as the Inuit muktuk, eaten with the attached skin — remains an important source of energy and of vitamins, particularly vitamin C.
