Breast milk

Breast milk (also written breastmilk) is milk produced by the mammary glands of female humans for feeding their infants. It is the primary source of nutrition for newborns before they can digest other foods, and its composition changes over the course of lactation and even during a single feed.

Composition

Mature breast milk contains roughly 3–5% fat, 0.8–0.9% protein, 6.9–7.2% carbohydrate (mainly lactose), and 0.2% minerals, providing about 70 kcal per 100 ml. The first milk, colostrum, is rich in antibodies, especially secretory IgA. Milk also contains live cells, enzymes, hormones, growth factors, and human milk oligosaccharides that feed the infant gut microbiome.

Benefits

Health authorities such as the World Health Organization recommend exclusive breastfeeding for the first six months of life, with continued breastfeeding alongside complementary foods for two years or beyond. Breastfeeding is associated with reduced rates of gastrointestinal and respiratory infections in infants and lower risks of breast and ovarian cancer in mothers.

Storage and donation

Expressed milk can be refrigerated or frozen for later use. Milk banks collect screened, pasteurized donor milk, primarily for premature and ill infants.
