Vilhelm Friman Koren Bjerknes (14 March 1862 – 9 April 1951) was a Norwegian physicist and meteorologist who founded the modern practice of weather forecasting. He was the father of Jacob Bjerknes, who made key contributions to the understanding of cyclones and El Niño.

Early work

Bjerknes began his career in electrodynamics, assisting Heinrich Hertz in Bonn, before turning to hydrodynamics. Inspired by his father Carl Anton Bjerknes's work on hydrodynamic forces, he formulated the circulation theorem (1898), extending Kelvin's circulation theorem to rotating, baroclinic fluids, and applied it to ocean and atmosphere currents.

Weather forecasting

In a famous 1904 paper, Bjerknes argued that weather prediction should be treated as an initial-value problem of physics, to be solved by integrating the equations of motion and thermodynamics forward from observed initial conditions — the conceptual foundation of numerical weather prediction. During and after the First World War, at the Geophysical Institute in Bergen, he and his collaborators (the "Bergen School") developed the polar front theory of mid-latitude cyclones, introducing the concepts of warm and cold fronts that remain central to synoptic meteorology.

Legacy

Bjerknes held professorships at Stockholm, Leipzig and Oslo. Craters on the Moon and Mars are named after him, and the Bjerknes Medal of the Norwegian Meteorological Society honours his memory.
