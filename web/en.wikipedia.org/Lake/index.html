Lake

A lake is an often naturally occurring, relatively large and fixed body of water on or near the Earth's surface in a depression or basin. It is localized in a basin or an interconnected group of basins surrounded by dry land. Lakes lie completely on land and are separate from the ocean, although, like the much larger oceans, they form part of the Earth's water cycle by serving as large standing pools of storage water.

Most lakes are freshwater and account for almost all the world's surface freshwater, but some are salt lakes with salinities even higher than that of seawater. Lakes vary greatly in surface area and volume. The largest lake in the world by area is the Caspian Sea, and the largest freshwater lake by area is Lake Superior in North America. The deepest lake is Lake Baikal in Siberia, reaching a depth of about 1,642 metres (5,387 ft).

Formation

Lakes form through many processes. Tectonic lakes occupy basins created by movements of the Earth's crust, such as the rift lakes of East Africa, including Lake Tanganyika. Glacial lakes fill basins scoured or dammed by ice sheets; most of the world's lakes are of glacial origin, concentrated in Canada, Scandinavia, and Russia. Volcanic lakes form in craters and calderas, while oxbow lakes form in abandoned river meanders. Humans create artificial lakes, or reservoirs, by damming rivers.

Classification

Limnologists classify lakes by nutrient content (oligotrophic, mesotrophic, eutrophic), by thermal stratification (dimictic, monomictic, polymictic, meromictic), and by origin. Lakes that drain to the sea are exorheic; closed or endorheic lakes lose water only by evaporation, often becoming saline.

Ecology

Lake ecosystems are divided into the sunlit surface zone, the deep dark zone, and the shallow littoral zone near shore. Temperature stratification in summer separates a warm epilimnion from a cold hypolimnion. Lakes support distinctive communities of plankton, fish, waterfowl, and invertebrates, and are sensitive indicators of pollution, acid rain, and climate change.

Disappearance

Lakes are temporary features on geological timescales. They fill with sediment, are drained by erosion of their outlets, or dry up as climates change. The Aral Sea, once the world's fourth-largest lake, has shrunk dramatically since the 1960s because of irrigation diversions.
