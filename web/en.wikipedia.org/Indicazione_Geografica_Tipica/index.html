Indicazione Geografica Tipica

Indicazione Geografica Tipica (Italian: [indikatˈtsjoːne dʒeoˈɡraːfika ˈtiːpika]), abbreviated IGT, is the third of four classifications of wine recognized by the government of Italy. Created to recognize the unusually high quality of some wines that did not qualify for DOC or DOCG status, the IGT classification is sometimes written in French as Indication Géographique Protégée in the Aosta Valley, or German as Landwein in South Tyrol. Under European Union law the category corresponds to the Protected Geographical Indication (PGI).

History

The IGT category was introduced by Law 164 of 10 February 1992, part of the reform of Italian wine law associated with Agriculture Minister Giovanni Goria — hence the law is often called the "Goria law". Its most famous motivation was the success of the so-called Super Tuscans: wines such as Sassicaia and Tignanello, made in Tuscany with French grape varieties and methods outside the DOC rules, which could previously be sold only as lowly vino da tavola (table wine) despite commanding prices above most DOCG wines. Wines such as these were rapidly admitted to the new Toscana IGT category.

Requirements

IGT rules are considerably more flexible than those of DOC and DOCG: the production zones are generally larger (often an entire region), permitted grapes and yields are broader, and there is no requirement for government tasting panels. The label must state the geographical area, and the wines must be produced within it from at least 85% grapes of that area. There are over 110 IGT zones in Italy, and IGT wines account for a large share of total Italian wine production by volume.
