Nicholas Georgescu-Roegen (born Nicolae Georgescu; February 4, 1906 – October 30, 1994) was a Romanian-American mathematician, statistician and economist. He is best known today for his 1971 magnum opus The Entropy Law and the Economic Process, in which he argued that all natural resources are irreversibly degraded when put to use in economic activity, a position that anticipated the field of ecological economics.

Georgescu-Roegen studied mathematics at the University of Bucharest and earned his doctorate at the Sorbonne in Paris under Karl Pearson's influence at University College London. He later worked with Joseph Schumpeter at Harvard University during the 1930s before returning to Romania, and eventually emigrated to the United States in 1948, joining the faculty of Vanderbilt University in Nashville, Tennessee.

Entropy and economics

Georgescu-Roegen's central claim was that the neoclassical model of production ignored the second law of thermodynamics: the entropy law. Economic processes transform low-entropy energy and materials into high-entropy waste, so perpetual material growth on a finite planet is physically impossible. He proposed a 'bioeconomic' program emphasizing the survival of the human species and a steady, minimal use of resources.

His work influenced the degrowth movement and thinkers such as Herman Daly, who studied under him and developed steady-state economics. Although largely marginalized by mainstream economics during his lifetime, Georgescu-Roegen's ideas have been revisited as concerns about resource limits and climate change have grown.

Selected works

His books include Analytical Economics: Issues and Problems (1966), The Entropy Law and the Economic Process (1971), and Energy and Economic Myths (1976). He also contributed to consumer theory, production theory and the theory of choice early in his career.
