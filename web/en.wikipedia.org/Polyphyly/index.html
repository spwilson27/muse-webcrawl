Polyphyly

A polyphyletic group is a set of organisms, or other evolving elements, that have been grouped together based on characteristics that do not imply that they share a common ancestor that is not also the ancestor of many other taxa (which would make them paraphyletic, and if such a grouping were to include the common ancestor and all its descendants, it would be monophyletic). The term is often used in phylogenetics, and was introduced as a scientific concept to distinguish groups formed by convergent features.

Definition

A polyphyletic group is characterized by one or more homoplasies: phenotypes which have converged or reverted so as to appear to be the same, but which have not been inherited. For example, warm-bloodedness evolved separately in mammals and birds, so a grouping of "warm-blooded animals" would be polyphyletic. Other familiar polyphyletic groupings include "flying vertebrates" (birds, bats and pterosaurs) and, in older classifications, algae and invertebrates treated as unified groups.

Avoidance in taxonomy

Modern cladistic taxonomy generally regards polyphyletic groupings as errors to be corrected, since a natural classification should reflect evolutionary descent. Nevertheless, polyphyletic terms remain convenient in everyday and even scientific language: "tree" describes a growth form evolved independently in many plant lineages, and "worm" a body plan found in many unrelated animal phyla.
