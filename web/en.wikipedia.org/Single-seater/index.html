A single-seater (also called an open-wheel car in North America) is a racing car with only one seat and, typically, wheels outside the car's main body. Single-seaters are purpose-built for circuit racing and are among the fastest closed-course racing machines, thanks to their light weight, low centre of gravity and extensive aerodynamic downforce.

Design

The driver sits in a narrow carbon-fibre monocoque cockpit, usually reclined, with the engine mounted behind the driver. Large front and rear wings, a diffuser and (in modern formulae) drag-reduction systems generate downforce that can exceed the car's own weight. Open wheels create heavy aerodynamic drag but allow large brakes and wide slick tyres to be cooled and changed quickly.

Major series

  • Formula One — the FIA Formula One World Championship, the premier single-seater series, with constructors building bespoke cars.
  • IndyCar Series — North America's top series, using a spec Dallara chassis, racing on ovals, road courses and street circuits including the Indianapolis 500.
  • Formula 2 and Formula 3 — FIA feeder series to Formula One.
  • Formula E — all-electric single-seater championship, held mainly on street circuits.
  • Super Formula (Japan), Indy NXT, and numerous national Formula 4 and regional series.

History

Single-seat racers emerged in the 1920s and 1930s as Grand Prix cars shed their riding mechanics. Mid-engine layouts, pioneered by Cooper in the late 1950s, became universal, and wings appeared in the late 1960s. Safety has been transformed by carbon monocoques (1981), the HANS device and the halo cockpit protection device (2018).
