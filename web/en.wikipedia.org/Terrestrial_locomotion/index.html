Terrestrial locomotion

Terrestrial locomotion is the movement of animals on land, as distinct from aquatic, aerial, and arboreal locomotion. Most terrestrial animals move with limbs against a solid substrate, converting muscle work into walking, running, hopping, or crawling; others, such as snakes and legless worms, move without appendages. Terrestrial locomotion evolved as animals colonised the land in the Devonian period, and its modes are strongly shaped by gravity, friction, and the need to support the body's weight — burdens aquatic animals largely escape.

Modes

Legged locomotion is described by gaits: symmetrical gaits such as the walk, trot, and pace, and asymmetrical gaits such as the gallop and bound. Animals are classified by foot posture — plantigrade (whole foot, as in bears and humans), digitigrade (toes, as in dogs), or unguligrade (hooves). Bipedalism evolved independently in birds, kangaroos, some rodents, and hominins. Limbless modes include lateral undulation, rectilinear and concertina locomotion in snakes, peristalsis in earthworms, and looping in inchworms. Rolling is rare in nature (some spiders and salamanders) but universal in human vehicles, whose wheels have no true biological counterpart.

Energetics and mechanics

Walking conserves energy by exchanging kinetic and potential energy like an inverted pendulum, while running treats the leg as a spring, storing energy in tendons. The minimum cost of transport scales with body size, so large animals move more cheaply per gram, and gaits change at characteristic Froude numbers. Legged locomotion contrasts with aquatic locomotion, where buoyancy bears the weight and drag dominates, and with flight, where lift must be generated continuously.

Evolution

Tetrapods descended from lobe-finned fishes whose fins could support the body in shallow water; early forms such as Tiktaalik and Acanthostega bridged the transition. Arthropods had already colonised land with jointed legs. On land, evolution produced cursors, diggers, climbers, and gliders, and the return of some lineages to the sea and air.
