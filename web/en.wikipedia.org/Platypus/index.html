The platypus (Ornithorhynchus anatinus), sometimes called the duck-billed platypus, is a semiaquatic, egg-laying mammal endemic to eastern Australia and Tasmania. It is one of only five living species of monotremes and the sole living representative of its family and genus.

Description

The platypus has a streamlined body covered in dense waterproof fur, a distinctive bill resembling a duck's beak, webbed feet, and a broad, flat tail like a beaver's. Its bill is packed with electroreceptors: the platypus hunts underwater with its eyes shut, detecting the electric fields of prey such as insect larvae, shrimp, and worms. Unusually among mammals, males are venomous, delivering venom through spurs on the hind feet — one of the few venomous mammal species.

Reproduction and biology

Females lay one to three leathery eggs, which they incubate in a burrow for about ten days; the young are nursed on milk secreted onto patches of skin, as monotremes lack nipples. When first described by European naturalists, the platypus's combination of features was so implausible that some suspected a hoax of sewn-together animal parts.

Conservation

Platypus numbers have declined through habitat loss, river regulation, drought, and predation, and the species is listed as Near Threatened. It is a protected species and an iconic symbol of Australia, appearing on the Australian twenty-cent coin.
