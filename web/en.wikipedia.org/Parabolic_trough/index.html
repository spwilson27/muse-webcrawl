Parabolic trough

A parabolic trough is a type of solar thermal energy collector. It is constructed as a long parabolic mirror, usually coated in silver or polished aluminium, with a receiver tube running the length of the trough along its focal line. Sunlight striking the mirror is reflected onto the receiver tube, heating a heat-transfer fluid — commonly synthetic oil or molten salt — that flows through it.

Parabolic troughs are the most commercially mature concentrating solar power (CSP) technology. Long rows of troughs track the sun across the sky through the day, and the heated fluid is used to generate steam that drives a turbine and electric generator. The best-known installations are the Solar Energy Generating Systems (SEGS) plants in California's Mojave Desert, built in the 1980s and totalling about 354 megawatts, and newer plants in Spain and North Africa.

Design

The reflector is a sheet of glass or metal shaped into a parabola, supported by a steel structure that rotates on a single axis to follow the sun. The receiver consists of a steel absorber tube enclosed in an evacuated glass envelope to reduce heat loss, often with a selective coating that absorbs sunlight efficiently while emitting little infrared radiation. Concentration ratios are typically around 70–100, and fluid temperatures can reach about 390 °C with synthetic oil, or higher with molten salt.

Efficiency and storage

Trough plants convert roughly 15–20% of incident sunlight into electricity on an annual basis. Because the heat is collected as a hot fluid, thermal energy storage — for example in tanks of molten salt — can be added so the plant can generate electricity after sunset or during cloudy periods.
