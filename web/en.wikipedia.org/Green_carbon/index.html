Green carbon

Green carbon is held in the biomass of terrestrial plants and soils.

Green carbon is the carbon stored in terrestrial ecosystems — in the living biomass of plants (especially forests), in dead plant material, and in soils. The term is used in colour-coded classifications of carbon stocks, alongside blue carbon (stored in coastal and marine ecosystems such as mangroves, salt marshes and seagrasses), black carbon (soot from incomplete combustion), and brown carbon (aerosols from burning organic matter).

Role in the carbon cycle

Through photosynthesis, plants draw carbon dioxide from the atmosphere and fix it into organic matter. Roughly half of the carbon in a forest is in the vegetation and the rest largely in the soil, where it may persist for centuries to millennia. Global vegetation stores on the order of 450–650 billion tonnes of carbon, and soils hold considerably more — roughly 1,500 billion tonnes in the top metre alone.

Climate significance

Protecting and restoring green-carbon stocks is a central strategy in climate-change mitigation. Deforestation and land degradation release stored carbon — land-use change accounts for roughly a tenth of anthropogenic greenhouse-gas emissions — while reforestation and improved soil management can sequester additional carbon. Initiatives such as REDD+ aim to reward developing countries for keeping forests standing. Critics note that terrestrial sequestration is reversible (through fire, drought or clearing) and must complement, not substitute for, reductions in fossil-fuel emissions.
