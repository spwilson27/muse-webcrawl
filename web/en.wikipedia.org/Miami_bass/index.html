Miami bass

OriginsMid-1980s, Miami, Florida
Stylistic rootsElectro-funk, hip hop

Miami bass (also called booty bass or booty music) is a subgenre of hip hop and electronic dance music that emerged in South Florida in the mid-1980s. It is characterized by deep, heavy bass, most famously the sustained kick drum of the Roland TR-808, fast tempos, chant-like call-and-response vocals, and frequently sexually explicit lyrics oriented toward partying and dancing.

History

The style developed from electro-funk records played in Miami clubs and car audio culture. Amos Larkins II's production of MC A.D.E.'s "Bass Rock Express" (1985) is often cited as the first Miami bass record. Luther Campbell's 2 Live Crew brought the genre national attention with 2 Live Crew Is What We Are (1986) and As Nasty As They Wanna Be (1989), the latter becoming the center of a landmark obscenity case that reached the federal appeals courts and affirmed the album's constitutional protection. DJ Magic Mike became renowned for virtuosic scratching over thunderous bass tracks aimed at testing car sound systems.

Characteristics and legacy

Miami bass tracks typically run at 120–135 BPM, layer 808 kick drums and hi-hats under simple synth riffs, and emphasize "bass check" passages designed for subwoofer-heavy car stereos; a competitive car-audio culture grew up around the records. The genre fed into Atlanta bass, crunk, and later bounce and EDM bass music, and its cleaned-up party hits — Tag Team's "Whoomp! (There It Is)" and Quad City DJ's "C'mon N' Ride It (The Train)" — crossed over to mainstream pop in the 1990s.
