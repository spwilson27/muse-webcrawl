Penn effect


Price levels rise systematically with income

The Penn effect is the economic finding that real exchange rates, measured as the ratio of price levels between countries converted at market exchange rates, tend systematically to be higher in wealthier countries: the same basket of goods and services costs more in a rich country than in a poor one. It is named after the University of Pennsylvania, whose Penn World Table has documented the pattern in systematic international comparisons since the 1950s.

Explanation

The standard explanation is the Balassa–Samuelson effect: rich countries are much more productive in traded goods (manufacturing, exportable goods), which bids up their wages; because those wages must also be paid in non-traded sectors (haircuts, restaurants, housing services) where productivity differences are small, non-traded goods become relatively expensive, raising the overall price level. Simply put: a haircut takes the same time everywhere, but in a rich country the barber must be paid a rich-country wage.

Implications

The effect means that converting poor countries' GDP at market exchange rates understates their real output, which is why international comparisons use purchasing power parity (PPP) adjustments. Over long periods, sustained catch-up growth is typically associated with real exchange-rate appreciation consistent with the Penn effect. The pattern is one of the most robust empirical regularities in international economics, though its strength varies with trade costs and the share of non-traded goods.
