1925 serum run to Nome

The 1925 serum run to Nome, also known as the Great Race of Mercy, was a transport of diphtheria antitoxin by dog sled relay across the U.S. territory of Alaska in the winter of 1925. Twenty mushers and about 150 sled dogs relayed the serum 674 miles (1,085 km) from Nenana to the icebound port of Nome in five and a half days, saving the town from an incipient epidemic.

The emergency

In January 1925, Dr. Curtis Welch, the only physician in Nome, diagnosed diphtheria among the town's children — including Alaska Native children at extreme risk from a disease to which they had little immunity. Nome's supply of antitoxin was expired, and the harbour was frozen shut. With aircraft unreliable in extreme cold, Governor Scott Bone chose a dog-sled relay along the Iditarod mail trail, combined with a rail shipment to Nenana.

The relay

The serum left Nenana on the night of January 27. Mushers including "Wild Bill" Shannon, Leonhard Seppala — whose lead dog was Togo and who covered the longest, most dangerous leg across the Norton Sound ice — Charlie Evans, and Gunnar Kaasen, whose lead dog Balto ran the final leg into Nome in the early hours of February 2, completed the run in a record 127.5 hours in temperatures reaching −60 °F (−51 °C) with blizzard winds.

Legacy

The run made national headlines and heroes of Kaasen and Balto; a statue of Balto was erected in New York's Central Park within the year. Modern scholarship has emphasised that Seppala and Togo covered the longest and most hazardous portion. The modern Iditarod Trail Sled Dog Race, founded in 1973, commemorates the serum run.
