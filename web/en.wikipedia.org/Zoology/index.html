Zoology

Zoology (from Ancient Greek ζῷον, zōion, "animal", and λόγος, logos, "knowledge, study") is the branch of biology that studies the animal kingdom, including the structure, embryology, evolution, classification, habits and distribution of all animals, both living and extinct, and how they interact with their ecosystems. The term is derived from the Ancient Greek words for "animal" and "study".

Although humans have always observed animals, zoology as a coherent discipline began with Aristotle, whose History of Animals (4th century BCE) described some 600 species and their anatomy and behaviour. Medieval bestiaries mixed observation with allegory, but the scientific revolution, the microscope, and voyages of exploration transformed the field. Carl Linnaeus established binomial nomenclature in the 18th century; Georges Cuvier founded comparative anatomy and palaeontology; and Charles Darwin's theory of evolution by natural selection (1859) supplied zoology with its unifying framework.

Subdisciplines

Zoology comprises many subfields, often organised by the group studied — mammalogy, ornithology, herpetology, ichthyology, entomology, malacology — or by approach: anatomy, physiology, ethology (behaviour), ecology, zoogeography, taxonomy and systematics, and evolutionary biology. Modern zoologists work in universities, museums, zoos, conservation agencies and biotechnology, and apply molecular and genomic methods alongside field observation.
