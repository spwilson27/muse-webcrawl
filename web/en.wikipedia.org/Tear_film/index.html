Tear film

The tear film is the thin layer of fluid that covers the exposed surface of the cornea and conjunctiva of the eye. It keeps the ocular surface moist, smooths the corneal surface so that light refracts evenly, and helps protect the eye against infection and debris.

Structure

The tear film is traditionally described as having three layers. The outer lipid layer, secreted mainly by the meibomian glands in the eyelids, is rich in meibum and slows evaporation of the watery layer beneath. The middle aqueous layer, produced by the lacrimal glands, is the thickest component and carries oxygen, electrolytes, and antimicrobial proteins such as lysozyme and lactoferrin. The inner mucin layer, produced by conjunctival goblet cells, makes the hydrophobic corneal epithelium wettable and anchors the tear film to the ocular surface. More recent models describe the aqueous and mucin components as a single mucoaqueous gradient.

Function

The tear film lubricates the eye during blinking, flushes away foreign material, and provides the first refractive surface of the eye — because light passes from air into the tear film and cornea, its smoothness is essential for clear vision. It also supplies the avascular cornea with oxygen from the atmosphere.

Clinical significance

Instability or deficiency of the tear film causes dry eye disease, in which the film breaks up too quickly between blinks, producing discomfort, fluctuating vision, and damage to the ocular surface. Tear film break-up time is a standard clinical test of its stability.
