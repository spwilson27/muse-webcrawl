Aghlabids

The Great Mosque of Kairouan, extensively rebuilt under the Aghlabids.

The Aghlabids (Arabic: الأغالبة) were an Arab dynasty of emirs from the Banu Tamim tribe who ruled Ifriqiya and parts of Sicily and southern Italy from 800 to 909, nominally on behalf of the Abbasid Caliphate. The dynasty was founded by Ibrahim ibn al-Aghlab, who was appointed hereditary governor of Ifriqiya by the Abbasid caliph Harun al-Rashid in exchange for an annual tribute, giving the region effective autonomy while it remained formally part of the caliphate.

Rule in Ifriqiya

The Aghlabid emirate covered roughly modern Tunisia, eastern Algeria and western Libya, with its capital first near Kairouan and later at Raqqada and al-Abbasiyya. The emirs subdued restive Berber tribes, maintained a professional army including Black African slave soldiers, and developed irrigation, ports and monumental building. The Great Mosque of Kairouan took much of its present form under Aghlabid patronage, and the dynasty built ribats (fortified monasteries) along the coast, such as those at Monastir and Sousse.

Conquest of Sicily

In 827, under Ziyadat Allah I, the Aghlabids began the Muslim conquest of Byzantine Sicily, invited in by the rebel Byzantine commander Euphemius. Palermo fell in 831 and became a flourishing Muslim city, but the conquest was slow: Syracuse fell only in 878, and the last Byzantine stronghold, Taormina, in 902. Aghlabid forces also raided and briefly held territory in southern Italy, including Bari and Taranto.

Decline and fall

The last emirs faced growing discontent over taxation, military factionalism and Ismaili Shi'a missionary activity among the Kutama Berbers of the west. The da'i Abu Abdallah al-Shi'i raised the Kutama in revolt in support of the Fatimid imam, and Aghlabid power collapsed in 909 when the last emir, Ziyadat Allah III, fled to Egypt. The Fatimids succeeded them in Ifriqiya and went on to conquer Egypt.
