The Shang dynasty (Chinese: 商朝; pinyin: Shāng cháo), also known as the Yin dynasty (殷代; Yīn dài), was a Chinese royal dynasty that ruled in the Yellow River valley during the second millennium BC, traditionally succeeding the Xia dynasty and followed by the Western Zhou dynasty. The classic account of the Shang comes from texts such as the Book of Documents, the Bamboo Annals, and the Records of the Grand Historian (Shiji) of Sima Qian.

The Shang is the earliest dynasty of traditional Chinese history supported by firm archaeological evidence. Excavation at the Ruins of Yin (near modern Anyang in Henan province), which has been identified as the last Shang capital, uncovered eleven major royal tombs, palace and temple foundations, and the earliest known complete Chinese writing system — the oracle bone script — inscribed on animal bones used for divination. Tens of thousands of bronze, jade, stone, bone, and ceramic artefacts have been recovered from the site.

Founding and early history

According to tradition, the Shang were led by Tang, a virtuous ruler who overthrew Jie, the last and tyrannical king of the Xia, at the Battle of Mingtiao around 1600 BC. The Shang kings moved their capital several times; the dynasty's early centres included Bo, Ao, Xiang, Geng, and Bi. Around 1300 BC, the king Pan Geng moved the capital to Yin, near modern Anyang, beginning the prosperous late-Shang or Yin period, from which the great bulk of archaeological remains comes.

Society and culture

Shang society was organized around the king, who was both political ruler and chief religious officiant. The royal court conducted frequent campaigns against neighbouring peoples, raised corvée labour for monumental construction, and presided over an elaborate ritual calendar of sacrifices to royal ancestors and to the high god Di (or Shangdi). Aristocratic lineages were buried with bronze vessels, weapons, and chariots; human sacrifice accompanied major royal burials and dedications.

The Shang practised scapulimancy — divination by heating turtle plastrons and ox scapulae until they cracked, then interpreting the cracks — and recorded questions to the ancestors on the bones themselves. Decipherment of these oracle bone inscriptions has confirmed the king list in the Shiji to a remarkable degree and provides direct evidence of Shang warfare, agriculture, weather, illness, and ritual.

Bronze working

Shang craftsmen cast bronze vessels of extraordinary sophistication using piece-mould casting, producing ritual ding tripods, jue wine vessels, and bells decorated with taotie (animal-face) motifs. The largest surviving piece, the Houmuwu ding, weighs 832.84 kg and is the heaviest Chinese bronze vessel ever found. Bronze metallurgy underpinned Shang military power: chariots fitted with bronze weapons, introduced around 1200 BC, transformed warfare in the region.

Kings

Traditional lists give some thirty Shang kings over about seventeen generations. Notable rulers include Tang the founder; Wu Ding (c. 1250–1192 BC), whose long reign marked the dynasty's height and whose consort Fu Hao — a general and priestess — was buried in an intact tomb excavated in 1976; and the last king, Di Xin (King Zhou), remembered in later tradition as a decadent tyrant.

Fall

The Shang were overthrown by the Zhou, a subordinate people from the Wei River valley to the west, at the Battle of Muye in 1046 BC (dates vary by scholarly reconstruction between c. 1050 and 1027 BC). King Wu of Zhou defeated Di Xin's army — tradition holds that Shang troops defected en masse — and Di Xin immolated himself in his palace. The Zhou justified the conquest with the doctrine of the Mandate of Heaven: the Shang had forfeited Heaven's mandate through misrule.

Archaeology

The oracle bones were identified as ancient writing in 1899 by the scholar Wang Yirong. Excavations at Yinxu by the Academia Sinica began in 1928. Earlier Bronze Age cultures in the region, such as the Erligang culture centred at Zhengzhou (identified with an early Shang capital) and the Erlitou culture (often associated with the Xia), provide context for the dynasty's emergence. The discovery of the contemporary, highly developed Sanxingdui culture in Sichuan has complicated the older picture of a single centre of civilization in the Yellow River valley.
