The Athanasian Creed, also called the Quicumque vult after its first words in Latin ("Whosoever will [be saved]"), is a Christian statement of belief focused on Trinitarian doctrine and Christology. Traditionally ascribed to Athanasius of Alexandria (c. 296–373), it is now dated by scholars to the late 5th or early 6th century in southern Gaul; the earliest manuscripts are later still.

Content

Unlike the Apostles' and Nicene Creeds, the Athanasian Creed is a detailed doctrinal exposition rather than a baptismal confession. Its first part carefully defines the Trinity — one God in three persons, "neither confounding the Persons nor dividing the Substance" — and its second part confesses Christ as "perfect God and perfect Man". It opens and closes with so-called "damnatory clauses", declaring that those who do not hold this faith "shall perish everlastingly", which have made its liturgical use controversial.

Use

The creed was widely used in the Western Church's office of Prime and is retained in some Anglican and Lutheran liturgies, appointed on certain feasts such as Trinity Sunday in the Book of Common Prayer. It is one of the three ecumenical creeds recognised in the Lutheran Book of Concord, though it was never adopted by the Eastern Orthodox Church.
