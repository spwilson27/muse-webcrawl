Marian Rejewski

Rejewski, whose mathematical analysis first broke the Enigma cipher.

Marian Adam Rejewski (16 August 1905 – 13 February 1980) was a Polish mathematician and cryptologist who in late 1932 reconstructed the German military Enigma cipher machine, sight unseen, purely by mathematical analysis combined with material supplied by French military intelligence. He and his Cipher Bureau colleagues Henryk Zygalski and Jerzy Różycki went on to devise practical methods and electromechanical devices — including the cryptologic bomb — for reading Enigma traffic, work that Poland shared with France and Britain in July 1939 and that laid the foundation for Allied Ultra intelligence.

Breaking Enigma

Rejewski studied mathematics at Poznań, where in 1929 he was recruited into a secret cryptology course run by the Polish Cipher Bureau. Working in Warsaw from 1932, he was assigned the new German machine cipher. Using permutation theory — and a set of equations exploiting the way Enigma operators repeated the message key at the start of each message — together with intelligence documents purchased by France from the German cipher clerk Hans-Thilo Schmidt, Rejewski deduced the internal wiring of the military Enigma's rotors by the end of 1932. It has been called one of the greatest feats in the history of cryptanalysis.

With the wiring known, the Bureau built Enigma doubles and read German traffic for most of the 1930s. To cope with German procedural changes, Rejewski designed the cyclometer and, in 1938, the bomba kryptologiczna (cryptologic bomb), an electromechanical device that tested rotor settings; Zygalski devised his perforated sheets for the same purpose. On 25 July 1939, at Pyry near Warsaw, the Poles handed their French and British allies replicas and full details of their methods, weeks before the invasion of Poland.

Later life and recognition

Rejewski and his colleagues escaped to France in 1939 and continued work at PC Bruno and later Cadix in Vichy France; after the German occupation of the south in 1942 he made his way via Spain and Portugal to Britain, where he worked on German hand ciphers but, to his lasting regret, was kept ignorant of Bletchley Park's Enigma work. He returned to Poland in 1946, worked as an accountant, and remained silent about his achievement for decades; his role became widely known only in the 1970s. He died in Warsaw in 1980. Posthumous honours include Poland's Grand Cross of the Order of Polonia Restituta and, in 2014, the IEEE Milestone award recognising the Polish team's pioneering Enigma work.
