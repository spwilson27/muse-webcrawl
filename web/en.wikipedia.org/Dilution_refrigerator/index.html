Dilution refrigerator

A dilution refrigerator is a cryogenic device that provides continuous cooling to temperatures as low as about 2 millikelvin (mK), with no moving parts in the low-temperature region. It is the standard means of reaching millikelvin temperatures in physics laboratories and is essential infrastructure for experiments in quantum computing, condensed-matter physics, and low-temperature detectors.

The gold-plated stages of a dilution refrigerator insert.

Principle

The device exploits a mixture of two helium isotopes, helium-3 and helium-4. Below about 0.87 K the mixture separates into a concentrated helium-3 phase and a dilute phase containing about 6.6% helium-3. Cooling occurs when helium-3 atoms cross the phase boundary from the concentrated to the dilute phase in the "mixing chamber"—analogous to evaporation, but continuing to the lowest temperatures because the dilute phase retains a finite helium-3 concentration at absolute zero.

Operation

Helium-3 is circulated in a closed loop: it is pumped away from the still (held near 0.7–1 K), recondensed, cooled through heat exchangers, and returned to the mixing chamber. Modern "dry" dilution refrigerators use a pulse-tube cryocooler for precooling to about 4 K instead of a liquid-helium bath, simplifying operation; such systems are used to cool superconducting quantum processors.

History

The method was proposed by Heinz London in the early 1950s and first realised in the mid-1960s; it soon displaced other techniques as the workhorse for reaching below 0.3 K continuously.
