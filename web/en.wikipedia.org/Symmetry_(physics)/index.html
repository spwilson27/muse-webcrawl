Symmetry (physics)

In physics, a symmetry of a physical system is a feature of the system — mathematical or physical, such as the value of an observable — that is preserved under some transformation. Symmetries may be discrete (such as reflection) or continuous (such as rotation), global (the same everywhere) or local (varying from point to point, as in gauge theories).

Symmetry as a guiding principle

Symmetries are among the most powerful tools of theoretical physics because, by Noether's theorem (1915), every continuous symmetry of a system's action corresponds to a conservation law: time-translation symmetry gives conservation of energy, spatial translation gives conservation of momentum, and rotation gives conservation of angular momentum. Einstein's special and general relativity are founded on Lorentz and general coordinate invariance, and the Standard Model of particle physics is built from the gauge symmetry group SU(3) × SU(2) × U(1).

Discrete symmetries and their violation

Three discrete symmetries are fundamental in particle physics: parity (P, mirror reflection), charge conjugation (C, swapping particles for antiparticles), and time reversal (T). The discovery in 1956–57, following the proposal of Lee and Yang, that the weak interaction violates parity — confirmed by Chien-Shiung Wu's cobalt-60 experiment — overturned the assumption that these symmetries were exact, and in 1964 CP violation was observed in kaon decays. The CPT theorem guarantees that the combined symmetry CPT holds in any local relativistic quantum field theory; the tiny CP violation remaining in the Standard Model cannot explain the universe's matter–antimatter asymmetry.

Symmetry breaking

Many physical phenomena arise when a symmetry of the underlying laws is not shared by a particular state: a ferromagnet picks a magnetisation direction, breaking rotational symmetry, and in the Higgs mechanism a spontaneously broken gauge symmetry gives mass to the W and Z bosons while producing the Higgs boson, discovered at CERN in 2012.
