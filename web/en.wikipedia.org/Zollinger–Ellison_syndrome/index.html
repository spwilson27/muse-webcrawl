Zollinger–Ellison syndrome

Zollinger–Ellison syndrome (ZES) is a rare disease in which one or more tumours in the pancreas or the upper part of the small intestine (duodenum), called gastrinomas, secrete large amounts of the hormone gastrin. The excess gastrin causes the stomach to produce far too much acid, leading to severe, recurrent and treatment-resistant peptic ulcers, gastro-oesophageal reflux and diarrhoea. The syndrome was described in 1955 by surgeons Robert Zollinger and Edwin Ellison at Ohio State University.

Cause and association

Gastrinomas are neuroendocrine tumours. About 75–80 percent of cases are sporadic, while 20–25 percent occur as part of multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 (MEN 1), an inherited syndrome involving tumours of the parathyroid, pituitary and pancreas. Roughly half to two-thirds of gastrinomas are malignant, though they often grow slowly and metastasise first to nearby lymph nodes and the liver.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on a markedly elevated fasting serum gastrin together with low gastric pH, confirmed with a secretin stimulation test and imaging to localise the tumour. High-dose proton pump inhibitors control acid secretion in nearly all patients, transforming a once frequently fatal condition into a manageable one; surgery may cure localised sporadic cases, while advanced disease is managed with therapies for neuroendocrine tumours.
