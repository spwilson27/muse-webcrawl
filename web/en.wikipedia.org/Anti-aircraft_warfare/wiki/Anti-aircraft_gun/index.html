Anti-aircraft gun

A German 8.8 cm Flak gun of the Second World War

An anti-aircraft gun is a cannon or autocannon designed to fire at aircraft from the ground or from ships. Anti-aircraft artillery (AAA, "triple-A", or "flak") developed rapidly during the First World War in response to military aeroplanes and airships, and reached its zenith in the Second World War, when guns ranging from machine guns to the German 12.8 cm Flak 40 defended cities, armies and fleets.

History

The first dedicated anti-aircraft weapons appeared before 1914, including Krupp and Vickers designs on high-angle mountings. During the First World War, weapons such as the British QF 3-inch 20 cwt engaged aircraft with shells fitted with time fuzes. The interwar years produced classic designs: the Swedish Bofors 40 mm, the German 8.8 cm "eighty-eight" — which also became a feared anti-tank gun — and the Swiss Oerlikon 20 mm cannon.

Second World War fire control combined optical predictors, radar direction and, late in the war, the proximity fuze, greatly increasing effectiveness. After 1945, guided surface-to-air missiles displaced heavy guns for high-altitude defence, but radar-directed autocannon remain in service for low-altitude and point defence, including the Soviet ZSU-23-4 Shilka and modern systems defending against drones and cruise missiles.

Characteristics

Anti-aircraft guns require high muzzle velocity, high rate of fire, steep elevation and rapid traverse. Ammunition is typically high-explosive with time, impact or proximity fuzes intended to burst close to the target, throwing fragments through the airframe. Guns are classed as light (up to about 40 mm), medium and heavy (75 mm and above).
