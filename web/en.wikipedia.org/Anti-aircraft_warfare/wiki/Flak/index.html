Flak

Flak bursts around a formation of American bombers over Germany, 1944

Flak originally referred to German anti-aircraft guns and their fire during the world wars, from the German word Flugabwehrkanone ("aircraft defence cannon"). In English usage it came to denote anti-aircraft fire generally, and especially the shell bursts and shrapnel encountered by aircrews. The word later acquired a figurative meaning: intense criticism or opposition, as in "to take flak" for an unpopular decision.

History

German Flak artillery grew into a vast arm of the Luftwaffe by the Second World War, employing hundreds of thousands of personnel and thousands of guns — including the famous 8.8 cm Flak — around cities, industrial targets and along bomber routes. Allied bomber crews regarded flak over targets such as Berlin, the Ruhr and Ploiești as a deadly hazard; barrage fire filled boxes of sky with shell bursts that aircraft had to fly through. Defence measures included "Window" (chaff) to confuse radar-directed guns, armour plate and flak jackets and helmets for crews — the origin of the term "flak jacket", later applied to all fragment-protective body armour.

Metaphorical use

From airmen's slang, "flak" entered general English after the war meaning hostile criticism or verbal attack. The related spelling "flack" for a press agent is a separate, coincidental usage.
