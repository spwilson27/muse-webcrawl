Close-in weapon system

A Phalanx CIWS aboard a United States Navy vessel

A close-in weapon system (CIWS) is a point-defence weapon system for detecting and destroying short-range incoming threats that have penetrated the outer defences of a warship or installation — principally anti-ship missiles and aircraft. The term is often applied to rapid-fire gun systems, but missile-based point-defence systems perform the same role. A CIWS is characterised by fully automatic operation: its own search and tracking radars detect a threat, evaluate it, and engage it with minimal human intervention because reaction times may be only seconds.

Gun systems

The best-known gun CIWS is the American Phalanx, built around a 20 mm M61 Vulcan Gatling cannon firing up to 4,500 rounds per minute, housed in a distinctive white radome. Other gun systems include the Dutch Goalkeeper (30 mm), the Russian AK-630 and Kashtan, the German Millennium gun, and China's Type 730/1130. Effective range is short, typically under 2–3 km, making the CIWS genuinely a last line of defence.

Missile systems

Missile-based CIWS such as the American RIM-116 Rolling Airframe Missile (RAM), the SeaRAM variant that uses the Phalanx mount, and various short-range naval SAMs offer greater range and a higher probability of destroying large or supersonic sea-skimming missiles whose debris from a gun kill might still strike the ship.

Land-based use

Land-based versions, such as the C-RAM (Counter Rocket, Artillery, and Mortar) adaptation of Phalanx deployed to Iraq and Afghanistan, intercept incoming rockets and mortar shells, their tracer streams becoming a familiar sight over bases.
