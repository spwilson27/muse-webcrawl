Man-portable air-defense system

A soldier aiming an FIM-92 Stinger MANPADS

Man-portable air-defense systems (MANPADS or MPADS) are shoulder-launched surface-to-air missiles small enough to be carried and fired by a single soldier. They are typically guided weapons employing infrared homing seekers that lock onto an aircraft's heat signature. MANPADS give infantry a defence against low-flying aircraft and helicopters and have been a significant factor in conflicts since the 1960s.

History

The first operational systems entered service in the late 1960s: the Soviet 9K32 Strela-2 (SA-7 Grail) and the American FIM-43 Redeye. Successive generations improved seeker sensitivity and resistance to countermeasures such as flares. The American FIM-92 Stinger, supplied to Afghan mujahideen in the 1980s, became famous for its effect against Soviet helicopters and aircraft. Other widely used systems include the Soviet/Russian Igla series, the British Starstreak (command-guided, unusually fast), the French Mistral and the Swedish RBS 70.

Characteristics

MANPADS typically weigh 13–18 kg complete, engage targets at ranges of about 3–6 km and altitudes up to roughly 3–4 km. Most are "fire-and-forget" infrared weapons; a few use laser beam-riding or command guidance. Their small size makes them concealable, and the proliferation of MANPADS to non-state groups is an international security concern: they have been used in attacks and attempted attacks on civil airliners, prompting export-control regimes such as the Wassenaar Arrangement and the fitting of missile-approach warning and countermeasure systems to some aircraft.
