Anti-aircraft warfare


Anti-aircraft guns in action during the Second World War.

Anti-aircraft warfare is the counter to aerial warfare, comprising all measures designed to nullify or reduce the effectiveness of hostile air action. It includes surface-based, subsurface-based (submarine-launched) and air-based weapon systems, associated sensor systems, command and control arrangements, and passive measures such as barrage balloons. Since the Second World War it has increasingly been dominated by guided surface-to-air missiles (SAMs), supplemented by anti-aircraft guns for short-range defence. In British and Commonwealth usage, anti-aircraft artillery was often called "ack-ack", from the phonetic alphabet of the era, while the German term Flak — from Fliegerabwehrkanone, "aircraft defence cannon" — entered English as a general word for anti-aircraft fire.

History

Armies first improvised weapons against military aircraft and balloons during the First World War, mounting machine guns and artillery pieces on high-angle mounts. Between the wars, purpose-built anti-aircraft guns such as the German 8.8 cm Flak and the Swedish-designed Bofors 40 mm became standard. The Second World War saw enormous anti-aircraft establishments: Britain's Anti-Aircraft Command defended cities during the Blitz, while Germany built flak towers and massed guns around industrial targets. The proximity fuze, which detonates a shell near its target, greatly increased effectiveness from 1943.

After 1945 the speed of jet aircraft outpaced gun systems, and development shifted to missiles. The Soviet S-75 Dvina (NATO: SA-2 Guideline) famously shot down a U-2 spy plane over the USSR in 1960. The Vietnam War and the 1973 Yom Kippur War demonstrated the lethality of integrated missile defences, driving the development of anti-radiation missiles, electronic countermeasures and dedicated suppression-of-enemy-air-defences (SEAD) missions. Shoulder-fired MANPADS such as the American Stinger proliferated from the 1980s.

Systems and methods

Modern air defence is layered. Long-range SAM systems (for example Patriot, S-400 and Aster) engage aircraft and ballistic missiles at distances over 100 km; medium- and short-range systems defend manoeuvre forces and point targets; and guns — often radar-guided, like the German Gepard or modern 35 mm revolver cannon — provide a final layer, increasingly valued against drones and loitering munitions. Sensors include early-warning and fire-control radars, passive infrared search systems and airborne early warning aircraft, all fused through command-and-control networks into an integrated air defence system (IADS).

Naval anti-aircraft warfare similarly layers long-range area-defence missiles, point-defence missiles and close-in weapon systems such as the Phalanx CIWS, a radar-guided Gatling gun that automatically engages incoming missiles and aircraft.

Recent developments

The wars of the 2020s, above all Russia's invasion of Ukraine, highlighted both the power of modern ground-based air defence — which denied either side air superiority — and the challenge posed by massed cheap drones and cruise missiles, prompting renewed investment in gun systems, electronic warfare, interceptor drones and directed-energy weapons such as high-energy lasers.
