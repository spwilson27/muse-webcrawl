Sociolinguistics
FieldLinguistics, sociology, anthropology
Key researchersWilliam Labov, Dell Hymes, John Gumperz, Peter Trudgill, Penelope Eckert
Core topicsLanguage variation and change, dialect, register, code-switching, language and identity

Sociolinguistics is the descriptive study of the effect of any or all aspects of society, including cultural norms, expectations, and context, on language, and of the effect of language on society. It differs from the sociology of language, which focuses on society's use of language, in that sociolinguistics focuses on language's variation and use as shaped by social factors such as class, gender, age, ethnicity, and social networks.

Variationist sociolinguistics

William Labov's pioneering studies—of department stores in New York City (1966), the Lower East Side, and Martha's Vineyard—founded quantitative variationist sociolinguistics. Labov showed that linguistic variables, such as the pronunciation of post-vocalic /r/, correlate systematically with social class and the formality of the speech situation (style-shifting), and that language change can be observed in progress through the "apparent time" distribution of variants across generations. His work on African American Vernacular English also established that non-standard dialects are fully systematic.

Key concepts

Central concepts include the speech community, register and style, code-switching between languages or varieties, diglossia, covert and overt prestige, and Basil Bernstein's contested distinction between restricted and elaborated codes. Dell Hymes proposed the ethnography of communication and the SPEAKING model for analysing speech events, while John Gumperz developed interactional sociolinguistics, examining how contextualisation cues guide interpretation in conversation.

Later waves

Penelope Eckert describes three waves of variation study: correlating variants with demographic categories; examining local, ethnographic categories such as "jocks" and "burnouts" in Detroit high schools; and analysing how speakers actively construct identities and styles from linguistic resources. Contemporary sociolinguistics also addresses language and gender, language policy and planning, multilingualism and globalisation, and the sociolinguistics of online communication.
