Ascus

An ascus (from Greek: ἀσκός, "sac" or "wineskin"; plural asci) is the sexual spore-bearing cell produced in ascomycete fungi. Each ascus usually contains eight ascospores, produced by meiosis followed, in most species, by a mitotic division. Asci are the defining feature of the phylum Ascomycota, the largest phylum of fungi, which includes yeasts, morels, truffles and many moulds.

Development and function

The ascus develops from a binucleate ascogenous hypha. The two nuclei fuse (karyogamy) to form a diploid nucleus, which immediately undergoes meiosis, producing four haploid nuclei; a further mitotic division in most species gives eight nuclei, around which ascospores are formed by free-cell formation. In many species the mature ascus builds up internal turgor pressure and discharges its spores forcibly through an apical pore or slit — in some species over distances of several centimetres. In truffles and other closed fruiting bodies, spores are released only when the ascus decays or is eaten.

Classification

Mycologists classify asci by their wall structure and discharge mechanism: prototunicate asci have a thin wall that dissolves; unitunicate asci have a single functional wall layer (operculate if it opens by a lid, inoperculate if by a pore); and bitunicate asci have a double wall whose inner layer extends during discharge. Ascus type is an important character in fungal taxonomy.
