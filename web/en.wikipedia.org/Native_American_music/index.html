Native American music

Native American music is the traditional music of the Indigenous peoples of North America. It encompasses a wide variety of ceremonial, social and storytelling traditions, varying greatly among the continent's hundreds of distinct tribal nations, yet sharing features such as the centrality of singing, drumming and the flute, and the integration of music with dance, ritual and communal identity.

Characteristics and instruments

Traditional Native American music is predominantly vocal and often monophonic, with songs built on descending melodic contours, vocables (non-lexical syllables) and call-and-response between a leader and chorus. Percussion — frame drums, large powwow drums, rattles and rasps — provides rhythm; the Native American flute, traditionally associated with Plains and Eastern Woodlands cultures and with courtship, produces a distinctive pentatonic sound. Regional styles include the high, tense singing of the Plains, the complex polyphony of the Northwest Coast, Pueblo song, and the social round dances of the Southwest.

History and contemporary music

Ceremonial music was suppressed during the era of forced assimilation and boarding schools in the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries, but survived and revived, with powwow culture spreading intertribally after the Second World War. Contemporary Native musicians work in every genre: folk singer Buffy Sainte-Marie, flautist R. Carlos Nakai, the rock band Redbone, powwow drum groups such as Northern Cree, and the Inuit throat-singing and electronic experiments of Tanya Tagaq. Since 2001 the Grammy Awards have included a category for Native American music (now within Best Regional Roots Music Album), and the Native American Music Awards were founded in 1998.
