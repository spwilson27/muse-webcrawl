Chechen (Нохчийн мотт, Noxçiyn mott, [ˈnoxtʃʰiːn muɔt]) is a Northeast Caucasian language spoken by the Chechen people, primarily in the Chechen Republic of Russia and by diaspora communities in Europe, Turkey and the Middle East. Together with Ingush it forms the Vainakh branch of the Nakh languages.

Grammar

Chechen is notable for its large consonant inventory with ejectives and pharyngeals, its system of noun classes (up to six, marked by agreement prefixes on verbs and adjectives), and ergative-absolutive alignment. Verbs agree with the absolutive argument, and the word order is typically subject–object–verb. The literary standard is based on the lowland (Grozny) dialect.

Writing systems

Chechen was historically written in Arabic script, used for religious texts. A Latin alphabet was introduced in the 1920s during Soviet latinisation, replaced by Cyrillic in 1938. After the Soviet collapse, a Latin-based alphabet was briefly adopted by the separatist government in the 1990s, but Cyrillic remains dominant in practice.
