Upper house

An upper house is one of two chambers of a bicameral legislature, the other chamber being the lower house. The house formally designated as the upper house is usually smaller and often has more restricted power than the lower house. A legislature composed of only one house (and which therefore has no upper or lower house) is described as unicameral.

Characteristics

Upper houses typically differ from lower houses in several respects: members may be appointed rather than elected, serve longer terms, represent constituent states or territories rather than population districts, and face higher age or property qualifications. Examples include the Senate of the United States, the House of Lords of the United Kingdom, the Senate of Canada, and the Bundesrat of Germany.

Powers

Upper houses commonly review and amend legislation, delay bills passed by the lower house, and in federal systems represent the constituent units of the federation. In many parliamentary systems the upper house cannot initiate money bills or bring down the government, while in presidential systems such as the United States the Senate holds distinctive powers such as ratifying treaties and confirming appointments.
