Maya mythology

Maya mythology is the body of myths of the Maya peoples of Mesoamerica, preserved in hieroglyphic inscriptions and codices, in colonial-era texts such as the Popol Vuh and the Books of Chilam Balam, and in living oral tradition. These myths describe the creation of the world and humanity, the deeds of gods and heroes, and the cycles of the calendar.

Sources

Only four pre-conquest Maya codices survive (the Dresden, Madrid, Paris, and Grolier codices). After the Spanish conquest, Maya scribes wrote new books in Latin script, most famously the Popol Vuh of the K'iche' Maya, compiled in the 1550s, which recounts the creation of humankind from maize and the adventures of the Hero Twins.

Major themes and figures

Creation in the Popol Vuh proceeds through failed attempts — humans of mud, then of wood — before the gods fashion people from maize dough. The Hero Twins, Hunahpu and Xbalanque, descend to the underworld of Xibalba, outwit its lords, and are transformed into the sun and moon. Deities include Itzamna, creator and god of writing; Kukulkan, the feathered serpent; Chaac, god of rain; Yum Kaax, god of maize and nature; and the death gods of Xibalba. The world tree (Yaxche) joins the underworld, earth, and sky, and the Milky Way was conceived as its celestial counterpart.

Cosmology

Maya cosmology envisioned a flat, four-cornered earth beneath a layered sky and above a nine-level underworld, with time unfolding in vast calendar cycles, including the 5,125-year Long Count cycle whose 2012 completion attracted modern popular attention.
