Rust is an iron oxide, usually red-orange in colour, formed by the reaction of iron and oxygen in the presence of water or air moisture. Rust consists of hydrous iron(III) oxides (Fe2O3·nH2O) and iron(III) oxide-hydroxide (FeO(OH), Fe(OH)3). Given sufficient time, any iron mass will eventually convert entirely to rust and disintegrate.

Chemistry

Rusting is an electrochemical process. At anodic sites on the metal surface, iron atoms lose electrons (Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻); at cathodic sites, oxygen is reduced in the presence of water (O2 + 2H2O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻). The resulting iron(II) hydroxide is further oxidised to the familiar reddish-brown iron(III) compounds. Unlike the protective oxide films on aluminium or stainless steel, rust is porous and flaky, so it does not shield the underlying metal and corrosion continues inward. Salts, especially chlorides from seawater or road de-icing, greatly accelerate rusting.

Prevention

Common protection methods include painting and coatings, galvanisation (a sacrificial zinc layer), alloying into stainless steel, bluing, oiling, and cathodic protection of ships, pipelines and bridges using sacrificial anodes or impressed current. Rust converters based on phosphoric or tannic acid transform rust into a stable, paintable layer.

Economic impact

Corrosion of iron and steel costs industrial economies several percent of GDP annually in maintenance, repair and replacement of bridges, vehicles, ships and infrastructure.
