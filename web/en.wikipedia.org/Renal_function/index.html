Renal function

The nephron carries out filtration, reabsorption, and secretion.

Renal function, in nephrology, is an indication of the kidney's condition and its role in renal physiology. The kidneys regulate the body's fluid volume, electrolyte and acid–base balance, excrete waste products such as urea, and produce hormones including erythropoietin and calcitriol. Glomerular filtration rate (GFR) describes the flow rate of filtered fluid through the kidney and is the most widely used overall index of renal function.

Measurement

GFR cannot easily be measured directly in routine practice, so it is usually estimated (eGFR) from the serum concentration of creatinine, an endogenous filtration marker, using equations such as the CKD-EPI formula that also account for age and sex. Cystatin C provides an alternative marker less dependent on muscle mass. Measured GFR, using exogenous markers such as inulin or iohexol, is reserved for situations requiring high accuracy. Creatinine clearance from a timed urine collection is another traditional approach.

Clinical significance

Declining renal function defines chronic kidney disease, which is staged by GFR category and the degree of albuminuria. An acute fall in function is termed acute kidney injury. Assessment of renal function guides drug dosing, the timing of dialysis, and evaluation for kidney transplantation.
