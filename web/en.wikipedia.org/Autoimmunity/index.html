Autoimmunity

Autoimmunity is the immune system's response against an organism's own healthy cells, tissues and organs. The immune system normally distinguishes self from non-self through mechanisms of tolerance established in the thymus and bone marrow (central tolerance) and in the periphery; when these fail, self-reactive lymphocytes and autoantibodies can cause autoimmune diseases.

Autoimmune diseases

More than eighty conditions are recognised as autoimmune, including type 1 diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus, multiple sclerosis, coeliac disease, Hashimoto's thyroiditis and psoriasis. They may be organ-specific, as when pancreatic beta cells are destroyed in type 1 diabetes, or systemic, as in lupus. Autoimmune diseases are more common in women than in men.

Causes and treatment

Development of autoimmunity reflects a combination of genetic susceptibility (notably certain HLA types) and environmental triggers such as infections, which may act through molecular mimicry or bystander activation. Low-level autoimmunity is also a normal feature of immune regulation and clearance of dead cells. Treatment typically involves immunosuppression — corticosteroids, disease-modifying drugs and targeted biologics — balanced against the risk of infection.
