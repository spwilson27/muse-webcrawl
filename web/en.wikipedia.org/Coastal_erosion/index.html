Coastal erosion

Coastal erosion is the loss or displacement of land along the coastline due to the action of waves, currents, tides, wind-driven water, and other impacts of storms or drainage. It operates on timescales from single storm events that strip beaches overnight to the slow retreat of cliffs over millennia. Erosion of one stretch of coast supplies sediment, transported by longshore drift, to accreting beaches and spits elsewhere; human interference with this sediment budget is a principal cause of accelerated erosion.

Processes

Four main processes erode rock coasts: hydraulic action, in which compressed air and water pressure exploit cracks; abrasion (corrasion), where sediment carried by waves grinds against the shore; attrition, the wearing down of the sediment itself; and solution or corrosion of soluble rocks such as limestone and chalk. At the base of cliffs, wave quarrying cuts a notch that destabilises the face above, leading to rockfalls and landslides. Softer coasts of sand and clay may retreat metres per year — the Holderness coast of England, cut in glacial till, is among Europe's fastest-eroding, losing on average around two metres annually.

Causes and accelerating factors

Natural drivers include storminess, sea-level rise, and the reduced sediment supply that follows the damming of rivers. Defences built to protect one site frequently starve downdrift beaches: groynes trap sand moving along the shore, while seawalls reflect wave energy and scour the beach in front of them. Dredging, sand mining, and the destruction of mangroves and dunes remove natural buffers.

Management

Coastal management strategies range from "hard" engineering — seawalls, revetments, groynes, breakwaters, and cliff drainage — through "soft" methods such as beach nourishment and dune restoration, to managed realignment, in which defences are deliberately breached or set back to create intertidal habitat and dissipate wave energy. In England, Shoreline Management Plans assign stretches of coast to policies of hold the line, advance the line, managed realignment, or no active intervention. Rising sea levels are expected to make defence of low-value land progressively uneconomic in many countries.
