In ancient Greece, a metic (Greek: μέτοικος, métoikos, plural metoikoi) was a resident foreigner living in a Greek polis who did not have the rights of a citizen. The institution is best documented in classical Athens, where metics formed a substantial and economically important part of the population — perhaps a third of the free inhabitants by the 4th century BC.

Status and obligations

Metics were free men (and women) who had migrated, or were descended from migrants, from other Greek cities or non-Greek lands. They were required to register with a citizen sponsor (prostates), pay a special tax (the metoikion, twelve drachmas a year for men), and perform military service, but they could not own land or houses unless granted the privilege of enktesis, could not vote or hold office, and had to have cases heard in special courts. Failure to meet obligations could result in enslavement.

Role in Athens

Metics were prominent in trade, banking, manufacturing, and intellectual life: the orator Lysias, the philosopher Aristotle, and the banker Pasion (before his naturalisation) were all metics. Although excluded from politics, wealthy metics contributed liturgies and taxes, and some were honoured for services to the city.
