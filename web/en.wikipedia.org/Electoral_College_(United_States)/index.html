United States Electoral College

The United States Electoral College is the group of presidential electors that is formed every four years during the presidential election for the sole purpose of voting for the president and vice president. The process is described in Article II of the United States Constitution. There are 538 electors, corresponding to the 100 senators plus 435 representatives, plus three electors for the District of Columbia under the Twenty-third Amendment. A candidate must receive an absolute majority of electoral votes (at least 270) to win.

Procedure

Since the mid-19th century, voters in each state vote directly for slates of electors pledged to candidates. Almost all states award all of their electors to the statewide popular-vote winner ("winner-take-all"); Maine and Nebraska instead use the congressional district method. Electors meet in their state capitals in December to cast their ballots, which Congress counts in January. "Faithless electors" who vote for someone other than their pledged candidate have never changed an outcome, and the Supreme Court held in Chiafalo v. Washington (2020) that states may penalise them.

History and controversies

The College was a compromise between election by Congress and by direct popular vote. Five elections have produced a president who lost the popular vote: 1824, 1876, 1888, 2000 and 2016. If no candidate reaches 270, the House of Representatives holds a contingent election, voting by state delegation, as happened in 1800 and 1824.

Debate

Supporters argue the system preserves federalism, requires geographically broad coalitions, and contains recount disputes. Critics argue it gives disproportionate weight to small states and a few "swing states", and can override the national popular vote. Reform proposals include the National Popular Vote Interstate Compact, under which states pledge their electors to the national popular-vote winner.
