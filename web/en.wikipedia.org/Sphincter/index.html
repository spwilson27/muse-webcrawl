Sphincter

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A sphincter is a circular muscle that normally maintains constriction of a natural body passage or orifice and that relaxes as required by normal physiological functioning. Sphincters are found in many animals; there are over 60 types in the human body, some microscopically small, in particular the precapillary sphincters that control blood flow into capillaries. Sphincters relax at death, often releasing fluids, in the phenomenon sometimes called the "sphincter release".

Function

Many sphincters are used every day in the normal course of digestion and vision. For example, the lower esophageal sphincter (cardiac sphincter) at the top of the stomach prevents acidic stomach contents from travelling back into the esophagus. Sphincters may be composed of smooth muscle under involuntary control or skeletal muscle under voluntary control, such as the external anal sphincter.

Examples in humans

Important sphincters include the upper and lower esophageal sphincters, the pyloric sphincter controlling emptying of the stomach into the duodenum, the sphincter of Oddi, the ileocecal sphincter, the internal and external anal sphincters, the urethral sphincters controlling urination, and the pupillary sphincter that constricts the pupil of the eye. Precapillary sphincters regulate blood flow in microcirculation.

Clinical significance

Dysfunction of sphincters underlies many disorders: gastroesophageal reflux disease (lower esophageal sphincter incompetence), achalasia (failure of that sphincter to relax), fecal and urinary incontinence, and pyloric stenosis in infants. Treatments range from medication and dilation to surgical procedures such as sphincterotomy and artificial sphincter implants.
