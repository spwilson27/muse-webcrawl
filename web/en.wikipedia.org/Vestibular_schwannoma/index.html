Vestibular schwannoma

A vestibular schwannoma (VS), also called an acoustic neuroma, is a benign primary intracranial tumour of the Schwann cells of the vestibular nerve (the vestibular division of cranial nerve VIII). It arises within the internal auditory canal and may expand into the cerebellopontine angle. Although histologically benign and slow-growing, it can cause serious symptoms by compressing neighbouring nerves and, if large, the brainstem.

Signs and symptoms

The typical presentation is slowly progressive, one-sided sensorineural hearing loss, often accompanied by tinnitus in the same ear. Imbalance or true vertigo may occur, though the slow growth usually allows central compensation. Larger tumours can affect the trigeminal nerve (facial numbness), the facial nerve, and eventually cause raised intracranial pressure. Sudden hearing loss occurs in a minority of patients.

Causes

Most vestibular schwannomas are sporadic and unilateral, and are associated with loss of function of the NF2 tumour-suppressor gene product merlin (schwannomin) on chromosome 22. Bilateral vestibular schwannomas are the hallmark of neurofibromatosis type II, an inherited disorder caused by germline NF2 mutations.

Diagnosis

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with gadolinium contrast is the definitive test, showing an enhancing mass centred on the internal auditory canal. Audiometry typically shows asymmetric sensorineural hearing loss, and auditory brainstem response testing may be abnormal.

Management

Options include watchful waiting with serial MRI ("wait and scan") for small, slow-growing tumours, especially in older patients; stereotactic radiosurgery (such as Gamma Knife) to arrest growth; and microsurgical resection via translabyrinthine, retrosigmoid or middle fossa approaches, chosen according to tumour size and whether hearing preservation is attempted. Facial nerve function is the major surgical concern; modern series report high rates of facial preservation for small tumours.
