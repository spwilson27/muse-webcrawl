Utopian socialism

Utopian socialism is the term given to the earliest currents of modern socialist thought, as exemplified by Henri de Saint-Simon, Charles Fourier, Étienne Cabet and Robert Owen. The label was popularised by Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels, who contrasted these thinkers' ideal communities with their own "scientific" socialism based on historical materialism.

Principal figures

Saint-Simon envisioned society reorganised by industrialists and scientists. Fourier designed self-sufficient communities called phalanstères, organised by his theory of passionate attraction, and notably coined the word féminisme. Owen, a Welsh mill owner, demonstrated at New Lanark that humane conditions and education could coexist with profit, and later founded the cooperative colony of New Harmony, Indiana.

Communities and influence

Followers founded dozens of experimental settlements in France, Britain and the United States, including Brook Farm and the Oneida Community. Most were short-lived, but the movement seeded the cooperative movement, trade unionism and later socialist parties, and pioneered ideas such as universal education, women's emancipation and the reduction of working hours.

Criticism

Marxists faulted utopian socialists for appealing to reason and moral example rather than class struggle, and for designing detailed blueprints of future society. Defenders note that their experiments were a practical response to the dislocations of early industrial capitalism and that many of their proposals later entered mainstream social policy.
