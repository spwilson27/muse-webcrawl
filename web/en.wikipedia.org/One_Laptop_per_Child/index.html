One Laptop per Child (OLPC) is a non-profit initiative launched in 2005 by Nicholas Negroponte of the MIT Media Lab. Its goal was to create a rugged, low-cost, low-power laptop — the XO, widely nicknamed the "$100 laptop" — and distribute it in the millions to schoolchildren in developing countries, on the theory, drawn from Seymour Papert's constructionism, that children learn best by exploring with a computer of their own.

The XO laptop

The XO-1, designed by Yves Béhar's fuseproject, had a distinctive green-and-white rubberised case with "rabbit ear" Wi-Fi antennas, a sunlight-readable dual-mode display, a sealed membrane keyboard, flash storage instead of a hard drive, and very low power consumption, with hand-crank and solar charging options. Its Sugar interface ran on Linux and emphasised activities and collaboration over conventional desktop metaphors. Later models included the dual-touchscreen XO-4 Touch and tablet spin-offs.

Deployment and criticism

Governments bought XOs in quantity: Uruguay's Plan Ceibal gave one to every primary schoolchild, and large programmes ran in Peru, Rwanda, Ethiopia and elsewhere; total distribution reached roughly three million units. Evaluations found improved computer access and some cognitive gains but little measurable effect on test scores, and the project was criticised for underestimating teacher training, maintenance, content and electricity needs, and for its real unit cost exceeding $100. Commercial netbooks and cheap tablets soon undercut its niche. OLPC wound down its original operations in the mid-2010s, though successor organisations and the Sugar software community continue.
