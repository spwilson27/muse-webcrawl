Statism in Shōwa Japan refers to the authoritarian political ideology and set of policies that came to dominate Japan during the first decades of the Shōwa era (1926–1989), especially in the 1930s and during the Second Sino-Japanese War and the Pacific War. It combined ultranationalism, reverence for the emperor and the kokutai (national polity), anti-liberalism and anti-communism, militarism, and extensive state direction of the economy.

Origins

The ideology drew on thinkers such as Kita Ikki, whose Outline Plan for the Reorganization of Japan (1919) called for a coup d'état, suspension of the constitution, and nationalization of industry under the emperor. The global depression after 1929, discontent in the countryside, and resentment of party governments and zaibatsu plutocracy strengthened ultranationalist movements within the armed forces, whose young officers staged incidents including the Manchurian Incident of 1931 and the February 26 Incident of 1936.

The Shōwa Restoration and wartime state

Advocates spoke of a "Shōwa Restoration" that would restore direct imperial rule and purge corrupt parties and business interests. Under Prime Minister Fumimaro Konoe, the New Order movement dissolved the political parties into the Imperial Rule Assistance Association in 1940, while the National Mobilization Law of 1938 gave the government sweeping control over labour, industry, and resources. The Peace Preservation Law and the Tokkō (Special Higher Police) suppressed dissent. Although Japan is sometimes described as fascist, historians debate the label: the system lacked a single mass party or dictator on the European model, and authority remained dispersed among the military, bureaucracy, court, and throne, with Hideki Tojo the most prominent wartime prime minister.

End

The statist system collapsed with Japan's defeat in 1945; the Allied occupation dissolved its organizations, purged many of its leaders, and introduced a new constitution in 1947.
