Santa Fe art colony

The Santa Fe art colony was a community of artists that formed in Santa Fe, New Mexico, in the early twentieth century, drawn by the region's high-desert light, dramatic landscapes, and the living cultures of Pueblo and Hispano communities. Together with the nearby Taos Society of Artists, it made northern New Mexico one of the most important regional art centers in the United States.

Origins

Artists and writers began arriving in numbers after New Mexico statehood in 1912 and the opening of the Museum of New Mexico in 1917, whose director Edgar L. Hewett encouraged artists by providing studio space. Figures such as William Penhallow Henderson, B.J.O. Nordfeldt, Marsden Hartley, John Marin, and later Georgia O'Keeffe (who settled in nearby Abiquiú) worked in the area. Promoter Mary Hunter Austin and patron Mabel Dodge Luhan helped publicize the colony, and a loose circle known as "Los Cinco Pintores" exhibited together in the 1920s.

Character and legacy

The colony's art ranged from realistic depictions of Pueblo life and adobe architecture to modernist abstraction inspired by the landscape. Santa Fe's Canyon Road developed into a dense gallery district, and the city remains a major art market — often described as the second or third largest in the United States — alongside institutions such as the Georgia O'Keeffe Museum and the annual Santa Fe Indian Market, which centers Native artists.
