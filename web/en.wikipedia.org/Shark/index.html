Shark

Sharks are a group of elasmobranch cartilaginous fishes characterised by a skeleton of cartilage, five to seven gill slits on the sides of the head, and pectoral fins not fused to the head. They belong to the superorder Selachimorpha and, together with rays, form the subclass Elasmobranchii.

Evolution and diversity

Sharks have existed for more than 450 million years, predating trees and dinosaurs. Over 500 living species are known, ranging from the tiny dwarf lanternshark (about 17 cm) to the whale shark, the largest fish in the world, which can exceed 12 metres. Well-known species include the great white shark, tiger shark, hammerhead sharks, and the mako sharks.

Anatomy and senses

A shark's skin is covered in dermal denticles that reduce drag. Teeth are replaced continuously throughout life. Sharks possess keen smell, hearing, and electroreception through the ampullae of Lorenzini, which detect the electric fields of prey. Most are cold-bodied, but lamnid sharks such as the great white maintain elevated body temperatures.

Ecology and conservation

Most sharks are apex or mesopredators vital to ocean ecosystems, though the largest species feed on plankton. Many populations have declined sharply due to overfishing, bycatch, and the shark fin trade; a substantial fraction of species are considered threatened. Shark attacks on humans are rare, and conservation efforts increasingly protect sharks through sanctuaries and trade controls.
