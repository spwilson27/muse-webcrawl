Peterborough Chronicle

The Peterborough Chronicle (also called the Laud manuscript) is a version of the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle maintained at Peterborough Abbey, in the English Midlands, into the 12th century. It is uniquely valuable because, alone among the Chronicle manuscripts, it continues beyond 1131: its "First Continuation" covers 1132–1131's aftermath through 1131–1154 annals and a "Final Continuation" extends the record to 1154, documenting the civil war known as the Anarchy in vivid detail.

The manuscript

The manuscript, now in the Bodleian Library, Oxford (MS Laud Misc. 636), was copied at Peterborough around 1121 after a fire had destroyed the abbey's earlier copy. The Peterborough monks borrowed a Chronicle exemplar — apparently from Canterbury — and then continued the annals locally for more than thirty years. It is written in Old English transitioning visibly toward Middle English, making it a key source for the history of the English language.

The Anarchy

The entry for 1137 contains the Chronicle's most famous passage, describing the suffering of England during the reign of King Stephen, when rival claimants Stephen and Matilda fought for the throne: castles were built across the land, their garrisons torturing and extorting the local people. The chronicler concludes that men said openly "that Christ and his saints slept". The annals end in 1154 with the accession of Henry II.

Linguistic significance

Because its later entries were written in the spoken language of their day rather than the standardised late West Saxon of earlier texts, the Peterborough Chronicle shows grammatical simplification in progress — the loss of many inflections and grammatical gender — and the early effects of French vocabulary entering English. Linguists treat it as a bridge between Old and Middle English.
