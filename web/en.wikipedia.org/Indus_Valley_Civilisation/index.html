The Indus Valley Civilisation (IVC), also known as the Harappan Civilisation, was a Bronze Age civilisation in the northwestern regions of South Asia, lasting from 3300 BCE to 1300 BCE, and in its mature form from 2600 BCE to 1900 BCE. Together with ancient Egypt and Mesopotamia, it was one of three early civilisations of the Near East and South Asia, and of the three, the most widespread. It flourished in the basins of the Indus River and along the seasonal Ghaggar-Hakra river.

Discovery

The ruins at Harappa were first described by Charles Masson in 1842, and excavations in the 1920s under the Archaeological Survey of India — by Rakhal Das Banerji at Mohenjo-daro and Daya Ram Sahni at Harappa, overseen by John Marshall — revealed a previously unknown Bronze Age civilisation. Its existence was announced to the world in 1924.

Cities and society

Harappan cities were notable for advanced urban planning: grid-pattern streets, standardised fired-brick construction, covered drainage systems, and public structures such as the Great Bath at Mohenjo-daro and the dockyard at Lothal. Weights and measures were highly standardised, based on a chert weight system. Craft production included bead-making in carnelian and steatite, shell working, metallurgy in copper and bronze, and a distinctive black-on-red pottery. Small steatite seals, often depicting a "unicorn" motif and bearing signs of the undeciphered Indus script, were used in trade; Harappan goods and seals reached Mesopotamia, where the Indus region may correspond to the land called Meluhha.

Remarkably, archaeologists have found little evidence of monumental palaces, temples, or warfare, and no clear evidence of kings or armies, leading to debate about how Harappan society was governed.

Decline

From about 1900 BCE the mature cities were gradually abandoned and populations shifted eastward and southward in the Late Harappan phase. Proposed causes include weakening monsoon rainfall and climate change, river course shifts and flooding, and disruption of trade — not invasion, as once proposed in the now-discredited "Aryan invasion" massacre theory.

Script and language

The Indus script, known from roughly 400 distinct signs on seals and other objects, remains undeciphered; inscriptions are short, averaging about five signs. The underlying language is unknown, though Dravidian is a leading hypothesis.
