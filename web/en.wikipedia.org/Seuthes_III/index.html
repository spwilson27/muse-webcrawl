Seuthes III

The bronze head of Seuthes III, discovered in 2004

Seuthes III (Ancient Greek: Σεύθης) was a king of the Odrysian kingdom of Thrace from the late 320s BCE until his death around 300–295 BCE. A contemporary of Alexander the Great's successors, he resisted Macedonian domination and asserted Thracian independence after Alexander's death, defeating forces of the Macedonian ruler Lysimachus in 313 BCE, though he later acknowledged Lysimachus's suzerainty.

Reign

Seuthes consolidated Odrysian power in the upper valley of the Tundzha river in central Bulgaria, where he founded a Hellenistic-style capital named Seuthopolis. Unlike earlier Thracian royal residences, Seuthopolis was a fortified city laid out on a Greek plan, with a palace, temples and inscriptions in Greek — evidence of the deep Hellenization of the Thracian elite.

Tomb and the bronze head

Seuthes III is best known today from his tomb, a monumental Thracian temple-tomb in the Golyama Kosmatka mound near Kazanlak, excavated in 2004 by the Bulgarian archaeologist Georgi Kitov. Before its entrance lay a remarkably lifelike bronze head, part of a statue of the king, with eyes inlaid with alabaster and glass paste. Inside the tomb were rich grave goods, including a golden wreath, armour, horse trappings and a wine vessel inscribed with the king's name, which confirmed the identification of the tomb's owner.

Legacy

After Seuthes's death the Odrysian kingdom fragmented, though Thracian dynasts continued to rule parts of the region for generations until its eventual absorption into the Roman world.
