Evapotranspiration

Evapotranspiration (ET) is the combined process of water movement to the atmosphere from a surface, including evaporation from soil, water bodies and intercepted precipitation, and transpiration from plants through their stomata. It is a key component of the water cycle and of the surface energy balance, and is central to hydrology, irrigation scheduling and climate science.

Definitions

Potential evapotranspiration (PET) is the amount of evapotranspiration that would occur if sufficient water were available. Reference evapotranspiration (ET₀) is calculated for a standard reference surface such as well-watered grass. Actual evapotranspiration is the flux that actually occurs under prevailing soil-moisture and plant conditions.

Estimation

The FAO-56 Penman–Monteith equation is the standard method for estimating ET₀ from meteorological data (net radiation, air temperature, humidity and wind speed). Direct measurement approaches include lysimeters, eddy covariance flux towers, and remote sensing from satellites. Simpler empirical methods include the Thornthwaite and Hargreaves equations.

Importance

Evapotranspiration returns a large fraction of continental precipitation to the atmosphere and strongly influences drought, soil moisture and agricultural water demand. Changes in ET are an important indicator of the hydrological response to climate change.
