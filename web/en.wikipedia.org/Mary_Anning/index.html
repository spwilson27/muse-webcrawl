Mary Anning

Mary Anning
Born: 21 May 1799, Lyme Regis, Dorset, England
Died: 9 March 1847 (aged 47), Lyme Regis
Known for: Fossil collecting; discovery of ichthyosaur, plesiosaur and pterosaur remains
Fields: Palaeontology

Mary Anning (21 May 1799 – 9 March 1847) was an English fossil collector, dealer and palaeontologist. Working the dangerous sea cliffs of Lyme Regis on England's Jurassic Coast, she made a series of extraordinary discoveries — including the first correctly identified ichthyosaur skeleton, the first complete plesiosaur, and the first pterosaur found outside Germany — that transformed scientific understanding of prehistoric life and extinction. Despite minimal formal education and the barriers faced by women of her era, her finds were studied by the leading geologists of the day.

Early life

Anning was born into a working-class family in Lyme Regis. Her father, Richard Anning, a cabinetmaker, supplemented the family income by selling fossils ("curiosities") collected from the fossil-rich Blue Lias cliffs to tourists. Mary learned to find and prepare fossils as a child; after her father's death in 1810, fossil collecting became the family's livelihood. She survived a lightning strike as an infant, an event local legend credited with her intelligence and curiosity.

Major discoveries

In 1811–1812, Mary and her brother Joseph excavated the first complete Ichthyosaurus skeleton. In 1823 she found the first complete Plesiosaurus, and in 1828 the first British pterosaur, Dimorphodon. She also recognized that "bezoar stones" were fossilized faeces (coprolites) and pioneered the study of fossil ink sacs in belemnites. Many of her specimens were sold to collectors and museums, ending up in the Natural History Museum in London and elsewhere, often without credit to her.

Legacy

Anning's work contributed to fundamental changes in scientific thinking about the history of life and the Earth. She died of breast cancer in 1847 at the age of 47. The Geological Society of London, which did not admit women until 1919, nonetheless published an obituary for her — an unusual honor. In 2010 the Royal Society included her in a list of the ten British women most influential in the history of science. She is popularly associated with the tongue-twister "She sells sea shells by the sea shore."
