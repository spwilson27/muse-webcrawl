The Four Corners is a region of the Southwestern United States consisting of the southwestern corner of Colorado, southeastern corner of Utah, northeastern corner of Arizona, and northwestern corner of New Mexico. The Four Corners area is named after the quadripoint at the intersection of approximately 37° north latitude with 109° 03′ west longitude, where the boundaries of the four states meet, and are marked by the Four Corners Monument. It is the only location in the United States where four states meet.

The majority of the Four Corners region belongs to semi-autonomous Native American nations, the largest of which is the Navajo Nation, followed by Hopi, Ute, and Zuni tribal reserves.

Geography

The region lies on the high Colorado Plateau, a landscape of mesas, canyons and desert. Nearby attractions include Monument Valley, Mesa Verde National Park, Canyon de Chelly, and Chaco Culture National Historical Park — centres of Ancestral Puebloan (Anasazi) culture.

The monument

The Four Corners Monument, administered by the Navajo Nation Parks and Recreation Department, features a granite and brass disc at the exact quadripoint, surrounded by stalls where Navajo and Ute artisans sell crafts and food. Visitors commonly photograph themselves with a limb in each state.

History of the boundaries

The boundaries stem from 19th-century territorial surveys: Congress defined the territories using meridians and parallels, and a survey marker was first placed in 1875. A 2009 media report suggested the monument lay 2.5 miles from the true corner, but the National Geodetic Survey confirmed the marker as legally correct, since state boundaries follow the original surveyed lines.

The wider region

"Four Corners" also describes the broader cultural and economic region — sometimes called the Four Corners states — known for archaeology, energy resources (oil, gas, uranium, coal), and tourism.
