Screw

Screw
ClassificationFastener; simple machine
ComponentsHead, shank, helical thread

RelatedBolt, nut, screw thread, Archimedes' screw

A screw is an externally helical threaded fastener capable of being inserted into holes in assembled parts, of mating with a preformed internal thread or forming its own thread, and of being tightened or released by torquing the head. Screws are among the most common fasteners in the world and are also one of the classical simple machines, converting rotational motion into linear motion and force.

Structure

A screw consists of a head, a shank, and a helical ridge — the thread — wrapped around a cylinder. The head bears the drive recess or external shape by which torque is applied: common forms include slotted, Phillips cross, Pozidriv, Torx (hexalobular), hex socket (Allen), and Robertson square drives. The thread is characterized by its pitch (distance between adjacent threads), major and minor diameters, and hand — most screws are right-handed, tightening clockwise.

Types

Machine screws are designed to mate with a nut or a tapped hole. Wood screws taper to a point and cut or form threads in timber. Self-tapping screws form threads in softer materials; self-drilling screws also drill their own pilot hole. Set screws have no head and secure one part against another, while lag screws are large heavy-duty wood screws. Sheet-metal, drywall, and chipboard screws are optimized for their respective materials.

History

The screw principle was known in antiquity: the water screw associated with Archimedes (3rd century BCE) lifted water for irrigation. Metal screws were rare and handmade until the Industrial Revolution, when screw-cutting lathes — notably Henry Maudslay's around 1800 — made them cheap and standardized. The Phillips drive was popularized in the 1930s for assembly-line use, and the ISO metric thread became the dominant global standard.

Mechanics

The screw is an inclined plane wrapped around a cylinder. A small torque applied at the head produces a large axial clamping force because of the mechanical advantage of the shallow helix angle. Friction in the thread usually makes the joint self-locking, so it does not loosen under ordinary static loads. See also screw thread, bolt, and simple machine.
