Billy Wilder (born Samuel Wilder; 22 June 1906 – 27 March 2002) was an Austrian-born American filmmaker, screenwriter and producer. Over a career spanning five decades he directed some of the most celebrated films in Hollywood history, moving with unusual ease between film noir, drama and comedy. He won six Academy Awards, including Best Director twice.

Early life

Wilder was born in Sucha, then in Austrian Galicia (now Poland), to Jewish parents who ran a chain of railway-station restaurants. The family moved to Vienna, and Wilder worked as a journalist in Vienna and then Berlin, writing screenplays for German films in the late 1920s and early 1930s. With the Nazi rise to power in 1933 he emigrated, first to Paris and then to Hollywood, arriving in 1934 with little English.

Hollywood career

Working as a screenwriter, Wilder formed a celebrated partnership with Charles Brackett, writing Ninotchka (1939) and Midnight (1939). His breakthrough as a director came with the film noir Double Indemnity (1944), followed by The Lost Weekend (1945), a stark portrait of alcoholism that won the Academy Award for Best Picture and Best Director. Sunset Boulevard (1950) dissected Hollywood itself, and Ace in the Hole (1951) attacked media cynicism.

In the later 1950s Wilder turned increasingly to comedy: The Seven Year Itch (1955), Some Like It Hot (1959) — frequently voted one of the greatest comedies ever made — and The Apartment (1960), which won Best Picture, Best Director and Best Screenplay. Later works included One, Two, Three (1961), Irma la Douce (1963) and The Private Life of Sherlock Holmes (1970).

Style and legacy

Wilder's films are noted for economical storytelling, dialogue-driven wit, unsentimental views of human self-interest, and endings that resist easy moralising. His maxim for screenwriters — let the audience add up two and two and reach four themselves — became a byword for narrative economy. He received the AFI Life Achievement Award in 1986 and remains a central figure in the history of American cinema.
