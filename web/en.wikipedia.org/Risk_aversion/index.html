Risk aversion

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Risk aversion is a concept in economics and finance describing the tendency of people to prefer outcomes with low uncertainty to those with high uncertainty, even if the expected value of the uncertain outcome is equal or higher. A risk-averse individual faced with a choice between a guaranteed amount and a gamble with the same expected value will choose the guaranteed amount.

Utility and measurement

In expected utility theory, risk aversion is represented by a concave utility function: each additional unit of wealth adds less utility than the previous one, a property known as diminishing marginal utility. Standard measures include the Arrow–Pratt measures of absolute and relative risk aversion, which relate the curvature of the utility function to the size of the risk premium an individual demands to bear risk.

Examples

Insurance is the classic market expression of risk aversion: policyholders pay a certain premium to avoid an uncertain but potentially large loss. Investors similarly demand higher expected returns from volatile assets, producing the equity premium observed in financial markets. Individuals whose preferences are the opposite are called risk-seeking, while those indifferent to risk are risk-neutral.

Behavioural findings

Behavioural economics shows that risk attitudes are not stable across contexts. Under prospect theory, developed by Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky, people tend to be risk-averse over gains but risk-seeking over losses, and to overweight losses relative to equivalent gains — a phenomenon called loss aversion.
