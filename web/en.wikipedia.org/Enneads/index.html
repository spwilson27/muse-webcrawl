Enneads

The Enneads (Greek: Ἐννεάδες), fully The Six Enneads, is the collection of the writings of the philosopher Plotinus, edited and compiled by his student Porphyry in about AD 270. It is the foundational text of Neoplatonism, the last great school of ancient Greek philosophy.

Structure

Porphyry arranged Plotinus's fifty-four treatises into six sets of nine — hence "enneads", from the Greek ennea, nine — ordering them thematically from the most elementary topics to the most advanced. The first three Enneads deal broadly with ethical, physical and cosmological questions; the Fourth concerns the soul; the Fifth the Intellect (Nous); and the Sixth the One and the Good.

Philosophy

The Enneads expound a hierarchical metaphysics in which all reality emanates from a single first principle, the One, beyond being and thought. From the One proceeds Intellect, and from Intellect the Soul, which in turn produces the material world. The philosophical life is a return (epistrophē) of the soul toward its source, culminating in a mystical union with the One — "the flight of the alone to the Alone", in the famous closing words of the work.

Influence

Through Porphyry, Macrobius, Augustine and the Arabic tradition, the Enneads profoundly shaped medieval Christian, Islamic and Jewish philosophy, and were returned to prominence in the Renaissance through Marsilio Ficino's Latin translation of 1492.
