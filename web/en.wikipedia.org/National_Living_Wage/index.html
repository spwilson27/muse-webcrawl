National Living Wage

Introduced: 1 April 2016

Country: United Kingdom

Applies to: Workers aged 21 and over (from April 2024)

Not to confuse with: The voluntary "real Living Wage" of the Living Wage Foundation

The National Living Wage is an obligatory minimum wage payable to workers in the United Kingdom aged 21 and over, introduced on 1 April 2016 by the Conservative government of David Cameron. It is the highest band of the National Minimum Wage structure. When launched, it applied to workers aged 25 and over at a rate of £7.20 per hour, with a target of reaching 60 percent of median earnings by 2020; the age threshold was lowered to 23 in 2021 and to 21 in 2024, and the rate rose to £11.44 per hour in April 2024 and £12.21 in April 2025.

Background

The National Minimum Wage had been introduced in 1999 by the Labour government. Chancellor George Osborne announced the National Living Wage in his July 2015 budget as a higher compulsory floor for older workers, alongside cuts to tax credits. The Low Pay Commission advises the government on rates. Employers must pay at least the statutory rate; enforcement is carried out by HM Revenue and Customs, with penalties and public naming of non-compliant employers.

Distinction from the "real Living Wage"

The government's National Living Wage is not calculated from living costs and is distinct from the voluntary "real Living Wage" promoted by the Living Wage Foundation, which is independently calculated each year based on the cost of living, carries separate rates for London and the rest of the UK, and is paid voluntarily by accredited employers.

Effects

Studies by the Low Pay Commission and independent researchers have generally found that the National Living Wage substantially raised pay for low earners with little measurable negative effect on employment, though some employers reported pressure on pay differentials and prices.
