Sun dog

A sun dog, known scientifically as a parhelion (plural parhelia), is an atmospheric optical phenomenon consisting of a bright spot on one or both sides of the Sun, at the same altitude above the horizon, often accompanied by a halo. Sun dogs are caused by the refraction of sunlight through hexagonal plate-shaped ice crystals in cirrus or cirrostratus clouds.

Formation

As plate crystals drift downward with their flat faces roughly horizontal, sunlight entering one side face and exiting another is bent by about 22 degrees, concentrating light at that angle from the Sun. The inner edge of a sun dog is reddish and the outer edge bluish, since refraction disperses the colours.

History

Sun dogs have been recorded since antiquity; Aristotle mentioned "mock suns" in his Meteorology. Famous depictions include the Vädersolstavlan of 1535, showing the phenomenon over Stockholm, and the Nuremberg celestial phenomenon of 1561. The English name reflects the spots' faithful following of the Sun like dogs following a master.
