Rotation in office

Rotation in office is the principle that officeholders should serve for limited periods and then yield their place to others, so that public office circulates among citizens rather than becoming a permanent possession. It is the idea behind term limits and mandatory retirement from posts.

History

Rotation was practised in classical Athens, where council members served short, non-consecutive terms chosen by lot, and in the Roman Republic's annual magistracies. In the early United States the Articles of Confederation limited delegates' service, and Andrew Jackson's Democratic politics championed rotation as a democratic, anti-corruption doctrine — though critics linked it to the spoils system.

Modern forms

Today rotation survives in presidential and legislative term limits (for example the US Twenty-second Amendment), rotating presidencies of bodies such as the UN Security Council and the Council of the European Union, and rotating chairs in committees and cooperatives. Debates weigh wider participation and prevention of entrenchment against loss of experience. See also term limit and sortition.
