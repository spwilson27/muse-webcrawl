Titan (mythology)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: The Fall of the Titans, by Cornelis van Haarlem (1588)]

In Greek mythology, the Titans (Greek: Τιτᾶνες) were the pre-Olympian gods: the twelve children of the primordial parents Uranus (Sky) and Gaia (Earth), with six male Titans — Oceanus, Coeus, Crius, Hyperion, Iapetus and Cronus — and six female Titanides — Theia, Rhea, Themis, Mnemosyne, Phoebe and Tethys.

Overthrow of Uranus and rule of Cronus

Gaia, angered that Uranus imprisoned their monstrous children within her, fashioned a sickle and persuaded Cronus, the youngest Titan, to castrate his father. Cronus then ruled during a mythical Golden Age, but, warned that he would be overthrown by his own son as he had overthrown Uranus, he swallowed each of his children by Rhea at birth. Rhea hid the infant Zeus on Crete and gave Cronus a stone to swallow instead.

The Titanomachy

Grown to adulthood, Zeus forced Cronus to disgorge his siblings and led the Olympians in a ten-year war against the Titans, the Titanomachy. With the help of the Cyclopes, who forged Zeus's thunderbolt, and the hundred-handed Hecatoncheires, the Olympians prevailed. The defeated Titans were imprisoned in Tartarus, guarded by the Hecatoncheires, while Atlas was condemned to hold up the sky. Some Titans, such as Oceanus and Themis, had not fought against Zeus and retained their honours.

Legacy

The Titans gave their name to the element titanium, to Saturn's largest moon Titan, and by extension to anything of enormous size or power — "titanic". Prometheus, a son of Iapetus, became the great benefactor of mankind in later myth, stealing fire from the gods.
