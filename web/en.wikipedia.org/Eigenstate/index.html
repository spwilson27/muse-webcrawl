Eigenstate

An eigenstate is a quantum state of a system whose value for a given observable is definite. More precisely, an eigenstate of an operator Â is a state |ψ⟩ such that applying the operator to the state returns the same state multiplied by a constant, the eigenvalue:

Â|ψ⟩ = a|ψ⟩.

When a measurement of the observable corresponding to Â is performed on a system in the eigenstate |ψ⟩, the result is certain to be the eigenvalue a. States that are not eigenstates of Â yield measurement outcomes distributed according to the Born rule over the eigenstates of Â.

Energy eigenstates

The most important example is the energy eigenstate: a state of definite energy, satisfying the time-independent Schrödinger equation Ĥ|ψ⟩ = E|ψ⟩, where Ĥ is the Hamiltonian. Energy eigenstates are stationary states: their probability densities do not change in time, and under time evolution they acquire only a phase factor e−iEt/ħ. A general state can be expanded as a superposition of energy eigenstates, and the expansion coefficients determine the probabilities of the possible energy measurement results.

Simultaneous eigenstates and degeneracy

Two observables can have simultaneous eigenstates only if their operators commute. A set of commuting observables whose simultaneous eigenstates uniquely specify a state is called a complete set of commuting observables (CSCO). If several linearly independent eigenstates share the same eigenvalue, the eigenvalue is called degenerate; degeneracy is often associated with symmetries of the Hamiltonian — for example, the (2l + 1)-fold degeneracy of hydrogen energy levels in the magnetic quantum number m (in the absence of external fields), and the deeper "accidental" degeneracy of the hydrogen spectrum explained by the Runge–Lenz symmetry.

Eigenstates in the formalism

In Dirac notation, eigenstates of position and momentum (improper, delta-normalised eigenstates) provide the bases in which wave functions are represented. Because observables correspond to Hermitian operators, their eigenstates form a complete orthonormal basis for the Hilbert space (the spectral theorem), which underlies the expansion postulate of quantum mechanics. The concept originates in linear algebra, where eigenvectors and eigenvalues characterise linear transformations; the German prefix eigen- means "own" or "characteristic".
