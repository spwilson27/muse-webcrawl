Exogamy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Exogamy is the social norm of marrying outside one's social group, clan, tribe, or other defined category. It is the opposite of endogamy, marriage within a specific group. The boundary of the group may be defined by kinship, as in clan exogamy, or by other criteria such as locality, religion, or caste-like divisions of society.

Kinship exogamy

In many societies, kin groups such as clans and lineages are strictly exogamous: members must find spouses from other clans, a rule often bound up with the incest taboo. In his study of kinship, Claude Lévi-Strauss argued in The Elementary Structures of Kinship (1949) that the prohibition on marrying within the group, and the resulting exchange of spouses between groups, is the foundation of social alliance and of culture itself. Exogamous moiety systems, in which a society is divided into two halves that must intermarry, are found among indigenous peoples of Australia, the Americas, and elsewhere.

Other forms

Surname exogamy historically barred marriage between people sharing a clan name in China and Korea, a rule that survived in South Korean law until a constitutional ruling in 1997 led to reform. Linguistic and religious communities may also practise exogamy, sometimes as a matter of policy, as with interfaith marriage, and sometimes in tension with rules of religious endogamy. In biology, the term is also used for outcrossing — mating between unrelated individuals — which increases genetic diversity.
