The Great Hanshin earthquake (阪神・淡路大震災, Hanshin-Awaji Daishinsai), also known as the Kobe earthquake, occurred on 17 January 1995 at 05:46 JST in the southern part of Hyōgo Prefecture, Japan. Measuring 6.9 on the moment magnitude scale (7.3 on the Japanese scale), its focus was 17 km beneath northern Awaji Island, just across the Akashi Strait from the port city of Kobe. It was the deadliest earthquake in Japan since the 1923 Great Kantō earthquake.

Damage

The shaking devastated Kobe and neighbouring cities including Ashiya, Nishinomiya and Takarazuka. Approximately 200,000 buildings were damaged or destroyed; large sections of the elevated Hanshin Expressway toppled sideways, and the port of Kobe — then one of the world's busiest — was crippled. Fires broke out in wooden neighbourhoods such as Nagata ward, burning for days. More than 6,400 people died, most of them in Kobe, many crushed in older wooden houses with heavy tile roofs.

Response and legacy

The national government's slow initial response was widely criticised, while the massive turnout of ordinary volunteers led 1995 to be dubbed "the first year of volunteerism" in Japan. The disaster triggered sweeping revisions to Japanese building codes, seismic retrofitting of expressways and railways, and reforms of disaster-management law. Economic losses were estimated at around ¥10 trillion (about $100 billion), making it at the time the costliest natural disaster in history. An annual memorial, the Kobe Luminarie light festival, commemorates the victims each December.
