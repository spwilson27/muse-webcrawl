Boolean algebra is a branch of algebra and mathematical logic in which the values of variables are the truth values true and false, usually denoted 1 and 0. It was introduced by George Boole in his works The Mathematical Analysis of Logic (1847) and An Investigation of the Laws of Thought (1854), and it forms the mathematical foundation of digital electronics and computer science.

Operations

The three basic operations are conjunction (AND, written x ∧ y), disjunction (OR, x ∨ y), and negation (NOT, ¬x). Unlike ordinary algebra, addition and multiplication do not behave the same way: in Boolean algebra x + x = x, and both operations distribute over each other. Derived operations include XOR (exclusive or), NAND, and NOR — either of the latter two alone suffices to express all Boolean functions (functional completeness).

Laws

Boolean algebra obeys laws including commutativity, associativity, distributivity, identity (x ∧ 1 = x, x ∨ 0 = x), complementation (x ∧ ¬x = 0, x ∨ ¬x = 1), idempotence, absorption, and De Morgan's laws: ¬(x ∧ y) = ¬x ∨ ¬y and ¬(x ∨ y) = ¬x ∧ ¬y. Abstractly, any structure satisfying these axioms — such as the subsets of a set under union, intersection, and complement — is called a Boolean algebra.

Applications

In 1937 Claude Shannon showed in his master's thesis that Boolean algebra describes switching circuits, laying the groundwork for digital circuit design. Boolean expressions are evaluated by logic gates, minimised with techniques such as Karnaugh maps, and used throughout computer science in conditional statements, database queries, and search operators.
