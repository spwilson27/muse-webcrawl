Jean Piaget

Jean William Fritz Piaget (9 August 1896 – 16 September 1980) was a Swiss psychologist known for his work on child development. His theory of cognitive development and his programme of "genetic epistemology" transformed the study of how children think and learn, and he founded the International Centre for Genetic Epistemology in Geneva in 1955.

Early life

Piaget was born in Neuchâtel, in French-speaking Switzerland. A precocious naturalist, he published a paper on an albino sparrow at the age of eleven and worked as a malacologist while still a schoolboy, earning a doctorate in natural sciences from the University of Neuchâtel in 1918. His interest shifted to psychology after work in Paris with standardised intelligence tests, where the wrong answers of children — and their systematic patterns — fascinated him.

Theory of cognitive development

Piaget proposed that children construct mental models of the world ("schemas") through the processes of assimilation and accommodation, and that their thinking passes through four qualitatively distinct stages: the sensorimotor stage (birth to about 2), the preoperational stage (2 to 7), the concrete operational stage (7 to 11) and the formal operational stage (from about 11). His observations of his own three children, and clinical interviews with many others, underpin classics such as The Language and Thought of the Child (1923) and The Origins of Intelligence in Children (1936).

Legacy

Piaget's constructivism deeply influenced education, encouraging active, discovery-based learning. Later research qualified some of his claims — infants and young children often show competencies earlier than his stages allowed — but his questions and methods remain foundational in developmental psychology. He died in Geneva in 1980.
