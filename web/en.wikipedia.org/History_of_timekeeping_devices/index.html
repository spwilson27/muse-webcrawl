History of timekeeping devices

The history of timekeeping devices — instruments for measuring time — spans sundials and water clocks of the ancient world, the mechanical clocks of medieval Europe, precision marine chronometers, and today's atomic clocks that define the second.

Ancient timekeepers

Shadow clocks and obelisks in Egypt (c. 1500 BC), sundials, and water clocks (clepsydrae) in Egypt, Babylon, China, and Greece divided day and night into hours. Ctesibius of Alexandria (3rd century BC) built elaborate regulated water clocks. Incense clocks and candle clocks served in Asia and Europe; the hourglass spread from the 8th–14th centuries, notably at sea.

Mechanical clocks

Weight-driven mechanical clocks with verge escapements appeared in European monasteries and towns in the late 13th century. Giovanni de' Dondi's Astrarium (1364) exemplified astronomical clockwork. Spring-driven clocks and portable watches emerged in the 15th–16th centuries (Peter Henlein's "Nuremberg eggs"). Christiaan Huygens's pendulum clock (1656) and anchor escapement (c. 1670) dramatically improved accuracy; John Harrison's marine chronometers (H4, 1761) solved the longitude problem.

Electric, quartz, and atomic time

The 19th century standardized time zones around railway schedules. Electric clocks, Warren Marrison's quartz clock (1927), and wristwatch quartz movements (Seiko Astron, 1969) successively improved precision and miniaturization. The first atomic clock (1955, NPL, London) led to the 1967 redefinition of the SI second by caesium-133 transitions. Today optical lattice clocks and satellite systems such as GPS deliver nanosecond-level global time.
