Na-Dene (also Nadene, Na-Dené) is a family of Native American languages that includes the Athabaskan languages, Eyak, and Tlingit. It is spoken by indigenous peoples in Alaska, western Canada, and parts of the southwestern United States, including the Navajo and Apache peoples. The most widely spoken Na-Dene language today is Navajo, with around 170,000 speakers.

The family was first proposed by Edward Sapir in 1915. In its classical formulation it also included Haida, but most linguists now exclude Haida for lack of evidence. Sapir took the name from Haida na "house" and Athabaskan dene "people".

Classification

  • Tlingit — spoken in southeast Alaska and parts of Canada
  • Athabaskan–Eyak
    • Eyak (extinct as a first language in 2008)
    • Athabaskan languages — Northern (e.g. Dene, Gwich'in), Pacific Coast (e.g. Hupa), and Southern (Apachean: Navajo, Western Apache, Chiricahua, Jicarilla, Lipan, Plains Apache)

Proposed external relations

The Dene–Yeniseian hypothesis, proposed by Edward Vajda in 2008, argues that Na-Dene is related to the Yeniseian languages of Siberia (of which Ket is the only survivor), based on systematic correspondences in verb morphology and phonology. If correct, it would be the first demonstrated language family linking the Old and New Worlds. The hypothesis has received cautious support from a number of specialists but is not universally accepted. Broader proposals linking Na-Dene to Sino-Tibetan ("Dene–Caucasian") remain unproven.

Characteristics

Na-Dene languages are famous for their complex, templatic verb morphology: a single verb may consist of many prefixes marking subject, object, aspect, mode and adverbial notions, built around a stem whose shape varies with aspect. Many are tonal, and Athabaskan languages use noun classifiers reflecting the shape and consistency of objects handled.
