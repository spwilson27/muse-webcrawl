Solid-state battery

A solid-state battery is a battery technology that uses solid electrodes and a solid electrolyte, instead of the liquid or polymer gel electrolytes found in lithium-ion or lithium polymer batteries. Materials proposed as solid electrolytes include ceramics (e.g., oxides and sulfides) and solid polymers. Solid-state batteries have applications in pacemakers, RFID, wearable devices, and, increasingly, electric vehicles.

Because solid electrolytes are generally non-flammable and can permit the use of lithium-metal anodes, solid-state designs promise higher energy density and improved safety compared with conventional lithium-ion cells. However, challenges such as interfacial resistance, dendrite growth through ceramic electrolytes, manufacturing cost, and the brittleness of ceramic materials have delayed mass production.

History

Solid electrolytes were studied as early as the 1830s, when Michael Faraday observed ionic conductivity in solid lead fluoride and silver sulfide. Practical thin-film solid-state batteries emerged in the late 20th century for medical and microelectronic devices. In the 2010s and 2020s, major carmakers and battery firms — including Toyota, Samsung, and QuantumScape — invested heavily in bringing solid-state cells to electric vehicles.

Materials

Leading solid electrolyte families include garnet-type oxides (LLZO), sulfide glasses and argyrodites, NASICON-type phosphates, and perovskite-type titanates. Research focuses on raising ionic conductivity to liquid-electrolyte levels while maintaining stable interfaces with electrodes.
