Ancient Agora of Athens

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Ancient Agora of Athens (Greek: Ἀρχαία Ἀγορά τῆς Ἀθήνας) is the best-known example of an ancient Greek agora, located to the northwest of the Acropolis in Athens, Greece. It was the centre of the city's public life — its marketplace, and the heart of its political, judicial, commercial, social and religious activity.

History

The area was in use from Neolithic times, and burials date to the Late Bronze Age. It became the civic centre of Athens in the 6th century BC, under the reforms associated with Solon and later Cleisthenes, when public buildings began to line its edges. The Agora reached its monumental height in the Classical period of the 5th century BC, the age of Pericles, and continued to be embellished under the Hellenistic kingdoms and the Roman Empire. The Herulian invasion of AD 267 destroyed many of its buildings, and the area was gradually abandoned in late antiquity, later covered by a residential neighbourhood of Byzantine and Ottoman Athens.

Principal buildings

  • Temple of Hephaestus — the best-preserved Doric temple in Greece, built c. 449–415 BC on the Kolonos Agoraios hill.
  • Stoa of Attalos — a two-storey Hellenistic stoa, gift of King Attalos II of Pergamon (159–138 BC); reconstructed in the 1950s and now the Museum of the Ancient Agora.
  • Bouleuterion — meeting place of the Boule, the council of 500.
  • Tholos — circular building where the prytaneis, the presiding committee of the council, dined.
  • Odeon of Agrippa — a Roman concert hall in the centre of the square.
  • Metroön and Temple of Apollo Patroos — shrines on the west side, the Metroön housing the state archives.

Excavations

Excavations by the American School of Classical Studies at Athens began in 1931 and required the demolition of more than 300 modern houses. Work has continued, with breaks, ever since, revealing roads such as the Panathenaic Way, boundary stones, law courts, the mint, and thousands of inscriptions, ostraka and everyday objects that illuminate Athenian democracy.
