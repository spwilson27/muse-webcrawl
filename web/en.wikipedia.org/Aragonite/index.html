Aragonite is a carbonate mineral and one of the three most common naturally occurring crystal forms of calcium carbonate, CaCO3 (the others being calcite and vaterite). It is formed by biological and physical processes, including precipitation from marine and freshwater environments. The mineral is named after Molina de Aragón in Spain, near where it was first described in 1797.

Properties

Aragonite crystallises in the orthorhombic system, often forming columnar or acicular (needle-like) crystals and cyclic twins that give pseudo-hexagonal forms. It is slightly harder and denser than calcite. Aragonite is thermodynamically unstable at standard temperature and pressure and tends to convert to calcite over geological time scales, so aragonite fossils older than the Carboniferous are rare.

Occurrence and biological role

The shells of most molluscs are composed largely of aragonite, as is the material of pearls and mother-of-pearl (nacre). The skeletons of warm-water reef-building corals are aragonitic, making them vulnerable to ocean acidification, which reduces the aragonite saturation state of seawater. Cave formations (speleothems), hot-spring deposits and some sedimentary oolites also consist of aragonite.
