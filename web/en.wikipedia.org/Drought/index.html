Drought

A drought is a period of drier-than-normal conditions that results in water-related problems. When rainfall is less than normal for a period of weeks to years, streamflows decline, water levels in lakes and reservoirs fall, and the depth to water in wells increases. If dry weather persists and water-supply problems develop, the dry period can become a drought.

Types

Experts distinguish several kinds of drought. Meteorological drought is defined by the degree of dryness compared to a normal amount of precipitation over time. Agricultural drought links meteorological conditions to impacts on crops and soil moisture. Hydrological drought concerns reduced streamflow, reservoir and groundwater levels, and may lag months behind the rainfall deficit. Socioeconomic drought arises when water demand exceeds supply for human and economic needs.

Causes and measurement

Droughts arise from persistent atmospheric patterns — high-pressure systems, shifts in sea-surface temperatures such as El Niño and La Niña, and reduced moisture transport — and are worsened by high temperatures and human water demand. Severity is tracked with indices such as the Palmer Drought Severity Index and the Standardized Precipitation Index, and by monitoring agencies such as the U.S. Drought Monitor.

Impacts

Consequences include crop failure and famine, livestock deaths, wildfires, dust storms, reservoir depletion, ecosystem damage and water conflicts. Historic examples include the American Dust Bowl of the 1930s and the prolonged Australian Millennium Drought (roughly 1997–2009). Climate change is expected to intensify drought in many subtropical regions.
