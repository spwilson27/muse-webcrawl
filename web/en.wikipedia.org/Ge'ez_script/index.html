The Ge'ez script (Ge'ez: ግዕዝ, Gəʽəz), also called the Ethiopic script, is an abugida used to write several languages of Ethiopia and Eritrea. It originated as an abjad (consonant-only alphabet) to write the Ge'ez language, historically the liturgical language of the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church; the addition of systematic vowel markings in the 4th century CE converted it into an abugida. It remains the writing system for Amharic (the working language of the Ethiopian federal government), Tigrinya and Tigre, among others.

Structure

The script is written in syllable characters called fidäl. Each of about 26–33 base consonants appears in seven forms ("orders") according to the following vowel — ä, u, i, a, e, ə, o — producing a core chart of over 200 signs, plus labialised variants and additional characters used by particular languages (Amharic uses 34 base characters, 238 syllable signs). Unlike most Semitic scripts it is written left to right. Words were traditionally separated by two vertical dots (፡) and sentences by ።; modern punctuation has largely adopted Western forms alongside Ethiopic marks.

History

The script developed from the ancient South Arabian alphabet, brought across the Red Sea in the first millennium BCE, and appears in inscriptions of the pre-Aksumite and Aksumite kingdoms. Vowel notation emerged around the reign of Ezana (4th century CE), making Ge'ez one of the earliest scripts to mark vowels systematically. It survives in computing in the Ethiopic Unicode block and on standard Ethiopian keyboards and typewriters developed in the 20th century.
