The Battle of Megiddo was fought between 19 and 25 September 1918 and was the climactic battle of the Sinai and Palestine campaign of World War I. The British Empire's Egyptian Expeditionary Force under General Edmund Allenby shattered the Ottoman Yildirim Army Group, opening the way to Damascus and the end of Ottoman rule in the Levant.

Background

After capturing Jerusalem in December 1917, Allenby's offensive paused while troops were diverted to France following the German spring offensives of 1918. Reinforced by divisions from the Indian Army, Allenby spent the summer deceiving the Ottoman command, led by the German general Otto Liman von Sanders, into expecting the main blow in the Jordan Valley, while he secretly concentrated opposite the coastal plain of Sharon.

The breakthrough

At dawn on 19 September 1918, after an intense fifteen-minute artillery barrage, British and Indian infantry of the XXI Corps broke through the Ottoman lines on the coast. Through the gap poured the Desert Mounted Corps — Australian, New Zealand, British and Indian cavalry — which galloped north across the Plain of Sharon and through the Musmus Pass to seize the road hubs of Afulah and Beisan behind the Ottoman front. Royal Air Force and Australian Flying Corps aircraft bombed the Seventh Army's headquarters at Nablus and destroyed retreating columns in the Wadi el Fara.

The encirclement

Within days the Ottoman Seventh and Eighth Armies were effectively destroyed, with tens of thousands taken prisoner. The Fourth Army east of the Jordan was driven back toward Amman and then Damascus, harassed by Arab forces of the Arab Revolt operating with T. E. Lawrence. Allenby's troops entered Damascus on 1 October; Aleppo fell on 25 October, and the Ottoman Empire signed the Armistice of Mudros on 30 October 1918.

Legacy

Megiddo is often cited as one of the most complete victories of the war, achieved with relatively few Allied casualties — under 6,000 — against Ottoman losses exceeding 25,000 killed or wounded and some 75,000 prisoners. The battle's name recalls the ancient site of Megiddo, near which the fighting's northern phase unfolded; Allenby took his viscountcy title from it.
