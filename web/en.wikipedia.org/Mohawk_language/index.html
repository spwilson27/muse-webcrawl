Mohawk (endonym: Kanien'kéha, "[language] of the Flint [Place]") is an Iroquoian language spoken by the Mohawk people (Kanien'kehá:ka) of the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois Confederacy), in communities in present-day Ontario, Quebec, and northern New York, including Kahnawake, Kanesatake, Akwesasne (St. Regis), Tyendinaga, and Six Nations of the Grand River.

Characteristics

Mohawk is a polysynthetic language: complex words built from many morphemes can express what English requires a whole sentence to say, making extensive use of pronominal prefixes that mark agent and patient, and of noun incorporation. The language has a small consonant inventory and a distinctive pitch/stress accent. Its three main modern dialect areas are western (Six Nations/Tyendinaga), central (Akwesasne), and eastern (Kahnawake/Kanesatake).

History and revitalisation

Fluency declined sharply during the 19th and 20th centuries under pressure from English and French schooling, and Mohawk is classified as endangered. Since the 1970s, however, community-led revitalisation has produced immersion schools (such as the Akwesasne Freedom School and Kahnawake's Kanien'kehá:ka Onkwawén:na), adult immersion programs that train new speakers, standardized orthographies, dictionaries, and media. These programs are frequently cited as models in Indigenous language revitalisation.
