Acritarch

Acritarchs are small organic microfossils, known from approximately 1,800 million years ago to the present. The term was coined in 1963 for organic-walled microfossils of uncertain biological affinity — a "wastebasket" grouping for any small, acid-resistant fossil structure that cannot be assigned to another group. Their diversity increased greatly during the Cambrian explosion.

Classification and affinities

Acritarchs most likely represent resting cysts or other life-cycle stages of various kinds of organisms, especially eukaryotic algae. Many forms resemble the cysts of dinoflagellates, while others may be related to green algae or even to the eggs of early animals. Because the group is defined by exclusion, individual acritarch genera are regularly reassigned as their affinities become clearer.

Occurrence and importance

Acritarchs are extracted from sedimentary rocks by dissolving the rock in hydrofluoric acid. They are abundant in Proterozoic and Paleozoic rocks and are important for biostratigraphy — dating and correlating rock layers, especially in petroleum exploration — and for tracing the early evolution of eukaryotes. Their abundance and diversity dropped during the end-Devonian extinctions, and they never regained their earlier prominence.
