The Vanity of Human Wishes: The Tenth Satire of Juvenal Imitated is a poem by the English writer Samuel Johnson, written in heroic couplets and published in January 1749. It is an imitation of the Tenth Satire of the Roman poet Juvenal, and is widely regarded as one of Johnson's finest works and one of the great poems of the eighteenth century. Although it sold modestly at first, Johnson considered it his greatest work of poetry.

Overview

Where Juvenal surveyed the follies of Roman ambition, Johnson surveys the whole of human history, showing how the universal desires — for wealth, power, military glory, political influence, learning, beauty, and long life — end invariably in disappointment or destruction. Its celebrated opening bids the reader "Let Observation with extensive View, / Survey Mankind from China to Peru." Historical examples include Wolsey, Charles XII of Sweden, and the scholar whose life ends in madness or penury.

Conclusion

Unlike Juvenal's near-despairing close, Johnson ends on a Christian and Stoic note: since earthly wishes are vain, the wise person asks instead for the goods that heaven can supply — "a healthful Mind, / Obedient Passions, and a Will resign'd" — concluding that celestial wisdom "calms the Mind, / And makes the Happiness she does not find."

Reception

The poem consolidated Johnson's reputation after London (1738). The publisher Robert Dodsley paid Johnson fifteen guineas for the copyright. Walter Scott admired it greatly, and T. S. Eliot praised its moral force. Its portraits and gnomic couplets, such as "Slow rises Worth, by Poverty deprest," have been quoted ever since.
