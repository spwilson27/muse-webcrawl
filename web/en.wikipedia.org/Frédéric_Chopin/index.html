Frédéric François Chopin (born Fryderyk Franciszek Chopin; 1 March 1810 – 17 October 1849) was a Polish composer and virtuoso pianist of the Romantic period who wrote primarily for solo piano. He has maintained worldwide renown as a leading musician of his era, one whose "poetic genius was based on a professional technique that was without equal in his generation".

Chopin was born in Żelazowa Wola in the Duchy of Warsaw and grew up in Warsaw, which in 1815 became part of Congress Poland. A child prodigy, he completed his musical education and composed his earlier works in Warsaw before leaving Poland at the age of 20, less than a month before the outbreak of the November 1830 Uprising. At 21, he settled in Paris. Thereafter — in the last 18 years of his life — he gave only 30 public performances, preferring the more intimate atmosphere of the salon.

Life

Chopin's father, Nicolas Chopin, was a Frenchman who had emigrated to Poland in 1787 and worked as a tutor and teacher of French. His mother, Justyna Krzyżanowska, was Polish. The young Fryderyk showed remarkable talent early: he began piano lessons at six and was composing and performing in public by the age of seven. His first published work, a polonaise in G minor, appeared when he was seven years old.

He studied at the Warsaw Conservatory under Józef Elsner. In 1829 he made successful debuts in Vienna, and in 1830 he left Poland for good, settling in Paris in September 1831. In Paris he was welcomed into aristocratic and artistic circles, formed friendships with Franz Liszt, Hector Berlioz, and Eugène Delacroix, and earned his living largely by teaching piano and selling his compositions.

From 1838 to 1847 Chopin was in a relationship with the French writer George Sand (Amantine Lucile Aurore Dupin). Their winters together on Mallorca and summers at Sand's estate at Nohant were among his most productive periods, though the relationship ended acrimoniously in 1847. His health, fragile throughout his life — he suffered from what is generally believed to have been tuberculosis — declined sharply thereafter. He died in Paris on 17 October 1849, aged 39. His body is buried at Père Lachaise Cemetery, while his heart, preserved in alcohol, was carried back to Warsaw and is immured in a pillar of the Church of the Holy Cross.

Music

All of Chopin's known works involve the piano, most of them for solo piano, though he also wrote two piano concertos, chamber works, and songs to Polish texts. His compositions are renowned for their melodic beauty, expressive depth, and technical demands. He invented the instrumental ballade and made major innovations to the piano sonata, mazurka, waltz, nocturne, polonaise, étude, impromptu, scherzo, and prelude.

Among his best-known works are the four Ballades, the Barcarolle in F-sharp major, the Fantaisie in F minor, the "Heroic" Polonaise in A-flat major, the "Funeral March" Sonata (No. 2 in B-flat minor), 21 Nocturnes, 24 Preludes, and 27 Études. His mazurkas and polonaises draw on Polish folk dance rhythms and are closely associated with the Polish national cause in exile.

Legacy

Chopin's music remains among the most beloved in the piano repertoire, and his influence on later composers — including Liszt, Wagner, Debussy, and Rachmaninoff — was profound. The International Chopin Piano Competition, held in Warsaw every five years since 1927, is one of the world's most prestigious piano competitions.
