Paracetamol poisoning

Paracetamol poisoning, also known as acetaminophen poisoning, is caused by excessive use of the medication paracetamol (acetaminophen). Most people experience few or non-specific symptoms in the first 24 hours following overdose. These may include feeling tired, abdominal pain, or nausea. This is typically followed by a couple of days without any symptoms, after which yellowish skin, blood clotting problems, and confusion may occur as a result of liver failure. Additional complications may include kidney failure, pancreatitis, low blood sugar, and lactic acidosis. If death does not occur, people tend to recover fully over a couple of weeks.

Signs and symptoms

The clinical course classically divides into four phases. Phase 1 (first 24 hours) features malaise, nausea, vomiting and sweating, though many patients are asymptomatic. Phase 2 (24–72 hours) is deceptively quiet, with right upper quadrant pain and rising liver enzymes. Phase 3 (72–96 hours) is the peak of hepatotoxicity, with jaundice, coagulopathy, encephalopathy and sometimes renal failure. Phase 4 (4 days to 2 weeks) is recovery in survivors.

Cause and mechanism

At therapeutic doses, paracetamol is mainly conjugated in the liver to harmless products; a small fraction is oxidized by cytochrome P450 to the toxic metabolite NAPQI (N-acetyl-p-benzoquinone imine), which is normally detoxified by glutathione. In overdose the conjugation pathways saturate, NAPQI production rises, glutathione stores are depleted, and free NAPQI binds to hepatocyte proteins, causing centrilobular necrosis. Risk is increased by chronic alcohol use, fasting, malnutrition and certain enzyme-inducing drugs.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis rests on the history of ingestion and a serum paracetamol level. For single acute ingestions, the level is plotted on the Rumack–Matthew nomogram (measured at least 4 hours post-ingestion) to estimate the risk of hepatotoxicity and guide antidote use. Liver function tests, coagulation studies (INR), creatinine and blood gases assess severity.

Treatment

Acetylcysteine (NAC) is the specific antidote: it replenishes glutathione and directly detoxifies NAPQI. It is most effective within 8 hours of ingestion but benefits patients even later, including those with established liver failure. Activated charcoal may be given within the first hour or two if the airway is protected. Patients with fulminant hepatic failure may require intensive care and, in the worst cases, liver transplantation, with prognosis assessed by criteria such as the King's College criteria.

Epidemiology

Paracetamol is one of the most common causes of poisoning worldwide and a leading cause of acute liver failure in the United States and United Kingdom. Many cases are unintentional, arising from taking multiple paracetamol-containing products simultaneously. Pack-size restrictions introduced in the UK in 1998 were followed by reductions in severe overdoses and deaths.
