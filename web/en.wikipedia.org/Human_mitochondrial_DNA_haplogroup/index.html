Human mitochondrial DNA haplogroup

Human mtDNA haplogroups
Definition: Lineages defined by mitochondrial DNA mutations
Inheritance: Matrilineal (mother to children)
Root: Haplogroup L / "Mitochondrial Eve"
Major branches: L, M, N, R and descendants (H, U, J, T, K, A, B, X...)

A human mitochondrial DNA haplogroup is a haplogroup defined by differences in human mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA). Because mtDNA is inherited almost exclusively from the mother and does not recombine, haplogroups trace direct maternal ancestry and are used by geneticists to reconstruct the deep history and migration of human populations.

Phylogeny

All living people's mtDNA descends from a single woman, dubbed "Mitochondrial Eve", who lived in Africa roughly 150,000–200,000 years ago. Her lineage — macro-haplogroup L, with branches L0–L6 — remains most diverse in Africa. Two lineages that left Africa, M and N (the latter giving rise to R), founded all non-African maternal lines: among their descendants are haplogroup H and U in Europe and West Asia, A, B, C, D and X in the Americas and East Asia, and J, T and K in Europe and the Near East.

Applications

MtDNA haplogroups are used in population genetics, archaeogenetics (including sequencing of ancient remains such as Ötzi and Cheddar Man), forensic identification, and consumer genetic genealogy. Because mtDNA mutates at a relatively measurable rate, differences serve as a molecular clock. Limitations include its single-lineage view — it records only one ancestral line among thousands — and the small size of the mitochondrial genome (~16,569 base pairs), which limits resolution compared with whole-genome data. The parallel paternal system uses Y-chromosome DNA haplogroups.
