Type: Animal fibre
Source: Sheep (mainly), also goats, alpacas, rabbits
Uses: Clothing, carpets, upholstery, insulation

Wool

Wool is the textile fibre obtained from sheep and certain other mammals, including goats (cashmere and mohair), alpacas, and rabbits (angora). It consists mainly of the protein keratin and is distinguished by its crimp, elasticity, warmth, and ability to absorb moisture without feeling wet.

Properties and production

Wool fibres have overlapping scales and a natural crimp that traps air, giving insulation. Wool can absorb up to about 30% of its weight in water, is naturally somewhat flame-resistant, and tends to felt when agitated in water and heat. Sheep are usually shorn once a year; the fleece is skirted, scoured to remove lanolin (wool grease), carded, and spun into yarn. Fine Merino wool from Australia — the world's largest producer — commands premium prices for apparel, while coarser wools go to carpets.

History

Sheep were among the first animals domesticated, and wool textiles date back thousands of years; wool was a foundation of medieval English and Flemish trade and of later textile industrialisation. Although synthetic fibres displaced wool in much of the clothing market in the 20th century, wool remains valued for garments, blankets, carpets and insulation, with modern concerns including animal welfare practices such as mulesing.
