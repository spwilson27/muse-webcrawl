Continuously variable transmission

A continuously variable transmission (CVT) is an automatic transmission that can change seamlessly through an infinite number of effective gear ratios between maximum and minimum values, rather than stepping through a fixed set of gears. This allows the engine to operate at its most efficient or most powerful speed for a given vehicle speed.

Types

The most common design uses two variable-diameter pulleys connected by a steel push-belt or chain: as one pulley widens the other narrows, changing the ratio continuously. Other types include toroidal (roller-based) CVTs, hydrostatic transmissions used in tractors and machinery, and the electronic e-CVT power-split devices used in hybrid vehicles such as the Toyota Prius.

History and use

Leonardo da Vinci sketched a stepless transmission in 1490; practical automotive CVTs arrived with DAF's Variomatic in 1958. Modern belt CVTs are widespread in small cars and scooters from manufacturers such as Nissan, Honda, Subaru and Toyota, prized for fuel economy but sometimes criticised for “rubber-band” engine drone and limits on torque capacity.

Advantages and drawbacks

Benefits include smooth acceleration, improved efficiency and fewer moving ratios to manage. Drawbacks include belt durability concerns, higher cost, and a driving feel some motorists dislike — leading some makers to program simulated gear steps.
