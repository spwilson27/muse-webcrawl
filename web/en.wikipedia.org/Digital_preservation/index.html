Digital preservation

Digital preservation is the series of managed activities necessary to ensure continued access to digital materials for as long as necessary. It involves the policies, strategies and actions that ensure access to digital content over time, countering the threats of media failure, technological obsolescence, and format obsolescence. Digital preservation is carried out by libraries, archives, museums, publishers, and other memory institutions, as well as by individuals managing their own digital files.

Challenges

Unlike physical materials, digital objects depend on a chain of hardware, operating systems, software, and file formats that change rapidly. Storage media degrade or become unreadable as drives and interfaces disappear; proprietary formats may become unsupported; and links and references on the web decay. Preservation therefore requires active management rather than passive storage.

Strategies

Common strategies include refreshing (copying data to new media), migration (converting files to newer formats), emulation (recreating the original hardware and software environment), and encapsulation (keeping digital objects together with the metadata needed to interpret them). The Open Archival Information System (OAIS) reference model, standardised as ISO 14721, provides a common framework for archival systems. Fixity checking with checksums verifies that files have not changed, and approaches such as the LOCKSS programme ("Lots of Copies Keep Stuff Safe") preserve content through distributed redundant copies.

Web archiving

Web archiving is the process of collecting and preserving portions of the World Wide Web, practised at large scale by the Internet Archive's Wayback Machine and by national libraries under legal deposit schemes. Born-digital publications, research data, and records all present distinct preservation requirements addressed by standards for metadata such as PREMIS.
