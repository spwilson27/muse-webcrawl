The watt (symbol: W) is the unit of power or radiant flux in the International System of Units (SI), equal to 1 joule per second or 1 kg·m²·s⁻³. It is used to quantify the rate at which energy is transferred or converted. The unit is named after the Scottish inventor James Watt (1736–1819), whose improvements to the steam engine were fundamental to the Industrial Revolution; it was recognised as an SI unit in 1960.

Definition

One watt is the power produced when work is done, or energy expended, at a rate of one joule per second. In electrical terms, one watt is the power dissipated when a current of one ampere flows through a potential difference of one volt (W = V × A). Common multiples include the kilowatt (10³ W), megawatt (10⁶ W) and gigawatt (10⁹ W); submultiples include the milliwatt and microwatt.

Uses

Electrical appliances are rated in watts: a typical LED light bulb uses 5–15 W, a microwave oven around 1,000 W, and a large power station may generate thousands of megawatts. In audio and radio engineering, power output and transmitter strength are quoted in watts (or in dBW, decibels relative to one watt). The watt should not be confused with the watt-hour, a unit of energy equal to one watt sustained for one hour; electricity consumption is usually billed in kilowatt-hours.

History

The name was proposed by C. William Siemens in 1882 and adopted at the second Congress of the British Association for the Advancement of Science in 1889, in honour of James Watt. Watt himself had popularised the "horsepower" as a measure of engine output; one mechanical horsepower is approximately 745.7 watts.
