Liwan

A liwan (Arabic: ليوان, from Persian eyvān) is a long, narrow-fronted vaulted hall or room in Levantine and broader Middle Eastern architecture, walled on three sides and open to a courtyard on the fourth. The term is closely related to the Persian iwan, and in the Levant it came to designate the central reception space of traditional courtyard houses.

Form and function

The liwan typically sits on the side of the courtyard opposite the entrance and is raised slightly above the courtyard level. Its open front is often framed by a wide arch, sometimes flanked by two smaller closed rooms called qubba. Because it is shaded, open to the air and oriented to catch breezes, the liwan served as the main living and reception area of the house in warm months, where the family sat, ate and received guests. Its floor was often divided into a lower entry zone and a raised sitting area (the tazar) along the back and side walls.

Regional use

Liwan houses are characteristic of Lebanon, Syria, Palestine and Jordan from the late Ottoman period into the 20th century. In Lebanese architecture, the "central hall house" or liwan house became a dominant vernacular type, frequently decorated with carved stone arches, marble flooring and painted wooden ceilings imported from Damascus workshops.
