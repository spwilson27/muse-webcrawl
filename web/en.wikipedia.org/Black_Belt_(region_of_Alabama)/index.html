The Black Belt is a region of the U.S. state of Alabama characterized by a band of dark, fertile soil stretching across the south-central part of the state. The name originally referred to the region's rich, black prairie soil, formed from the underlying Selma Chalk limestone deposits. The term has since come to describe both the geological region and its distinctive cultural and demographic character.

History

The fertile soil made the Black Belt one of the most productive cotton-growing regions of the antebellum South. During the early nineteenth century, enslavers brought tens of thousands of enslaved African Americans to work the cotton plantations, making the region's population majority Black — a demographic legacy that persists today. The region was economically and politically dominant within Alabama before the Civil War.

Civil rights movement

The Black Belt was a center of the civil rights movement. Selma, in Dallas County, was the starting point of the 1965 Selma to Montgomery marches, and nearby Lowndes County was an early site of Black voter registration organizing and the origin of the Lowndes County Freedom Organization, whose black panther emblem influenced later movements.

Economy and demographics

Following the decline of cotton agriculture, the region shifted toward cattle grazing, catfish farming, and timber. Many Black Belt counties remain among the poorest in Alabama and the United States, with persistent challenges in infrastructure, education, and healthcare, alongside a rich tradition of African American culture, music, and cuisine.
