Tower of Hanoi

A five-disk Tower of Hanoi

The Tower of Hanoi (also called the Tower of Brahma or the Lucas Tower) is a mathematical game or puzzle consisting of three rods and a number of disks of various diameters, which can slide onto any rod. The puzzle begins with the disks stacked on one rod in order of decreasing size, the smallest at the top, making a conical shape. The objective is to move the entire stack to another rod, obeying three rules: only one disk may be moved at a time; each move consists of taking the upper disk from one stack and placing it on top of another stack or on an empty rod; and no disk may be placed on top of a disk that is smaller than it.

History

The puzzle was invented by the French mathematician Édouard Lucas in 1883. It is accompanied by a legend about a temple in India (sometimes located in Hanoi, Vietnam) where priests move 64 golden disks according to the same rules; when the last move is completed, the world will end. With the minimal solution requiring 264 − 1 = 18,446,744,073,709,551,615 moves, at one move per second the task would take more than 585 billion years.

Solution

The minimal number of moves required to solve a tower with n disks is 2n − 1. The puzzle has a simple recursive solution: to move n disks, move the top n − 1 to a spare rod, move the largest disk to the target, then move the n − 1 onto it. An iterative solution alternates moves of the smallest disk cyclically with the only available non-smallest move. The puzzle is a classic teaching example of recursion in computer science and is related to the binary reflected Gray code and the Sierpiński triangle.

Variants

Variants include the Reve's puzzle (four rods), for which the Frame–Stewart conjecture gives the presumed optimal counts, and magnetic and coloured-disk versions that restrict legal moves.
