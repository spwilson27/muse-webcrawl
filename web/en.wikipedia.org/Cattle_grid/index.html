Cattle grid

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A cattle grid — also known as a stock grid in Australia and New Zealand, a cattle guard in American English, and a Texas gate in Canada — is a barrier used to prevent livestock from leaving or entering a fenced area along a road, while allowing vehicles and pedestrians to pass without opening a gate. It consists of a set of parallel metal bars or pipes laid over a shallow pit in the roadway, spaced so that animals cannot comfortably place their hooves between them and perceive the footing as unstable or see a drop beneath.

Design and operation

A typical grid is 2–4 metres long in the direction of travel, built of steel rails or tubes welded to side frames and set into a concrete or steel pit. The rounded, widely spaced bars present no secure foothold to hooved animals, which generally refuse to attempt the crossing; many grids also rely on animals' poor depth perception of the pit and bars. Most installations include a side gate for pedestrians, cyclists, horse riders and the occasional animal that must be herded across, since grids can trap or injure hooves. "Virtual" cattle grids — painted stripes, sometimes with a switchable audio/roller unit — are occasionally used where physical grids are impractical.

History and use

Patents for cattle guards date to the 19th-century American railroad era, when devices were needed to keep cattle off tracks at fence crossings. On the open ranges and moorlands of the US, Britain, Australia and New Zealand, grids replaced gates on public roads crossing unfenced grazing land, saving drivers from repeatedly opening gates. They are standard on British moorland roads (Dartmoor, the New Forest, the Pennines) and on Australian outback stations.

Limitations

Sheep and deer sometimes learn to cross or jump narrow grids, and grids must be wider to deter deer. Snow, ice or accumulated debris in the pit can fill the gaps and defeat the barrier. Cyclists and motorcyclists treat the bars with caution, particularly in wet weather.
