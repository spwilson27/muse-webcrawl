Metaethics

Metaethics is the branch of ethics that seeks to understand the nature of ethical properties, statements, attitudes, and judgments. Whereas normative ethics asks which actions are right or wrong, and applied ethics addresses specific moral issues, metaethics investigates what morality itself is: what it means to say an action is right, whether moral statements can be true or false, whether moral facts exist, and how moral judgments motivate action.


Metaethics examines the foundations of moral judgment itself.

History

Questions now classed as metaethical are as old as moral philosophy: Plato's Euthyphro asks whether the pious is loved by the gods because it is pious, or pious because it is loved — an early statement of the problem of whether moral standards depend on divine commands. David Hume, in the eighteenth century, observed that writers slide from statements about what is to statements about what ought to be without explanation — the is–ought problem — and argued that moral distinctions are founded on sentiment rather than reason alone.

Metaethics as a self-conscious field dates from G. E. Moore's Principia Ethica (1903), which argued for the "naturalistic fallacy": the claim that goodness cannot be defined in natural terms, since for any natural property proposed, it remains an open question whether things with that property are good — the open-question argument. In the 1930s, logical positivists such as A. J. Ayer developed emotivism, holding that moral utterances express attitudes rather than state facts, while C. L. Stevenson elaborated this into a theory of persuasive definition. R. M. Hare's prescriptivism analysed moral language as universal prescriptions. The second half of the twentieth century saw revived defenses of moral realism by philosophers including John Mackie (who opposed realism), Peter Railton, and David Brink, and the development of constructivist positions following John Rawls and Christine Korsgaard.

Central questions

Meaning and truth

Cognitivism holds that moral statements express beliefs and can be true or false. Non-cognitivist theories — emotivism, prescriptivism, and Allan Gibbard's norm-expressivism and Simon Blackburn's quasi-realism — hold that moral statements primarily express attitudes, plans, or prescriptions, though quasi-realists argue such expressions can earn the right to truth-talk. The Frege–Geach problem challenges non-cognitivists to explain how moral terms function in unasserted contexts, such as the antecedents of conditionals.

Moral ontology

Moral realism holds that there are objective moral facts, independent of human opinion. Naturalist realists identify moral properties with natural properties; non-naturalists, following Moore, treat them as sui generis. Error theory, defended by J. L. Mackie, accepts that moral statements aim at objective truth but argues they are all false because nothing in the world answers to the required objectivity — Mackie's "argument from queerness." Constructivism locates moral truth in the outcome of an idealized procedure of deliberation or agreement.

Motivation and reasons

Moral judgment internalism holds that sincerely judging an action right necessarily provides some motivation to perform it; externalism denies the necessary connection. Related debates concern whether reasons for action are internal (dependent on an agent's desires) or external, a distinction associated with Bernard Williams. Amoralists — people who profess moral judgments without any motivation — are used as test cases by both sides.

Epistemology

If moral facts exist, how are they known? Intuitionists appeal to self-evident moral principles; coherentists, following Rawls's method of reflective equilibrium, justify beliefs by mutual adjustment between principles and considered judgments. Moral disagreement — persistent divergence between cultures and individuals — is used both as evidence against realism and, by realists, as no more troubling than disagreement in science.

Relation to normative ethics

Metaethical positions are often, though not invariably, paired with normative ones. Some philosophers argue that metaethics should remain neutral between first-order theories; others, such as proponents of particular versions of expressivism, hold that metaethics constrains acceptable normative theories. Debates over moral relativism — whether moral truth is relative to cultures or frameworks — straddle the boundary between metaethics and normative ethics.
