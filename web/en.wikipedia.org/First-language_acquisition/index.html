First-language acquisition

First-language acquisition is the process by which infants and children learn their native language, progressing from babbling to single words, two-word combinations and full grammar within roughly the first five years of life, without formal instruction.

Stages

Typical milestones include cooing, canonical babbling around six months, first words near the first birthday, a vocabulary spurt and two-word "telegraphic" speech in the second year, and increasingly complex sentences thereafter. Comprehension runs ahead of production at every stage.

Theories

Behaviourist accounts (Skinner) emphasised imitation and reinforcement; Chomsky's nativism proposed an innate language faculty and universal grammar, citing the poverty of the stimulus; usage-based and social-interactionist theories (Tomasello, Bruner) stress statistical learning and joint attention. Deaf children exposed to sign language follow parallel milestones in the manual modality.
