Chinese music

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Chinese music is one of the oldest musical traditions in the world, with documentary evidence going back to the Shang dynasty oracle bones and the Classic of Poetry. Classical theory was systematized in the Zhou dynasty by Confucius and the Ritual classics, which assigned music a central role in moral cultivation and state ritual.

Traditional instruments

The bayin (eight sounds) classification organizes instruments by material: silk, bamboo, wood, stone, metal, clay, gourd, and hide. Iconic instruments include:

  • Erhu: two-stringed bowed fiddle, called the "Chinese violin".
  • Pipa: pear-shaped plucked lute with a distinctive virtuosic repertoire.
  • Guzheng: long zither with movable bridges, ancestor of the Japanese koto.
  • Guqin: seven-stringed zither prized by scholars for its subtle tone; UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage.
  • Dizi: transverse bamboo flute; sheng: mouth organ, ancestor of the accordion and harmonica.
  • Suona: piercing double-reed horn; bianzhong: sets of tuned bronze bells from antiquity.

Forms and genres

Historical court music (yayue) served ritual functions, while regional traditions include Cantonese music, Jiangnan sizhu ("silk and bamboo"), kunqu and Peking opera orchestras, and the narrative traditions of drum-singing. Chinese folk song repertoires number in the hundreds of regional styles, and China's 56 ethnic groups maintain distinct traditions — Tibetan long songs, Uyghur muqam suites, Mongolian throat singing and morin khuur.

Modern Chinese music

In the 20th century Western classical music, military band traditions, and pop took root. The Chinese orchestra — large ensembles of modernized traditional instruments — became a standard concert form. Cantopop and Mandopop emerged in Hong Kong and Taiwan from the 1970s, dominating Chinese-language pop; rock appeared in mainland China in the 1980s with Cui Jian, followed by hip-hop, electronic, and indie scenes in the 2000s.
