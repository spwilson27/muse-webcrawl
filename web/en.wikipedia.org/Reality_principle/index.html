Reality principle

In Freudian psychoanalytic theory, the reality principle (German: Realitätsprinzip) is the ability of the mind to assess the reality of the external world and to act upon it accordingly, as opposed to acting on the pleasure principle. It is the governing principle of the ego, which mediates between the instinctual demands of the id and the constraints of the physical and social world.

Formulation

Sigmund Freud introduced the contrast in his 1911 paper "Formulations on the Two Principles of Mental Functioning". The infant's psyche at first operates only under the pleasure principle, seeking immediate gratification and hallucinating satisfaction. As development proceeds, the ego learns to postpone discharge, tolerate tension, test reality, and pursue longer-term satisfaction — what Freud called the education of the instincts by reality. The reality principle does not abolish the pleasure principle but safeguards it: a pleasure deferred and secured is preferred to a momentary one with painful consequences.

Later usage

Jacques Lacan reinterpreted the reality principle within his orders of the Imaginary, Symbolic, and the Real. Herbert Marcuse, in Eros and Civilization (1955), historicised the concept with his notion of the "performance principle", the historically specific form the reality principle takes under capitalism.
