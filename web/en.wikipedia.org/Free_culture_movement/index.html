Free-culture movement

The free-culture movement is a social movement that promotes the freedom to distribute and modify creative works, and opposes overly restrictive copyright laws and practices. Drawing on the free and open-source software movement, it argues that culture flourishes through sharing, remixing, and building upon earlier works, and that excessive intellectual-property restrictions hamper creativity and access to knowledge.

Origins and figures

The movement grew alongside the internet in the late 1990s and early 2000s. Lawrence Lessig's books Code (1999) and Free Culture (2004) supplied much of its intellectual framework; Lessig co-founded Creative Commons in 2001, which provides standard licences letting creators reserve some rights while granting others. Students for Free Culture, founded in 2003, spread campus chapters advocating open access, patent and copyright reform, and net neutrality. Projects such as Wikipedia, the Internet Archive, and Project Gutenberg embody free-culture goals.

Criticism and debate

Critics, including composer and publisher organisations and commentators such as Andrew Keen, argue that free culture undervalues professional creators and quality control. Internal debates concern whether non-commercial licences count as free, how to treat indigenous cultural heritage, and how creators should be supported financially in an ecosystem of free distribution. Pirate Parties, founded first in Sweden in 2006, carried parts of the agenda into electoral politics.
