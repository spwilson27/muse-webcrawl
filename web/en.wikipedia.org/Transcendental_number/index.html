Transcendental number

In mathematics, a transcendental number is a real or complex number that is not algebraic — that is, not a root of any non-zero polynomial equation with integer coefficients. The best-known transcendental numbers are π (pi) and e.

History

Joseph Liouville first proved the existence of transcendental numbers in 1844, exhibiting explicit examples (Liouville numbers). Charles Hermite proved e transcendental in 1873, and Ferdinand von Lindemann proved π transcendental in 1882, settling the ancient problem of squaring the circle. Georg Cantor showed that almost all real numbers are transcendental, since the algebraic numbers are countable while the reals are not.

Open problems

Proving specific numbers transcendental is hard: it is unknown whether π + e or Euler's constant γ is even irrational. The Gelfond–Schneider theorem (1934) resolved Hilbert's seventh problem, showing that ab is transcendental for algebraic a ≠ 0, 1 and irrational algebraic b.
