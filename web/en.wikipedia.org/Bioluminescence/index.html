Bioluminescence

Bioluminescence is the production and emission of light by living organisms. It is a form of chemiluminescence in which a chemical reaction creates excited molecules that release energy as photons. Bioluminescence is widespread in the ocean — among fish, squid, jellyfish, crustaceans, and microorganisms — and on land is best known in fireflies and some fungi. It has evolved independently many dozens of times across the tree of life.

Chemistry

Most bioluminescent reactions involve a light-emitting substrate called a luciferin and an enzyme, a luciferase, which catalyses its oxidation. The product, an excited-state oxyluciferin, emits light as it relaxes. Firefly luciferin, coelenterazine (used by many marine animals), and bacterial luciferin systems are chemically distinct. Because the reaction is highly efficient — producing light with very little heat — it is sometimes called "cold light". Some organisms do not synthesise their own chemistry: many marine animals host symbiotic luminescent bacteria, and glowing plankton such as dinoflagellates create the sparkling "milky seas" and red-tide glows seen in coastal waters.

Functions

In the deep sea, where sunlight is absent, bioluminescence serves for communication, attracting mates, luring prey (as in the anglerfish's esca), camouflage by counter-illumination — matching downwelling light to hide a silhouette — and defence, including startling predators, sacrificial glowing decoys, and "burglar alarm" flashes that attract larger predators to attack the attacker. On land, fireflies use species-specific flash patterns for courtship, while some fungi glow possibly to attract spore-dispersing insects.

Distribution

An estimated 76% of marine animals are bioluminescent, and the trait occurs in at least 94 phyla-wide lineages of marine organisms. Terrestrial examples are rarer and mostly concentrated in insects (fireflies, glow-worms, some click beetles), fungi, and a few other invertebrates. No naturally bioluminescent plants or vertebrates on land produce their own light, although many amphibians fluoresce.

Applications

Luciferase genes are widely used as reporter genes in molecular biology to track gene expression. The green fluorescent protein (GFP) — discovered in the bioluminescent jellyfish Aequorea victoria, though GFP itself fluoresces rather than luminesces — revolutionised cell imaging and earned the 2008 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Bioluminescence assays are also used to detect ATP and thus microbial contamination in food and medicine.
