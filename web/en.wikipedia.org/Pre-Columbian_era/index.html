Pre-Columbian era

The pre-Columbian era is the period in the history of the Americas before the arrival of Christopher Columbus in 1492 and the ensuing European colonisation. It spans from the first human settlement of the continents during the Upper Paleolithic — at least 15,000 years ago, and possibly earlier — to the early 16th century, when indigenous civilisations were transformed or destroyed by conquest, disease, and colonisation.

Settlement and development

The Americas were peopled by migrants from Siberia who crossed the Beringia land bridge and spread through both continents. Over millennia, societies developed agriculture independently — domesticating maize, beans, squash, potatoes, and other crops — and progressed, in some regions, to cities and states. Archaeologists commonly divide the sequence into Paleo-Indian, Archaic, Formative (Preclassic), Classic, and Postclassic stages (in North America: Paleo-Indian, Archaic, Woodland, and Mississippian traditions).

Civilisations

In Mesoamerica, the Olmec, Zapotec, Maya, Teotihuacan, Toltec, and Aztec civilisations built monumental cities, developed writing (Maya script) and precise calendars, and practised intensive agriculture such as chinampas. In the Andes, cultures including Chavín, Nazca, Moche, Tiwanaku, Wari, and Chimú preceded the Inca Empire, the largest state in the pre-Columbian Americas, with its road network, terrace agriculture, and quipu record-keeping. Elsewhere, complex societies included the Mississippian culture (Cahokia), the Ancestral Puebloans of the Southwest, Amazonian chiefdoms, and the diverse peoples of the Caribbean, whom Europeans encountered first.

End of the era

After 1492, European diseases such as smallpox caused catastrophic population losses among indigenous peoples who lacked immunity, and Spanish conquistadors overthrew the Aztec (1519–1521) and Inca (1532–1572) empires. The term "pre-Columbian" remains standard in archaeology and art history for the cultures of this long period.
