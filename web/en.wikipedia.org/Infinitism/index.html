Infinitism

Infinitism is the view in epistemology that knowledge is justified by an infinite chain of reasons. It is one of the classical responses to the regress problem — Agrippa's (Münchhausen) trilemma — which observes that a chain of justification must either stop arbitrarily (foundationalism), circle back on itself (coherentism), or proceed infinitely. Infinitists, most prominently the contemporary philosopher Peter Klein, embrace the third option: a belief is justified if there is an infinite, non-repeating series of reasons available to the believer, each reason justifying the previous one.

Arguments

Klein argues that infinitism alone avoids both the arbitrariness of foundations and the circularity of coherentist webs, and that it accords with the Principle of Avoiding Arbitrariness and the Principle of Avoiding Circularity. A common objection is the finite-mind problem: human beings cannot hold infinitely many beliefs. Klein replies that justification requires the dispositional availability of reasons — the believer need only be able to produce further reasons as each is challenged, so the infinite chain is required in principle, not actually traversed or stored. Critics respond that merely dispositional chains do not confer actual justification, or that the view confuses the possibility of continued inquiry with completed warrant.
