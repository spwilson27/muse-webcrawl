Consideration

Consideration is a concept of English common law and is a necessity for simple contracts but not for special contracts (contracts by deed). A contract is binding only if it is supported by consideration — something of value exchanged by each party — or made by deed. The traditional definition comes from Currie v Misa (1875): a valuable consideration may consist in some right, interest, profit or benefit accruing to one party, or some forbearance, detriment, loss or responsibility given, suffered or undertaken by the other.

Rules

  • Consideration must be sufficient but need not be adequate. Courts will not generally inquire into the adequacy of the bargain — a peppercorn, or nominal payment, can suffice (Chappell & Co v Nestlé held even chocolate wrappers could be part of the consideration) — but it must have some value in the eyes of the law.
  • Past consideration is not good consideration. Something already done before the promise was made generally cannot support it, subject to exceptions such as acts done at the promisor's request in expectation of payment.
  • Consideration must move from the promisee. A person to whom a promise is made can enforce it only if they provided the consideration.
  • Existing duties. Performing an existing contractual or public duty is traditionally not consideration, though exceeding the duty, or conferring a practical benefit in the context of a renegotiated contract (Williams v Roffey Bros), may be.

Related doctrines

Promissory estoppel may restrain a promisor from going back on a promise even without consideration. Civil-law systems generally do not require consideration, relying instead on doctrines such as causa or simply on agreement and intention to create legal relations.
