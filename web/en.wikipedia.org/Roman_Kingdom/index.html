Roman Kingdom

The Roman Kingdom (Latin: Regnum Romanum) was the earliest form of government of Rome and its territory. According to tradition, it was founded by Romulus in 753 BC and ended with the overthrow of the monarchy in 509 BC, following the tyranny of Lucius Tarquinius Superbus, which led to the establishment of the Roman Republic. Seven kings are named by the tradition: Romulus, Numa Pompilius, Tullus Hostilius, Ancus Marcius, Lucius Tarquinius Priscus, Servius Tullius, and Tarquinius Superbus.

Kings and institutions

Kings were not hereditary but elected for life by the Roman people on the advice of the Senate, an advisory council of elders (patres). The king held supreme military command (imperium), served as chief priest and judge, and convened the assemblies. Tradition credits Romulus with founding the Senate and the legions, Numa with Rome's religious institutions and calendar, and Servius Tullius with the first census, the division of citizens into classes and tribes, and the Servian Wall.

Society and expansion

Archaic Rome grew from a cluster of hilltop settlements on the Tiber into the dominant city of Latium. Under the later kings — of Etruscan origin, according to tradition — Rome gained monumental temples, the Cloaca Maxima drainage system, and the Forum's first paving, reflecting Etruscan and Greek influence.

Overthrow and historicity

The monarchy fell after the rape of Lucretia by the king's son Sextus, provoking a revolt led by Lucius Junius Brutus, who became one of the first two consuls. Modern historians treat the regal narrative as a mixture of legend and genuine memory: archaeology confirms an increasingly urban, wealthy Rome in the 6th century BC, though details of individual kings remain uncertain.
