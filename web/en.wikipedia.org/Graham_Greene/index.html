Henry Graham Greene (2 October 1904 – 3 April 1991) was an English novelist, short-story writer, screenwriter, and journalist, regarded by many as one of the greatest writers of the 20th century. Combining literary acclaim with widespread popularity, he explored moral and political ambiguity — often through the lens of his Roman Catholic faith — in thrillers he called "entertainments" and in serious novels alike. He was shortlisted for the Nobel Prize in Literature repeatedly but never won it.

Life

Greene was born in Berkhamsted, Hertfordshire, where his father was headmaster of the school Greene attended — an unhappy period marked by bullying and suicide attempts, followed by psychoanalysis in London. At Balliol College, Oxford, he edited a student magazine and briefly joined the Communist Party. He converted to Catholicism in 1926 and married Vivienne Dayrell-Browning in 1927. After work at The Times, the success of Stamboul Train (1932) let him write full-time. During the Second World War he worked for MI6 in Sierra Leone, under Kim Philby — experiences that fed novels such as The Heart of the Matter (1948). Extensive travel in Africa, Mexico, Vietnam, Cuba, and Central America supplied settings for much of his fiction.

Major works

Greene's "Catholic novels" — Brighton Rock (1938), The Power and the Glory (1940), The Heart of the Matter (1948), and The End of the Affair (1951) — examine sin, grace, and damnation. His political novels include The Quiet American (1955), prescient about American involvement in Vietnam; Our Man in Havana (1958), a satire of espionage; The Comedians (1966), set in Duvalier's Haiti; and The Human Factor (1978). He wrote the novella and screenplay for the classic film The Third Man (1949).

Legacy

Greene's seedy, morally compromised fictional world was dubbed "Greeneland". He died in Vevey, Switzerland, in 1991, and remains among the most widely read English novelists of his century.
