Chinampa

A chinampa (Nahuatl: chināmitl) is a type of Mesoamerican agriculture that used small, rectangular areas of fertile arable land to grow crops on the shallow lake beds in the Valley of Mexico. Often referred to in English as "floating gardens," chinampas were built by the Aztecs (Mexica) and earlier peoples to support intensive food production around cities such as Tenochtitlan.

Construction

Chinampas were created by staking out a rectangular enclosure, typically about 30 by 2.5 metres, in a shallow lake bed. Woven reed fencing was driven into the mud, and the enclosure was filled with alternating layers of lake sediment and decaying vegetation until the plot rose just above the water surface. Willow trees (ahuejote, Salix bonplandiana) were often planted at the corners to anchor the structure with their roots and provide shade.

Agriculture

The plots were exceptionally fertile. Water from the surrounding canals kept the soil constantly moist, and nutrient-rich mud dredged from canal bottoms was periodically spread over the beds. Maize, beans, squash, tomatoes, chilies, and flowers were commonly grown, and chinampas could yield multiple harvests per year, helping feed the large population of Tenochtitlan, which may have exceeded 200,000 people.

History

Chinampa agriculture predates the Aztec Empire and was practiced by the Xochimilca and other peoples of the southern lakes. The Mexica expanded the system dramatically as Tenochtitlan grew. After the Spanish conquest, many chinampas were abandoned as lakes were drained, but the system survives in the borough of Xochimilco in modern Mexico City, where chinampas remain in use and have been designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
