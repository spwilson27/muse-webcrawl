Model theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Model theory is a branch of mathematical logic that studies mathematical structures (models) in relation to the formal languages used to describe them. A model theorist asks which sentences of a language are true in a given structure, how structures sharing a theory are related, and which sets can be defined within a structure. Alfred Tarski, who founded much of the field, characterised it through the interplay between syntax and semantics: a sentence is true in a model according to precise rules of interpretation.

Basic concepts

A structure consists of a domain of objects together with interpretations of the constant, function and relation symbols of a signature. A theory is a set of sentences; a model of a theory is a structure in which all of them hold. Central classical results include the compactness theorem, the Löwenheim–Skolem theorems (a first-order theory with an infinite model has models of every infinite cardinality), and completeness: a sentence is provable in first-order logic if and only if it holds in every model of the premises.

Modern classification theory

From the 1960s, work by Michael Morley, Saharon Shelah and others developed stability theory, which classifies first-order theories by the complexity of their families of models and definable sets. Key notions include categoricity, types, forking independence, and dividing lines such as NIP and simplicity. These ideas have found striking applications outside logic, for example in algebraic geometry — where Hrushovski's work used model theory to prove cases of the Mordell–Lang conjecture — and in combinatorics and number theory through o-minimal structures.

Finite and applied model theory

Finite model theory studies structures such as graphs and databases and underlies the descriptive complexity of computation: Fagin's theorem characterises NP by existential second-order logic. Model-theoretic methods also appear in database theory, verification and the semantics of programming languages.
