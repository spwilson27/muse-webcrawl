Koi

Scientific name: Cyprinus rubrofuscus (Amur carp)
Origin: Japan (Niigata), bred from East Asian carp
Lifespan: commonly 25–35 years; reported far longer

Koi (Japanese: 鯉, "carp"), more specifically nishikigoi (錦鯉, "brocaded carp"), are coloured varieties of the Amur carp (Cyprinus rubrofuscus) kept for decorative purposes in outdoor ponds and water gardens. Koi breeding began in Japan in the 19th century among rice farmers in Niigata Prefecture, and the hobby has since spread worldwide.

Varieties

More than a hundred named varieties are recognised, judged by colour, patterning, and scalation. The most prized group, the Gosanke, comprises the Kōhaku (white with red markings), Taishō Sanshoku or Sanke (white with red and black), and Shōwa Sanshoku (black with red and white). Other popular varieties include the metallic Ogon, the butterfly-like long-finned forms, and the German-scaled Doitsu types.

Keeping and symbolism

Koi require spacious, well-filtered ponds with clean, oxygenated water. They are omnivorous, hardy, and recognisably responsive to their keepers. In Japanese culture the koi symbolises perseverance, courage, and good fortune; legend tells of a carp that climbed a waterfall and was transformed into a dragon. Exceptional specimens can sell for very large sums — a record Kōhaku sold for about US$1.8 million in 2018. Koi are susceptible to diseases such as koi herpesvirus, a serious concern for the international trade.
