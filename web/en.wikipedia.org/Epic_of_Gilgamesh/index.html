The Epic of Gilgamesh is an epic poem from ancient Mesopotamia. The literary history of Gilgamesh, the historical king of Uruk, begins with five Sumerian poems from the early 3rd millennium BC; these were later combined into the Akkadian epic, regarded as one of the earliest surviving notable works of literature.

The epic tells of Gilgamesh's friendship with the wild man Enkidu, their adventures, Enkidu's death, and Gilgamesh's anguished quest for the secret of immortality — a quest that ends in failure but in wisdom about the impermanence of human life.

Versions and discovery

The most complete surviving version, the "standard Babylonian" edition attributed to the scholar Sîn-lēqi-unninni, was recovered as twelve cuneiform tablets from the library of Ashurbanipal at Nineveh in 1853. George Smith's 1872 translation of the flood tablet (Tablet XI), with its close parallels to the Noah story, created a sensation and prompted further excavations.

Plot

Gilgamesh, two-thirds divine and one-third human, oppresses Uruk until the gods create Enkidu to check him; after wrestling, the two become inseparable friends. Together they slay the monster Humbaba in the Cedar Forest and kill the Bull of Heaven sent by the spurned goddess Ishtar. Enkidu is condemned to die; his death drives Gilgamesh to journey to Utnapishtim, survivor of the great flood, seeking eternal life. He fails the tests set him and returns to Uruk, consoled by the permanence of the city's walls — his works, and his story.

Themes

Central themes include mortality and the fear of death, friendship, civilization versus wilderness, the responsibilities of kingship, and the acceptance of human limits.

Influence

Motifs from the epic — especially the flood narrative and the quest for immortality — influenced later literature, including passages of the Hebrew Bible and Greek epic tradition. The epic has been translated many times and remains the best-known work of ancient Mesopotamian literature.
