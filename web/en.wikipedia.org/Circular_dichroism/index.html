Circular dichroism

Circular dichroism (CD) is the difference in the absorption of left-handed and right-handed circularly polarized light by a substance. It occurs when a molecule contains one or more chiral chromophores. CD spectroscopy, usually measured in the ultraviolet and visible regions, is a widely used technique in chemistry and biochemistry for studying the structure of chiral molecules.

Principle

When circularly polarized light passes through an optically active medium, the two circular components are absorbed to different extents; linearly polarized light emerging from the sample becomes elliptically polarized, and the ellipticity is reported as the CD signal. The effect is related to circular birefringence and optical rotation through the Kramers–Kronig relations.

Applications

In structural biology, far-ultraviolet CD spectra report on the secondary structure of proteins — alpha helices, beta sheets and disordered regions each give characteristic spectra — and can be used to follow folding and unfolding, estimate secondary-structure content, and detect binding events. Near-ultraviolet CD reflects the environments of aromatic side chains and disulfide bonds. CD is also used to determine absolute configuration in organic chemistry and to study nucleic acid structures.
