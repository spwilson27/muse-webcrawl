The Great Prussian Uprising (Polish: powstanie pruskie) was the largest and longest uprising of the Old Prussians, a Baltic people, against the Teutonic Knights during the Prussian Crusade in the 13th century. Beginning in 1260, inspired in part by the Lithuanian victory over the Livonian Order at the Battle of Durbe, the uprising engulfed five of the Prussian clans and lasted fourteen years.

Course of the uprising

Prussian leaders such as Herkus Monte of the Natangians—who had been educated in Germany and knew the enemy's tactics—led coordinated attacks on Teutonic castles and settlements. Several Prussian clans elected leaders and besieged major strongholds, though the lack of siege equipment and unified command limited their success. The Knights, reinforced by crusaders from the Holy Roman Empire, gradually wore down the rebellion clan by clan through the early 1270s.

Aftermath

The defeat of the uprising by 1274 ended organized large-scale Prussian resistance and accelerated German colonization and the gradual assimilation and extinction of the Old Prussian language. A later, smaller uprising followed in 1295.
