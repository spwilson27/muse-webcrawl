Snoring

Snoring arises from vibration of relaxed tissues in the upper airway

Snoring is the vibration of respiratory structures and the resulting sound due to obstructed air movement while breathing during sleep. In some cases the sound may be soft, but in most cases it can be loud and unpleasant. Snoring during sleep may be a sign of, or the first alarm of, obstructive sleep apnea (OSA). Researchers estimate that roughly 45% of adults snore occasionally and about 25% snore habitually.

Causes

During sleep, the muscles of the throat and tongue relax, narrowing the airway. As air passes through, tissues such as the soft palate, uvula, tonsils and tongue base flutter and produce sound. Snoring is aggravated by alcohol or sedatives (which increase relaxation), nasal congestion, sleeping on one's back, obesity, and anatomical features such as a long soft palate or enlarged tonsils. Mouth breathing and a deviated nasal septum also contribute.

Health and social effects

Simple snoring mainly disturbs the sleep of others, but it is associated with sleep fragmentation and, when it reflects obstructive sleep apnea, with daytime sleepiness, hypertension and increased cardiovascular risk. Habitual loud snoring is a common reason for sleep studies (polysomnography) to distinguish primary snoring from apnea.

Treatment

Management ranges from lifestyle measures — weight loss, avoiding alcohol before bed, positional therapy — to devices such as mandibular advancement splints and, where apnea is present, continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP). Surgery, including uvulopalatopharyngoplasty and newer hypoglossal nerve stimulation, is reserved for selected cases. Nasal strips and throat sprays have limited evidence of benefit.
