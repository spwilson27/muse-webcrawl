Blowout preventer

A blowout preventer (BOP) is a large, specialised valve or set of valves used to seal, control, and monitor oil and gas wells to prevent blowouts—the uncontrolled release of crude oil or natural gas from a well. Blowout preventers were developed to cope with extreme erratic pressures and uncontrolled flow (formation kick) emanating from a well reservoir during drilling. They are installed atop the well casing and form a critical part of well control safety systems on both land rigs and offshore platforms.

A blowout preventer stack combines ram and annular preventers.

Types

Ram blowout preventer

The ram BOP uses a pair of opposing steel plungers, or rams, that seal the wellbore when extended. Different ram types serve different purposes: pipe rams close around the drill pipe, blind rams seal an open hole, and shear rams are designed to cut through the drill pipe and seal the well in an emergency. The first successful ram-type preventer was brought to market in 1922 by James Abercrombie and Harry Cameron.

Annular blowout preventer

The annular preventer, invented by Granville Sloan Knox in 1946, uses a large rubber packing element reinforced with steel fingers that can seal around drill pipe of various sizes or close an open hole entirely. It is generally placed at the top of the BOP stack.

Subsea preventers

On offshore floating rigs, the BOP stack sits on the seabed and is connected to the rig by a marine riser. Subsea stacks are much larger and more complex, operated remotely by hydraulic control systems with acoustic and remotely operated vehicle (ROV) backup intervention capability. They may weigh several hundred tonnes and contain multiple ram cavities.

Deepwater Horizon

The blowout preventer became a focus of worldwide attention after the Deepwater Horizon explosion and oil spill in the Gulf of Mexico in April 2010. The rig's subsea BOP failed to shear the drill pipe and seal the Macondo well, in part because the pipe had buckled off-centre under the force of the blowout. The disaster prompted extensive regulatory reform of well control rules in the United States and internationally, including stricter requirements for shear ram capability and third-party verification.

Regulation and testing

BOPs are subject to regular function and pressure testing, typically every 7 to 30 days depending on jurisdiction and operation type. Standards bodies such as the American Petroleum Institute (API) publish specifications (e.g. API 53 for blowout prevention equipment systems) governing their design, testing, and maintenance.
