The International Criminal Police Organization, commonly known as Interpol (stylised INTERPOL), is an international organisation that facilitates worldwide police cooperation and crime control. Founded in 1923 and headquartered in Lyon, France, it is the world's largest international police organisation, with 196 member countries. Despite its name and popular portrayal, Interpol is not a supranational police force: it has no agents who make arrests, which remain the responsibility of national law enforcement agencies.

History

The idea of international police cooperation was advanced at the first International Criminal Police Congress in Monaco in 1914. In 1923 the International Criminal Police Commission (ICPC) was established in Vienna, Austria. After the Second World War the organisation was revived, and in 1956 it adopted a new constitution and the name ICPO–INTERPOL, taking its telegraphic address "Interpol" as its common name. The headquarters moved to Saint-Cloud near Paris in 1966 and to Lyon in 1989.

Structure and work

Interpol's General Secretariat coordinates day-to-day activities. Each member country maintains a National Central Bureau (NCB) staffed by national police officers, which serves as the liaison point with the organisation. Interpol operates secure communications networks and criminal databases covering stolen and lost travel documents, fingerprints, DNA profiles, stolen vehicles and works of art.

Notices

Interpol is best known for its colour-coded notices, issued at the request of member countries. A Red Notice seeks the location and arrest of a person wanted by a judicial jurisdiction or an international tribunal with a view to extradition. Other notices include Blue (locate or identify a person), Green (warnings about habitual offenders), Yellow (missing persons), Black (unidentified bodies), Orange (imminent threats) and Purple (criminal methods and modus operandi). An Interpol notice is not an international arrest warrant, and member countries decide what action to take under their own laws.

Criticism

The organisation has been criticised for the alleged misuse of Red Notices by some governments against political opponents, journalists and refugees, and reforms have introduced stronger review by the Commission for the Control of Interpol's Files. Its constitution forbids interventions of a political, military, religious or racial character.
