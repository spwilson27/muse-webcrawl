Lewis acid

Lewis acid
Concept introduced1923, by Gilbert N. Lewis
DefinitionElectron-pair acceptor
ExamplesBF3, AlCl3, FeCl3, metal cations

A Lewis acid is a chemical species that contains an empty orbital and is able to accept an electron pair from a Lewis base to form a Lewis adduct. The concept was introduced by the American chemist Gilbert N. Lewis in 1923 and generalizes the idea of acidity beyond proton transfer.

Definition and theory

Under the Lewis definition, an acid is an electron-pair acceptor and a base is an electron-pair donor. This broadens the Brønsted–Lowry definition, in which an acid must donate a proton. Many classic Lewis acids are metal cations and electron-deficient compounds such as boron trifluoride (BF3) and aluminium chloride (AlCl3).

Applications

Lewis acids are widely used as catalysts. In the Friedel–Crafts reaction, aluminium chloride catalyses the alkylation and acylation of aromatic rings. Lewis acids also play key roles in Diels–Alder reactions, carbonyl chemistry, and polymerization. Frustrated Lewis pairs, combinations of bulky Lewis acids and bases that cannot form an adduct, can activate small molecules such as hydrogen.

Hard and soft acids

The HSAB (hard and soft acids and bases) principle classifies Lewis acids by polarizability: hard acids such as H+ and Al3+ bind preferentially to hard bases, while soft acids such as Ag+ prefer soft bases. The strength of Lewis acids can be quantified by scales such as the Gutmann acceptor number and fluoride ion affinity.
