Quasi-contract

A quasi-contract (or implied-in-law contract) is a fictional contract recognised by a court. The notion of a quasi-contract can be traced to Roman law and is still a concept in use in some legal systems. Unlike an implied-in-fact contract, which arises from the conduct of the parties, a quasi-contract is imposed by law to prevent the unjust enrichment of one party at the expense of another, regardless of the parties' intentions.

Requirements

A claimant seeking recovery in quasi-contract must typically show that:

  • the claimant conferred a benefit upon the defendant;
  • the defendant knew of and appreciated the benefit;
  • the defendant's acceptance or retention of the benefit under the circumstances would be inequitable without payment.

History

The doctrine derives from the Roman action indebitatus assumpsit and was developed in the English common law, notably in Moses v Macferlan (1760), where Lord Mansfield explained the action for money had and received as lying "for money which, ex aequo et bono, the defendant ought to refund". In modern English law, the quasi-contractual actions have largely been absorbed into the law of unjust enrichment and restitution. In the United States, quasi-contract remains a recognised cause of action or theory of recovery, codified in the Restatement (Third) of Restitution and Unjust Enrichment (2011).

Examples

Typical cases include recovery of money paid by mistake, payment for emergency services rendered to an incapacitated person, and recovery for goods or services accepted under a void or unenforceable contract — the latter often pleaded as quantum meruit.
