The Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union enshrines certain political, social, and economic rights for citizens and residents of the European Union into EU law. It was drafted by the European Convention and solemnly proclaimed on 7 December 2000 by the European Parliament, the Council of Ministers and the European Commission. Under the Treaty of Lisbon, which entered into force on 1 December 2009, the Charter has the same legal value as the EU treaties.


The Charter gathers the EU's fundamental rights in a single document.

Contents

The Charter's 54 articles are grouped under seven titles: Dignity, Freedoms, Equality, Solidarity, Citizens' Rights, and Justice, preceded by a general provisions title. It guarantees, among other rights, human dignity, the right to life, the prohibition of torture, freedom of thought and expression, equality before the law, non-discrimination, workers' rights, environmental and consumer protection, the right to an effective remedy and a fair trial, and the presumption of innocence. It also contains rights not found in the older European Convention on Human Rights, such as data protection, bioethics guarantees, and freedom to conduct a business.

Legal status and scope

Under Article 51, the Charter binds the institutions and bodies of the EU, and binds member states only when they are implementing Union law. It does not extend the competences of the Union. The Court of Justice of the European Union uses the Charter to review EU acts and national measures within the scope of EU law; notable rulings include the Digital Rights Ireland judgment (2014), which annulled the Data Retention Directive, and the Schrems judgments on transatlantic data transfers. A protocol negotiated by the United Kingdom and Poland sought to clarify (not opt out of) the Charter's application; the UK ceased to be bound after Brexit.

History

The European Council at Cologne in 1999 decided to consolidate the fundamental rights applicable at EU level in a single charter. A Convention chaired by Roman Herzog drafted the text, drawing on the European Convention on Human Rights, national constitutional traditions, the European Social Charter, and the case law of the Court of Justice. The adapted Charter was proclaimed again in Strasbourg in 2007 ahead of the signing of the Lisbon Treaty.
