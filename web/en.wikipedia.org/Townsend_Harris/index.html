Townsend Harris (3 October 1804 – 25 February 1878) was an American merchant and politician who served as the first United States Consul General to Japan. He negotiated the "Harris Treaty" (Treaty of Amity and Commerce) of 1858, which opened Japanese ports to American trade and established formal diplomatic relations between the two countries following Commodore Matthew Perry's Convention of Kanagawa.

Early life

Born in Sandy Hill (now Hudson Falls), New York, Harris moved to New York City, where he prospered as a merchant trading with China. A civic leader, he served as president of the city's Board of Education and was a principal founder of the Free Academy of the City of New York in 1847, which became the City College of New York; Townsend Harris High School in Queens also bears his name.

Mission to Japan

After a mission to Siam (Thailand), where he revised the 1833 treaty in 1856, Harris arrived at Shimoda, Japan, in August 1856 as consul general under the Convention of Kanagawa. Working patiently through audiences and negotiations — including a historic personal audience with the shogun Tokugawa Iesada in Edo — he persuaded the Tokugawa shogunate, without the show of force that marked Perry's visit, to sign the Treaty of Amity and Commerce on 29 July 1858. The treaty opened additional ports including Edo and Osaka to trade, fixed tariffs, granted extraterritoriality to Americans, and provided for diplomatic exchange; similar treaties with European powers (the Ansei Treaties) followed.

Later life and legacy

Harris left Japan in 1861 in poor health and returned to New York, where he died in 1878. He is remembered in Japan, particularly in Shimoda, where a museum and annual festivals commemorate his residence; his journal of the mission is a key source on late-Edo Japan.
