East Slavic languages

The East Slavic languages are one of the three branches of the Slavic languages, spoken by roughly 240 million people in Eastern Europe and northern Asia. They descend from the language of Kievan Rus' and are written in the Cyrillic script.

Languages

The living standard languages are Russian (the most widely spoken Slavic language, with well over 150 million native speakers), Ukrainian and Belarusian. Rusyn, spoken in the Carpathian region, is recognized as a separate language in some countries and treated as a dialect of Ukrainian in others.

History

East Slavic developed from Proto-Slavic; its earliest written form is Old East Slavic, the language of Kievan Rus', heavily influenced by Old Church Slavonic in religious texts. After the Mongol invasions and the political division between the Grand Duchy of Lithuania and Muscovy, the southwestern and northeastern varieties diverged into the Ruthenian and Russian traditions. Distinct Ukrainian and Belarusian literary standards crystallized in the 18th–19th centuries, while Russian was codified under Peter the Great and the 18th-century academies.

Features

Shared East Slavic characteristics include pleophony (the development of sequences such as -oro- and -olo-, e.g. Russian gorod "town"), the Cyrillic alphabet, rich inflection with six or seven cases, and free word order. The languages remain partially mutually intelligible, with Ukrainian and Belarusian particularly close.
