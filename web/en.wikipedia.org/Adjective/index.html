Adjective

Part of speechWord class (lexical category)
FunctionModifies a noun or noun phrase
Examplesbig, red, happy, former, utter

An adjective (abbreviated adj.) is a word that describes or defines a noun or noun phrase. Its semantic role is to change information given by the noun. Traditionally, adjectives are considered one of the main parts of speech of the English language, although historically they were classed together with nouns.

Adjectives can be used attributively, directly before (occasionally after) the noun they modify — as in "a red car" — or predicatively, after a linking verb — as in "the car is red". Many adjectives can form comparatives and superlatives, by inflection (big, bigger, biggest) or with the adverbs more and most. Some adjectives, such as former or mere, are purely attributive, while others cannot precede a noun.

Adjectives across languages

Not all languages have a distinct adjective class. In some languages, property words behave grammatically like verbs ("stative verbs"), needing no copula; in others they pattern with nouns. In languages such as French and Spanish, adjectives typically agree with their nouns in gender and number and usually follow the noun. Japanese has two distinct types of adjectival words, inflecting i-adjectives and nominal na-adjectives.

Ordering and types

When several adjectives precede a noun, English speakers follow a fairly consistent order of opinion, size, age, shape, colour, origin, material and purpose — "a lovely small old square brown French wooden table". Grammarians also distinguish descriptive adjectives from determiners and quantifiers, and recognise participial adjectives formed from verbs (interesting, broken) and compound adjectives (well-known).
