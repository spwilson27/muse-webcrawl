Anaphora

Churchill's "We shall fight..." speeches are textbook examples of anaphora.

Anaphora has two related meanings in the study of language. In rhetoric, an anaphora (Greek: ἀναφορά, "carrying back") is a device that consists of repeating a sequence of words at the beginnings of neighboring clauses, thereby lending them emphasis. In linguistics, an anaphor is an expression whose interpretation depends on another expression in context — typically a pronoun such as "she" or "it" referring back to an earlier noun phrase, its antecedent.

Rhetorical anaphora

Repetition at the start of successive phrases is one of the oldest poetic and oratorical devices, abundant in the Hebrew Bible's Psalms and in classical oratory. Famous modern examples include Winston Churchill's "we shall fight on the beaches, we shall fight on the landing grounds..." (1940) and Martin Luther King Jr.'s repeated "I have a dream" (1963). Its converse, repetition at the ends of clauses, is called epistrophe; the combination of both is symploce.

Anaphora in linguistics

In syntax and semantics, anaphora is central to the study of binding and coreference: in "Mary saw herself", "herself" is an anaphor bound by "Mary", while in "Mary arrived. She sat down", the pronoun "she" is anaphoric on "Mary". Resolving anaphors is a major task in natural language processing, where systems must determine which entity each pronoun or definite description refers to.
