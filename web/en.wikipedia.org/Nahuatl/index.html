Nahuatl

Nahuatl (English: /ˈnɑːwɑːtəl/; Nahuatl: Nāhuatlahtōlli) is a language of the Uto-Aztecan family spoken by about 1.7 million Nahua people in central Mexico, making it the most widely spoken Indigenous language of Mexico. It was the language of the Aztec Empire and served as a lingua franca across Mesoamerica.

History

Classical Nahuatl was the prestige variety of Tenochtitlan; after the Spanish conquest it was written in the Latin alphabet by friars and Nahua scribes, producing a large colonial literature including the Florentine Codex compiled under Bernardino de Sahagún. Nahuatl loaned many words to Spanish and English, including tomato, chocolate, avocado and coyote.

Characteristics

Nahuatl is an agglutinative, polysynthetic language with complex verb morphology, an absolutive suffix (-tl/-tli/-li/-in), and a distinction between animate and inanimate nouns. Modern varieties, sometimes considered separate languages, are spoken mainly in Puebla, Veracruz, Hidalgo, Guerrero and San Luis Potosí. Under Mexico's General Law of Linguistic Rights, Nahuatl is recognised as a national language.
