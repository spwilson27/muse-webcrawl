Erie Canal

The Erie Canal is a historic canal in upstate New York, United States, that runs east–west between the Hudson River and Lake Erie. Completed in 1825, the canal was the first navigable waterway connecting the Atlantic Ocean to the Great Lakes, vastly reducing the cost of transporting people and goods between the East Coast and the interior, and is widely regarded as an engineering marvel that helped make New York City the chief port of the United States.

History

Proposed as early as the eighteenth century and championed by New York governor DeWitt Clinton — derisively, by opponents, as "Clinton's Ditch" — construction began on 4 July 1817 near Rome, New York. Built largely by immigrant labour with little formal engineering expertise available in the young republic, the original canal was 363 miles (584 km) long, 40 feet (12 m) wide and 4 feet (1.2 m) deep, with 83 locks to climb a total rise of about 565 feet. It opened on 26 October 1825, when Clinton travelled by packet boat from Buffalo to New York City and poured water from Lake Erie into the Atlantic in the "Wedding of the Waters" ceremony.

Impact and enlargement

Freight charges between Buffalo and New York fell from about $100 per ton by wagon to under $10 by canal, and the journey shortened from weeks to days. The canal spurred the growth of Buffalo, Rochester, Syracuse, and Albany, funnelled settlers into the Old Northwest, and anchored New York's commercial supremacy. Enlargements followed in 1836–1862; the modern New York State Barge Canal opened in 1918 along a partly new course. Today the Erie Canal is managed as part of the New York State Canal System, used chiefly for recreation, and recognised as a National Heritage Corridor.
