Extended mind thesis

In the philosophy of mind, the extended mind thesis (EMT) says that the mind does not exclusively reside in the brain or even the body, but extends into the physical world. On this view, external objects can play a constitutive role in cognitive processes when an agent interacts with them in the right way.

Background

The thesis was proposed by Andy Clark and David Chalmers in their 1998 paper "The Extended Mind". Their central example contrasts Inga, who remembers the location of the Museum of Modern Art from biological memory, with Otto, who has Alzheimer's disease and relies on a notebook he carries everywhere. Clark and Chalmers argue that Otto's notebook plays the same functional role as Inga's memory, so Otto's beliefs extend into the notebook.

The parity principle

The argument rests on the parity principle: if, as we confront some task, a part of the world functions as a process which, were it done in the head, we would have no hesitation in recognizing as part of the cognitive process, then that part of the world is part of the cognitive process.

Criticism

Critics such as Fred Adams and Ken Aizawa have raised the "coupling-constitution fallacy" objection, arguing that causal coupling with an external object does not make that object part of cognition, and have proposed that genuine cognitive processes require certain "marks of the cognitive" such as non-derived content. Clark has replied that the thesis concerns the vehicles of cognition rather than its contents.
