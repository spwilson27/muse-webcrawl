Acute radiation syndrome

Acute radiation syndrome (ARS), also known as radiation sickness or radiation poisoning, is a collection of health effects caused by exposure of most or all of the body to a high dose of penetrating ionizing radiation over a short period of time. It follows events such as nuclear accidents, detonations, and certain industrial or medical accidents involving radioactive sources.

Stages and subsyndromes

ARS typically progresses through a prodromal stage (nausea, vomiting, fatigue), a latent stage of apparent improvement, a manifest illness stage, and then recovery or death. Depending on dose, three classic subsyndromes dominate: the hematopoietic syndrome (roughly 0.7–10 Gy), with loss of blood cell production and infection risk; the gastrointestinal syndrome (above roughly 10 Gy), with destruction of the intestinal lining; and the cerebrovascular / neurovascular syndrome at very high doses, which is rapidly fatal. Cutaneous radiation injury to the skin often accompanies these.

Causes and history

Major episodes include the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki, the Chernobyl disaster of 1986 (in which 134 plant workers and firefighters were confirmed to have ARS, 28 dying in the following months), and accidents such as the Goiânia incident in Brazil. Dose is measured in grays (Gy); the LD50/60 for humans without treatment is approximately 4 Gy.

Treatment

There is no cure that reverses ARS, but supportive care — infection control, transfusions, and colony-stimulating factors such as filgrastim — improves survival in the hematopoietic syndrome. Treatment of internal contamination uses decorporation agents such as Prussian blue for caesium and DTPA for transuranics.
