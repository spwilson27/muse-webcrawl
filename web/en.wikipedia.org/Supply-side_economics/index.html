Supply-side economics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Supply-side economics is a school of macroeconomic thought arguing that economic growth is most effectively created by lowering taxes and decreasing regulation to increase the supply of goods and services. Consumer demand, on this view, follows from greater employment and production. The approach is closely associated with the policies of US President Ronald Reagan in the 1980s, often called "Reaganomics".

Key ideas

Supply-siders such as Arthur Laffer, Jude Wanniski, Jack Kemp, and Robert Mundell argued that high marginal tax rates discourage work, saving, and investment, and that cutting them can raise output and possibly even revenue — an idea popularised by the Laffer curve. Policy prescriptions typically include lower marginal income and capital-gains tax rates, deregulation, free trade, and restrained government spending.

History and evaluation

The ideas influenced the Economic Recovery Tax Act of 1981 in the United States and similar policies under Margaret Thatcher in Britain. Economists remain divided: supporters credit supply-side measures with contributing to the long 1980s expansion and investment; critics argue the tax cuts mainly increased deficits and inequality, that growth was chiefly driven by monetary policy and demand recovery, and that broad-based evidence that tax cuts "pay for themselves" is lacking. The term "trickle-down economics" is frequently used by critics, though rarely by proponents.
