Eddy current

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Eddy currents flow in closed loops within a conductor subjected to a changing magnetic field.

Eddy currents (also called Foucault currents) are loops of electrical current induced within conductors by a changing magnetic field in the conductor according to Faraday's law of induction or by the relative motion of a conductor in a magnetic field. Eddy currents flow in closed loops within conductors, in planes perpendicular to the magnetic field.

They can be induced within nearby stationary conductors by a time-varying magnetic field created by an AC electromagnet or transformer, or by relative motion between a magnet and a nearby conductor. In accordance with Lenz's law, the induced currents create a magnetic field that opposes the change that produced them.

History

Eddy currents were first observed in 1824 by François Arago, who noticed that a rotating copper disc could deflect a compass needle ("Arago's rotations"). Michael Faraday supplied the theoretical explanation through electromagnetic induction in 1831. The French physicist Léon Foucault gave the phenomenon its detailed study, hence the alternative name.

Properties

The magnitude of the current depends on the strength of the magnetic field, the area of the loop, the rate of change of flux, and the conductivity of the material. At higher frequencies, eddy currents concentrate near the surface of the conductor (the skin effect).

Eddy current loss

In transformers and electric motors, eddy currents in the iron core dissipate energy as heat — a loss engineers reduce by building cores from thin, mutually insulated laminations oriented parallel to the field, or by using high-resistivity materials such as ferrites.

Applications

  • Induction heating — cooking hobs and metal melting
  • Eddy-current brakes — trains and roller coasters
  • Non-destructive testing — detecting cracks and corrosion in metal structures
  • Metal detectors and proximity sensors
  • Magnetic levitation effects
