Musée des Beaux Arts (poem)

Author: W. H. Auden
Written: 1938
First published: 1939
Subject: Suffering and indifference, via Old Master paintings

"Musée des Beaux Arts" is a poem by W. H. Auden, written in December 1938 after a visit to the Royal Museums of Fine Arts of Belgium (Musées royaux des Beaux-Arts) in Brussels, and first published in 1939. It is one of Auden's most admired short poems, a meditation on how human suffering takes place while everyday life goes indifferently on.

Content

The poem opens with the famous lines: "About suffering they were never wrong, / The Old Masters: how well they understood / Its human position: how it takes place / While someone else is eating or opening a window or just walking dully along". The first stanza evokes scenes from Bruegel and other painters — children skating on a pond while the martyrdom proceeds unnoticed, dogs living their "doggy life," a horse scratching its behind on a tree. The second stanza turns to Bruegel's Landscape with the Fall of Icarus: the ploughman ploughs on, the sun shines as it should, and the "expensive delicate ship" sails calmly away from the boy falling out of the sky — "had somewhere to get to and sailed calmly on."

Analysis and legacy

Written on the eve of the Second World War, the poem has often been read as a commentary on the world's indifference to catastrophe, though its tone is reflective rather than accusatory: indifference is presented as the human condition the Old Masters captured with honesty. The painting Auden describes is now thought to be a copy of a lost Bruegel original. The poem is widely anthologised and taught, and its Icarus imagery has inspired later works, including William Carlos Williams's poem "Landscape with the Fall of Icarus" and much commentary on the ethics of attention.
