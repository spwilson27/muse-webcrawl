Daisy (advertisement)

ClientLyndon B. Johnson 1964 presidential campaign
AgencyDoyle Dane Bernbach (DDB)
Aired7 September 1964 (NBC, once as a paid spot)
Running time60 seconds
Child actorMonique Luiz
Voice-overLyndon B. Johnson (audio from a speech)

"Daisy", sometimes referred to as "Daisy Girl" or "Peace, Little Girl", is an American political advertisement that aired on television during the 1964 United States presidential election by incumbent president Lyndon B. Johnson's campaign. Though officially aired only once, it is considered one of the most important factors in Johnson's landslide victory over the Republican Party's candidate Barry Goldwater, and a major turning point in political and advertising history.

Description

The ad opens with a three-year-old girl (Monique Luiz) standing in a meadow, picking petals from a daisy and counting them, her counting naive and out of order. As she reaches "nine," a male voice begins a countdown as if for a missile launch; the camera zooms into her eye until the pupil fills the screen, which is replaced by the flash and mushroom cloud of a nuclear explosion. Over the explosion, Johnson's voice declares: "These are the stakes! To make a world in which all of God's children can live, or to go into the dark. We must either love each other, or we must die." An announcer concludes: "Vote for President Johnson on November 3. The stakes are too high for you to stay home."

Background and impact

The ad was created by Doyle Dane Bernbach, with Tony Schwartz responsible for the sound design concept (adapting his "daisy" audio concept). It implicitly attacked Goldwater's hawkish statements on nuclear weapons without naming him. Republicans protested, and the spot was pulled after one paid airing on NBC's Monday Night at the Movies on 7 September 1964 — but news coverage replayed it repeatedly, multiplying its effect. Johnson won the election with 61.1% of the popular vote.

The "Daisy" ad is widely credited with creating the modern form of negative political advertising and demonstrating the power of television imagery in campaigns. It has been parodied and imitated many times, and the girl in the ad, Monique Luiz (later Monique Corzilius), reappeared in campaign-related retrospectives.
