Aerial reconnaissance

Aerial reconnaissance is reconnaissance for military or strategic purposes carried out using reconnaissance aircraft. The role can include intelligence collection through visual observation and photography or other sensors, monitoring enemy movements, artillery spotting, and assessing bomb damage. It is one of the oldest military uses of flight: observers in balloons directed artillery in the American Civil War and the wars of the 19th century.

History

In the First World War, aircraft and systematic aerial photography transformed reconnaissance, producing trench maps and stereoscopic imagery. The Second World War saw specialised high-speed, high-altitude photo-reconnaissance aircraft such as the de Havilland Mosquito and photographic Spitfires. During the Cold War, strategic reconnaissance by the U-2 and SR-71 Blackbird, and later by satellites, became central to superpower intelligence; aerial photographs revealed the Soviet missiles in Cuba in 1962.

Modern practice

Today reconnaissance is performed by satellites, high-altitude aircraft, and especially unmanned aerial vehicles (drones) carrying electro-optical, infrared, and radar sensors that stream imagery in real time. Imagery intelligence (IMINT) analysis turns this material into assessments for commanders and governments.
