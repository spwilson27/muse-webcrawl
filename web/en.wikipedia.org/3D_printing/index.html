3D printing

A desktop 3D printer extruding molten thermoplastic filament to build an object layer by layer.

3D printing, or additive manufacturing, is the construction of a three-dimensional object from a CAD model or a digital 3D model. The term may also refer to a variety of processes in which material is deposited, joined or solidified under computer control to create a three-dimensional object, with material being added together (such as plastics, liquids or powder grains being fused), typically layer by layer.

One of the key advantages of 3D printing is the ability to produce very complex shapes or geometries that would be otherwise infeasible to construct by hand, including hollow parts or parts with internal lattice structures. The most widely used 3D-printing process is a material extrusion technique called fused deposition modeling (FDM) or fused filament fabrication (FFF).

History

The earliest additive manufacturing equipment and materials were developed in the 1980s. In 1981, Hideo Kodama of the Nagoya Municipal Industrial Research Institute published the first account of a rapid prototyping system using photopolymers. In 1984 Chuck Hull invented stereolithography and founded 3D Systems, while Scott Crump developed fused deposition modeling in the late 1980s and commercialised it through Stratasys. The expiration of key patents in the 2000s, together with open-source projects such as RepRap (founded by Adrian Bowyer in 2005), drove a dramatic fall in the price of desktop printers and a wave of consumer interest in the 2010s.

Processes

  • Material extrusion — a filament of thermoplastic is melted and deposited (FDM/FFF).
  • Vat photopolymerisation — liquid resin is cured by light (stereolithography, DLP, LCD).
  • Powder bed fusion — powder is fused by a laser or electron beam (SLS, SLM, EBM), enabling metal parts.
  • Binder jetting — a liquid binding agent is selectively deposited onto a powder bed.
  • Material jetting and sheet lamination — droplets of material, or bonded sheets, are built up layer by layer.

Applications

3D printing is used for rapid prototyping, customised medical implants and prosthetics, dental products, aerospace and automotive components, tooling, architectural models, fashion, and food. Technologies such as deep learning-assisted generative design are increasingly combined with additive manufacturing to optimise part geometry. Large-scale construction printing has produced experimental houses, while bioprinting research aims to fabricate tissues and organs.
