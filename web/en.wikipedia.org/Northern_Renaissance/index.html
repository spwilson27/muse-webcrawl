Northern Renaissance

The Northern Renaissance was the Renaissance that occurred in Europe north of the Alps, roughly from the late 15th century through the 16th century. It spread from the Italian Renaissance through France, the Burgundian Netherlands, the Holy Roman Empire, England, and the rest of northern Europe, developing distinctive characteristics shaped by Gothic traditions, local conditions, and—after 1517—the Protestant Reformation.

Characteristics

Whereas Italian art emphasised classical antiquity, linear perspective, and idealised human anatomy, Northern art retained a Gothic inheritance of meticulous surface detail, symbolism, and intense observation of nature. The perfection of oil painting in the Low Countries—associated with Jan van Eyck—allowed luminous glazes and minute textures. Northern humanism, led by Desiderius Erasmus, focused more on Christian scholarship, biblical philology, and social satire than on classical pagan themes.

Painting

The Early Netherlandish masters—Jan van Eyck, Rogier van der Weyden, Robert Campin, and Hugo van der Goes—transformed panel painting. Later figures include Hieronymus Bosch, with his fantastical moralising imagery; Albrecht Dürer, who fused Northern detail with Italian theory; Hans Holbein the Younger, portraitist at the Tudor court; and Pieter Bruegel the Elder, famed for peasant scenes and landscapes.

Printing and learning

The movable-type printing press of Johannes Gutenberg (c. 1450) in Mainz revolutionised the diffusion of texts, enabling the rapid spread of humanist scholarship and Reformation pamphlets. Erasmus's editions of the Greek New Testament and his satire In Praise of Folly (1509) exemplify Northern humanist print culture. In England, the movement culminated later in the Elizabethan age of Shakespeare.
