p53, also known as tumour protein P53, is a protein encoded in humans by the TP53 gene on chromosome 17. A transcription factor and the product of a tumour suppressor gene, p53 regulates the cell cycle and prevents cancer formation — functions that led David Lane to dub it the "guardian of the genome". TP53 is the most frequently mutated gene in human cancer, altered in roughly half of all tumours.

Function

In healthy cells p53 levels are kept very low by its negative regulator MDM2, which targets it for degradation. DNA damage, oncogene activation, or other stresses stabilize p53, which then activates the transcription of target genes. Depending on the extent of damage, the result is cell-cycle arrest (via p21) to allow DNA repair, cellular senescence, or programmed cell death (apoptosis, via BAX, PUMA, and NOXA). p53 also regulates metabolism, autophagy, and antioxidant responses.

Cancer and disease

Most cancer-associated TP53 mutations are missense changes in the DNA-binding domain that abolish normal function and can act dominantly or even gain new oncogenic functions. Germline mutations cause Li–Fraumeni syndrome, a hereditary predisposition to early-onset cancers including breast cancer, sarcomas, brain tumours, and leukaemia. Restoring p53 function in tumours is a longstanding goal of drug development; MDM2 inhibitors and mutant-p53 reactivators have been widely investigated.

Discovery

p53 was identified in 1979 as a protein bound to the SV40 large T antigen in virus-transformed cells. Initially mistaken for an oncogene because early clones came from tumours, it was recognized as a tumour suppressor in 1989 through work by Bert Vogelstein and others.
