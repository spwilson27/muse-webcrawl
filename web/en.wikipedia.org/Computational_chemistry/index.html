Computational chemistry

FieldTheoretical chemistry, computer simulation
MethodsAb initio quantum chemistry, DFT, molecular mechanics, molecular dynamics, semi-empirical methods
ApplicationsDrug design, materials science, catalysis, spectroscopy
Nobel recognition1998 (Kohn, Pople); 2013 (Karplus, Levitt, Warshel)

Computational chemistry is a branch of chemistry that uses computer simulations to assist in solving chemical problems. It employs methods of theoretical chemistry, incorporated into computer programs, to calculate the structures and properties of molecules and materials — from electronic energies and geometries to reaction pathways, spectra, and the dynamics of large biomolecular systems.

Methods

  • Ab initio quantum methods — solve approximations to the Schrödinger equation from first principles: Hartree–Fock theory and post-Hartree–Fock corrections (MP2, coupled cluster) that recover electron correlation.
  • Density functional theory (DFT) — computes the electronic structure from the electron density rather than the full wavefunction, offering a favorable accuracy-to-cost ratio that made it the workhorse of the field.
  • Semi-empirical methods — simplify quantum calculations with parameters fitted to experiment, enabling much larger systems.
  • Molecular mechanics — models atoms and bonds classically with force fields, allowing geometry optimization and molecular dynamics simulations of proteins and materials over time.
  • Hybrid QM/MM — treats a reactive region quantum mechanically within a classically modeled environment, recognized by the 2013 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

History and impact

The field grew with electronic computers from the 1950s–60s, accelerated by programs such as John Pople's Gaussian series, and was honored by the 1998 Nobel Prize to Walter Kohn (density functional theory) and Pople (computational methods). Today it is integral to pharmaceutical discovery (docking, free-energy calculations), catalyst and battery-materials design, and the interpretation of experimental spectra, and it increasingly combines physics-based simulation with machine learning.
