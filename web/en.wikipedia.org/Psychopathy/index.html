Psychopathy is a personality construct characterised by enduring patterns of callousness, lack of empathy and remorse, superficial charm, manipulativeness, impulsivity, and antisocial behaviour. Although long discussed in psychiatry — from Hervey Cleckley's The Mask of Sanity (1941) onward — psychopathy is not a stand-alone diagnosis in the DSM-5, where it overlaps with, but is not identical to, antisocial personality disorder. The most widely used research and forensic instrument is the Hare Psychopathy Checklist–Revised (PCL-R), a 20-item scale scored from interviews and file records.

Traits and models

The PCL-R distinguishes interpersonal-affective traits (glibness, grandiosity, deceit, shallow affect, lack of empathy) from lifestyle-antisocial traits (impulsivity, irresponsibility, poor behavioural controls, criminal versatility). The triarchic model describes psychopathy in terms of boldness, meanness, and disinhibition. Prevalence in the general population is estimated around 1%, rising to roughly 15–25% among incarcerated offenders in North American studies.

Causes and treatment

Research implicates both genetic factors and early environment; neurobiological studies report reduced fear conditioning and amygdala reactivity and dysfunction in prefrontal regions. Psychopathy has historically been considered difficult to treat, and some interventions with adult offenders have appeared ineffective or counterproductive, though structured early interventions with callous-unemotional youth show more promise. The construct remains controversial regarding its boundaries, its application in legal sentencing, and the stigma attached to the label.
