Onchocerciasis

Also calledRiver blindness
CauseOnchocerca volvulus (nematode)
VectorSimulium blackflies
TreatmentIvermectin; doxycycline

Onchocerciasis, commonly known as river blindness, is a disease caused by infection with the parasitic filarial worm Onchocerca volvulus, transmitted between people by the bites of blackflies of the genus Simulium, which breed in fast-flowing rivers and streams — hence the disease's name. It is one of the world's leading infectious causes of blindness.

Disease

Adult worms live for 10–15 years in fibrous nodules (onchocercomas) under the skin, producing millions of microscopic larvae (microfilariae) that migrate through skin and eyes. Most symptoms come from the immune response to dying microfilariae: intense itching, depigmentation ("leopard skin"), thickened skin ("lizard skin"), and, in the eye, inflammation leading through sclerosing keratitis and chorioretinitis to blindness.

Epidemiology and control

More than 99 percent of cases occur in sub-Saharan Africa; smaller foci exist in Yemen and in the Americas. Mass drug administration with ivermectin — donated by Merck under the Mectizan Donation Program since 1987 — kills microfilariae and, given annually or twice yearly for many years, interrupts transmission. Doxycycline kills the worms' Wolbachia bacterial endosymbionts and thereby sterilises and shortens the life of adults. Large control programmes (OCP, APOC and their successors) have protected hundreds of millions of people, and elimination of transmission has been verified in Colombia, Ecuador, Mexico and Guatemala, with several African countries approaching the same milestone.
