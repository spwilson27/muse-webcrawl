Train ferry

A train ferry is a ship (ferry) designed to carry railway rolling stock. Typically, one level of the ship is fitted with railway tracks, and the vessel has a door at the front and/or rear to give access to the wharves. In the United States, train ferries are sometimes referred to as "car ferries", as distinguished from an automobile ferry that transports automobiles. The wharf (sometimes called a "slip") has a ramp, and a linkspan or "apron", balanced by weights, that connects the railway proper to the ship, allowing for the water level to rise and fall with the tides.

History

The first train ferry, the Leviathan, designed by Thomas Bouch, entered service in 1849–1850 across the Firth of Forth in Scotland. Bouch's successor route across the Firth of Tay ended in the Tay Bridge disaster of 1879. Train ferries later became vital links where bridges were impractical: across Lake Baikal during construction of the Trans-Siberian Railway, across the Great Lakes of North America, between Denmark and Germany (the "Bird Flight Line"), across the Strait of Messina between mainland Italy and Sicily, and on the Caspian Sea.

Operations

Loading requires shunting wagons or coaches onto the ship's tracks in a balanced sequence, using an adjustable linkspan to align rails. Because trains must be broken into short cuts and secured against rolling, train ferries are slow to load and have largely been replaced by bridges and tunnels where feasible, such as the Channel Tunnel and the Øresund Bridge. Surviving operations include the Sicily service, the Caspian Sea routes, routes in China across the Qiongzhou Strait to Hainan, and some services on Lake Van in Turkey and in North America.
