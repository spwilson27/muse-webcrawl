Situated learning

Situated learning Key figures: Jean Lave; Étienne Wenger
Key concepts: Legitimate peripheral participation; communities of practice
Key text: Situated Learning (1991)

Situated learning is a theory of learning developed by anthropologist Jean Lave and educational theorist Étienne Wenger, first set out in their 1991 book Situated Learning: Legitimate Peripheral Participation. The theory holds that learning is not primarily the internalisation of abstract knowledge but a fundamentally social process, situated in a specific context and embedded within a particular social and physical environment.

Legitimate peripheral participation

The central concept of the theory is legitimate peripheral participation. Newcomers join a community of practice — such as a group of tailors, midwives, butchers or recovering alcoholics, examples studied by Lave — and initially participate in simple, low-risk tasks at the periphery of the community. As they gain competence and trust, they move toward fuller participation in the community's core activities. Learning, identity and membership in the community are thus bound together.

Communities of practice

Wenger later developed the related concept of communities of practice, groups of people who share a concern or passion for something they do and learn how to do it better through regular interaction. The framework has been applied widely in education, organisational studies, healthcare training and online learning design.

Criticism

Critics have argued that situated learning underplays the role of explicit instruction and individual cognition, and that the emphasis on participation makes the theory difficult to apply in formal schooling, where learners are not yet legitimate participants in the practices they study. Others question how well the model transfers from informal apprenticeship settings to classroom contexts.
