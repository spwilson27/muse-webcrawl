Human Microbiome Project

Human Microbiome Project
Launched2007
SponsorU.S. National Institutes of Health (NIH Common Fund)
Budget~$170 million (first phase)
GoalCharacterize the microbial communities of the healthy human body

The Human Microbiome Project (HMP) was a United States National Institutes of Health research initiative launched in 2007 to improve understanding of the microbial flora involved in human health and disease. Modeled on the Human Genome Project, it aimed to identify and characterize the microorganisms found in association with both healthy and diseased humans.

Phase one

The first phase (2007–2014) sequenced samples from some 300 healthy volunteers at five major body sites — the gut, mouth, skin, nose, and vagina — using 16S rRNA sequencing and whole-genome shotgun metagenomics, and sequenced hundreds of reference genomes of cultured bacteria. Landmark papers in 2012 reported that healthy adults carry thousands of microbial species, that microbial metabolic pathways are more stable across people than species composition, and estimated that microbial cells in the body are roughly comparable in number to human cells.

Phase two

The Integrative Human Microbiome Project (iHMP), the second phase, followed cohorts in pregnancy and preterm birth, inflammatory bowel disease, and prediabetes, collecting longitudinal multi-omic data — host and microbial genomes, transcripts, proteins, and metabolites — to study how the microbiome changes with disease.

Significance

The HMP created reference datasets, computational tools, and ethical frameworks that underpin modern microbiome research, and helped establish links between the microbiome and conditions ranging from obesity and inflammatory bowel disease to responses to drugs.
