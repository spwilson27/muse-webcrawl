Storage ring

A storage ring is a type of circular particle accelerator in which a beam of charged particles circulates at constant energy for hours at a time, held on a closed orbit by bending and focusing magnets. Unlike a synchrotron, which repeatedly ramps its magnets to accelerate the beam, a storage ring is optimised to keep the beam stored stably at a fixed energy.

Uses

Storage rings serve two great purposes. In particle physics, two counter-rotating beams can be stored and collided head-on, as in the LEP and LHC colliders at CERN, the Tevatron, and electron–positron factories such as KEKB and BEPCII; storing antimatter particles such as antiprotons and positrons is essential because they can only be produced in small numbers. In photon science, electron storage rings are operated as synchrotron light sources: as the electrons are bent by magnets and undulators they emit intense X-rays used at beamlines for crystallography, materials science and imaging. Facilities include the ESRF, APS, SPring-8, Diamond Light Source and MAX IV.

Beam physics

A stored beam's lifetime is limited by scattering on residual gas, intra-beam scattering, and — for electrons — the balance between synchrotron radiation losses (replaced by radio-frequency cavities) and quantum excitation. Radiation damping in electron rings naturally shrinks the beam, enabling the extremely small emittances modern light sources exploit; "ultimate" or diffraction-limited storage rings use multi-bend achromat lattices to push emittance lower still.
