Destroyers for Bases Agreement

The Destroyers for Bases Agreement was an agreement between the United States and the United Kingdom concluded on 2 September 1940, by which the U.S. Navy transferred fifty mothballed destroyers of the Caldwell, Wickes and Clemson classes to the Royal Navy in exchange for 99-year rent-free leases of naval and air base sites in British territories across the western Atlantic and Caribbean. The deal marked a decisive step away from American neutrality in the Second World War, more than a year before the United States formally entered the conflict.

Background

After the fall of France in June 1940, Britain stood alone against Germany and desperately needed escorts for the Battle of the Atlantic, where U-boats were sinking merchant shipping faster than it could be replaced. Prime Minister Winston Churchill repeatedly pressed President Franklin D. Roosevelt for fifty or sixty of the United States' over-age destroyers held in reserve. American neutrality legislation and public opinion constrained Roosevelt, but an opinion from Attorney General Robert H. Jackson found the transfer lawful if certified as non-essential to U.S. defence and framed as an exchange rather than a sale.

Terms and reception

Britain received fifty flush-deck destroyers, renamed for towns common to both countries (the Town class). Many were in poor condition and required extensive refits, but they filled convoy escort gaps in late 1940 and several were in turn transferred to the Soviet Union later in the war. One, HMS Campbeltown (formerly USS Buchanan), was expended as a bomb ship in the St Nazaire Raid of March 1942. In return the United States obtained base rights from Newfoundland to British Guiana, building airfields and naval stations that underpinned the wartime defence of the Americas; some leases and sites remained in use for decades after 1945.

Significance

Roosevelt described the exchange as the most important American action since the Louisiana Purchase; historians see it as the moment the United States became a de facto co-belligerent in the Atlantic. It paved the way for the far larger Lend-Lease programme enacted in March 1941.
