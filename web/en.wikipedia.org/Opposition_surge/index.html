Opposition surge

Opposition surge
FieldPhotometry, planetary science
MechanismsShadow hiding; coherent backscatter
Observed onMoon, asteroids, Saturn's rings, icy satellites
Also calledOpposition effect; Seeliger effect

The opposition surge (also called the opposition effect or Seeliger effect) is the sharp brightening of a rough surface or particulate medium—such as a planetary regolith—when it is illuminated from directly behind the observer, that is, at a phase angle near zero. It is most familiar from the full Moon, whose brightness increases disproportionately in the last few degrees before exact opposition.

Mechanisms

Two physical mechanisms are recognised. In shadow hiding, grains of regolith cast shadows on their neighbours at oblique illumination; as the Sun moves directly behind the observer, those shadows fall behind the grains that cast them and disappear from view, so the surface returns more light. This produces a broad surge extending over tens of degrees of phase angle.

In coherent backscatter, light waves travelling along reciprocal paths through a scattering medium interfere constructively in the exact backward direction, while interfering randomly at other angles. This produces a narrow, intense spike within about one or two degrees of zero phase, prominent on bright, icy bodies.

Observations

The effect is measured in the phase curves of the Moon, asteroids, comets, and planetary rings. Saturn's rings brighten dramatically at opposition because their particles shadow one another; the surge of the icy moons of the outer planets shows a strong coherent-backscatter component. Apollo astronauts observed a related phenomenon—bright haloes around their own shadows on the lunar surface (the "opposition halo" or heiligenschein-like glow) caused by the same shadow-hiding geometry. Accounting for the surge is essential when comparing the intrinsic albedos of solar-system bodies observed at different phase angles.
