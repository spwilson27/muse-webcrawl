Eugène Freyssinet (13 July 1879 – 8 June 1962) was a French structural and civil engineer. He is regarded as the father of prestressed concrete, a technology he patented in 1928 that transformed bridge and building construction worldwide.

Early work

Trained at the École Polytechnique and the École des Ponts et Chaussées, Freyssinet built daring reinforced-concrete arch bridges, including the Pont du Veurdre and the Pont de Plougastel (1930), then the world's longest concrete arch span. At Orly he built vast parabolic airship hangars of corrugated concrete shell. His observations of concrete creep and shrinkage in arches led him to the idea of deliberately pre-compressing concrete with tensioned steel.

Prestressed concrete

Freyssinet's 1928 patent covered prestressing by bonded, pre-tensioned high-strength steel wires, anchored with his conical "Freyssinet anchorages" and tensioned with double-acting jacks. Prestressing counteracts tensile stresses in service, allowing longer, slimmer spans. He founded the firm STUP to develop and license the system.

Later career

Prestressed concrete was applied to bridges, dams, pressure vessels, railway sleepers, and the containment structures of nuclear plants. Freyssinet's work underlies much of modern post-tensioned construction, and international societies for concrete engineering commemorate his name.
