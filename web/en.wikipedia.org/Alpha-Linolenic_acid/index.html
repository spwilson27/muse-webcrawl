α-Linolenic acid

IUPAC name: (9Z,12Z,15Z)-octadeca-9,12,15-trienoic acid
Formula: C18H30O2
Shorthand: ALA, 18:3 n−3

α-Linolenic acid (ALA) is an n−3 (omega-3) essential fatty acid: an 18-carbon carboxylic acid with three cis double bonds at positions 9, 12, and 15. It is called "essential" because mammals cannot synthesise it and must obtain it from the diet. ALA is found in many plant seeds and oils, notably flaxseed (linseed), chia, perilla, hemp, walnut, and canola (rapeseed) oils.

Biochemistry

In animals, dietary ALA can be elongated and desaturated to the longer omega-3 fatty acids eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), though in humans this conversion is limited — typically well under 10% to EPA and much less to DHA. ALA itself is also oxidised for energy. In plants, ALA is a major membrane lipid component and a precursor of the signalling compound jasmonic acid. It should not be confused with γ-linolenic acid (an omega-6 fatty acid) or with lipoic acid, which is also sometimes abbreviated ALA.

Dietary sources and health

Flaxseed oil is the richest common source, at roughly 55% ALA; chia and perilla oils are comparably rich, while canola oil contains about 9–11%. Adequate intakes of around 1.1–1.6 g per day are recommended for adults in several national guidelines. Epidemiological studies associate higher ALA intake with modest reductions in cardiovascular risk, though evidence from trials is less conclusive than for marine omega-3s.

History

Linolenic acid was isolated in the nineteenth century and its structure established in the early twentieth; its essentiality for animals was demonstrated in feeding experiments in the 1930s, and its role as the parent of the omega-3 family was clarified by the work of Ralph Holman and others.
