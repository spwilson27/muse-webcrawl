United States courts of appeals

The United States courts of appeals are the intermediate appellate courts of the United States federal judiciary. They hear appeals from the district courts within their assigned geographic circuits, as well as appeals from certain federal administrative agencies. Because the Supreme Court reviews only a small fraction of cases, the courts of appeals are the final word on most federal litigation.

Structure

There are thirteen courts of appeals: eleven numbered circuits covering groups of states (First through Eleventh), the District of Columbia Circuit, and the Federal Circuit, which has nationwide jurisdiction over specialized subjects such as patent law and international trade. Judges are nominated by the president and confirmed by the Senate for life tenure. Cases are ordinarily heard by randomly assigned panels of three judges; occasionally a court sits en banc, with all active judges participating, to reconsider a panel decision or resolve an important question.

Procedure

The courts of appeals do not hold trials. They review the record of the district court for legal error, taking the facts as found by the trial court unless clearly erroneous. Litigants submit written briefs and may present oral argument. Published opinions of the circuit courts are binding precedent within their circuits, which can produce "circuit splits" that the Supreme Court may resolve. Tens of thousands of appeals are filed annually.

History

Congress created separate appellate circuit courts in the Judiciary Act of 1891 (the Evarts Act) to relieve the Supreme Court and the circuit trial courts of an overwhelming caseload. The circuit courts were fully separated from trial duties in 1911, and the modern structure with the Federal Circuit emerged in 1982.
