Star fort

A star fort seen from the air, showing its geometric bastions.

A star fort, or trace italienne, is a style of fortification developed in Italy during the 15th and 16th centuries in response to the introduction of gunpowder artillery. Its walls are arranged in a pattern of angular bastions projecting like the points of a star, a geometry related to the star polygon, so that defenders could direct interlocking fields of fire along every wall and eliminate the dead ground that plagued medieval curtain walls.

Design

Star forts replaced tall, thin medieval walls—easily shattered by cannon—with low, thick, earth-backed ramparts that absorbed shot. Pointed bastions at the corners mounted cannon and muskets covering the adjacent faces. Beyond the walls lay a sloped glacis exposing attackers to fire, plus ditches, ravelins and covered ways. The design was systematized in the 17th century by engineers such as the French marshal Vauban, whose fortresses and siege methods dominated European warfare.

Spread and examples

The style spread across Europe and to colonial outposts worldwide. Notable examples include the citadel of Palmanova in Italy, the fortress of Bourtange in the Netherlands, Fort Ticonderoga in New York and the star-shaped Fort Monroe in Virginia. Many European cities retain star-shaped outlines in their street plans where the walls were later demolished.

Decline

Star forts became obsolete in the 19th century with the advent of explosive shells and rifled artillery, which could destroy masonry bastions from afar, leading to dispersed polygonal and then concrete fortification systems.
