The Third French Republic (French: Troisième République) was the system of government adopted in France from 4 September 1870, after the fall of Napoleon III and the Second Empire in the Franco-Prussian War, until 10 July 1940, after France's defeat by Nazi Germany in the Second World War led to the creation of the Vichy government. At nearly seventy years it was the longest-lasting French regime since the Revolution of 1789.

History

Proclaimed in the aftermath of Sedan, the Republic survived the Paris Commune of 1871 and was given constitutional form by the Constitutional Laws of 1875. It endured chronic ministerial instability — more than a hundred governments — yet proved resilient through crises such as the Boulanger affair and the Dreyfus Affair (1894–1906), which divided the nation over the wrongful conviction of a Jewish artillery officer.

The Republic oversaw the consolidation of secular public education under Jules Ferry, the separation of church and state in 1905, and the expansion of the French colonial empire. France survived the enormous sacrifices of the First World War, emerging victorious in 1918, but the interwar years brought economic depression and political polarisation, including the Popular Front government of 1936.

Fall

After the German breakthrough of May–June 1940 and the armistice of 22 June, the National Assembly voted full powers to Marshal Philippe Pétain on 10 July 1940, effectively ending the Republic and inaugurating the authoritarian Vichy regime.
