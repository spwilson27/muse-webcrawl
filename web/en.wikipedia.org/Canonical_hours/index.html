Canonical hours

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The canonical hours are the divisions of the day in terms of fixed times of prayer in Christian liturgy. Also known as the Divine Office, the Liturgy of the Hours, or the opus Dei ("work of God"), the practice consists of psalms, hymns, scripture readings, and prayers recited at set times from before dawn to nightfall. The term derives from the canonical requirement—canon law obligation—binding clergy, religious, and in some traditions the faithful to pray them.

The hours

In the traditional Roman arrangement, the day's office comprises:

Matins (during the night or very early morning), Lauds (dawn prayer), Prime (first hour, about 6 a.m.), Terce (third hour, about 9 a.m.), Sext (sixth hour, noon), None (ninth hour, about 3 p.m.), Vespers (evening prayer at sunset), and Compline (night prayer before retiring). The names of Terce, Sext, and None survive in the English word "noon," which originally referred to the ninth hour.

Development

Fixed-hour prayer derives from Jewish practice and is attested in the early Church; the third-century Apostolic Tradition describes prayer at specific hours. Monastic communities, following the Rule of Saint Benedict in the sixth century, systematized the office around the chanting of the entire Psalter each week. The Second Vatican Council's reforms simplified the Roman office into the modern Liturgy of the Hours, with Morning Prayer (Lauds), Evening Prayer (Vespers), Night Prayer (Compline), the Office of Readings, and daytime prayer.

The hours also structured civil timekeeping in medieval Europe: church bells announced the offices, and Books of Hours—lavishly illuminated devotional manuals for laypeople—were bestsellers of the late Middle Ages.
