A local anaesthetic (American English: local anesthetic) is a drug that reversibly blocks nerve conduction in the region where it is applied, producing loss of sensation—especially pain—without loss of consciousness. Chemically, nearly all agents share an aromatic ring, an intermediate linking chain (either an amide or an ester bond, by which the two classes are named), and an amine group.

History

The first local anaesthetic was cocaine, isolated from coca leaves and introduced into clinical practice in 1884, when Carl Koller demonstrated its use in eye surgery and William Halsted developed nerve block techniques; cocaine's toxicity and addictiveness drove the search for substitutes. Procaine (Novocain), synthesized by Alfred Einhorn in 1905, dominated for decades until Nils Löfgren synthesized lidocaine in 1943. Lidocaine, introduced clinically in 1948, set the modern standard for onset and safety and remains the most widely used agent.

Pharmacology

Local anaesthetics cross the nerve membrane in their uncharged form and then, in the charged form, bind the intracellular portion of voltage-gated sodium channels, stabilizing the inactivated state and stopping action-potential propagation. Potency correlates with lipid solubility, duration with protein binding, and onset with the proportion of uncharged drug (pKa) at tissue pH—so infection, which acidifies tissue, can make blocks fail. Amides are metabolized in the liver; esters are hydrolysed by plasma cholinesterase to para-aminobenzoic acid (PABA) derivatives, which are more often allergenic.

Clinical agents

Lidocaine has a rapid onset and intermediate duration and is used for infiltration, blocks, topical preparations, and intravenously for certain arrhythmias. Bupivacaine and ropivacaine are long-acting agents favoured for epidurals and peripheral blocks; articaine is popular in dentistry; prilocaine is combined with lidocaine in EMLA cream for skin anaesthesia. Agents are frequently combined with adrenaline to prolong action, or formulated in liposomes (liposomal bupivacaine) for extended postoperative analgesia.

Safety

Safety depends on respecting weight-based maximum doses and avoiding intravascular injection. Excessive plasma levels cause local anaesthetic systemic toxicity—perioral numbness, tinnitus, seizures, and in severe cases cardiovascular collapse—treated with 20% lipid emulsion. Prilocaine and benzocaine in high doses can cause methaemoglobinaemia. True allergy is rare and usually involves ester agents or preservatives.
