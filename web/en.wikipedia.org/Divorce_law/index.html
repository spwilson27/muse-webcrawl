Divorce law

Divorce law is the body of law governing the legal dissolution of a marriage. Divorce terminates a marriage and resolves the legal consequences between the former spouses, including the division of marital property, alimony (spousal support or maintenance), child custody and child support. The grounds, procedures and effects of divorce vary widely between legal systems and have changed substantially over time, generally in the direction of greater availability.

History

In many early legal systems divorce was available only to husbands, or only for grave causes such as adultery or abandonment. In Roman law divorce was relatively freely available in the classical period but was restricted under Christian emperors. The Roman Catholic Church regarded marriage as indissoluble, permitting only annulment or separation, a position that shaped European law for centuries; after the Reformation, Protestant jurisdictions permitted divorce on limited grounds. Secularisation from the eighteenth century onwards transferred jurisdiction to civil courts—France legalised divorce in 1792, banned it in 1816 and re-established it in 1884—while England and Wales allowed judicial divorce only from 1857 and broadened the grounds in 1937 and 1969. Ireland, Italy, Spain, Argentina and Chile legalised divorce only in the later twentieth century, and the Philippines remains one of the few places with no general civil divorce law.

Grounds for divorce

Traditional systems required proof of a matrimonial offence—a "fault" ground such as adultery, cruelty, desertion or conviction of a crime. Modern reforms have shifted toward breakdown-based grounds:

  • Fault divorce — granted on proof of a specified ground of misconduct by one spouse.
  • No-fault divorce — granted on the basis of irretrievable breakdown, separation for a stated period, or mutual consent, without proof of wrongdoing. California adopted the first modern no-fault statute in 1969, and by the early twenty-first century no-fault divorce was available throughout the United States and in most Western countries.
  • Summary divorce — simplified procedures for short marriages with no children and limited property.

Procedure and ancillary matters

Divorce proceedings are usually brought in the courts of the jurisdiction where one spouse resides. Alongside dissolution itself, courts determine the division of marital property (under equitable distribution or community property regimes), spousal support based on need and ability to pay, and arrangements for children. Custody decisions are governed by the best interests of the child, and child support is usually calculated by guideline formulas. Mediation and collaborative law are increasingly used to settle these matters without contested litigation.

Religious divorce

Because civil divorce may not affect religious status, some faiths have their own processes: Judaism requires a get delivered by the husband; Islam recognises talaq, khul' and judicial dissolution; the Catholic Church does not recognise divorce but may declare a marriage annulled, that is, never validly contracted.
