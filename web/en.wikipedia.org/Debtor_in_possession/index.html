Debtor in possession

A debtor in possession or DIP in United States bankruptcy law is a person or corporation that has filed a bankruptcy petition but remains in possession of property over which creditors have a claim secured by a lien. The concept is central to reorganization cases under Chapter 11 of the United States Bankruptcy Code, where section 1107(a) gives the debtor in possession most of the rights, powers, and duties of a bankruptcy trustee.

Powers and duties

Unlike liquidation under Chapter 7, where a trustee takes control, a Chapter 11 debtor ordinarily stays in charge of its business and assets as debtor in possession. The DIP may operate the business, use, sell, or lease property (with court approval for transactions outside the ordinary course), object to claims, and avoid certain pre-bankruptcy transfers using a trustee's "strong-arm" powers, including preferences and fraudulent transfers. The DIP owes fiduciary duties to creditors and the estate.

DIP financing

Because existing lenders' collateral is protected by the automatic stay, a DIP often needs new "DIP financing" under section 364 of the Code. To induce lenders to extend credit to a bankrupt company, courts may authorize superpriority administrative claims, liens on unencumbered assets, junior liens, or — controversially — "priming" liens senior to existing lienholders, provided those holders receive adequate protection. DIP loans frequently include milestones and covenants that effectively steer the course of the case.

Loss of status

On motion of a party in interest, the court may appoint a trustee under section 1104 "for cause," including fraud, dishonesty, incompetence, or gross mismanagement, or if appointment is in the interests of creditors. Courts regard this as an extraordinary remedy; an examiner with expanded powers is a common intermediate step. If a trustee is appointed, the debtor loses its in-possession status and control of the estate.

Rationale and criticism

The DIP model reflects a policy choice that incumbent management best understands the business and that keeping it in place maximizes the chance of reorganization and preserves going-concern value. Critics argue it can entrench failed management and favour secured lenders who control DIP financing terms.
