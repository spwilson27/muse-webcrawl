Flora of Australia

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The flora of Australia comprises a vast assemblage of plants estimated at more than 27,000 species of vascular plants, characterised by very high levels of endemism — roughly 85–90 per cent of its flowering plants occur nowhere else. Its distinctive character reflects Australia's long isolation after the breakup of Gondwana, its drift into arid latitudes, nutrient-poor soils, and a fire-prone climate.

Origins and character

Some lineages are Gondwanan relics — rainforest families such as Proteaceae, Myrtaceae, and the southern conifers of the Araucariaceae and Podocarpaceae shared with South America and New Zealand. Over the last 30 million years, drying of the continent favoured sclerophyll plants with hard, tough leaves: eucalypts (more than 800 species), acacias (around 1,000 species of Acacia), banksias, grevilleas, and hakeas dominate the open forests, woodlands, and shrublands (kwongan, mallee, mulga) that cover most of the continent.

Adaptations

Australian plants are adapted to fire (lignotubers, epicormic resprouting, serotinous cones, and smoke-triggered germination), to impoverished phosphorus-poor soils (cluster roots in Proteaceae), and to drought through sclerophylly, deep roots, and ephemeral desert life cycles that erupt into wildflower displays after rain.

Documentation and threats

The multi-volume Flora of Australia project, published from 1981, is the national inventory. Since European colonisation in 1788, land clearing, introduced weeds, feral herbivores, changed fire regimes, and pathogens such as Phytophthora dieback have placed many hundreds of species at risk of extinction.
