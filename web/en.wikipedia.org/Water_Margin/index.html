Water Margin (Chinese: 水滸傳; pinyin: Shuǐhǔ Zhuàn), also translated as Outlaws of the Marsh and All Men Are Brothers, is a 14th-century Chinese novel attributed to Shi Nai'an. Considered one of the Four Great Classical Novels of Chinese literature, it tells the story of 108 outlaws who gather at Liangshan Marsh to form a rebel army before receiving imperial amnesty and being sent on campaigns against foreign invaders and rebel forces.

Content

Set in the late Northern Song dynasty during the reign of Emperor Huizong, the novel draws on folk tales about the historical bandit Song Jiang and his companions. Its early chapters are semi-independent biographical episodes about individual heroes — among them Lu Zhishen the "Flowery Monk", Wu Song, who kills a tiger with his bare hands, and Lin Chong, the "Panther Head" — showing how corruption and injustice drive honest men into outlawry. The heroes assemble at Liangshan, ranked as the 108 Stars of Destiny (36 Heavenly Spirits and 72 Earthly Fiends), led after Chao Gai's death by Song Jiang, who favours eventual reconciliation with the throne.

Versions and influence

The novel exists in 70-, 100- and 120-chapter versions; Jin Shengtan's popular 70-chapter edition (1641) ends with the gathering of the band and omits the amnesty and campaigns, which Jin regarded as inferior. Notable translations include Pearl S. Buck's All Men Are Brothers (1933) and Sidney Shapiro's Outlaws of the Marsh (1980). The novel has been adapted countless times in opera, film, television — including a famous 1970s Japanese series — and video games such as the Japanese Suikoden series, whose name is the Japanese reading of "Water Margin". Its celebration of outlaw brotherhood made it politically controversial in some periods, including criticism during the Cultural Revolution-era campaign against "capitulationism" attributed to Song Jiang.
