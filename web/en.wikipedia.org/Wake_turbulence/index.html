Wake turbulence

Wake turbulence is the disturbed air trailing behind an aircraft in flight, dominated by counter-rotating wingtip vortices generated as a by-product of lift. These vortices can persist for minutes, sink below the flight path, and pose a serious rolling hazard to following aircraft, especially smaller planes behind heavy jets.

Hazard and separation

An aircraft flying through a vortex can experience an induced roll exceeding its roll authority, structural loads, or loss of altitude — dangers greatest during takeoff and landing, when planes fly close together near the ground. Air traffic control therefore applies distance- and time-based separation minima according to the weight categories of leading and following aircraft; the Airbus A380 prompted a "Super" category. Pilots are taught to stay above and upwind of a preceding aircraft's flight path and to rotate before its rotation point.

Incidents

Wake turbulence has contributed to several accidents, most notably American Airlines Flight 587 in 2001, where aggressive rudder inputs during a wake encounter from a preceding Boeing 747 led to vertical stabilizer failure. Research continues into vortex prediction, detection with lidar, and revised, more granular separation standards (such as RECAT) to increase airport capacity safely.
