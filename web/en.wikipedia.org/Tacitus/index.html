Tacitus

Publius Cornelius Tacitus (c. AD 56 – c. 120) was a Roman historian and politician, widely regarded as one of the greatest prose stylists of Latin literature. His surviving works — the Annals and the Histories — examine the Roman Empire from the death of Augustus to the Flavian dynasty with unmatched psychological and political insight.

Life

Tacitus rose through the senatorial career under Vespasian, Titus and Domitian, becoming praetor in 88 and consul in 97, when he delivered the funeral oration for Verginius Rufus. He later governed the province of Asia. His career under Domitian's terror marked his writing with a lasting preoccupation with tyranny, informers and senatorial servility.

Works

The Agricola (98) is a biography of his father-in-law, the conqueror of Roman Britain, blended with ethnography. The Germania describes the peoples beyond the Rhine and has had an outsized later influence. The Dialogus de oratoribus debates the decline of oratory. His mature histories are darker: the Histories covered AD 69–96 (only the first years survive), and the Annals — his masterpiece — narrate Tiberius through Nero, surviving in part, including the famous account of the Great Fire of Rome and the persecution of Christians.

Style and legacy

Tacitus wrote a compressed, epigrammatic Latin full of irony and pointed brevity, painting emperors such as Tiberius with devastating indirectness. Rediscovered in the Renaissance, he became a founding text of political realism and a favourite of thinkers from Machiavelli to the American Founders.
