Axis powers

The Axis powers, originally the Rome–Berlin Axis, were the coalition that opposed the Allies in the Second World War. Its principal members were Nazi Germany, Fascist Italy and Imperial Japan, bound by the Tripartite Pact of 27 September 1940. The alliance grew from the 1936 Rome–Berlin understanding and the German-Japanese Anti-Comintern Pact, both directed against the Soviet Union and the Western democracies.

Members

Germany under Adolf Hitler and Italy under Benito Mussolini intervened together in the Spanish Civil War before formalising their partnership. Japan, led militarily by figures such as Hideki Tojo, joined as the third major partner. Signatories of the Tripartite Pact also included Hungary, Romania, Slovakia, Bulgaria, Yugoslavia (briefly) and Croatia; Finland, Thailand and Iraq fought alongside the Axis as co-belligerents without formally joining the pact.

War

The Axis achieved rapid conquests in 1939–42: Germany overran most of Europe, Italy campaigned in the Mediterranean and Africa, and Japan seized much of East and Southeast Asia and the Pacific. Coordination among the partners was, however, weak — Germany and Japan fought largely separate wars and never combined strategy. The tide turned at Stalingrad, El Alamein, Midway and Guadalcanal in 1942–43. Italy surrendered in September 1943; Germany capitulated in May 1945, and Japan in September 1945 after the atomic bombings and Soviet entry into the war.

Ideology and crimes

The Axis states shared expansionist, authoritarian ideologies. Nazi Germany perpetrated the Holocaust, murdering six million Jews and millions of others, while Japanese forces committed mass atrocities in China and Southeast Asia. Axis leaders were tried for war crimes at Nuremberg and Tokyo after the war.
