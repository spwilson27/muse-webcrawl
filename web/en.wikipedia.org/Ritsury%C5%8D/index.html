Ritsuryō

Ritsuryō (Japanese: 律令) is the historical legal system of Japan based on Confucianism and Chinese legal philosophy, imported from China and adapted in the Asuka and Nara periods (late 6th to 10th centuries). The name combines ritsu (律), the penal code, and ryō (令), the administrative code that structured the imperial bureaucracy, taxation, land allotment and court ranks.

Development

Following the Taika Reforms of 645, Japan's rulers modelled a centralised state on Tang-dynasty China. The first full codes were the Ōmi Code (668) and Asuka Kiyomihara Code (689), culminating in the Taihō Code of 701 and its revision, the Yōrō Code of 718 (enforced in 757). Under the system, land was nominally public, periodically redistributed under the handen-shūju allotment system, and the population was registered for tax and conscription.

Government structure

The ryō organised the state under the Council of State (Daijōkan) and the Department of Divinities (Jingikan), with eight ministries and a hierarchy of court ranks, and divided the country into provinces, districts and villages. Commentary traditions — such as the Ryō no Gige (833) and Ryō no Shūge (920) — glossed the code for officials.

Decline

From the 9th century the system's fiscal base eroded as tax-exempt private estates (shōen) spread and hereditary officeholding displaced meritocratic appointment. Although formally never abolished until the Meiji Restoration drafted modern codes, effective rule passed to the imperial regents, cloistered emperors and eventually the warrior governments of the shogunates.
