Vacuum tube

A vacuum tube, also called a thermionic valve, is a device that controls electric current flow in a high vacuum between electrodes to which a voltage difference has been applied. It relies on the thermionic emission of electrons from a hot cathode. Vacuum tubes were fundamental components of electronic technology in the first half of the twentieth century, enabling radio, television, radar, telephony, and the first electronic computers.

The simplest vacuum tube, the diode, was invented in 1904 by John Ambrose Fleming. Lee de Forest's addition of a control grid in 1906 created the triode (the "Audion"), which could amplify signals. Later designs — tetrodes, pentodes, and multi-element tubes — improved performance and variety.

Decline and survival

From the 1950s onward, the transistor and other semiconductor devices displaced vacuum tubes in most applications because of their smaller size, lower power consumption, and reliability. Vacuum tubes nevertheless survive in niche uses: high-power radio transmitters, microwave devices such as magnetrons and traveling-wave tubes, and guitar amplifiers and high-end audio equipment, where their distortion characteristics are prized.

In computing

First-generation computers such as ENIAC (which used some 17,000 tubes) and the Colossus machines were built around vacuum tubes, whose heat and failure rates motivated the shift to solid-state electronics.
