Mendelson's syndrome

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Mendelson's syndrome is chemical pneumonitis caused by the aspiration of acidic gastric contents into the lungs, classically during general anaesthesia in pregnancy and childbirth. It was described in 1946 by the American obstetrician Curtis Lester Mendelson, who reviewed cases of aspiration in obstetric patients and distinguished the chemical injury from bacterial aspiration pneumonia.

Causes and presentation

Aspiration of stomach acid — typically of a low pH and in volumes above roughly 0.3–0.4 mL/kg — directly burns the airways and alveoli, producing bronchospasm, dyspnoea, cyanosis, pulmonary oedema, and hypoxaemia within hours. Pregnancy, emergency surgery, reduced consciousness, and a full stomach all increase the risk.

Prevention and management

Mendelson's work transformed obstetric anaesthetic practice. Prevention includes fasting before elective surgery, antacid prophylaxis (such as sodium citrate) and H2-receptor blockers or proton-pump inhibitors, rapid sequence induction with cricoid pressure, and preferring regional anaesthesia for caesarean delivery where appropriate. Treatment is supportive: oxygen, suction, bronchodilators, and ventilatory support as needed; antibiotics are reserved for secondary infection.
