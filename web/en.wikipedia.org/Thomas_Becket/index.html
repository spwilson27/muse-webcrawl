Thomas Becket (c. 1119/1120 – 29 December 1170), also known as Saint Thomas of Canterbury and Thomas à Becket, was Archbishop of Canterbury from 1162 until his murder in 1170. He is venerated as a saint and martyr by the Catholic Church and the Anglican Communion. He engaged in conflict with Henry II, King of England, over the rights and privileges of the Church and was murdered by followers of the king in Canterbury Cathedral. Soon after his death, he was canonised by Pope Alexander III.

Early life

Becket was born in Cheapside, London, on 21 December, the feast day of St Thomas the Apostle, to Gilbert de Bailleul and Matilda, both of Norman descent. His father was a prosperous merchant who later became sheriff of London. Becket was educated first at Merton Priory and later in Paris, where he studied civil and canon law. He entered the household of Theobald of Bec, Archbishop of Canterbury, who sent him on missions to Rome and to study law at Bologna and Auxerre.

Royal chancellor

In January 1155 Becket was appointed Lord Chancellor by the young Henry II. The two men became close companions, hunting, playing chess and governing together. As chancellor Becket enforced the king's authority, raised revenues — including the controversial scutage that helped finance Henry's campaigns in France — and lived in conspicuous splendour, travelling with a retinue of hundreds.

Archbishop of Canterbury

After Theobald's death in 1161, Henry had Becket elected Archbishop of Canterbury; he was ordained a priest on 2 June 1162 and consecrated bishop the next day. To Henry's dismay, Becket resigned the chancellorship, adopted an austere and devout lifestyle, and began to defend the independence of the Church with the same zeal he had once applied to the king's business.

Conflict with Henry II

The conflict centred on whether clergy convicted of crimes in church courts could be retried and punished by royal courts — the issue of the "criminous clerks". At the Council of Clarendon in January 1164 Henry issued the Constitutions of Clarendon, asserting royal customs over the Church. Becket initially assented, then repudiated his agreement. At Northampton in October 1164 he was put on trial on charges of contempt and embezzlement; he fled to France, where he spent six years in exile under the protection of Louis VII and Pope Alexander III, excommunicating several of Henry's ministers. A formal reconciliation at Fréteval in July 1170 allowed Becket to return to Canterbury on 1 December 1170, but he refused to lift the excommunications, and the dispute reignited within weeks.

Murder

On hearing Becket's defiance, Henry — then in Normandy — is said to have cried out in frustration, traditionally rendered as "Will no one rid me of this turbulent priest?" Four knights — Reginald FitzUrse, Hugh de Morville, William de Tracy and Richard le Breton — took the words literally, crossed to England, and confronted Becket in Canterbury Cathedral on 29 December 1170. Refusing to flee or bar the doors, Becket was struck down before the altar of St Benedict, the crown of his head cut away by sword blows.

Legacy

Becket was canonised barely two years after his death, and his shrine in Canterbury became one of the great pilgrimage sites of medieval Europe — the destination of Chaucer's pilgrims in The Canterbury Tales. Henry II performed public penance at the shrine in 1174. The shrine was destroyed in 1538 under Henry VIII, who had Becket's name erased from service books. Becket's life has inspired works including T. S. Eliot's Murder in the Cathedral (1935) and Jean Anouilh's Becket (1959).
