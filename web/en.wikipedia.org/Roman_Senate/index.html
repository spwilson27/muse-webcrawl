Roman Senate

The Roman Senate (Latin: Senātus Rōmānus) was the highest and most powerful political institution in ancient Rome. It was founded in the early days of the city as an advisory council of the king, and maintained its power throughout the Republic and the Empire, surviving the fall of the Western Roman Empire until the 7th century in the East. The word senatus derives from senex, "old man", and originally meant a council of elders.

History

Tradition held that Romulus created a Senate of 100 patricians. Under the Republic, membership grew to about 300, then 600 after Sulla's reforms and 900 under Julius Caesar, with entry gained mainly through election to the quaestorship and enrolment by the censors. Senators were drawn from the leading families and served for life, subject to expulsion for misconduct.

Powers and procedure

The Senate did not formally legislate — laws were passed by the popular assemblies — but its decrees (senatus consulta) carried immense authority. It controlled state finances, assigned provinces and commands to magistrates, conducted foreign policy, and received embassies. Meetings were convened by consuls or tribunes in the Curia Julia or another consecrated space, proceeded by seniority of speaking order, and voted by division. In emergencies it could pass the senatus consultum ultimum, urging magistrates to defend the republic.

Under the Empire

Augustus reduced the Senate to 600 members and retained it as a prestigious body: it tried certain cases, administered some provinces, and ratified imperial succession. Real power, however, lay with the emperor, and the Senate's role became increasingly ceremonial, though it persisted in Constantinople as the Byzantine Senate into the 14th century.
