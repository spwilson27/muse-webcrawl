Socialist market economy

The socialist market economy is the official designation of the economic system of the People's Republic of China since the early 1990s, and in modified form of Vietnam (the "socialist-oriented market economy"). It combines market allocation and a large private sector with dominant state ownership in strategic industries, state-owned enterprise groups, macroeconomic guidance through five-year plans and industrial policy, and the political leadership of the ruling communist party — presented by its theorists as a stage in "socialism with Chinese characteristics."

Development

China's reforms began in 1978 under Deng Xiaoping: decollectivization through the household responsibility system, special economic zones, township and village enterprises, dual-track pricing, and opening to foreign investment. At the 14th Congress of the Chinese Communist Party in 1992, Jiang Zemin formally proposed building a "socialist market economy," written into the constitution in 1993; subsequent reforms corporatized state enterprises, created stock exchanges in Shanghai and Shenzhen, unified the currency, and culminated in WTO accession in 2001. Vietnam's Đổi Mới reforms from 1986 followed a parallel path.

Features and debate

Defining features include public ownership described as the "mainstay" alongside diverse ownership forms; state control of finance, energy, telecommunications, and transport; industrial policy and state planning by guidance rather than command; and extensive party presence within firms, including private ones. Scholars debate whether the system is best characterized as state capitalism, a developmental state, or a genuinely distinct socialist model, and how its combination of markets and party-state direction has produced both rapid growth and poverty reduction and problems of debt, overcapacity, inequality, and corruption.
