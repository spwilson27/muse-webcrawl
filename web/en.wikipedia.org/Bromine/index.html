Bromine is a chemical element; it has symbol Br and atomic number 35. It is a volatile red-brown liquid at room temperature that evaporates readily to form a similarly coloured vapour. Bromine is the third-lightest halogen and is one of only two elements that are liquid at or near room temperature, the other being mercury. It was discovered independently by Carl Jacob Löwig (1825) and Antoine Balard (1826).

Properties

Elemental bromine is diatomic (Br2), highly reactive, and a strong oxidising agent. Its properties are intermediate between those of chlorine and iodine. Bromine is corrosive and toxic, with a sharp, irritating odour—its name derives from the Greek bromos, meaning "stench". It is slightly soluble in water and readily soluble in organic solvents.

Occurrence and production

Bromine occurs naturally in seawater and in brine deposits, at concentrations much lower than chloride. Commercial production involves oxidising bromide-rich brines with chlorine gas and stripping the liberated bromine with air or steam. The largest producers include the United States, Israel (from Dead Sea brines), and Jordan.

Applications

Organobromine compounds are used as flame retardants, in agriculture as fumigants, and in photography (silver bromide). Historically, bromides were used as sedatives in medicine. Because many brominated compounds contribute to ozone depletion, some uses, such as methyl bromide fumigation, have been restricted under the Montreal Protocol.
