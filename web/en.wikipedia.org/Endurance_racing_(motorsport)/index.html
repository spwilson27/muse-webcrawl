Endurance racing (motorsport)

Highest governing bodyFIA / ACO / IMSA
First contestedEarly 1900s (24 Hours of Le Mans, 1923)
CharacteristicsTeam sport, timed/distance races, multiple driver changes
VenueRoad courses and circuits worldwide

Endurance racing is a form of motorsport racing which is meant to test the durability of equipment and the endurance of participants. Teams of multiple drivers attempt to cover a large distance in a single event, with participants given the opportunity to change during the race. Endurance races can be run either to cover a set distance in the shortest time possible, or to cover the most distance in a predetermined time.

One of the more common lengths of endurance races has been running for 1,000 kilometres (620 mi), or roughly six hours. Longer races can run for 1,000 miles (1,600 km), 12 hours, or even 24 hours. Teams can consist of anywhere from two to four drivers per event, which is dependent on the driver's endurance abilities, length of the race, or even the rules for each event.

Origins

At the beginning of the century, long-distance reliability trials and city-to-city races such as the Paris–Rouen and Paris–Bordeaux events tested the young automobile over public roads. The Coppa Florio, first held in 1900, and the Mille Miglia carried this tradition forward. The defining event arrived in 1923 with the first 24 Hours of Le Mans, organised by the Automobile Club de l'Ouest (ACO) in France.

Major races and series

The so-called "Triple Crown" of endurance racing comprises the 24 Hours of Le Mans, the 24 Hours of Daytona and the 12 Hours of Sebring. The FIA World Endurance Championship (WEC), launched in 2012, is the premier global series for prototype and grand tourer machinery, while the IMSA SportsCar Championship sanctions the leading North American events. Historic multi-class formats mix purpose-built prototypes with production-based GT cars on track simultaneously.

Format and strategy

Unlike sprint racing, endurance events reward reliability, fuel and tyre management, and efficient pit stops. Driver changes take place during pit stops, and rules limit how long any one driver may remain at the wheel. Night driving, traffic management between classes of differing speed, and changing weather are central challenges. Safety car periods and "Full Course Yellow" neutralisations can compress or stretch gaps dramatically over a long race.

Notable teams and drivers

Porsche holds the record for overall Le Mans victories as a manufacturer, while drivers such as Tom Kristensen — nicknamed "Mr Le Mans" for his nine wins — became synonymous with the discipline. Audi's diesel and hybrid prototypes dominated the 2000s and 2010s, and Toyota Gazoo Racing carried the hybrid era forward into the Hypercar regulations introduced in 2021.
