Kwashiorkor
SpecialtyPaediatrics, nutrition
SymptomsOedema (especially feet and legs), distended abdomen, fatty liver, skin lesions, hair depigmentation, irritability, growth failure
CausesSevere acute malnutrition — inadequate protein intake relative to energy, with contributions from infection and other factors
Risk factorsPoverty, food insecurity, early weaning onto starchy staples, infectious disease
TreatmentCareful nutritional rehabilitation (e.g. F-75/F-100 therapeutic milks, ready-to-use therapeutic food), treatment of infections and electrolyte imbalances

Kwashiorkor is a form of severe acute malnutrition characterised by oedema — swelling, particularly of the feet and legs — together with skin and hair changes, fatty enlargement of the liver, and a distended abdomen. It occurs mainly in young children in low-income settings, often at weaning, when a diet dominated by starchy staples provides enough energy but too little protein and micronutrients. It is distinguished from marasmus, the other principal form of severe acute malnutrition, in which extreme wasting occurs without oedema.

Name

The word comes from a Ga language term from Ghana, often translated as "the sickness a child gets when the next baby is born" — referring to its frequent onset in a toddler displaced from breastfeeding by a younger sibling. The condition was described in the medical literature by the Jamaican paediatrician Cicely Williams in the 1930s.

Signs and symptoms

Affected children are apathetic and irritable, fail to grow, and lose muscle mass, though subcutaneous fat may be partly retained. Pitting oedema begins in the feet and may generalise. The liver enlarges because of fat accumulation. Skin lesions progress from darkened patches to peeling and ulceration ("flaky paint" dermatosis), and the hair becomes sparse, straight and reddish or pale from loss of pigment. Diarrhoea, anaemia and vulnerability to infections such as measles, gastroenteritis and pneumonia are common; untreated, the condition is frequently fatal.

Causes and pathophysiology

Kwashiorkor develops when protein intake is severely inadequate relative to energy intake. Falling blood albumin lowers plasma oncotic pressure, contributing to oedema; impaired hepatic lipoprotein synthesis leads to fatty liver. The full picture, however, is not explained by protein lack alone: oxidative stress, aflatoxin exposure, infections, and differences in the gut microbiome have all been implicated, and the reasons why one malnourished child develops kwashiorkor while another develops marasmus remain incompletely understood.

Treatment and prognosis

Treatment follows stabilisation-then-rehabilitation protocols (such as the WHO regimen): initial cautious feeding with low-protein therapeutic milk (F-75), correction of dehydration, hypothermia, hypoglycaemia and electrolyte disturbances, and antibiotics for infection, followed by energy-dense feeds (F-100 or ready-to-use therapeutic food) to achieve catch-up growth. Refeeding must be gradual because of the risk of refeeding syndrome. With proper care, mortality has fallen substantially, but survivors may suffer lasting effects on growth and development.
