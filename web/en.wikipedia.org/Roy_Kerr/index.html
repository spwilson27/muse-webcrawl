Roy Kerr

Roy Patrick Kerr (born 16 May 1934) is a New Zealand mathematician best known for discovering the Kerr geometry, an exact solution to the Einstein field equations of general relativity describing the spacetime around a rotating, uncharged, axially symmetric black hole.

Life and career

Kerr was born in Kurow, in the South Island of New Zealand, and educated at St Andrew's College, Christchurch. He studied mathematics at Canterbury University College (now the University of Canterbury) and completed his PhD at Cambridge in 1960. After postdoctoral work at Syracuse University and a period with the United States Air Force research laboratory at Wright-Patterson Air Force Base, he joined the University of Texas at Austin, where in 1963 he found his famous solution. In 1971 he returned to New Zealand as professor of mathematics at the University of Canterbury, where he remained until his retirement in 1993.

The Kerr metric

Before 1963 the only known black hole solution was the non-rotating Schwarzschild metric of 1916, yet real astronomical objects rotate. Working with algebraic properties of the field equations later formalised in the Petrov classification, Kerr produced a solution with two parameters — mass and angular momentum — that describes the exterior field of a spinning mass. It predicts an ergosphere in which spacetime itself is dragged around (frame dragging), ring singularities, and the rotational energy that Roger Penrose showed could in principle be extracted.

The Kerr solution later proved central to astrophysics: accreting stellar-mass and supermassive black holes spin, and observations from X-ray astronomy to the Event Horizon Telescope images are interpreted using Kerr geometry. Ezra Newman generalised the solution to charged rotating holes (the Kerr–Newman metric) in 1965, and the no-hair theorem established Kerr–Newman geometry as the unique end-state of gravitational collapse.

Recognition

Kerr was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 2019's predecessor honours include the Hughes Medal of the Royal Society (1984), New Zealand's Rutherford Medal (1993), the Albert Einstein Medal (2013) and the Crafoord Prize in Astronomy (2016), shared for foundational work on rotating black holes.
