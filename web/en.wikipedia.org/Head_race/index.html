Head race

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A head race is a time-trial competition in the sport of rowing, in which boats race in procession against the clock over a long course, typically between 4 and 10 kilometres. Crews start one after another at intervals of about 10–15 seconds — usually in an order determined by the previous year's results — and the crew with the fastest finishing time wins. The winning crew is called the "Head of the River".

Format and rules

Because crews start in sequence, overtaking is common and is governed by rules requiring the slower crew to yield the fastest line (on the Thames, the racing line follows the fastest stream). Head races are traditionally held in the autumn and winter "head season", when long-distance endurance work dominates training and when river conditions may be unsuitable for side-by-side regatta racing. Events are organised by boat class (eights, fours, quads, doubles, pairs, singles) and by status categories for club, university, school, masters, and novice crews.

Major head races

The original and most prestigious head race is London's Head of the River Race, founded in 1926 by Steve Fairbairn and raced over the Championship Course on the tidal Thames. Other major events include the Head of the Charles Regatta in Boston, USA — the world's largest two-day rowing event, attracting hundreds of thousands of spectators — the Head of the Hooch, the Rivanna and other North American heads, and numerous British events such as the Fours Head of the River and the Pairs Head.
