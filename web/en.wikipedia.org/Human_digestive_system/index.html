The human digestive system consists of the gastrointestinal tract plus the accessory organs of digestion (the tongue, salivary glands, pancreas, liver and gallbladder). Digestion breaks food into progressively smaller components — carbohydrates, proteins, fats, vitamins and minerals — until they can be absorbed into the bloodstream, a process combining mechanical chewing and peristalsis with the chemical action of enzymes and acids.

Structure

Food passes through the mouth, pharynx and oesophagus to the stomach, where it is churned with gastric juice into chyme. Most digestion and absorption then occurs in the roughly six-metre small intestine (duodenum, jejunum and ileum), aided by bile from the liver and gallbladder and by pancreatic juice. The large intestine absorbs water and electrolytes, hosts much of the gut microbiota, and compacts waste into faeces for elimination through the rectum and anus.

Process and control

Digestion proceeds in cephalic, gastric and intestinal phases, coordinated by the enteric nervous system and hormones such as gastrin, secretin and cholecystokinin. Salivary amylase begins starch digestion in the mouth; pepsin and hydrochloric acid act in the stomach; intestinal brush-border and pancreatic enzymes complete the breakdown of nutrients, whose absorption is multiplied by the villi and microvilli of the intestinal wall.

Clinical significance

Common disorders include gastroesophageal reflux disease, peptic ulcers, inflammatory bowel disease, coeliac disease, gallstones and colorectal cancer, the speciality concerned being gastroenterology.
