Pseudonym

[Placeholder image: Title page published under a pen name]

A pseudonym (/ˈsuːdənɪm/; from Ancient Greek ψευδώνυμος pseudṓnumos, "falsely named") or alias is a fictitious name that a person assumes for a particular purpose, which differs from their original or true name (orthonym). This also differs from a new name that entirely or legally replaces an individual's own. Many pseudonym holders use pseudonyms because they wish to remain anonymous; however, pseudonyms can also serve branding, artistic, or social purposes.

Types

Common forms include the pen name (nom de plume) used by authors—for example, Mark Twain (Samuel Clemens), George Eliot (Mary Ann Evans), and Lewis Carroll (Charles Dodgson); the stage name used by performers; the nom de guerre used by combatants and revolutionaries; ring names of wrestlers; and usernames or handles online. In science, collective pseudonyms such as Nicolas Bourbaki have been used by groups of collaborators. Some pseudonyms become better known than the bearer's real name, and some individuals, such as the artist Banksy, maintain pseudonymity as a core part of their public identity.

Reasons for use

Pseudonyms may be adopted to conceal identity for safety or privacy; to avoid prejudice (as when women writers published under male names); to separate distinct bodies of work or genres; to create a more memorable or marketable persona; for satire and political dissent under censorship; or as part of traditions such as the religious names taken by monks and popes (the latter being regnal names). In some cases, such as that of actor and musician stage names, an organization or guild may require a unique professional name.

Legal status

In many jurisdictions, using a pseudonym is lawful provided it is not used to commit fraud. Authors can register copyrights in works published under pseudonyms; in the United States such works are protected for 95 years from publication or 120 years from creation, whichever is shorter, unless the author's identity is recorded with the Copyright Office. Trademark and publicity law may protect famous stage names, while "doing business as" rules govern trade names for businesses.
