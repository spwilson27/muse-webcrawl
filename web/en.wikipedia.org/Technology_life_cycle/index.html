Technology life cycle

The technology life cycle (TLC) describes the commercial gain of a product through the expense of research and development phase, and the financial return during its "vital life." Some technologies may incur lengthy research periods (intramural or extramural), while others grow quickly through a rapid development phase, but the pattern of innovation, growth, maturity, and decline is broadly similar.

Stages

The technology life cycle is commonly divided into four stages. In the research and development stage, investment is high and returns are negative or speculative. In the growth (or ascent) stage, the technology is adopted by early users, a dominant design may emerge, and sales increase sharply. In the maturity stage, the market becomes saturated, improvement is incremental, and competition shifts to price and efficiency. In the decline stage, the technology is displaced by a substitute and usage falls away.

S-curves

Technology performance and adoption are often represented as S-curves: slow initial progress, rapid improvement as the technology is refined, and a plateau as physical or practical limits are approached. A new technology's S-curve may eventually cross and supersede the old one, a pattern analyzed in theories of disruptive innovation.

Relation to product life cycle

The technology life cycle differs from the product life cycle: a single technology can support many generations of products, and individual products may have much shorter lives than the underlying technology. Managers use TLC models to time investment, plan licensing, and decide when to shift resources to successor technologies.
