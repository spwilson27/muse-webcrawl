Diplomatic recognition

Diplomatic recognition in international law is a unilateral declarative political act of a state that acknowledges an act or status of another state or government in control of a state. States can be recognised, and also governments of states. Recognition may determine the status of a state under international law, including the ability to enter into bilateral treaties, join international organisations such as the United Nations, and establish formal diplomatic relations.

Theories of recognition

Two principal theories govern state recognition. The declarative theory, codified in the 1933 Montevideo Convention, holds that a state exists when it possesses a permanent population, a defined territory, a government, and the capacity to enter into relations with other states — recognition by others merely declares that fact. The constitutive theory, by contrast, holds that a state only becomes a person of international law through recognition by other states.

Recognition of governments

Recognition of a government normally implies readiness to assume diplomatic relations. States may withhold recognition as a mark of disapproval of a regime, for example where a government has come to power by force. The United Kingdom announced in 1980 that it would no longer formally recognise governments, a practice followed by a number of other states.

Collective recognition and non-recognition

States may act collectively in recognising or refusing to recognise a situation. The doctrine of non-recognition associated with U.S. Secretary of State Henry L. Stimson held that territorial acquisitions made by force should not be recognised. Disputed cases of limited recognition include Kosovo, Taiwan, and Palestine.
