The Polaris missile (UGM-27) was a two-stage, solid-fuelled, nuclear-armed submarine-launched ballistic missile (SLBM) built by Lockheed for the United States Navy. It was the first SLBM to enter service, transforming nuclear deterrence by giving the United States an invulnerable second-strike capability based at sea. Development was led by the Navy's Special Projects Office under Rear Admiral William Raborn, using the then-novel PERT management technique, and the programme moved from authorisation in 1956 to first deterrent patrol in November 1960.

Service

The first Polaris submarine, USS George Washington, launched two Polaris A-1 missiles on 20 July 1960 — the first ever submerged ballistic launch — and began the first deterrent patrol on 15 November 1960. A total of 41 "Forty-One for Freedom" fleet ballistic missile submarines were built, each carrying 16 missiles. Successive variants increased capability: the A-2 (1962) extended range, and the A-3 (1964) added greater range still and a multiple re-entry vehicle warhead. Polaris was succeeded in US service by the Poseidon and Trident missiles.

Royal Navy

Under the 1962 Nassau Agreement and the 1963 Polaris Sales Agreement, the United Kingdom purchased Polaris A-3 missiles for four Resolution-class submarines, with British-built warheads; the system provided the UK's strategic nuclear deterrent from 1968 until replaced by Trident in the 1990s. The British Chevaline upgrade improved the re-entry system's ability to penetrate Soviet missile defences around Moscow.

Significance

Polaris established the submarine leg of the nuclear triad and the doctrine of assured retaliation. Its compact solid-fuel design, inertial guidance and rapid development made it one of the defining weapons systems of the Cold War.
