A sofer (Hebrew: סופר, "scribe"; plural sofrim) is a Jewish scribe trained to transcribe Torah scrolls and other ritual documents by hand in accordance with Jewish law (halakha). The craft, known as sofrut, requires rigorous training, religious observance, and mastery of hundreds of detailed rules governing letter forms, materials, and procedure.

Work and rules

A sofer writes on parchment (klaf) prepared from the hide of a kosher animal, using a quill and black ink made to traditional specifications. Every letter must be formed exactly; a Torah scroll contains 304,805 letters, and a single malformed, missing, or touching letter can render the scroll unfit (pasul) for ritual use until corrected. Before writing the divine names, the scribe must take special care, and the names themselves, once written, cannot be erased.

In addition to Torah scrolls, sofrim write the scrolls inside tefillin (phylacteries) and mezuzot, the five megillot (notably the Book of Esther scroll read at Purim), and legal documents such as the ketubah (marriage contract) and get (divorce decree).

Checking and training

Finished works are proofread meticulously, today often with computer scanning to detect missing or extra letters, supplemented by human checking of letter forms. Becoming a sofer requires apprenticeship and certification (kabbalah, in this technical sense, a licence) from a recognized teacher, plus ongoing study of the laws of sofrut in texts such as the Keset ha-Sofer.
