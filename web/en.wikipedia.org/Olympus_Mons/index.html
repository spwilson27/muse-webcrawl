Olympus Mons

Olympus Mons
Planet: Mars
Location: Tharsis region, 18.65°N 226.2°E
Height: ~21.9 km (about 2.5× Everest)
Diameter: ~600 km
Type: Shield volcano

Olympus Mons is a large shield volcano on the planet Mars, the tallest volcano and the tallest known mountain in the Solar System. Located in the Tharsis region near the Martian equator, it rises about 21.9 km above the surrounding plains — roughly two and a half times the height of Mount Everest — and spans some 600 km in diameter, about the size of France.

Discovery

Nineteenth-century telescopic observers saw a bright patch they called Nix Olympica ("Snows of Olympus"), suspecting a high feature. NASA's Mariner 9 orbiter confirmed in 1971–72 that the feature was a colossal volcano, after which it received its present name, Latin for "Mount Olympus."

Geology

Olympus Mons is a shield volcano built up by countless flows of very fluid basaltic lava over billions of years. Mars's low surface gravity and lack of plate tectonics allowed the hotspot to keep building a single edifice. Its summit caldera, about 80 km wide, is a composite of overlapping pit craters. A cliff or escarpment up to 8 km high rings much of the base, possibly carved by erosion or glacial processes. The flanks show lava channels, tubes and signs of past glaciation, and crater counts suggest some lava flows are as young as a few tens of millions of years.

Atmosphere

The summit rises above most of the Martian atmosphere: atmospheric pressure at the top is only about 12 percent of the mean Martian surface pressure, so the peak often pokes through dust and thin clouds visible in telescope observations.
