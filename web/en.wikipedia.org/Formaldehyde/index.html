Formaldehyde

Chemical formulaCH2O
Molar mass30.03 g/mol
AppearanceColourless gas; aqueous solution (formalin)
Boiling point−19.3 °C (gas)
Main usesResins, disinfectant, preservative (formalin)

Formaldehyde (systematic name methanal) is an organic compound with the formula CH2O. It is the simplest of the aldehydes and a pungent, colourless gas that polymerises readily. The common commercial form is a 37% aqueous solution called formalin, used as a disinfectant and tissue preservative. Formaldehyde is produced industrially on a very large scale, principally for the manufacture of resins.

Production

Formaldehyde was first reported in 1859 by the Russian chemist Aleksandr Butlerov and definitively identified by August Wilhelm von Hofmann in 1869. Industrially it is made by the catalytic oxidation of methanol over silver or iron-molybdenum oxide catalysts (the Formox process), yielding tens of millions of tonnes annually.

Uses

Most formaldehyde is consumed in producing urea-formaldehyde, phenol-formaldehyde (Bakelite) and melamine resins used in plywood, particleboard, adhesives and moulded products. It is also a precursor to polyoxymethylene plastic and the explosive RDX. Formalin preserves biological specimens and embalms cadavers, and formaldehyde chemistry is a staple of forensic science laboratories.

Health effects

Formaldehyde irritates the eyes, nose and throat. The International Agency for Research on Cancer classifies it as a Group 1 carcinogen, based on evidence linking occupational exposure to nasopharyngeal cancer and leukaemia. Indoor emissions from pressed-wood products have prompted strict product standards in many countries.
