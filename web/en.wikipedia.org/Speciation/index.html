Speciation

Speciation
The four modes: allopatric, peripatric, parapatric, sympatric
FieldEvolutionary biology
Key conceptEvolution of reproductive isolation between populations
Key figuresCharles Darwin, Ernst Mayr, Theodosius Dobzhansky

Speciation is the evolutionary process by which populations evolve to become distinct species — groups whose members can no longer interbreed to produce fertile offspring with other such groups. It is the mechanism that converts microevolutionary change within populations into the macroevolutionary pattern of biodiversity.

Modes

Speciation is classified by the geographic relationship of the diverging populations. In allopatric speciation a physical barrier (a river, mountain range, or colonisation of an island) splits a population; the isolated groups diverge genetically until they are reproductively incompatible — Darwin's finches of the Galápagos are the classic case. Peripatric speciation is a variant involving a small population at the edge of the range (founder effect). In parapatric speciation populations are neighbours with a contact zone but limited gene flow, often across an environmental gradient. Sympatric speciation occurs without geographic separation, through disruptive selection or, commonly in plants, polyploidy — chromosome doubling that instantly isolates a lineage.

Mechanisms

Reproductive isolation evolves via pre-zygotic barriers (habitat, timing, behaviour, mechanical or gametic incompatibility) and post-zygotic barriers (hybrid inviability or sterility, as in the mule). Genetic drift, natural and sexual selection, and Dobzhansky–Muller incompatibilities between diverged genomes all drive the process. Reinforcement can strengthen pre-mating barriers where hybrids are unfit.

Evidence

Speciation has been observed in the laboratory (fruit flies), in nature in progress (apple maggot flies shifting hosts, crater-lake cichlids, ring species such as the greenish warbler), and in the fossil record, where punctuated equilibrium describes its often rapid appearance.
