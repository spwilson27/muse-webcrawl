Two-source hypothesis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The two-source hypothesis is the most widely accepted solution to the synoptic problem — the question of the literary relationship among the Gospels of Matthew, Mark and Luke, which share extensive verbatim material.

The hypothesis

It proposes two principal sources:

  • Mark: the Gospel of Mark, the earliest of the three, was used independently by both Matthew and Luke. This rests on Markan priority: nearly all of Mark appears in Matthew and Luke, and where the three differ in order, Matthew and Luke each agree with Mark but not with each other.
  • Q: a hypothetical sayings source (from German Quelle, "source"), containing mainly teachings of Jesus and no passion narrative, used by both Matthew and Luke to supplement Mark. Material shared by Matthew and Luke but absent from Mark — the "double tradition", such as the Lord's Prayer and the Beatitudes — is attributed to Q.

Material unique to Matthew ("M") and to Luke ("L") is explained by special sources or oral tradition, yielding the related four-source hypothesis developed by Streeter in the 1920s.

History and rivals

The theory grew out of nineteenth-century German scholarship (Weisse, Holtzmann) and was consolidated by B. H. Streeter. Its main rivals are the Farrer hypothesis, which accepts Markan priority but dispenses with Q by having Luke use Matthew; the two-gospel (Griesbach) hypothesis, which makes Mark a conflation of Matthew and Luke; and the Augustinian hypothesis of Matthean priority. Q has never been found as a manuscript, and its existence remains the principal point of dispute.
