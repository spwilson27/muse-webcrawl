The Principia Mathematica is a three-volume work on the foundations of mathematics written by Alfred North Whitehead and Bertrand Russell and published in 1910, 1912, and 1913. Its aim was to realise the logicist programme: to show that all mathematical truths could be derived from a small set of axioms and inference rules of logic alone, using a precise symbolic notation based on the work of Gottlob Frege and Giuseppe Peano.

Contents

The work builds up mathematics step by step: propositional and predicate logic, the theory of types (introduced to avoid Russell's paradox), cardinal and ordinal arithmetic, and the foundations of geometry. In a famous passage, the proposition "1 + 1 = 2" is proved only hundreds of pages into the first volume, with the dry comment that "the above proposition is occasionally useful". A second edition appeared in 1925–27 with important revisions, partly influenced by Ludwig Wittgenstein.

Significance

The Principia is one of the most influential works in the history of logic and appears on the Modern Library's list of the 100 best English-language nonfiction books of the 20th century. Its programme was profoundly affected by Kurt Gödel's incompleteness theorems of 1931, which showed that any sufficiently strong consistent formal system cannot prove its own consistency, undermining the hope of a complete logical foundation for mathematics. Nevertheless, the work shaped modern mathematical logic, type theory, and, through them, computer science.

Not to be confused with Isaac Newton's Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica.
