Turán's theorem

FieldExtremal graph theory
Proved byPál Turán (1941)
SubjectMaximum edges in a Kr+1-free graph

Turán's theorem is a fundamental result in extremal graph theory, proved by Hungarian mathematician Pál Turán in 1941. It determines the maximum number of edges that a graph on n vertices can have without containing a complete graph Kr+1 as a subgraph.

Statement

The extremal graph — the unique edge-maximal Kr+1-free graph — is the Turán graph T(n, r): the complete r-partite graph on n vertices whose parts are as equal in size as possible. The maximum number of edges is (1 − 1/r)·n²/2, rounded appropriately; for r = 2 this recovers Mantel's theorem that a triangle-free graph has at most ⌊n²/4⌋ edges.

Proof ideas

Turán's original proof used induction on n. Later proofs, notably Zykov's symmetrisation argument of 1949, show that merging vertices with identical neighbourhoods never reduces the edge count, forcing the extremal graph to be complete multipartite.

Significance

The theorem founded extremal graph theory. Its generalisations — the Erdős–Stone theorem for arbitrary forbidden subgraphs and Turán-type problems for hypergraphs, which remain largely open — shape a major branch of combinatorics.
