A land-grant university is an institution of higher education in the United States designated under the Morrill Acts to receive the benefits of federal land grants. The Morrill Act of 1862 gave each state 30,000 acres of federal land per member of Congress, the proceeds of whose sale endowed colleges teaching agriculture and the mechanic arts "without excluding other scientific and classical studies".

Development

The Second Morrill Act of 1890 required states with racial segregation to provide separate land-grant institutions for Black students, giving rise to the 1890 land-grant historically Black colleges and universities. The Hatch Act (1887) funded agricultural experiment stations, and the Smith–Lever Act (1914) created the Cooperative Extension Service, extending the universities' mission to farmers and communities statewide.

Today

There are over 100 land-grant institutions, including major public research universities such as Cornell (the statutory land-grant for New York), the University of California, Texas A&M, and the University of Wisconsin–Madison. Tribal colleges received land-grant status in 1994. The system remains central to American agricultural research and public higher education.
