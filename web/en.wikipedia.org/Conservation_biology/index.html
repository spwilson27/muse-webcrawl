Conservation biology

Field research and monitoring of threatened species are core tools of conservation biology.

Conservation biology is the study of the conservation of nature and of Earth's biodiversity with the aim of protecting species, their habitats, and ecosystems from excessive rates of extinction and the erosion of biotic interactions. It is an interdisciplinary subject drawing on the natural and social sciences, and the practice of natural resource management.

The discipline coalesced in the late 1970s and 1980s — Michael Soulé's edited volume Conservation Biology: An Evolutionary-Ecological Perspective (1980) and the founding of the Society for Conservation Biology (1985) are conventional milestones — in response to accelerating habitat loss and extinctions.

Core concepts

  • Minimum viable population and population viability analysis — estimating extinction risk for small populations, including those hit by disease events comparable to the bubonic plague in wildlife reservoirs.
  • Habitat fragmentation and corridors — how isolation affects gene flow and persistence.
  • Reserve design — size, shape and connectivity of protected areas; the SLOSS debate (single large or several small).
  • Umbrella, flagship and keystone species — strategic foci for protection.
  • Ex situ conservation — captive breeding, seed banks such as Svalbard, and reintroduction programmes.

Practice and policy

Conservation biologists work through protected areas, restoration ecology (including agroforestry buffers and reforestation), legislation such as the US Endangered Species Act (1973), and international frameworks such as CITES and the Convention on Biological Diversity. The field explicitly carries a normative mission — sometimes called a "crisis discipline" — accepting value judgements (biodiversity is good; extinction is bad) within scientific practice.
