Phronesis

Phronesis (Ancient Greek: φρόνησις, romanized: phrónēsis) is a type of wisdom or intelligence concerned with practical action, in the sense of practical wisdom or practical reason. It is one of the five intellectual virtues identified by Aristotle in the Nicomachean Ethics, alongside episteme (scientific knowledge), techne (craft), sophia (theoretical wisdom) and nous (intellect).

In Aristotle

For Aristotle, phronesis is the virtue of practical thought: the ability to deliberate well about what is good and advantageous for living a flourishing life (eudaimonia), and to act accordingly in particular situations. Unlike episteme, which concerns unchanging truths, phronesis deals with things that could be otherwise, and unlike techne, its end is good action itself rather than a product.

Later thought

The concept was taken up by philosophers including Hans-Georg Gadamer, who made it central to philosophical hermeneutics, and by contemporary virtue ethicists and social scientists interested in practical, context-dependent knowledge.
