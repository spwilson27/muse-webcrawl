Golden Legend

The Golden Legend (Latin: Legenda aurea or Legenda sanctorum) is a collection of lives of the saints compiled by Jacobus de Voragine, archbishop of Genoa, around 1260. Organised according to the liturgical year, it contains some 153 chapters recounting the lives of saints and explanations of feasts and seasons. It became one of the most popular books of the later Middle Ages, surviving in more than a thousand manuscripts and shaping medieval iconography for centuries.

Contents

Jacobus drew on earlier legendaries, chronicles, and theological works, retelling stories of apostles, martyrs, virgins, and confessors, together with legendary accounts of the Virgin Mary and Christological feasts. Many of its tales — Saint George and the dragon, Saint Christopher carrying the Christ child, the Seven Sleepers — passed into European folklore and art. The work's title reflects the esteem in which it was held: aurea, "golden".

Influence and decline

The Golden Legend was among the first books printed in several countries; William Caxton published an English translation in 1483. Its narratives supplied subjects for painters from Giotto's successors to the Renaissance masters, and its stories remain a primary reference for identifying saints in art. Humanist critics of the 16th century, and later the Bollandists, attacked its historical reliability, and its authority declined after the Reformation and Counter-Reformation, though it remains valued as a window into medieval devotion and storytelling.
