Angiotensin-converting enzyme

Angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) is a central component of the renin–angiotensin system (RAS), a hormone system that regulates blood pressure and fluid balance. ACE is an enzyme that converts the hormone angiotensin I to the active vasoconstrictor angiotensin II. It is therefore a target for drugs that lower blood pressure, known as ACE inhibitors.

Function

ACE is a zinc-dependent dipeptidyl carboxypeptidase that removes dipeptides from the carboxyl end of peptide substrates. Its best-known substrate is angiotensin I, a decapeptide produced when renin cleaves angiotensinogen. ACE converts angiotensin I into the octapeptide angiotensin II, a potent vasoconstrictor that also stimulates aldosterone release from the adrenal cortex, promoting sodium and water retention. ACE also inactivates bradykinin, a vasodilator peptide, which contributes further to its pressor effect.

Distribution

ACE is found on the surface of endothelial cells, most abundantly in the pulmonary circulation, where blood-borne angiotensin I is efficiently converted during its passage through the lungs. It is also present in the kidneys, heart, brain, and other tissues, where locally produced angiotensin II may act in a paracrine fashion. A soluble form circulates in plasma.

Genetics

The human ACE gene is located on chromosome 17q23. A well-studied insertion/deletion (I/D) polymorphism in intron 16 is associated with variation in circulating ACE levels: individuals with the DD genotype have roughly twice the plasma ACE activity of those with the II genotype. The association of this polymorphism with cardiovascular disease and athletic performance has been studied extensively, with mixed results.

Clinical significance

ACE inhibitors such as captopril, enalapril, lisinopril, and ramipril are widely used to treat hypertension, heart failure, diabetic nephropathy, and after myocardial infarction. A common side effect is a persistent dry cough, attributed to accumulation of bradykinin, which ACE normally degrades. Elevated serum ACE levels can also be a marker of sarcoidosis and other granulomatous diseases.
