Gender identity

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Gender identity is the personal sense of one's own gender. Gender identity can correlate with a person's assigned sex or can differ from it. In most individuals, the various biological determinants of sex are congruent, and consistent with the individual's gender identity. Gender expression typically reflects a person's gender identity, but this is not always the case. While a person may express behaviours, attitudes, and appearances consistent with a particular gender role, such expression may not necessarily reflect their gender identity. The term gender identity was coined by psychiatry professor Robert J. Stoller in 1964 and popularised by psychologist John Money.

Formation

In most cases, the formation of gender identity is theorised to happen early in life, and generally has formed by the age of three. However, it has been suggested that a gender identity is formed as early as conception, though the consensus is that it forms in early childhood through a combination of biological and environmental influences. Biological factors that may influence it include pre- and post-natal hormone levels. Environmental factors include gender expectations, which a child picks up from parents and culture. Gender identity likely results from numerous factors, and it is unknown exactly how much each contributes.

Distinction from sex and gender roles

Gender identity is distinguished from sex, which refers to biological characteristics such as chromosomes, gonads and genitalia, and from gender roles, which are sets of socially defined activities, behaviours and attributes considered appropriate for men and women. People whose gender identity does not match their assigned sex are described as transgender; those whose gender identity matches their assigned sex are described as cisgender. Some people do not identify with a specific gender and identify as non-binary, agender, bigender or genderfluid, among other terms.

Recognition

A growing number of jurisdictions legally recognise gender identity, in some cases allowing legal change of gender on the basis of self-declaration and in others requiring medical assessment. Recognition remains a subject of public and political debate in many countries.
