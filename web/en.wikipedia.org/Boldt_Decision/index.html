Boldt Decision

The Boldt Decision, formally United States v. Washington, 384 F. Supp. 312 (W.D. Wash. 1974), was a landmark ruling by U.S. District Judge George Hugo Boldt concerning Native American fishing rights in the U.S. state of Washington. It affirmed that tribes who signed the Stevens Treaties of the 1850s retained the right to take up to 50% of the harvestable salmon and steelhead at their traditional fishing grounds, and made the tribes co-managers of the state's fisheries.

Background

The treaties negotiated by Governor Isaac Stevens reserved to the tribes "the right of taking fish at usual and accustomed grounds and stations... in common with all citizens of the Territory". For decades the state restricted tribal fishing, arresting Native fishers; in the 1960s activists including Billy Frank Jr. staged "fish-ins" that drew national attention. The United States sued Washington on the tribes' behalf in 1970.

The ruling

After a three-week trial, Judge Boldt ruled on February 12, 1974, that "in common with" meant the tribes were entitled to up to half the harvestable fish destined for their traditional grounds, that hatchery fish counted toward the allocation, and that the state could regulate tribal fishing only for conservation purposes. The decision was largely upheld by the Ninth Circuit and the U.S. Supreme Court in 1979 (Washington v. Washington State Commercial Passenger Fishing Vessel Association).

Aftermath

The decision provoked fierce backlash, including protests and effigy hangings of Judge Boldt, but over time it reshaped fisheries management in the Pacific Northwest. Tribes became co-managers through bodies such as the Northwest Indian Fisheries Commission, and later phases of the case addressed shellfish and habitat protection, including a 2013 injunction requiring the state to repair road culverts that blocked salmon runs.
