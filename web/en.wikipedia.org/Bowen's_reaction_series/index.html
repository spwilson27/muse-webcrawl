Bowen's reaction series


Bowen's reaction series: crystallization order of minerals from a cooling magma

Bowen's reaction series is the work of the Canadian petrologist Norman L. Bowen, who summarized, from experiments and field observations, the sequence in which common silicate minerals crystallize from a cooling basaltic magma undergoing fractional crystallization. It explains why certain minerals occur together in igneous rocks while others rarely coexist.

The series

The series has two branches. In the discontinuous series, early-formed ferromagnesian minerals react with the melt to form new minerals with different structures: olivine → pyroxene → amphibole → biotite. In the continuous series, plagioclase feldspar changes composition continuously, from calcium-rich anorthite to sodium-rich albite, without changing its structure. As cooling proceeds, both branches converge toward potassium feldspar, muscovite, and finally quartz, which crystallizes last at the lowest temperatures (~570 °C).

Implications

If early crystals settle out of the magma (crystal fractionation), the residual melt becomes progressively enriched in silica and alkalis, evolving from basaltic toward andesitic and rhyolitic compositions. The series also predicts weathering susceptibility: minerals crystallizing at high temperatures (olivine, Ca-plagioclase) weather fastest at Earth's surface, the reverse of the crystallization order—an observation formalized in Goldich's dissolution series.
