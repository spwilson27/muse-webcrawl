Computer programming in the punched card era

From the late 19th century into the 1970s, computer programming in the punched card era meant encoding programs and data as holes punched in stiff paper cards, most famously the 80-column card introduced by IBM in 1928.

Workflow

Programmers wrote code on coding sheets, keypunch operators punched decks, and operators submitted decks to batch-processing mainframes. A single misplaced card could force a rerun hours later, encouraging careful desk checking.

Legacy

Card formats shaped early languages such as Fortran and COBOL, and the 80-column line limit survived for decades in terminals and style guides after cards themselves disappeared.
