Black Stone

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Black Stone (Arabic: ٱلْحَجَرُ ٱلْأَسْوَد, al-Ḥajar al-Aswad) is a rock set into the eastern corner of the Kaaba, the ancient building in the centre of the Grand Mosque in Mecca, Saudi Arabia. It is revered by Muslims as a relic dating, according to Islamic tradition, to the time of Adam and Eve, and pilgrims ritually touch or kiss it — or point towards it — during the circumambulation (tawaf) of Hajj and Umrah.

Description

The stone is now a set of fragments, the largest about the size of a date, cemented into a matrix and enclosed in a silver frame. It is roughly 30 cm across, and the exposed surface is dark reddish-black and polished smooth by the hands of millions of pilgrims. Its geological nature is debated; suggestions have included basalt, agate, impact glass, or a meteorite, but it has never been scientifically tested.

History

The stone predates Islam as an object of veneration at the Kaaba. Islamic tradition holds that it was given to Abraham by the angel Gabriel and was originally white, blackened by the sins of humankind. It was damaged by fire and siege over the centuries, and in 930 CE it was carried off by the Qarmatians, who held it in eastern Arabia for about two decades before it was ransomed and returned, by then in pieces.

Ritual role

Muslims do not worship the stone; kissing it follows the example (sunnah) of the Prophet Muhammad. The caliph Umar is reported to have said that he knew it was only a stone that neither benefits nor harms, and that he kissed it only because he saw the Prophet do so.
