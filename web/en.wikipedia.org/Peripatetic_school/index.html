Peripatetic school

The Peripatetic school was a school of philosophy in ancient Greece founded by Aristotle. Its teachings derived from its founder's works, and it owed its name to the peripatos — the covered walkway of the Lyceum in Athens where Aristotle and his students walked and discussed philosophy. Members of the school were known as Peripatetics.

History

Aristotle founded the school in 335 BC when he opened his own philosophical school at the Lyceum, after leaving Plato's Academy. On his death in 322 BC, leadership passed to Theophrastus, a wide-ranging thinker who expanded the school's work in botany, logic and character studies. Later heads included Strato of Lampsacus, noted for an emphasis on natural science, and Lyco of Troas. The school's library, which passed eventually to Rome, preserved Aristotle's treatises for posterity.

Philosophy

The Peripatetics pursued systematic inquiry across logic, physics, metaphysics, ethics, politics, rhetoric and poetics. Unlike the more dogmatic later schools, the early Lyceum prized empirical research: Theophrastus classified hundreds of plants, and Eudemus wrote histories of mathematics and astronomy. In ethics, the school developed Aristotle's doctrine of eudaimonia — human flourishing achieved through a life of virtue guided by reason and cultivated by habit.

Legacy

Peripatetic philosophy was absorbed into the commentary tradition of late antiquity and profoundly influenced medieval Islamic and Christian scholasticism, particularly through the works of Al-Farabi, Avicenna, Averroes and Thomas Aquinas. The term "peripatetic" later entered English as an adjective meaning "wandering" or "itinerant".
