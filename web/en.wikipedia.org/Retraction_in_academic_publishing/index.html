Retraction in academic publishing

In academic publishing, a retraction is a public statement made about an earlier article, indicating that the article should not have been published or that its conclusions are no longer considered reliable. Retractions are issued by the journal, sometimes together with the authors, when serious problems come to light after publication: these include scientific misconduct such as data fabrication or plagiarism, major errors that invalidate the findings, duplicate publication, or unethical research practices. A retraction is distinct from a correction (erratum or corrigendum), which addresses smaller errors, and from an expression of concern, which flags unresolved doubts.

Retracted articles usually remain in the journal's online archive, clearly marked as retracted, together with a notice explaining the reasons; the Committee on Publication Ethics (COPE) publishes guidelines recommending that notices be freely accessible, linked to the article, and state who is retracting it and why.

Reasons for retraction

Studies of retraction notices attribute retractions to misconduct — including fraud, duplicate publication, and plagiarism — in a majority of cases, with honest error accounting for a substantial minority. Other grounds include fake peer review (in which authors or brokers supplied fabricated reviewer reports, a cause of mass retractions from some publishers), authorship disputes, legal issues such as copyright infringement or privacy violations, and compromised editorial processes at paper mills, which produce fabricated manuscripts for sale.

Process

Concerns may be raised by readers, other researchers, whistleblowers, editors, or post-publication peer-review platforms such as PubPeer. Institutions may investigate allegations against their researchers, though journals sometimes retract on their own authority when evidence is clear. Retraction Watch, founded in 2010, maintains a database that grew to tens of thousands of entries and has become a standard reference on the phenomenon.

Frequency and trends

The number of retractions has risen sharply since the early 2000s, far faster than the growth of the literature overall; in 2023 the annual number exceeded 10,000 for the first time, driven substantially by mass retractions of paper-mill articles from special issues. Whether the increase reflects more misconduct, better detection, or a greater willingness of journals to retract remains debated. Retraction is uneven across fields and journals, and some studies find articles continue to be cited positively long after retraction.

Notable retractions

Among the most famous cases is the 1998 Lancet paper by Andrew Wakefield and colleagues suggesting a link between the MMR vaccine and autism; it was fully retracted in 2010 after findings of dishonesty and ethical violations, and is regarded as a landmark of scientific fraud. The 2020 retractions of high-profile COVID-19 papers based on Surgisphere data in The Lancet and The New England Journal of Medicine highlighted the risks of unverifiable datasets. Earlier examples include the Schön scandal in physics and the retracted work of Hwang Woo-suk on cloned stem cells.

Consequences

Retraction can end careers: researchers found guilty of misconduct may lose funding, positions, and degrees. Co-authors may also suffer reputational harm. At the same time, scholars of science studies argue that retraction is an essential self-correcting mechanism, and that visible, prompt retractions are a sign of a healthy literature rather than of failure.
