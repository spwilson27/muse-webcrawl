Charbagh

TypeParadise garden layout
OriginPersia (Achaemenid period)
LayoutQuadrilateral divided into four parts by water channels
Famous examplesTaj Mahal, Humayun's Tomb, Shalimar Gardens

A charbagh or chaharbagh (from Persian čahār bāğ, "four gardens") is a Persian and Indo-Persian quadrilateral garden layout based on the four gardens of Paradise mentioned in the Quran. The quadrangle is divided by walkways or flowing water into four smaller parts. Charbaghs are found throughout the lands of the former Persianate world, from Spain to western India.

Origins and symbolism

The concept descends from the gardens of the Achaemenid Empire, notably the palace garden of Cyrus the Great at Pasargadae (c. 6th century BCE), where stone channels divided the planting into regular plots. In Islamic interpretation the four parts represent the four elements and the four rivers of Paradise — of water, milk, wine, and honey — and the garden as a whole an earthly image of heaven. The raised channels also solved a practical problem: how to irrigate plots evenly on a slight slope.

Development

Under the Timurids of Central Asia, charbaghs became monumental enclosed pleasure grounds outside cities such as Samarkand and Herat. The Mughal emperors carried the form to India, where Babur laid out early examples and where it reached its most famous expression in tomb gardens: Humayun's Tomb in Delhi, which set its mausoleum at the center of the fourfold plot, and the Taj Mahal at Agra, which placed the tomb at the garden's end beside the Yamuna river. The Shalimar Gardens of Lahore and Kashmir adapted the scheme to terraced hillsides.

Elements

Typical elements include enclosing walls, axial water channels (often with fountains and cascades), raised walkways, planting beds set below path level, pavilions at intersections or termini, and a central pool or structure. The geometry is fundamentally axial and symmetrical, reflecting cosmic order.
