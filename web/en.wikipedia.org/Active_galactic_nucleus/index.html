Active galactic nucleus

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An active galactic nucleus (AGN) is a compact region at the centre of a galaxy that emits enormous amounts of energy across the electromagnetic spectrum, from radio waves to gamma rays, far exceeding what its stars produce. The standard model attributes this luminosity to the accretion of matter onto a supermassive black hole of millions to billions of solar masses. AGN are among the most luminous persistent objects in the universe.

Standard model

Infalling gas forms a hot, luminous accretion disc around the black hole. Surrounding the disc is a doughnut-shaped torus of gas and dust, and some AGN launch twin relativistic jets of plasma perpendicular to the disc. In the unification model, different classes of AGN—quasars, Seyfert galaxies, radio galaxies, and blazars—are the same kind of object viewed at different angles: a blazar is seen nearly along the jet, while in type-2 Seyferts and radio galaxies the dusty torus hides the central engine from direct view.

Types

Quasars are the most luminous AGN, visible across billions of light-years. Seyfert galaxies are nearer, lower-luminosity spirals with bright nuclei; type 1 Seyferts show broad emission lines, type 2 only narrow lines. Radio galaxies emit vast lobes of radio radiation extending far beyond the host galaxy. Blazars, including BL Lacertae objects and optically violent variable quasars, show rapid variability because their jets point almost directly at Earth and their emission is relativistically beamed.

Significance

Nearly every large galaxy is thought to host a supermassive black hole, but only a fraction are active at any given time, when gas is funnelled to the centre. AGN feedback—the energy and jets the nucleus injects into its host—helps regulate star formation and galaxy growth, and the tight correlation between black hole mass and galactic bulge properties suggests black holes and galaxies evolve together.
