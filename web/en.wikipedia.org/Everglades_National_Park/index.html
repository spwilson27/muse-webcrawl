Everglades National Park

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Everglades National Park is a United States national park that protects the southern twenty percent of the original Everglades in Florida. The park is the largest tropical wilderness in the United States and the largest wilderness of any kind east of the Mississippi River, covering 1,508,938 acres (2,357 square miles). It was established on December 6, 1947, and was the first national park created primarily to protect a fragile ecosystem rather than scenic or geological features.

Ecosystems

The park encompasses a mosaic of habitats fed by the slow sheet flow of water — the "River of Grass" — from Lake Okeechobee to Florida Bay: sawgrass marshes, wet prairies, sloughs, cypress swamps, tropical hardwood hammocks, pinelands, mangrove forests, and the marine waters of Florida Bay and the Gulf of Mexico. An average of 152 cm of rain falls each year, mostly in summer, and water levels swing dramatically between wet and dry seasons.

Wildlife

The Everglades host hundreds of species of birds (including roseate spoonbills, wood storks, and snail kites), the American alligator and the American crocodile (one of the few places where the two coexist), West Indian manatees, and the endangered Florida panther. Invasive species — above all the Burmese python, which has devastated populations of raccoons, opossums, and bobcats in the park — are a major management challenge.

Threats and restoration

Twentieth-century water management for agriculture and the cities of South Florida diverted roughly half of the Everglades' historic water flow, shrinking wildlife populations dramatically by the 1980s. The Comprehensive Everglades Restoration Plan, authorised by Congress in 2000, is one of the largest ecosystem-restoration projects in the world, intended to restore more natural water flows. The park was placed on the List of World Heritage in Danger in 1993–2007 and again from 2010 because of water pollution and reduced flows.
