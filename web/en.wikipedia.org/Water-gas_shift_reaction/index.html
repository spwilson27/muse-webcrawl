Water-gas shift reaction

The water-gas shift reaction (WGSR) is a chemical reaction in which carbon monoxide reacts with water vapor to form carbon dioxide and hydrogen:

CO + H2O ⇌ CO2 + H2   (ΔH = −41.1 kJ/mol)

The reaction is moderately exothermic and is one of the most important reactions in industrial chemistry, used to increase the hydrogen content of synthesis gas and to adjust the H2/CO ratio for downstream processes.

Industrial application

The shift reaction is central to the production of hydrogen by steam reforming of natural gas and to the manufacture of ammonia via the Haber–Bosch process. Industrially it is usually carried out in two stages: a high-temperature shift (310–450 °C) over an iron oxide–chromium oxide catalyst, followed by a low-temperature shift (around 200–250 °C) over a copper–zinc oxide catalyst to drive CO conversion to very low levels. It is also used in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis and in fuel-cell fuel processors, where removing carbon monoxide protects platinum catalysts.

History

The reaction was discovered by the Italian physicist Felice Fontana in 1780. Its industrial significance grew with large-scale hydrogen production in the 20th century.
