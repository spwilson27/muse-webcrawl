Commonwealth of England

The Commonwealth of England was the political structure during the period from 1649 to 1660 when England and Wales, and later Ireland and Scotland, were governed as a republic after the end of the Second English Civil War and the trial and execution of Charles I. The existence of the Commonwealth was declared through "An Act declaring England to be a Commonwealth", adopted by the Rump Parliament on 19 May 1649.

History

1649–1653: The Commonwealth

Following the execution of Charles I on 30 January 1649, the monarchy was abolished and a republic was declared. Executive power was vested in the English Council of State, while legislative authority rested with the remaining members of the Long Parliament, known as the Rump Parliament. The Commonwealth period saw the conquest of Ireland by Oliver Cromwell's New Model Army and the subjugation of Scotland, which was incorporated into the Commonwealth in 1652. The First Anglo-Dutch War (1652–1654) was fought against the Dutch Republic, England's chief commercial rival.

1653–1659: The Protectorate

In April 1653 Cromwell dissolved the Rump Parliament, and after the short-lived Barebone's Parliament, the Instrument of Government established the Protectorate, with Cromwell as Lord Protector. Though the Protectorate formally covered the same period, the Commonwealth is often distinguished from it. After Cromwell's death in 1658, his son Richard succeeded him but proved unable to control the army and resigned in May 1659.

1659–1660: Collapse and Restoration

The recalled Rump Parliament and army factions struggled for power through 1659. General George Monck marched south from Scotland, and in 1660 a newly elected parliament declared Charles II king, ending the republican experiment and restoring the monarchy.
