Bhakti movement

The Bhakti movement was a significant religious movement in medieval Hinduism that sought a path of devotion (bhakti) — loving devotion to a personal god — as a means of salvation open to all, regardless of caste or gender. Originating in Tamil South India between the 6th and 9th centuries CE, it spread across the subcontinent by the 15th–17th centuries and profoundly shaped Indian religion, literature and society.

Origins

The movement began with the Tamil poet-saints: the Alvars, devotees of Vishnu, and the Nayanars, devotees of Shiva, who composed emotional hymns in the vernacular Tamil rather than Sanskrit. Thinkers such as Ramanuja (11th–12th century) gave bhakti a philosophical foundation in qualified non-dualism (Vishishtadvaita), while Madhvacharya developed dualist theology.

Spread and saints

In Maharashtra the Varkari tradition centred on Vithoba of Pandharpur produced saints such as Dnyaneshwar, Namdev and Tukaram. In the north, Ramananda, Kabir — who blended Hindu and Muslim ideas and rejected caste — the blind poet Surdas, the princess Mirabai, and Tulsidas, author of the Ramcharitmanas, spread devotion to Rama and Krishna in regional languages. In Bengal, Chaitanya Mahaprabhu inspired ecstatic Krishna worship; in Assam, Srimanta Sankardev founded Ekasarana Dharma.

Features and impact

Common themes included the equality of all devotees before God, rejection of empty ritual and caste privilege, the importance of the guru, congregational singing (kirtan, bhajan), and composition in local languages, which enriched regional literatures enormously. The movement also interacted with Sufism and influenced the early Sikh tradition; Guru Nanak emerged from the same devotional milieu of the Punjab.
