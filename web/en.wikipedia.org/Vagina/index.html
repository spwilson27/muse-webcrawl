The vagina is an elastic, muscular canal in the female reproductive system that extends from the cervix of the uterus to the vulva. In humans it is about 9 cm long along its posterior wall and serves as the passage for menstrual flow, the receptive organ in sexual intercourse, and the birth canal during childbirth.

Anatomy

The vaginal wall has three layers: an inner mucous membrane of stratified squamous epithelium thrown into folds called rugae, a middle muscular layer, and an outer adventitia. The cervix protrudes into the upper vagina, creating recesses called fornices. Unlike many other tissues, the vagina has no glands; lubrication comes from cervical mucus and transudation through the vaginal wall.

Microbiome and health

In most women of reproductive age the vagina is colonised by Lactobacillus species that produce lactic acid, keeping pH between about 3.8 and 4.5 and protecting against infection. Disruption of this flora is associated with bacterial vaginosis, and the vagina can also be affected by candidiasis, sexually transmitted infections and, rarely, cancer.

Development

The upper vagina develops from the paramesonephric (Müllerian) ducts and the lower portion from the urogenital sinus. The hymen is a thin membrane that partially covers the vaginal opening at birth and typically changes long before first intercourse through normal activity and hormonal effects.
