Pit latrine

A pit latrine or pit toilet is a type of toilet that collects human feces in a hole in the ground. It consists of a pit, typically at least 3 metres deep, covered by a slab or floor with a drop hole, and often a small shelter for privacy. Pit latrines are among the most common sanitation technologies in the world, used by roughly a billion or more people, especially in rural and low-income areas where sewers and running water are unavailable.

Design and improvements

The simplest form is a plain pit with a slab. The ventilated improved pit (VIP) latrine adds a vent pipe screened against flies, which reduces odor and insect breeding; a dark interior and the pipe's airflow draw smells and flies upward. Pour-flush pit latrines use a water seal to block odors. When a pit fills, it is either emptied or covered and a new pit dug; twin-pit designs allow one side to rest and decompose while the other is used.

Health and environment

Well-sited latrines greatly reduce fecal contamination of the environment compared with open defecation. Risks include groundwater pollution if pits are dug too close to wells or in high water tables, pit collapse in loose soils, and exposure during emptying. Public health programs promote latrine construction and safe emptying as a low-cost step on the "sanitation ladder".
