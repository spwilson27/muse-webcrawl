Pit latrine

A pit latrine, also known as a pit toilet, is a type of toilet that collects human faeces in a hole in the ground. Urine and faeces enter the pit through a drop hole in the floor, which might be connected to a toilet seat or squatting pan for user comfort. Pit latrines are a form of on-site sanitation that generally use little or no water.

Pit latrines are one of the most common forms of sanitation worldwide. As of the mid-2010s, they were used by an estimated 1.77 billion people, mostly in developing countries and rural areas. They are inexpensive to build, but poorly designed or sited pits can contaminate groundwater and surface water and can spread disease if not properly maintained.

Design

A basic pit latrine consists of a pit at least 3 metres deep and about 1 metre across, lined where necessary to prevent collapse, covered by a slab or floor with a drop hole, and sheltered by a superstructure for privacy. Pits fill slowly — typically taking years for a household — because anaerobic digestion and leaching reduce the volume of solids; when full, the pit is covered and a new one dug, or the contents are emptied.

Improved designs

The ventilated improved pit (VIP) latrine adds a vent pipe screened against flies, which reduces odour and traps flies that enter the pit, interrupting disease transmission. Other improvements include pour-flush latrines with a water seal, twin pits used alternately so waste can compost safely before emptying, raised pits for high water tables, and arborloo designs in which a tree is planted on the filled pit.

Health and environment

The World Health Organization classifies safely managed pit latrines as an "improved" sanitation facility when they are not shared and the waste is safely disposed of in situ. Locating pits a safe distance from wells (commonly at least 15–30 metres) and above the water table reduces the risk of pathogen contamination.
