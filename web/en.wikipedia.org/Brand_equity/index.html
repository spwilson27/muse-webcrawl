Brand equity

Brand equity is a phrase used in the marketing industry to describe the commercial value that derives from consumer perception of the brand name of a particular product or service, rather than from the product or service itself. It is the tangible and intangible value a company gains from a product with a recognisable name when compared to a generic equivalent — reflected in premium pricing, customer loyalty, market share resilience and the ease of launching new products under the same name. The concept entered mainstream marketing in the 1980s and is now treated as a major, if hard-to-measure, corporate asset.

FieldMarketing, brand management
Key theoristsDavid Aaker, Kevin Lane Keller
ComponentsBrand awareness, perceived quality, brand associations, brand loyalty
RelatedGoodwill, brand valuation, trademark

Definitions and models

David Aaker defined brand equity as "a set of assets and liabilities linked to a brand, its name and symbol, that add to or subtract from the value provided by a product or service to a firm and/or to its customers", grouping the assets into brand loyalty, name awareness, perceived quality, brand associations and proprietary assets such as patents and trademarks. Kevin Lane Keller's customer-based brand equity (CBBE) model frames equity from the consumer's side: the differential effect of brand knowledge on consumer response to marketing, built as a pyramid from brand salience through performance and imagery to judgements, feelings and finally resonance (loyal engagement).

Measurement

Financial approaches estimate the brand's contribution to earnings, for example by price-premium analysis or by valuing the firm with and without the brand; consultancies such as Interbrand publish annual league tables valuing the largest global brands using earnings, the "role of brand" in purchase decisions, and a brand-strength discount factor. Consumer-based approaches track awareness, associations, perceived quality and loyalty through surveys. Accountancy rules generally bar internally generated brands from balance sheets, though acquired brands are recognised as intangible assets, often under goodwill-related headings.

Effects

High brand equity allows firms to charge price premiums, lowers the cost of customer acquisition, provides leverage with distributors and gives a platform for brand extensions — as well as a buffer during product failures or crises. Negative brand equity, where consumers would prefer a generic product, can arise from scandals or declining quality. Because equity accumulates slowly but can collapse quickly, firms invest in consistent positioning, trademark protection and careful licensing.
