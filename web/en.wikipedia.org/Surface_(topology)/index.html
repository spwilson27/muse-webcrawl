Surface (topology)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In the part of mathematics referred to as topology, a surface is a two-dimensional manifold. Some surfaces are the boundaries of three-dimensional solids; for example, the sphere is the boundary of the solid ball. Other surfaces arise as graphs of functions of two variables in three-dimensional Euclidean space and as more abstract constructions.

More precisely, a topological surface is a topological space in which every point has a neighbourhood homeomorphic to an open subset of the Euclidean plane (for surfaces without boundary) or of a closed half-plane (for surfaces with boundary). This means that, locally, a surface looks like a flat sheet, although globally it may be curved, twisted, or closed upon itself.

Examples

Familiar examples of surfaces include:

  • The sphere, the boundary of a ball in three dimensions;
  • The torus, the surface of a doughnut shape, which may be obtained by revolving a circle around an axis in its plane;
  • The plane and the open disk, which are non-compact surfaces;
  • The Möbius strip, a surface with only one side and one boundary component;
  • The Klein bottle, a closed non-orientable surface that cannot be embedded in three-dimensional space without self-intersection; and
  • The real projective plane, another closed non-orientable surface.

Classification of surfaces

The classification theorem for surfaces states that every connected compact surface is homeomorphic to one of a standard list. A closed orientable surface is determined up to homeomorphism by its genus, the number of "holes" or "handles": the sphere has genus 0, the torus genus 1, the double torus genus 2, and so on. A closed non-orientable surface is determined by its non-orientable genus, the number of projective planes in a connected-sum decomposition. Surfaces with boundary are classified by the corresponding closed surface together with the number of boundary components, each obtained by removing an open disk.

Euler characteristic

Every compact surface has an Euler characteristic, an integer invariant under homeomorphism. For a closed orientable surface of genus g, the Euler characteristic is χ = 2 − 2g; for a closed non-orientable surface of genus k, it is χ = 2 − k. The classification can thus be summarised by two data: orientability and Euler characteristic. When a surface is triangulated, χ can be computed as V − E + F, the number of vertices minus edges plus faces.

Surfaces in geometry

Surfaces are central objects in differential geometry, where they carry notions of curvature. The Theorema Egregium of Gauss shows that Gaussian curvature is intrinsic, and the Gauss–Bonnet theorem relates the total curvature of a compact surface to its Euler characteristic. Every compact surface can be given a geometry of constant curvature — spherical, flat, or hyperbolic — according to the sign of its Euler characteristic, a fact tied to the uniformization theorem.
