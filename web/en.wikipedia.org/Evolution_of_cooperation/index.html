Evolution of cooperation

The evolution of cooperation concerns how cooperative behaviour can arise and persist in populations of self-interested individuals through the mechanisms of evolution and natural selection. Cooperation poses an apparent puzzle for Darwinian theory: an individual that pays a cost to benefit another would seem to be outcompeted by selfish "free riders," yet cooperation is ubiquitous in nature, from bacteria forming biofilms to human societies.

Mechanisms

Research, much of it using game theory and the prisoner's dilemma as a model, has identified several mechanisms by which cooperation can evolve:

  • Kin selection — helping relatives propagates shared genes, summarized by Hamilton's rule (rB > C).
  • Direct reciprocity — repeated interactions allow strategies such as tit for tat to reward cooperation and punish defection, as shown in the tournaments of Robert Axelrod.
  • Indirect reciprocity — cooperation builds a good reputation, which others reward.
  • Network reciprocity — spatial structure or social networks let cooperators cluster and outcompete defectors.
  • Group selection — groups of cooperators can outproduce groups of defectors, a mechanism debated since Darwin and revived in multilevel selection theory.

Martin Nowak has argued that these five mechanisms cover most known routes to cooperation, and that cooperation is a third principle of evolution alongside mutation and selection, necessary for major transitions such as the origin of cells, multicellular organisms, and human language.

Examples in nature

Vampire bats regurgitate blood meals to roost-mates that failed to feed; cleaner fish service client fish without eating them; social insects such as ants and bees exhibit extreme altruism, with sterile workers raising the queen's offspring. In microbes, cooperation includes the production of public goods such as siderophores, which cheats can exploit, providing a model system for studying the maintenance of cooperation.
