Cholinesterase

The cholinesterases are a family of enzymes that catalyze the hydrolysis of the neurotransmitter acetylcholine into choline and acetic acid, a reaction necessary to allow a cholinergic neuron to return to its resting state after activation. There are two main types: acetylcholinesterase and butyrylcholinesterase (also called pseudocholinesterase or plasma cholinesterase).

A cholinesterase enzyme; hydrolysis of acetylcholine terminates cholinergic signaling

Types

Acetylcholinesterase (AChE) is found primarily in neuromuscular junctions and chemical synapses of the cholinergic type, where it serves to terminate synaptic transmission rapidly. It is one of the fastest enzymes known, hydrolyzing thousands of acetylcholine molecules per second.

Butyrylcholinesterase (BChE) is found in the liver, plasma, and many other tissues. It hydrolyzes a broader range of choline esters, including the muscle relaxant succinylcholine; people with genetically low BChE activity can experience prolonged paralysis after succinylcholine administration.

Clinical significance

Inhibitors of cholinesterase — cholinesterase inhibitors — are used medically to treat myasthenia gravis and Alzheimer's disease, and include drugs such as donepezil and pyridostigmine. Irreversible inhibitors include organophosphate insecticides and nerve agents such as sarin, whose toxicity results from uncontrolled accumulation of acetylcholine. Blood cholinesterase levels are monitored in workers exposed to organophosphate pesticides.
