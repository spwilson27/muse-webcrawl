Christmas truce

The Christmas truce (German: Weihnachtsfrieden; French: Trêve de Noël) was a series of widespread unofficial ceasefires along the Western Front of the First World War around Christmas 1914. In the week leading up to 25 December, French, German and British soldiers crossed trenches to exchange seasonal greetings and talk. In some areas, men from both sides ventured into no man's land on Christmas Eve and Christmas Day to mingle and exchange food and souvenirs.

Background

By December 1914 the war had been under way for five months and the opposing armies were dug into static trench lines. The first Christmas of the war provided a natural occasion for informal fraternisation between troops who in many places were stationed only tens of metres apart.

Events

On Christmas Eve, German troops placed candles on their trenches and sang Christmas carols, to which British troops responded with carols of their own. On Christmas Day, soldiers met in no man's land, exchanged gifts such as food, tobacco and alcohol, held joint burial ceremonies and prisoner swaps, and in a few places played impromptu games of football. The truce also allowed a breathing space during which recently killed soldiers could be brought back behind their lines by burial parties.

Aftermath

High commands on both sides disapproved of the fraternisation and issued orders forbidding it; in later years artillery bombardments were deliberately ordered on Christmas Eve to prevent a repeat, although small-scale local truces and quiet periods still occurred. The Christmas truce of 1914 has since become a popular symbol of shared humanity amid the horrors of war, retold in books, films, songs and commemorations.
