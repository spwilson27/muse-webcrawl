Projection (psychology)

Psychological projection is a defence mechanism in which the ego defends itself against disowned and highly negative internal impulses by denying their existence in oneself and attributing them to others. A person experiencing an unacceptable urge or emotion — for example hostility, envy, or desire — instead perceives that motive in another person, often accusing the other of precisely the trait or intention they themselves harbour.

History and theory

The concept originates in psychoanalysis. Sigmund Freud described projection in letters of the 1890s and in his analysis of the Schreber case (1911), where he interpreted paranoid delusions as projections of repressed wishes. His daughter Anna Freud catalogued projection among the ego's defences in The Ego and the Mechanisms of Defence (1936). Later theorists distinguished related forms: projective identification, developed by Melanie Klein, in which the projector unconsciously induces the projected feelings in the recipient; and complementary projection, assuming others share one's own feelings. Carl Jung's notion of the "shadow" incorporates a similar attribution of one's disowned qualities to others.

Examples and function

Classic examples include a person who is unfaithful in inclination accusing their partner of infidelity, or a bully attributing hostile intent to a victim. Projection reduces anxiety in the short term by externalising conflict, but distorts interpersonal perception and can fuel prejudice — out-groups are readily cast as carriers of a majority's disowned traits. In everyday speech, "projection" has broadened to mean any accusation that appears to describe the accuser, and the related concept of the Freudian slip-adjacent "accusation in a mirror" is studied in propaganda research.

Research

Experimental social psychology has studied the related "false consensus effect" and defensive attribution. Some laboratory work finds that suppressing a trait in oneself increases its perceived prevalence in others, partially supporting the classical account, though the strong psychoanalytic version remains difficult to test.
