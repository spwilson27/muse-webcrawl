Hair follicle

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

SystemIntegumentary system
LocationSkin (dermis and epidermis)
FunctionProduction of hair
Associated structuresSebaceous gland, arrector pili muscle, dermal papilla

The hair follicle is an organ found in mammalian skin. It resides in the dermal layer of the skin and is made up of 20 different cell types, each with distinct functions. The hair follicle regulates hair growth via a complex interaction between hormones, neuropeptides, and immune cells. This complex interaction induces the hair follicle to produce different types of hair as observed on different body parts.

Structure

The follicle is a downward pocket of the epidermis extending into the dermis. At its base sits the hair bulb, which encloses the dermal papilla — a cluster of connective tissue carrying capillaries and nerve endings that nourish the growing hair. Rapidly dividing matrix cells above the papilla produce the hair shaft and its inner root sheath; melanocytes among them supply pigment. The outer root sheath surrounds the follicle, and a bulge region partway up houses epithelial stem cells that regenerate the follicle each cycle. Each follicle is typically associated with a sebaceous gland, which secretes sebum into the follicular canal, and a small arrector pili muscle whose contraction raises the hair ("goose bumps").

Hair growth cycle

  • Anagen — the active growth phase; lasts 2–7 years for scalp hair, which is why scalp hair can grow so long.
  • Catagen — a short transitional phase (about 2–3 weeks) in which growth stops and the follicle shrinks.
  • Telogen — the resting phase (about 3 months), after which the old hair is shed (exogen) and a new anagen phase begins.

Humans have roughly 100,000 scalp follicles, formed before birth; no new follicles normally develop in adult life. Follicle density and the duration of anagen vary with body site, age, sex hormones and genetics.

Clinical significance

Disorders of the follicle include alopecia areata (autoimmune attack on the bulb), androgenetic alopecia (hormone-driven miniaturisation of follicles), folliculitis (infection and inflammation, often staphylococcal) and hirsutism. Hair transplantation redistributes follicular units from resistant scalp regions to balding areas.
