Hiragana

Hiragana (平仮名, ひらがな) is a Japanese syllabary, one component of the Japanese writing system alongside katakana, kanji and, occasionally, Latin script (rōmaji). Each hiragana character represents a mora — most often a syllable — of the language. Hiragana is used to write native Japanese words for which there are no kanji, grammatical elements such as particles and inflections, and as furigana, small glosses above kanji to show their pronunciation.

History

Hiragana developed from man'yōgana, Chinese characters used phonetically, which were simplified by cursive writing into flowing forms during the Heian period (794–1185). Because cursive phonetic writing was cultivated especially by women of the court — formally educated men wrote in Chinese — it was called onnade ("women's hand"), and it became the script of classics such as Murasaki Shikibu's The Tale of Genji. Variant forms (hentaigana) coexisted for centuries until a 1900 script reform standardised one hiragana per mora; the modern set was fixed in the reforms of 1946.

The syllabary

The basic inventory comprises 46 characters arranged in the gojūon ("fifty sounds") table: five vowels (a i u e o), and consonant–vowel combinations across the k, s, t, n, h, m, y, r, w rows, plus the single consonant ん (n). Diacritics modify sounds: the dakuten (゛) voices consonants (か ka → が ga) and the handakuten (゜) turns h into p. Small versions of や, ゆ, よ form palatalised combinations (きゃ kya), and the small っ doubles the following consonant.

Usage

Hiragana is usually the first script Japanese children learn, and texts for beginners may be written entirely in it. In ordinary mixed text, kanji carry the roots of content words while hiragana write the inflections (okurigana) and function words, giving Japanese prose its characteristic alternation of the two scripts.
