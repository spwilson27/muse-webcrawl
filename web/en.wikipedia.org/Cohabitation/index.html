Cohabitation

Cohabitation is an arrangement where two people are not married but live together. They are often involved in a romantic or sexually intimate relationship on a long-term or permanent basis. Such arrangements have become increasingly common in Western countries during the past few decades, being led by changing social norms, especially as religious significance attached to marriage diminishes. Cohabitation is also the norm before, or instead of, marriage in many other societies.

Prevalence

In Scandinavia, cohabitation has long been socially accepted and widespread, often as a stable, long-term family form. In the United States and much of Europe, the number of cohabiting couples rose sharply from the 1970s onward; cohabitation is now the most common living arrangement for young adults in many countries, and a majority of marriages are preceded by a period of cohabitation.

Legal status

Legal treatment varies widely. Some jurisdictions (for example, France with the PACS, and several Canadian provinces and Australian states) grant cohabiting partners many rights similar to married couples, especially after a qualifying period or where children are involved. Others recognize few rights, leaving property division, inheritance, and parental rights to contract or litigation. "Common-law marriage" persists in a shrinking number of jurisdictions; in England and Wales the widespread belief in it is known as the "common law marriage myth".

Research findings

Sociologists debate the meaning of cohabitation: it may serve as a trial marriage, a stage in courtship, or an alternative to marriage. Older studies suggested a "cohabitation effect" of higher divorce risk among couples who cohabited before engagement, though more recent research attributes this largely to selection effects and age at first coresidence rather than cohabitation itself.
