A dipole antenna is the simplest and most widely used class of antenna. It consists of two identical conductive elements, such as metal wires or rods, usually arranged symmetrically end to end, with the driving signal applied between them. The most common form is the half-wave dipole, in which each element is approximately one quarter of a wavelength long, so the whole antenna is about half a wavelength.

The dipole was invented by German physicist Heinrich Hertz around 1886, who used it in his pioneering experiments demonstrating the existence of electromagnetic waves predicted by James Clerk Maxwell.

Characteristics

A center-fed half-wave dipole in free space has a feedpoint impedance of approximately 73 ohms, close to the 75-ohm impedance of common coaxial cable, making it easy to match. Its radiation pattern is toroidal: maximum radiation is broadside to the antenna axis, with nulls off the ends. The gain of a half-wave dipole is 2.15 dBi, and the dipole is used as the reference antenna (dBd) against which the gain of other antennas is often expressed.

Variations

Variants include the folded dipole, whose impedance is roughly four times that of a simple dipole (~300 Ω), suited to balanced transmission lines; the short dipole, much shorter than a wavelength; and dipole-derived designs such as the ground-plane antenna, the Yagi–Uda antenna, in which a dipole serves as the driven element among parasitic reflectors and directors. Dipoles are used for FM radio reception, television, shortwave listening, and as elements in large antenna arrays.
