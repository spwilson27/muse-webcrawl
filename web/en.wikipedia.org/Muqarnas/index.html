Muqarnas

Muqarnas vaulting in Islamic architecture

Muqarnas (Arabic: مقرنص), also known in Iranian architecture as Ahoopay and in Iberian architecture as mocárabe, is a form of ornamented vaulting in Islamic architecture. It is sometimes called "honeycomb vaulting" or "stalactite vaulting" because of its resemblance to those forms.

Description

A muqarnas structure is composed of individual three-dimensional cells, arranged in tiers, that subdivide the transition between walls and domes or decorate vaults, cornices, and niches. The cells create a complex, fractal-like geometry that dissolves the solidity of a surface, often interpreted symbolically as representing the celestial sphere or the atoms of creation described in Islamic philosophy.

History

Muqarnas developed around the 10th century in northeastern Iran and North Africa and spread throughout the Islamic world. Famous examples include the Alhambra in Granada, the Shah Mosque in Isfahan, and numerous mosques, madrasas, and palaces from Spain to Central Asia. Materials ranged from wood and stucco to stone and tilework.

Geometry

Muqarnas are constructed over a two-dimensional geometric plan of squares, rhombi, and triangles, projected upward into tiers of niches. Despite the apparent complexity, the underlying plans are based on regular tessellations understood by master builders for centuries.
