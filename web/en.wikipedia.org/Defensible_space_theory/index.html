Defensible space theory

Defensible space theory is a criminological and architectural theory developed by architect Oscar Newman in the early 1970s, set out in his 1972 book Defensible Space. It proposes that the design of residential environments can reduce crime by encouraging residents to exercise territorial control over their surroundings.

Principles

Newman's theory rests on territoriality (design that lets residents claim and identify with spaces), natural surveillance (windows and layouts that allow residents to observe public areas), image (avoiding designs that stigmatise a development), and milieu (locating housing near safe, well-used facilities). His comparisons of adjacent high-rise and low-rise public housing in New York — notably the Van Dyke and Brownsville houses — showed much higher crime in the high-rise blocks, which he attributed to anonymous, undefended common spaces.

Influence and criticism

The theory strongly influenced crime prevention through environmental design (CPTED). Critics note that social and economic factors also drive the observed differences, and that design alone cannot determine crime rates.
