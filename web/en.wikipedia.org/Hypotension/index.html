Hypotension

Blood pressure is measured with a sphygmomanometer.

Hypotension, also known as low blood pressure, is a cardiovascular condition characterised by abnormally low arterial blood pressure. Blood pressure is the force of blood pushing against the walls of the arteries as the heart pumps it out. Hypotension is generally considered to be a systolic blood pressure of less than 90 mmHg or a diastolic pressure of less than 60 mmHg. However, in practice, blood pressure is considered too low only if noticeable symptoms are present.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms include dizziness or lightheadedness, fainting (syncope), blurred vision, nausea, fatigue, and lack of concentration. Extreme hypotension can result in shock, a life-threatening state of inadequate tissue perfusion with cold clammy skin, rapid shallow breathing, and a weak rapid pulse.

Causes

Causes include hypovolaemia from blood loss, dehydration, or sepsis; reduced cardiac output from heart failure, arrhythmias, or heart attack; and excessive vasodilation from anaphylaxis, medication, or autonomic failure. Orthostatic (postural) hypotension is a drop of at least 20 mmHg systolic or 10 mmHg diastolic within three minutes of standing, caused by failure of autonomic reflexes, volume depletion, or drugs; it is common in the elderly and in Parkinson's disease and diabetes. Postprandial hypotension occurs after meals.

Treatment

Mild asymptomatic hypotension needs no treatment. Management includes treating the underlying cause, increasing fluid and salt intake where appropriate, compression stockings, physical counter-pressure manoeuvres, and medications such as fludrocortisone or midodrine for chronic orthostatic hypotension. Acute shock requires urgent intravenous fluids, vasopressors, and cause-specific therapy.
