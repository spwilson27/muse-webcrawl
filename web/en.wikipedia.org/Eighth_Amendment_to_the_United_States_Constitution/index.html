Eighth Amendment to the United States Constitution

The Eighth Amendment (Amendment VIII) to the United States Constitution prohibits the federal government from imposing excessive bail, excessive fines, or cruel and unusual punishments. It was adopted on December 15, 1791, as part of the Bill of Rights. Its text reads:

"Excessive bail shall not be required, nor excessive fines imposed, nor cruel and unusual punishments inflicted."

Origins

The amendment was modelled on the English Bill of Rights of 1689, which contained nearly identical language responding to abuses under the Stuart kings, and on the Virginia Declaration of Rights of 1776, drafted by George Mason.

Cruel and unusual punishments

The Supreme Court has interpreted the Cruel and Unusual Punishments Clause using "evolving standards of decency" (Trop v. Dulles, 1958). The clause was applied against the states through the Fourteenth Amendment in Robinson v. California (1962), which struck down criminalising the status of addiction. Major rulings have barred the death penalty for juveniles (Roper v. Simmons, 2005) and for people with intellectual disabilities (Atkins v. Virginia, 2002), prohibited mandatory life without parole for juveniles (Miller v. Alabama, 2012), and regulated prison conditions and methods of execution. The Court has nonetheless upheld capital punishment itself (Gregg v. Georgia, 1976).

Excessive bail and fines

The Excessive Bail Clause limits pretrial bail to an amount reasonably calculated to ensure the defendant's appearance (Stack v. Boyle, 1951), though it does not guarantee a right to bail in all cases. The Excessive Fines Clause was incorporated against the states in Timbs v. Indiana (2019), a case concerning civil asset forfeiture.
