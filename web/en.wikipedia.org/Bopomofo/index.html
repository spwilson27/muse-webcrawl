Bopomofo

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Bopomofo, also called Zhuyin Fuhao (注音符號; 'phonetic symbols'), is a transliteration system for Standard Chinese and other Sinitic languages. It is the principal phonetic system in Taiwan for teaching reading and writing and is also used to transcribe other character-based languages, such as Taiwanese Hokkien and Hakka. Its name comes from the first four letters of the system: b, p, m, and f (ㄅㄆㄇㄈ).

The system

Bopomofo consists of 37 letters and four tone marks (five counting the neutral tone). The letters represent the sounds of Mandarin: 21 initials (consonants), three medials, and 13 finals (rhymes). Unlike pinyin, which uses the Latin alphabet, Bopomofo symbols are derived from stylized forms of Chinese characters — for example, ㄅ (b) from 勹, and ㄇ (m) from 冖. Tone is indicated by diacritics placed after the syllable, with the first tone often unmarked.

History

The system was developed by the Commission on the Unification of Pronunciation of the Republic of China and promulgated in 1918, based on a proposal associated with the philologist Zhang Binglin. It was the official phonetic system of the Republic of China and remains standard in Taiwan, where schoolchildren learn it before Chinese characters and where it is the most common input method for typing Chinese on computers and phones. On the mainland, it was largely replaced by Hanyu Pinyin after 1949.

Usage

In Taiwan, Bopomofo appears in children's books, dictionaries, and textbooks as an aid to pronunciation, printed beside or above characters in a style analogous to Japanese furigana. Extended Bopomofo symbols exist for transcribing Taiwanese Hokkien, Hakka, and other languages of Taiwan.
