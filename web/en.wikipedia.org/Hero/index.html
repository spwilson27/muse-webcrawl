Hero

A hero (feminine: heroine) is a real person or a main fictional character who, in the face of danger, combats adversity through feats of ingenuity, courage, or strength. The term derives from the ancient Greek hērōs, a class of figures in Greek mythology who were often of partly divine descent and whose deeds were celebrated in epic poetry and cult worship.

In mythology and literature

Greek heroes such as Heracles, Achilles, and Odysseus embodied exceptional ability alongside human flaws. In classical literary theory the hero is the protagonist whose character drives the narrative; Joseph Campbell's influential monomyth, described in The Hero with a Thousand Faces (1949), proposed a common pattern — departure, initiation, and return — underlying hero stories across cultures. Lord Raglan and Otto Rank similarly sought recurring patterns in hero myths.

Modern usage

In modern usage "hero" extends to people recognised for exceptional bravery or selflessness — rescuers, soldiers, and whistleblowers among them — as well as to figures of moral admiration, sometimes called personal heroes. Psychology and social research examine heroism as prosocial behaviour involving voluntary risk for the benefit of others. The concept is also analysed critically: historians such as Thomas Carlyle placed heroes at the centre of history ("great man theory"), while others warn that hero-worship can distort public judgement, and literature since the twentieth century has popularised the antihero, a protagonist lacking conventional heroic virtues.

Heroism studies

The Heroic Imagination Project and researchers such as Philip Zimbardo study whether heroism can be taught and encouraged. Qualities commonly associated with heroism include moral integrity, empathy, persistence, and willingness to act despite personal cost.
