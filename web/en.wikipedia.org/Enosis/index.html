Enosis

Enosis (Greek: Ένωσις, "union") is the movement of various Greek communities living outside Greece for incorporation of the regions they inhabit into the Greek state. The most famous case was the Greek Cypriot movement for the union of Cyprus with Greece, which became the dominant political aim of the island's Greek majority during British colonial rule and shaped the Cyprus problem throughout the twentieth century.

The Megali Idea

Enosis was an expression of the Megali Idea, the nineteenth-century vision of a Greek state encompassing all Greek-inhabited lands of the Ottoman Empire and the Mediterranean. Crete achieved enosis with Greece in 1913 after decades of revolt, and the Ionian Islands had been ceded by Britain in 1864; Cyprus, under British administration from 1878, remained the outstanding goal.

Cyprus

Greek Cypriot demands for enosis grew after the Second World War. A church-organised plebiscite in 1950 recorded overwhelming Greek Cypriot support for union. When Britain refused, the paramilitary organisation EOKA, led by Georgios Grivas, launched an armed campaign from 1955 to 1959. The struggle ended not with enosis but with the Zurich and London Agreements, which created an independent Republic of Cyprus in 1960 with a constitution barring both union and partition.

1974 and aftermath

The demand for enosis was invoked by the Greek military junta when it sponsored a coup against President Makarios in July 1974. Turkey responded with a military intervention, and the island has remained divided ever since. Enosis thereafter ceased to be a practical programme of Greek Cypriot politics, displaced by the pursuit of reunification of the island itself.
