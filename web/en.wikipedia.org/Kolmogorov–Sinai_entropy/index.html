Kolmogorov–Sinai entropy

Chaotic systems generate information at a rate measured by the Kolmogorov–Sinai entropy.

In mathematics and dynamical systems theory, the Kolmogorov–Sinai entropy, also called metric entropy or measure-theoretic entropy, is a numerical invariant of a measure-preserving dynamical system that quantifies its degree of randomness or unpredictability. It was introduced by Andrey Kolmogorov in 1958 and refined by Yakov Sinai in 1959, adapting Claude Shannon's information entropy to dynamics.

Definition

Given a probability space with a measure-preserving transformation T and a finite partition of the space, one measures the Shannon entropy of the joint partition obtained by following the partition's evolution over n time steps; the average entropy per step, in the limit of long times, gives the entropy relative to that partition. The Kolmogorov–Sinai entropy is the supremum of this quantity over all finite partitions. For a generating partition this supremum is attained, which makes the quantity computable in favourable cases.

Significance

Positive entropy characterises chaotic behaviour: by Pesin's theorem, for smooth systems the Kolmogorov–Sinai entropy equals the sum of the positive Lyapunov exponents, linking information production to the exponential divergence of nearby trajectories. Kolmogorov and Sinai used the invariant to prove that Bernoulli shifts of different entropy are not isomorphic, solving a longstanding problem in ergodic theory and showing that measure-theoretic isomorphism is far finer than topological conjugacy. The concept is a cornerstone of ergodic theory, chaos theory and information dynamics.
