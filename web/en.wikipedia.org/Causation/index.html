Causation

Causation is a term with several related meanings. In philosophy it refers to the relation between cause and effect (see causality). In law, causation is the relationship between a defendant's conduct and the resulting harm or damage, which must be proved to establish liability. In statistics and epidemiology, causation refers to the production of an effect by a cause, distinguished from mere correlation.

In law

Legal causation generally has two components: factual causation ("cause in fact"), often tested by the "but-for" test — but for the defendant's act, would the harm have occurred? — and proximate cause, a policy-based limitation on liability for consequences that are too remote or unforeseeable. Variations on these tests appear across tort, contract, and criminal law in common-law jurisdictions.

In philosophy

Philosophers have debated the nature of causation since antiquity: Aristotle's four causes, Hume's regularity theory, and modern counterfactual and interventionist accounts all attempt to say what it is for one event to cause another.

In science

Establishing causation in science requires more than observing association; randomized experiments, natural experiments, and criteria such as the Bradford Hill criteria are used to support causal claims.
