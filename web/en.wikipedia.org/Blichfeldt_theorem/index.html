Blichfeldt theorem

A region of area greater than 1 can be translated to cover two lattice points.

Blichfeldt's theorem is a result in the geometry of numbers, proved by Hans Blichfeldt in 1914. It states that any measurable set in Euclidean space ℝd with volume greater than 1 can be translated so that the translated set contains at least two distinct points of the integer lattice ℤd. Equivalently, any region of area (in the plane) greater than 1 contains two points whose coordinates differ by integers.

Idea of the proof

The proof follows a pigeonhole argument. The set is cut into pieces by the unit squares of the integer grid, and all the pieces are translated back into a single unit square. Because the total area exceeds 1 while the unit square has area exactly 1, at least two pieces must overlap at some point; those overlapping points correspond to points of the original set differing by an integer vector.

Applications

Blichfeldt's theorem is a key step in the standard proof of Minkowski's theorem, which guarantees that a centrally symmetric convex body in ℝd of volume greater than 2d contains a nonzero lattice point. Minkowski's theorem in turn underlies classical results on sums of squares, approximations of real numbers by rationals (Diophantine approximation), and lower bounds in lattice-based cryptography. Generalizations of Blichfeldt's theorem replace the integer lattice with arbitrary lattices and arbitrary measurable sets, and related counting principles bound the number of lattice points a large region can be translated to contain.
