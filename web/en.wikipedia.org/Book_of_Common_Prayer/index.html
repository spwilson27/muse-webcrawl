Book of Common Prayer

Title page of the 1662 Book of Common Prayer

The Book of Common Prayer (BCP) is the traditional service book of the Church of England and the foundational liturgical text of the Anglican Communion. It contains the forms for daily and Sunday worship — Morning and Evening Prayer, the Litany, and Holy Communion — as well as baptism, confirmation, marriage, burial, ordination services, the Psalter, and the ordinal. Thomas Cranmer was the principal author of the first editions.

History

The first Book of Common Prayer appeared in 1549 under Edward VI, replacing the Latin service books of the medieval church with a single English liturgy. A more explicitly Reformed revision followed in 1552. After the book was suppressed under Mary I and restored in a moderated form in 1559 under Elizabeth I, it became a defining symbol of the Church of England's identity.

The 1662 edition, produced after the Restoration of Charles II, remained the official and largely unaltered prayer book of the Church of England for more than three centuries and is still authorized for use. It was imposed by the Act of Uniformity 1662, whose enforcement led to the Great Ejection of nonconforming clergy.

Influence

Versions of the prayer book spread throughout the British Empire and were adapted by Anglican churches worldwide, including the Episcopal Church in the United States (whose current book dates from 1979) and the Anglican Church of Canada. The BCP's prose — much of it Cranmer's — profoundly influenced the English language; phrases such as "speak now or forever hold your peace," "ashes to ashes, dust to dust," and "in sickness and in health" entered everyday speech from its pages. Its collects and liturgy, alongside the King James Bible and Shakespeare, are regarded as pillars of English literary heritage.

Contents

The book provides the daily offices, the eucharistic rite, occasional services for the milestones of life, the Thirty-nine Articles' companion catechism, and a complete metrical and prose Psalter drawn from the Great Bible translation. In the 20th and 21st centuries, many Anglican provinces adopted contemporary-language alternatives, such as the Church of England's Common Worship (2000), while the 1662 book retains devoted adherents.
