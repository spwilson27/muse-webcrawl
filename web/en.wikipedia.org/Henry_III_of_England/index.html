Henry III of England

Henry III (1 October 1207 – 16 November 1272), also known as Henry of Winchester, was King of England, Lord of Ireland, and Duke of Aquitaine from 1216 until his death in 1272. The son of King John and Isabella of Angoulême, Henry assumed the throne when he was only nine in the middle of the First Barons' War. Cardinal Guala Bicchieri declared the war against the rebel barons to be a religious crusade and Henry's forces, led by William Marshal, defeated the rebels at the battles of Lincoln and Sandwich in 1217. Henry promised to abide by the Great Charter of 1225, a later version of the 1215 Magna Carta, which limited royal power and protected the rights of the major barons.

Reign

During his early reign, the country was ruled by a series of regencies: William Marshal, then Hubert de Burgh. Henry's personal rule was marked by piety — he rebuilt Westminster Abbey in honour of Edward the Confessor — and by recurring conflict with the barons over the cost of his foreign policies and the influence of his Poitevin half-brothers, the Lusignans.

The Barons' War

In 1258 Henry agreed to the Provisions of Oxford, which transferred effective power to a council of barons led by Simon de Montfort. The ensuing Second Barons' War saw Henry captured at the Battle of Lewes in 1264; de Montfort summoned the first directly elected parliament in medieval Europe. Henry's son Edward defeated and killed de Montfort at the Battle of Evesham in 1265, and royal authority was restored.

Later life and death

Henry died at Westminster in 1272 and was buried in Westminster Abbey, which he had rebuilt during his reign. He was succeeded by his eldest son, Edward I. His reign of 56 years was the longest of any medieval English king and would not be surpassed until that of George III in the 18th and 19th centuries.
