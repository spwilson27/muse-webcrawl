Tonic immobility

Tonic immobility (TI) is a behaviour in which an animal becomes apparently unresponsive and temporarily paralysed, often in response to a threat. It is also known as thanatosis, animal hypnosis, death feigning or "playing dead". The state may be induced by physical restraint — for example inversion and gentle pressure in sharks, rays, chickens and rabbits — or occur spontaneously when an animal perceives an inescapable threat. Despite the older term "animal hypnosis", tonic immobility is now generally understood as a fear- and defence-related response rather than a hypnotic state.

Characteristics

During tonic immobility the animal shows reduced responsiveness to external stimuli, waxy flexibility or rigidity of the limbs, tremors in some species, slowed heart rate and altered breathing, while remaining conscious. Episodes last from seconds to hours depending on the species and context, and the animal typically recovers abruptly and flees. In insects and other invertebrates, thanatosis is often accompanied by the release of defensive secretions or by tucking in appendages to resemble an inanimate object.

Function

The most widely supported interpretation is that tonic immobility is a last-resort anti-predator defence: many predators are stimulated by movement and lose interest in apparently dead prey, allowing the prey a chance of escape. In some species the behaviour serves other functions — male nursery web spiders that feign death during courtship, for example, increase their mating success — and in ducks and some other animals it may reduce injury during capture.

In sharks and rays

Tonic immobility can be induced in many sharks and rays by inverting the animal and restraining it gently; the shark relaxes, its breathing deepens, and it remains immobile for several minutes on average. Researchers use the technique to handle sharks safely for tagging and measurement. There is evidence that some orcas exploit the response by flipping rays and sharks onto their backs, immobilising them before eating them.

In humans

A comparable involuntary freeze response has been reported in humans under extreme threat, including during sexual assault and combat, and is studied in relation to trauma and post-traumatic stress. It is distinguished from fainting by preserved awareness and muscle tone changes, and its recognition has clinical and legal significance.
