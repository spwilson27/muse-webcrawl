Iconostasis

In Eastern Christianity, an iconostasis (Greek: εἰκονοστάσιον) is a wall of icons and religious paintings, separating the nave from the sanctuary in a church. It typically has three doors: the central Holy Doors (or Royal Doors) and two deacon's doors.

Development

The iconostasis evolved from the Byzantine templon, a low barrier with icons, into the tall, multi-tiered screen familiar in Russian Orthodox churches by the 15th–17th centuries. Rows (tiers) typically include the Sovereign tier with Christ, the Theotokos, and the patron saint of the church; a Deesis tier; a tier of feasts; and tiers of prophets and patriarchs.

Theology

The screen does not divide so much as unite heaven and earth: the icons depict the saints and events witnessing the worship. The Holy Doors are opened at set points in the Divine Liturgy, and only clergy pass through them during services.

Variations

Greek and Balkan iconostases are often carved wood or marble; some modern and Western parishes use lower screens or dispense with a full-height wall. Oriental Orthodox and Eastern Catholic churches use related forms.
