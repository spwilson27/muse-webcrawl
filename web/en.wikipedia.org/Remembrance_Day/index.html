Remembrance Day

Remembrance Day (also known as Poppy Day owing to the tradition of wearing a remembrance poppy) is a memorial day observed in Commonwealth member states since the end of the First World War to honour armed forces members who died in the line of duty. Following a tradition inaugurated by King George V in 1919, the day is also marked in many non-Commonwealth countries.

Observance

Remembrance Day falls on 11 November, the anniversary of the Armistice that ended fighting on the Western Front at the eleventh hour of the eleventh day of the eleventh month of 1918. At 11 a.m. a silence — traditionally two minutes — is observed. Services centre on war memorials and cenotaphs; in the United Kingdom the national service is held at the Cenotaph in Whitehall, London, on Remembrance Sunday, the Sunday nearest to 11 November.

The poppy symbol derives from John McCrae's 1915 poem "In Flanders Fields" and funds charities supporting veterans, organised in Britain by the Royal British Legion. Comparable observances include Anzac Day in Australia and New Zealand, Veterans Day in the United States, and Volkstrauertag in Germany.
