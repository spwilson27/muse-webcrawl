Oncolite

Oncolite
TypeBiogenic sedimentary structure (microbialite / coated grain)
CompositionCalcium carbonate (calcite)
Formed byCyanobacteria and algae, accretion in agitated shallow water
SizeMillimetres to ~10 cm in diameter

Oncolites are small, roughly spherical to ellipsoidal sedimentary structures composed of concentric layers of calcium carbonate built up around a nucleus — such as a shell fragment or grain of sand — by the trapping, binding, and cementing activity of microbial mats, especially cyanobacteria. They are a type of coated grain and one of the group of structures known as microbialites.

Formation

Oncolites grow in shallow, sunlit, periodically agitated water — lakes, lagoons, tidal flats, and rivers — where rolling by currents or waves exposes all sides of the growing nodule to the mat community, producing successive irregular laminae. Unlike the closely related stromatolites, which grow attached in place into domes and columns, oncolites are unattached and free-rolling. Their laminae are typically more irregular and discontinuous than those of ooids, the chemically precipitated coated grains to which they are often compared.

Occurrence

Oncolites occur in the rock record from the Precambrian to the present day and are forming now in places such as the Bahamas, the Great Salt Lake region, and various karst lakes. Rocks rich in them — oncolitic limestones — can serve as decorative building stone. Fossil oncolites help geologists interpret ancient shallow-water environments, and their presence signals well-lit, carbonate-saturated waters of low sedimentation rate. In the Paleozoic, the dasycladacean algae and the problematic organism Girvanella were prolific oncolite-formers, their thread-like filaments preserved in the laminae.
