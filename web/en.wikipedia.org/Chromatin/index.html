Nucleosomes — DNA wound around histone octamers — are the fundamental packing units of chromatin.

Chromatin is a complex of DNA and proteins found in eukaryotic cells, whose primary function is packaging very long DNA molecules into compact, dense structures within the cell nucleus. Chromatin also prevents DNA damage, reinforces the molecule to allow mitosis and meiosis, and controls gene expression and DNA replication. Its protein components are mainly histones, which organize the DNA into structural units called nucleosomes.

Structure

The fundamental repeating unit of chromatin is the nucleosome: a segment of DNA of about 146 base pairs wrapped roughly 1.65 times around a core of eight histone proteins (two each of H2A, H2B, H3, and H4), resembling beads on a string. A fifth histone, H1, binds the linker DNA between nucleosomes and promotes further compaction. Under the electron microscope, nucleosome arrays appear as a fibre about 11 nm wide; this folds into higher-order structures, traditionally modelled as a 30 nm fibre, and ultimately into the dense chromatids of mitotic chromosomes — a packaging ratio of thousands to one, fitting some two metres of human DNA into a nucleus a few micrometres across.

Euchromatin and heterochromatin

Chromatin exists in two broad states. Euchromatin is loosely packed, stains lightly, and is rich in genes that are actively transcribed. Heterochromatin is densely packed, stains darkly, and is generally transcriptionally silent; it occurs as constitutive heterochromatin (permanently condensed, as at centromeres and telomeres) and facultative heterochromatin (conditionally silenced, as in the inactivated X chromosome of female mammals, visible as the Barr body).

Remodelling and epigenetics

Cells dynamically control access to DNA through chemical modifications of histones — acetylation, methylation, phosphorylation, and ubiquitination — and methylation of the DNA itself, collectively forming the field of epigenetics. ATP-dependent chromatin remodelling complexes slide or eject nucleosomes to expose or hide regulatory sequences. These mechanisms allow differentiated cells with identical genomes to maintain distinct patterns of gene expression, and their disruption is implicated in cancer and developmental disorders.
