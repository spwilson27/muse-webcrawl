Provocation (law)

In law, provocation is when a person is considered to have committed a criminal act partly because of a preceding set of events that might cause a reasonable individual to lose self-control. This excuses, or mitigates, an otherwise criminal act, particularly a violent one. In common law jurisdictions it is best known as a partial defence to murder, reducing the offence to manslaughter.

Common law defence

Traditionally, the defence required that the accused acted in the heat of passion caused by provocation, that a reasonable person would have been similarly provoked, and that there was no cooling-off period. Qualifying provocations historically included assault, mutual combat, and witnessing adultery.

Reform

In England and Wales, the Coroners and Justice Act 2009 abolished the defence of provocation and replaced it with the partial defence of "loss of control", which excludes sexual infidelity as a qualifying trigger and requires a fear of serious violence or circumstances of an extremely grave character. In Australia, some states, including New South Wales, have restricted the defence to cases of "extreme provocation", while others, such as Victoria, have abolished it altogether.
