Hurdle rate

Summary

Field: Corporate finance, capital budgeting

Definition: Minimum acceptable rate of return on an investment

Often based on: Weighted average cost of capital (WACC)

The hurdle rate is the minimum rate of return that a company or investor requires before undertaking a project or investment. In capital budgeting, a project's expected internal rate of return (IRR) is compared against the hurdle rate: if the IRR exceeds the hurdle rate, the project is considered acceptable; alternatively, the hurdle rate is used as the discount rate in a net present value (NPV) calculation, and projects with positive NPV pass the test.

Determining the hurdle rate

The hurdle rate is commonly based on the company's weighted average cost of capital (WACC), adjusted upward to reflect the risk of the specific project and sometimes to include a margin of safety. Riskier projects warrant higher hurdle rates. In practice, surveys show that many firms set hurdle rates above their measured cost of capital, partly to ration limited capital and partly to counteract over-optimistic project forecasts.

In private equity and hedge funds

In investment fund structures, "hurdle rate" also refers to the minimum return that must be delivered to investors before the fund manager can share in profits through carried interest or performance fees. A common arrangement is an 8% preferred return hurdle, above which profits are split, often 80/20, between investors and the manager. A "hard hurdle" applies only to profits above the threshold, while a "soft hurdle" allows fees on all profits once the threshold is passed.
