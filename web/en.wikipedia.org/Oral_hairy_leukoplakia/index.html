Oral hairy leukoplakia

Oral hairy leukoplakia
Specialty: Gastroenterology, Infectious disease
Symptoms: White, corrugated patches on the tongue
Causes: Epstein–Barr virus in the setting of immunosuppression
Treatment: Treatment of the underlying immunodeficiency

Oral hairy leukoplakia is a white lesion of the tongue and other oral mucosal surfaces that is strongly associated with immunosuppression, most notably HIV/AIDS, and with infection by the Epstein–Barr virus (EBV). The lesion appears as raised, corrugated or "hairy" white plaques on the lateral borders of the tongue that, unlike fungal candidiasis, cannot be scraped off.

Cause

The condition is caused by the Epstein–Barr virus replicating in the epithelial cells of the tongue. It occurs when the immune system is weakened, so it was first described in 1984 in people with HIV/AIDS, before effective antiretroviral therapy. It also occurs in people immunosuppressed for organ transplantation and, less commonly, in other immunodeficiency states. It is generally not seen in people with competent immune systems.

Signs and symptoms

Oral hairy leukoplakia typically presents as painless white patches on the sides of the tongue, though the floor of the mouth, buccal mucosa and palate may also be affected. The surface is irregular with vertical folds giving a hairy or corrugated appearance. The lesions are usually asymptomatic, though changes in taste or mild discomfort are sometimes reported.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is usually clinical, sometimes confirmed by biopsy. Histopathology shows hyperkeratosis, parakeratosis, ballooning degeneration of spinous layer cells and a characteristic absence of significant inflammation. EBV can be demonstrated in the tissue by in situ hybridisation. The appearance of oral hairy leukoplakia in an HIV-positive person was historically used as a marker of disease progression and immune decline.

Treatment

Treatment is directed at the underlying cause. In HIV infection, the lesions typically regress with effective antiretroviral therapy. Antiviral agents such as aciclovir, topical retinoids and surgical removal have been described but are rarely needed, and lesions frequently recur while immunosuppression persists.
