Ardennes

The Ardennes (French: Ardenne; Dutch: Ardennen; Luxembourgish: Ardennen) is a region of extensive forests, rough terrain, rolling hills and ridges located primarily in Belgium and Luxembourg, extending into Germany and France. It is the oldest mountain range in the region, formed during the Hercynian orogeny.

The region takes its name from the ancient Arduenna Silva, a vast forest mentioned by Julius Caesar in his account of the Gallic Wars.

Geography

The Ardennes covers much of the Walloon Region of Belgium, the northern third of Luxembourg (the Ösling), and parts of the German Eifel. The highest point is the Signal de Botrange in Belgium at 694 metres (2,277 ft). The region is drained by rivers including the Meuse, Ourthe, Amblève, and Semois, whose deep, winding valleys are characteristic of the landscape.

History

The heavily forested terrain of the Ardennes was the scene of major battles in both world wars. In August 1914, French and German forces clashed in the Battle of the Ardennes during the opening weeks of the First World War. In May 1940, German armoured divisions advanced through the Ardennes in the opening of the Battle of France, catching the Allied command by surprise. In December 1944, the region was the site of the Battle of the Bulge, Germany's last major offensive on the Western Front.

Economy and tourism

Slate quarrying, forestry, and agriculture historically dominated the economy. Today the Ardennes is a popular destination for hiking, cycling, kayaking, and camping, with towns such as Durbuy, La Roche-en-Ardenne, and Bouillon attracting visitors. The region is also famous for its cured ham, jambon d'Ardenne.
