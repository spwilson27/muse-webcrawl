Aristotle (384–322 BC) was an Ancient Greek philosopher and polymath. His writings cover a broad range of subjects spanning the natural sciences, philosophy, linguistics, economics, politics, psychology, and the arts. As the founder of the Peripatetic school of philosophy in the Lyceum in Athens, he began the wider Aristotelian tradition that followed, which set the groundwork for the development of modern science.

Born in Stagira in northern Greece, Aristotle joined Plato's Academy in Athens at about seventeen and remained there for twenty years. After Plato's death he left Athens, and at the request of Philip II of Macedon he tutored his son Alexander the Great. He later returned to Athens and founded his own school, the Lyceum.

Life

Aristotle's father, Nicomachus, was court physician to King Amyntas of Macedon. Orphaned young, Aristotle was raised by a guardian, Proxenus of Atarneus. At seventeen or eighteen he went to Athens to study under Plato. In 343 BC he became tutor to the young Alexander. In 335 BC he returned to Athens and founded the Lyceum, teaching there for twelve years. After Alexander's death in 323 BC, anti-Macedonian feeling led Aristotle to withdraw to Chalcis, where he died the following year, traditionally said to be from a stomach ailment (modern scholars have suggested other natural causes).

Thought

Aristotle's surviving works — roughly thirty treatises, compiled as the Corpus Aristotelicum — include the first formal study of logic, built around the syllogism; a metaphysics centred on substance, form and matter, and the four causes (material, formal, efficient, and final); an ethics of character and habit aiming at eudaimonia (flourishing), with virtue as a mean between extremes; a politics analysing constitutions and praising the middle-class polity; and pioneering empirical studies of animals that remained authoritative for centuries. His Poetics shaped literary theory, and his Rhetoric systematised the art of persuasion.

Influence

Aristotle's works were transmitted through Hellenistic, Syriac, and Arabic scholarship — where he was known as "the First Teacher" — and through Latin scholasticism, in which Thomas Aquinas aimed to reconcile Aristotelian philosophy with Christian theology. His natural philosophy underpinned medieval science until challenged in the Scientific Revolution, while his logic remained canonical into the nineteenth century.
