Triangulation (surveying)
A surveying method based on measuring angles in a network of triangles

In surveying, triangulation is the process of determining the location of a point by measuring only angles to it from known points at either end of a fixed baseline by using trigonometry, rather than measuring distances directly as in trilateration.

Method

A baseline of carefully measured length is established, and angles to a distant point are observed with a theodolite from each end. By trigonometry the position of the new point is computed, and it in turn serves as a vertex for further triangles, allowing a network to be extended across a whole country from a single measured baseline.

History

Willebrord Snellius developed the modern method in 1615. Great national triangulations — in India under George Everest, and in Britain under the Ordnance Survey — mapped entire countries in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. The Struve Geodetic Arc, a chain of triangulations from Norway to the Black Sea, is a World Heritage Site.
