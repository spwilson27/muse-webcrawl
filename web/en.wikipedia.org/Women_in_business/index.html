Women in business

Women in business refers to the participation of women as entrepreneurs, executives, and employees in the commercial sector. Although women's participation in the paid workforce rose dramatically during the twentieth century, women remain underrepresented in senior corporate leadership and among founders of venture-backed companies.

History

Women ran businesses in the pre-industrial economy as shopkeepers, innkeepers, and artisans, often through family enterprises. Industrialisation separated home and workplace, and legal regimes such as coverture limited married women's property rights in many jurisdictions until reforms of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.

Leadership representation

In the early twenty-first century women held roughly ten percent of Fortune 500 chief executive positions. The persistent shortfall is commonly attributed to the "glass ceiling", pipeline effects, and unequal access to networks and capital. Several European countries have introduced board quotas, notably Norway's requirement of forty percent female representation on corporate boards.

Entrepreneurship

Women-owned firms account for a substantial share of small businesses in many economies but receive a small fraction of venture capital funding. Microfinance programmes in the developing world have disproportionately targeted women borrowers.
