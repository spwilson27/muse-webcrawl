United States Note

A United States Note, also known as a Legal Tender Note, is a type of paper money that was issued from 1862 to 1971 in the United States. Having been current for over 100 years, they were among the longest-issued types of U.S. paper money. They were known popularly as greenbacks, a name inherited from the earlier Demand Notes that they replaced in 1862. They were called United States Notes by the First Legal Tender Act, which authorized them as a form of fiat currency.

History

During the American Civil War, the federal government faced severe financial strain and, under the Legal Tender Acts of 1862 and 1863, authorized the issuance of paper notes that were not backed directly by gold or silver but were declared legal tender for most debts. Unlike National Bank Notes and later Federal Reserve Notes, United States Notes were issued directly by the United States Treasury. Their constitutionality was upheld by the Supreme Court in the Legal Tender Cases, including Hepburn v. Griswold (initially limiting) and the subsequent decisions in Knox v. Lee and Juilliard v. Greenman.

Characteristics

United States Notes were distinguishable by their red Treasury seal and serial numbers. After 1928 they were issued only in small-size format in denominations of $1, $2, $5, and $100. The Resumption Act and later legislation fixed the amount outstanding at $322,539,016 for many decades. The final issues were the Series 1963 $5 notes and Series 1966 and 1966-A $100 notes; production ceased in 1971, though the notes remain legal tender.
