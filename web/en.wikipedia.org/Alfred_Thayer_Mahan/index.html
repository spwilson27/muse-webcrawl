Alfred Thayer Mahan

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Born: 27 September 1840, West Point, New York, US

Died: 1 December 1914 (aged 74), Washington, D.C., US

Service: United States Navy

Rank: Rear admiral

Known for: The Influence of Sea Power upon History

Alfred Thayer Mahan (27 September 1840 – 1 December 1914) was a United States naval officer and historian, whom John Keegan called "the most influential American strategist of the nineteenth century". His concepts of sea power profoundly shaped naval strategy and shipbuilding around the world in the decades before the First World War.

Career

Mahan was the son of Dennis Hart Mahan, a professor at West Point. He graduated from the United States Naval Academy in 1859 and served in the Union Navy during the American Civil War. A competent but not distinguished sea officer, he found his vocation as a lecturer in naval history and tactics at the Naval War College in Newport, Rhode Island, of which he twice served as president.

Sea power theory

In The Influence of Sea Power upon History, 1660–1783 (1890), Mahan argued that national greatness and prosperity were inseparably linked to maritime commerce and command of the sea, which in turn depended on geography, population, national character, government policy, overseas bases and a powerful battle fleet capable of winning decisive engagements against an enemy's fleet. The book and its sequels were translated widely and were read avidly in Britain, Germany and Japan; Kaiser Wilhelm II ordered copies for his officers. Mahan's ideas helped justify the great-power naval arms races of the early twentieth century and the American drive for a large navy, coaling stations and an isthmian canal.
