Endemic (epidemiology)

In epidemiology, an infection is said to be endemic in a population area when that infection is constantly present at a baseline level in a geographic area without external inputs. For example, chickenpox is endemic in the United Kingdom, but malaria is not; malaria cases seen there are usually acquired abroad. By contrast, a disease that appears irregularly or only occasionally is called sporadic.

Definitions and related terms

An epidemic occurs when case numbers rise substantially above the endemic baseline in a community or region. A pandemic is an epidemic that has spread across multiple countries or continents. When an endemic disease affects a very large proportion of a population it may be called hyperendemic; when endemic levels are unusually stable across wide areas the term holoendemic is sometimes used, particularly for malaria in parts of Africa.

Mathematically, an endemic state corresponds to an effective reproduction number hovering around 1, so that each infection, on average, replaces itself. The disease persists without dying out but does not grow explosively, in contrast to an emerging epidemic where the reproduction number exceeds 1.

Endemic equilibrium

For infections that confer lasting immunity, an endemic equilibrium can be maintained by the replenishment of susceptible people through births and migration. Childhood infections such as measles historically settled into recurrent epidemic cycles around an endemic level, driven by the accumulation of new susceptible children. Vaccination campaigns aim to push the reproduction number below 1 durably, eliminating endemic transmission.

Common usage

During and after the COVID-19 pandemic, "endemic" entered everyday language, sometimes loosely to mean "mild" or "acceptable". Epidemiologists stress that endemicity describes persistence and predictability, not severity: endemic diseases such as malaria and tuberculosis kill large numbers of people every year.
