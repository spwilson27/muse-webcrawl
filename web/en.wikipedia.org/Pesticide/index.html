Pesticide

A pesticide is a substance used to kill, repel or control form of life considered to be pests. The category includes insecticides (against insects), herbicides (against weeds), fungicides, rodenticides, molluscicides and nematicides, as well as plant growth regulators and some disinfectants. Pesticides have been used since ancient times — the Sumerians applied sulfur compounds some 4,500 years ago — and modern synthetic pesticides became widespread after the Second World War.

Uses

Pesticides are used chiefly in agriculture to protect crops and increase yields, and in public health to control disease vectors such as mosquitoes. Roughly 3.5 million tonnes of pesticides are used worldwide each year. The Food and Agriculture Organization defines a pesticide as any substance intended for preventing, destroying, attracting, repelling or controlling any pest during the production, storage or transport of food and other commodities.

Environmental and health effects

Most pesticides are not perfectly selective and can harm non-target organisms, including pollinators, aquatic life and birds. Concerns over the organochlorine insecticide DDT, highlighted by Rachel Carson's Silent Spring (1962), led to bans in many countries and to modern regulatory regimes. Other persistent issues include pesticide resistance in target pests, residues in food and water, acute poisoning of farm workers, and the decline of insect populations linked in part to neonicotinoid insecticides.

Alternatives

Integrated pest management (IPM) combines biological control, crop rotation, resistant varieties and targeted chemical use to reduce dependence on broad-spectrum pesticides. Organic agriculture restricts most synthetic pesticides, relying instead on permitted substances such as copper compounds and naturally derived agents.
