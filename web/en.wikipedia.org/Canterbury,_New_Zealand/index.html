Canterbury (Māori: Waitaha) is a region of New Zealand located in the central-eastern part of the South Island. It is the country's largest region by land area, stretching from the Kaikōura Ranges in the north to the Waitaki River in the south, and from the main divide of the Southern Alps in the west to the Pacific Ocean in the east. Its main city, Christchurch, is the largest in the South Island and the third largest in New Zealand.

Geography

The Canterbury Plains, built from the gravel outwash of braided rivers such as the Waimakariri and Rakaia, form one of New Zealand's great lowland landscapes, backed by the foothills and high country of the Southern Alps, including Aoraki / Mount Cook, the country's highest peak, in the Mackenzie Country. Banks Peninsula, an eroded volcanic complex southeast of Christchurch, contains the harbours of Lyttelton and Akaroa. The region is administered by Environment Canterbury (the Canterbury Regional Council) and comprises ten territorial authorities.

History

Māori of Ngāi Tahu are the tangata whenua of the region. Organised European settlement was led by the Canterbury Association, whose "Pilgrims" arrived at Lyttelton in December 1850 to found a model Anglican colony centred on Christchurch. Canterbury became one of New Zealand's original provinces (1853–1876) and prospered on pastoral farming — sheep runs on the plains and high country — and later on irrigated dairying and cropping.

The Canterbury earthquakes

A magnitude 7.1 earthquake near Darfield in September 2010 and a devastating magnitude 6.3 aftershock beneath Christchurch on 22 February 2011 killed 185 people and destroyed much of the city's centre, triggering a multi-year recovery overseen by the Canterbury Earthquake Recovery Authority. The earthquakes reshaped the region's economy, housing, and identity, and led to strengthened national building standards.
