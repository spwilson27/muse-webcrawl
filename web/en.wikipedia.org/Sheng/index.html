Sheng


A sheng, the Chinese free-reed mouth organ.

The sheng (Chinese: 笙) is a Chinese mouth-blown free-reed instrument consisting of vertical pipes. It is one of the oldest Chinese instruments, with images depicting it dating back to 1100 BCE, and it is traditionally said to have been invented by the goddess Nüwa. The sheng is a polyphonic instrument able to sound several notes at once, and it is an ancestor of Western free-reed instruments such as the harmonica, accordion, and harmonium.

Construction

A traditional sheng consists of a wind chamber — historically made from a gourd, later from wood or metal — into which a set of bamboo pipes of varying lengths is inserted. Each pipe has a free reed at its base, cut from brass or bronze, and a finger hole; the reed sounds when the hole is closed, on both inhalation and exhalation, allowing continuous tone. Standard instruments have 17 pipes, of which 13 or 14 are sounding; modern concert shengs may have 21, 24, 36, or more pipes with an extended range and key mechanisms.

History and use

The sheng was an important instrument in the ritual court music of ancient China and features prominently in Confucian ceremonial ensembles. It later became common in kunqu and other Chinese opera accompaniment, in folk wind-and-percussion ensembles, and in regional traditions such as the lusheng of the Miao and Dong peoples. In the 20th century, redesigned soprano, alto, and bass shengs became standard members of the modern Chinese orchestra, and contemporary composers have written concertos exploiting its chords and glissandi.

Acoustics

Because its reeds are set in pipes whose resonant frequencies must match the reed, the sheng produces a soft, flute-like tone. Unusually among wind instruments, it can produce chords combining notes in fifths and fourths, and its sound can swell and fade by breath pressure alone.
