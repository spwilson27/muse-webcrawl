John Quincy Adams (11 July 1767 – 23 February 1848) was an American statesman and diplomat who served as the sixth president of the United States from 1825 to 1829. The eldest son of President John Adams and Abigail Adams, he had one of the longest public careers in early American history, culminating in nearly 17 years in the House of Representatives after his presidency.

Diplomat and secretary of state

Educated in Europe and at Harvard, Adams served as minister to the Netherlands, Prussia, Russia and Britain, was a U.S. senator from Massachusetts, and helped negotiate the Treaty of Ghent ending the War of 1812. As James Monroe's secretary of state he was a principal architect of American foreign policy: he negotiated the Adams–Onís Treaty (1819), acquiring Florida from Spain and defining the western boundary, and formulated the Monroe Doctrine (1823) warning Europe against further colonisation in the Americas.

Presidency

In the four-way election of 1824, Andrew Jackson won a plurality of electoral and popular votes but no majority; the House of Representatives chose Adams, who then appointed Henry Clay secretary of state — prompting Jackson's charge of a "corrupt bargain". Adams proposed an ambitious programme of internal improvements, a national university and scientific expeditions, much of it blocked by Congress. He signed the protectionist Tariff of 1828 (the "Tariff of Abominations") and lost heavily to Jackson in the 1828 rematch.

Congressman and abolitionist

Uniquely among ex-presidents, Adams returned to elective office, serving Massachusetts in the House from 1831 until his death. He became a leading opponent of slavery in Congress, fighting the "gag rule" against antislavery petitions until its repeal in 1844, and argued the case of the Amistad Africans before the Supreme Court in 1841, securing their freedom. He collapsed on the House floor on 21 February 1848 and died two days later in the Capitol.
