Uralic languages

Distribution of the Uralic languages, from Hungary and Finland across Russia to Siberia.

The Uralic languages form a language family of about 38 languages spoken by roughly 25 million people, principally in Northern and Eastern Europe and northwestern Asia. The family is named after the Ural Mountains, near which its homeland is traditionally located. Its best-known members are Hungarian, Finnish, and Estonian — the family's only national languages of European Union member states outside Russia.

Branches

The traditional branches include Finnic (Finnish, Estonian, Karelian, and others), Sami, Mordvinic, Mari, Permic (Komi and Udmurt), Hungarian, and the Samoyedic languages (Nenets, Enets, Nganasan, Selkup) of Siberia. The reconstructed ancestor, Proto-Uralic, is thought to have been spoken around 4,000–6,000 years ago, possibly east of the Urals or in the Volga region, and to have spread with forest-zone hunter-gatherer cultures.

Typological features

Uralic languages are characteristically agglutinative, building words from chains of suffixes; many use vowel harmony, extensive case systems (Finnish has about fifteen cases), a negative auxiliary verb rather than a negative particle, and lack grammatical gender. Similarities between Uralic languages can be subtle: Finnish käsi "hand" corresponds to Hungarian kéz, and core vocabulary such as words for "water" (Finnish vesi, Hungarian víz) remains recognizably related.

Status

Most Uralic languages besides Hungarian, Finnish, and Estonian are endangered, spoken by minority communities in Russia where Russian dominates public life. Several, such as Livonian, have recently lost their last native speakers, while others — including Northern Sami and Komi — are the objects of revitalization efforts.
