Lymphocyte

A lymphocyte is a type of white blood cell (leukocyte) in the immune system of most vertebrates. Lymphocytes include T cells, B cells, and natural killer (NK) cells. They are the principal cells of the adaptive immune system, though NK cells belong to the innate immune system.

Types

B cells mature in the bone marrow and produce antibodies specific to particular antigens; they are the basis of humoral immunity. T cells mature in the thymus and mediate cell-mediated immunity: helper T cells coordinate the immune response by releasing cytokines, while cytotoxic T cells kill infected or cancerous cells directly. Natural killer cells provide rapid, non-specific responses against virus-infected cells and tumours. Plasma cells, derived from B cells, secrete large quantities of antibody.

Development

All lymphocytes originate from haematopoietic stem cells in the bone marrow. B cells complete their maturation there, whereas T-cell precursors migrate to the thymus. After maturation, lymphocytes circulate in the blood and lymph and populate secondary lymphoid organs such as the lymph nodes, spleen, and tonsils, where they encounter antigens presented by antigen-presenting cells.

Clinical significance

Lymphocytes normally make up about 20–40% of circulating white blood cells in adults. An elevated count (lymphocytosis) often accompanies viral infections, while a low count (lymphocytopenia) can result from HIV infection, chemotherapy, or immunodeficiency. Cancers of lymphocytes include the lymphomas and lymphocytic leukaemias.
