Sandhi (Sanskrit: संधि saṃdhi, "joining") is a term in phonology for the fusion or modification of sounds that occurs at the boundary between morphemes or words. The concept was systematically described by the ancient Indian grammarian Pāṇini in his Aṣṭādhyāyī, and the term is now used generally in linguistics for similar processes in many languages.

Sanskrit sandhi

In Sanskrit, sandhi rules are pervasive: in classical texts words are conventionally written joined together, with final and initial sounds modifying one another. Traditional grammar divides the rules into three groups:

  • Vowel sandhi (svara sandhi) — for example, a + i → e (guṇa), or a + e → ai (vṛddhi); identical vowels merge into a single long vowel.
  • Consonant sandhi (vyañjana sandhi) — final consonants assimilate in voicing, place or manner to a following sound; for example, a final -t may become -d before a voiced sound.
  • Visarga sandhi — the final ḥ (visarga) changes according to its environment, becoming o, r, ś/ṣ/s, or disappearing.

Other languages

Comparable phenomena include French liaison, the tone sandhi of Mandarin and other Chinese varieties (such as the third-tone sandhi), and Welsh initial consonant mutations, which are sometimes described as sandhi. Japanese rendaku (sequential voicing) is another boundary effect. Historical linguistics uses "external sandhi" for changes between words and "internal sandhi" for changes within words.

Significance

The precise formulation of sandhi rules in the Indian grammatical tradition is considered one of the earliest achievements of phonological analysis. Because written Sanskrit applies sandhi continuously, editors and students must reverse the rules ("splitting" the text) to recover the underlying words — a task that remains a challenge in computational processing of Sanskrit.
