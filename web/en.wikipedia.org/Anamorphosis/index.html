Anamorphosis

Anamorphosis is a distorted projection that requires the viewer to occupy a specific vantage point, use special devices, or view the image in a mirror or reflective surface to reconstitute it into a recognisable form. The word derives from the Greek ana ("again") and morphe ("shape, form").

History

Early examples appear in the notebooks of Leonardo da Vinci. The most famous anamorphic artwork is the elongated skull stretched across the foreground of Hans Holbein the Younger's painting The Ambassadors (1533): viewed head-on it appears as a strange floating shape, but seen from a steep angle at the painting's edge it resolves into a human skull, a memento mori. In the 17th century, anamorphic images were popular as optical curiosities and were sometimes used to conceal politically sensitive or erotic images, revealed only in a cylindrical mirror.

Types

Perspective anamorphosis resolves when viewed from a particular oblique angle, while mirror (catoptric) anamorphosis requires a curved mirror. Modern applications include distorted road markings designed to appear correct from a driver's viewpoint, pavement art, and large-scale installations and stadiums lettering.
