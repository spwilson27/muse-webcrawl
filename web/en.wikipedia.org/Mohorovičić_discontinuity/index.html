Mohorovičić discontinuity

The Mohorovičić discontinuity, usually called the Moho boundary, Moho discontinuity, or just the Moho, is the boundary between the Earth's crust and the mantle. It is defined by the distinct change in velocity of seismological waves as they pass through changing densities of rock.

Discovery

The discontinuity is named after the Croatian seismologist Andrija Mohorovičić. Studying the 1909 Kulpa Valley earthquake, Mohorovičić observed that some seismic waves arrived earlier than expected, and inferred the existence of a boundary roughly 54 km beneath the surface at which wave velocity increased abruptly — evidence that the crust is underlain by denser material.

Depth and character

The Moho lies at a depth of about 5–10 km below the ocean floor and 20–90 km, typically about 35 km, beneath continents. Above the boundary, primary seismic waves (P-waves) travel at roughly 6–7 km/s through crustal rocks such as basalt and granite; immediately below, velocities rise to about 8 km/s in the peridotite-rich upper mantle. The discontinuity marks a compositional change rather than a phase change, although in some regions its depth coincides with a basalt-to-eclogite transition zone.

Exploration

Attempts to drill through the Moho, beginning with Project Mohole in the 1960s, have so far been unsuccessful; the boundary's depth beneath continents places it beyond the reach of current ocean-drilling technology, though scientific ocean drilling programs continue to target it.
