Taste bud

Taste buds are clusters of taste receptor cells, which are located on the tongue, soft palate, upper esophagus, the cheek, and epiglottis. These structures are involved in detecting the five elements of taste perception: saltiness, sourness, bitterness, sweetness and savoriness (umami). A popular myth assigns these to different regions of the tongue; in reality all tastes can be detected by any area of the tongue.

On average, the human tongue has 2,000–8,000 taste buds. The average lifespan of these is estimated to be 10 days.

Structure

Taste buds are composed of groups of columnar epithelial cells bundled together along their long axes, with an arrangement similar to the staves of a wooden barrel. They are embedded in the epithelium of the tongue papillae. Each taste bud contains several types of taste receptor cells together with basal and supporting cells. At the top of the bud is a small opening, the taste pore, through which dissolved food molecules contact the receptor cells' microvilli.

Types of papillae

Taste buds are housed in three kinds of lingual papillae: fungiform papillae, scattered over the front of the tongue; circumvallate papillae, arranged in a V-shape at the back; and foliate papillae, along the rear edges. Filiform papillae, the most numerous, contain no taste buds and serve a mechanical role.

Signal transduction

Different stimuli activate the receptor cells by different mechanisms. Salts and acids act directly through ion channels, while sweet, bitter, and umami compounds bind to G protein-coupled receptors. Activated cells release neurotransmitters onto fibers of the facial, glossopharyngeal, and vagus nerves, which carry the signal to the nucleus of the solitary tract in the medulla and onward to the gustatory cortex, where the sensation of taste arises.
