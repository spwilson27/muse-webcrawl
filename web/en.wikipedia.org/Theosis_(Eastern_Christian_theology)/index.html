Theosis (Eastern Christian theology)

In Eastern Christian theology, theosis (from the Greek θέωσις, also called deification or divinization) is a transformative process whose aim is likeness to or union with God. As a process of transformation, theosis is brought about by the effects of catharsis (purification of mind and body) and theoria, the contemplation of God. According to Eastern Christian teaching, theosis is very much the purpose of human life; it is considered achievable only through a synergy between human effort and God's uncreated energies.

Patristic foundations

The doctrine draws on scriptural passages such as 2 Peter 1:4 ("partakers of the divine nature") and the teaching of the Church Fathers. Athanasius of Alexandria famously wrote that the Son of God "became man, that we men might become gods". Maximus the Confessor, John of Damascus, and Symeon the New Theologian all developed the teaching, and it was systematised in the fourteenth century by Gregory Palamas, whose defence of the hesychast monks' practice of contemplative prayer and his distinction between God's essence and energies were affirmed by Orthodox councils.

Stages

The path is classically divided into purification (catharsis), illumination (theoria), and union or deification (theosis) proper. Through ascetic practice, prayer — especially the Jesus Prayer — participation in the sacraments, and the grace of the Holy Spirit, the believer is gradually conformed to the image of Christ. Theosis does not mean becoming God in essence; the distinction between Creator and creature is preserved even in union.

Related concepts

Similar ideas appear in Western traditions under other names, such as sanctification and divinization in Catholic theology, and the term theosis itself has been taken up by some Western theologians. Critics in some traditions have worried that the language of deification compromises the uniqueness of God, while its defenders stress its biblical and patristic roots.
