Mamluk architecture

Mamluk architecture is the architecture built under the Mamluk Sultanate (1250–1517), which ruled Egypt, the Levant and the Hijaz from Cairo. It is regarded as a high point of medieval Islamic architecture, noted for monumental stone construction, elaborate domes, tall minarets and rich surface decoration.

Characteristics

Typical features include ablaq masonry (alternating light and dark stone), muqarnas (stalactite vaulting), carved and inlaid marble and woodwork, monumental portals, and minarets combining square shafts, octagonal sections and cylindrical tops. Religious complexes founded by sultans and emirs commonly combined a mosque, madrasa (often teaching all four Sunni schools of law), mausoleum and sometimes a hospital or Sufi convent, sustained by charitable endowments (waqf).

Notable examples

Major monuments in Cairo include the complex of Sultan Qalawun (1280s), the Mosque-Madrasa of Sultan Hasan (1356–1363), the funerary complex of Sultan Qaitbey (1470s) in the Northern Cemetery, and the Citadel of Qaitbey in Alexandria. Mamluk building also flourished in Damascus, Aleppo, Tripoli, Jerusalem and Mecca. The style continued to influence Egyptian architecture under the Ottomans and was revived in the 19th and 20th centuries as neo-Mamluk or neo-Pharaonic/Islamic revival styles.
