Filipino cuisine (Philippine cuisine) is composed of the cuisines of more than a hundred distinct ethnolinguistic groups across the Philippine archipelago. It developed from Austronesian foundations combined with Spanish, Chinese, American, and other influences over centuries of trade and colonization. Meals are typically centered on rice eaten with one or more dishes (ulam).

Flavor profile and ingredients

Filipino food characteristically balances sour, salty, and sweet flavors. Vinegar and sour fruits such as tamarind and calamansi are central, as are soy sauce, fish sauce (patis), and shrimp paste (bagoong). Coconut milk features prominently in Bicolano and southern dishes, while annatto gives dishes like kare-kare their orange color.

Popular dishes

Adobo, meat braised in vinegar, soy sauce, and garlic, is often called the unofficial national dish. Sinigang is a sour tamarind soup; lechon, whole roasted pig, crowns celebrations. Other staples include pancit noodles of Chinese origin, lumpia spring rolls, sisig made from chopped pork and calamansi, and halo-halo, a shaved-ice dessert. Street foods range from grilled skewers (inihaw) to balut, a fertilized duck egg.

Meals and customs

Filipinos traditionally eat three meals plus merienda (snacks). Kamayan, eating with the hands from banana leaves, remains popular in boodle-fight communal feasts. Regional cuisines—Ilocano, Kapampangan, Bicolano, Visayan, and Moro—differ markedly in ingredients and spicing.
