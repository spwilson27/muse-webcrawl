Indirect election

An indirect election is an election in which voters do not choose office-holders directly, but instead elect an intermediate body — such as an electoral college, a legislature, or a council of delegates — that then makes the final selection. Indirect elections contrast with direct elections, in which voters choose the office-holder themselves. They were historically common when large-scale direct voting was impractical, and they persist today in various federal and parliamentary systems.

Examples

The best-known modern example is the election of the President of the United States: voters in each state choose members of the Electoral College, who formally elect the president. This system has produced several presidents who lost the national popular vote, most recently in 2000 and 2016. Other examples include the election of India's president by an electoral college of parliamentarians and state legislators; the election of the German Federal President by the Federal Convention; and the election of members of upper houses such as India's Rajya Sabha and, before the Seventeenth Amendment (1913), United States senators, who were chosen by state legislatures.

Rationale and criticism

Supporters argue that indirect election can filter momentary popular passions, balance regional interests in federations, and allow informed electors to judge candidates' fitness. Critics respond that it is undemocratic, can distort the popular will, and creates opportunities for intrigue or corruption among a small body of electors. Over the past two centuries the general trend, exemplified by the direct election of U.S. senators, has been toward direct election of most offices.
