The Song of Roland

The Song of Roland (French: La Chanson de Roland) is an Old French epic poem, the oldest surviving major work of French literature. Composed in the late 11th century (c. 1040–1115), it is the most famous of the chansons de geste and dramatises the Battle of Roncevaux Pass in 778, during Charlemagne's campaign in Spain.

Story

Betrayed by his stepfather Ganelon, Roland, Charlemagne's nephew and commander of the rearguard, is ambushed in the Pyrenees by a vastly larger Saracen army (in history, the attackers were Basques). Refusing out of pride to blow his horn, the olifant, to summon help until it is too late, Roland fights heroically alongside his companion Oliver and Archbishop Turpin before dying with his face turned toward Spain. Charlemagne avenges him, and Ganelon is tried and executed.

Significance

The poem's roughly 4,000 lines, arranged in assonanced stanzas called laisses, exalt feudal loyalty, Christian faith, and heroic sacrifice. The Oxford manuscript, the earliest and most complete version, ends with the line "Ci falt la geste que Turoldus declinet" — "Here ends the geste that Turoldus relates." The poem has shaped the legendary image of Charlemagne's paladins across European literature.
