Gregory of Nyssa

Saint Gregory of Nyssa
Bornc. 335, Neocaesarea, Cappadocia
Diedc. 395, Nyssa, Cappadocia
Venerated inEastern Orthodox Church, Roman Catholic Church, others
Feast10 January (Eastern); 9 March (Western)

Gregory of Nyssa (Greek: Γρηγόριος Νύσσης; c. 335 – c. 395), also known as Gregory Nyssen, was an early Christian theologian and bishop of Nyssa in Cappadocia (modern-day Turkey) from 372 to 376 and again from 378 until his death. He is venerated as a saint in Eastern Orthodoxy, Catholicism, Oriental Orthodoxy, Anglicanism, and Lutheranism. Gregory, his elder brother Basil of Caesarea, and their friend Gregory of Nazianzus are collectively known as the Cappadocian Fathers.

Life

Gregory was born into a deeply Christian family in Cappadocia; his parents Basil the Elder and Emmelia raised several children who became saints, including Macrina the Younger and Basil the Great. Educated in rhetoric, he worked as a teacher before embracing the ascetic life around 371. Basil consecrated him bishop of the small town of Nyssa in 372. Gregory's episcopate was troubled: he was deposed by Arian opponents in 376 on charges that were widely regarded as contrived, but was restored after the death of the emperor Valens in 378. He played a leading role at the Council of Constantinople in 381, where he helped complete the Nicene-Constantinopolitan Creed and defend orthodox Trinitarian doctrine.

Theology and writings

Gregory is regarded as one of the most philosophically sophisticated of the Church Fathers. His major works include the Life of Moses, an allegorical treatise on spiritual growth; the Catechetical Oration, a systematic summary of Christian doctrine; On the Soul and the Resurrection, a dialogue with his dying sister Macrina; and commentaries on Ecclesiastes, the Song of Songs, and the Beatitudes. He strongly affirmed the doctrine of apophatic (negative) theology, teaching that God is infinite and beyond all concepts, and developed the idea of epektasis — the soul's eternal progress into the infinite goodness of God. His defence of the full divinity of the Holy Spirit and his contributions to Trinitarian terminology were decisive for later orthodoxy.
