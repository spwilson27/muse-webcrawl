Language of thought hypothesis

The language of thought hypothesis (LOTH), sometimes called mentalese, is a view in philosophy of mind and cognitive science, associated especially with Jerry Fodor, that thought occurs in a mental language with a syntax and semantics similar in key respects to natural language, but realised in the brain rather than spoken or written.

Claims

According to the hypothesis, mental representations — the objects of beliefs, desires and other propositional attitudes — are structured, sentence-like entities. Thinking is a computational process operating on these representations: their combinatorial syntax and semantics explain how a finite mind can entertain an unlimited number of novel thoughts (productivity) and why thoughts come in systematically related families (systematicity).

Arguments and criticism

Fodor argued that concepts being learned presupposes a representational system in which hypotheses can be formulated. Critics include connectionists, who model cognition with neural networks rather than symbol systems; philosophers who deny that thought requires language-like structure; and those who point to non-linguistic cognition in infants and animals. Fodor himself limited the hypothesis chiefly to conceptual, propositional thought.
