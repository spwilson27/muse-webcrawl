Marshallian demand function

In microeconomics, a consumer's Marshallian demand function (named after Alfred Marshall) specifies the quantity of each good the consumer will purchase as a function of the prices of goods and the consumer's income (or wealth). It is the solution to the utility maximisation problem: the bundle that maximises utility subject to the budget constraint that total expenditure not exceed income.

Definition

Formally, for prices p and income m, Marshallian demand x(p, m) = arg max u(x) subject to p·x ≤ m. The maximum utility attained as a function of prices and income is the indirect utility function v(p, m) = u(x(p, m)). Marshallian demand is contrasted with Hicksian (compensated) demand, which solves the expenditure minimisation problem and holds utility constant.

Properties

Marshallian demand is homogeneous of degree zero in prices and income — doubling both leaves demand unchanged. It exhibits both substitution and income effects when prices change, which is why an ordinary demand curve can slope upward for a Giffen good, whereas Hicksian demand always obeys the compensated law of demand. The relationship between the two is expressed by the Slutsky equation, which decomposes the total price effect into substitution and income components. Roy's identity allows Marshallian demand to be recovered from the indirect utility function.

Marshall's original formulation

Marshall treated demand in partial-equilibrium terms, assuming — approximately — a constant marginal utility of money, so that consumer surplus could be measured by the area under the demand curve. Modern general-equilibrium treatments retain his demand function while relaxing that assumption.
