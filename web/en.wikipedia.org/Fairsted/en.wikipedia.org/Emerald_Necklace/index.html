The Back Bay Fens, part of the Emerald Necklace

The Emerald Necklace is a 1,100-acre (4.5 km²) chain of parks linked by parkways and waterways in Boston and Brookline, Massachusetts. It was designed by landscape architect Frederick Law Olmsted, who began work on the project in 1878. The name is said to derive from the way the chain of green spaces "hangs" around the neck of the city of Boston. The system includes Boston Common, the Public Garden, Commonwealth Avenue Mall, the Back Bay Fens, the Riverway, Olmsted Park, Jamaica Pond, the Arnold Arboretum, and Franklin Park.

History

Olmsted's master plan addressed practical problems as well as scenery: the Back Bay Fens was engineered to cleanse the polluted, tidal Muddy River and prevent flooding and sewage backups, while presenting a naturalistic salt marsh landscape. Work continued through the 1890s with the Riverway and Leverett Pond, Jamaica Pond (Boston's water supply, reshaped as a scenic kettle pond), the Arnold Arboretum — designed jointly with the Harvard botanist Charles Sprague Sargent and leased to the university — and Franklin Park, the system's largest and final "country park." Much of Olmsted's work for the Necklace was administered from his Brookline office and home, Fairsted, now preserved as the Frederick Law Olmsted National Historic Site.

Conservation

During the 20th century the Necklace suffered from deferred maintenance, highway encroachments, and water pollution. The Emerald Necklace Conservancy, founded in 1998, works with public agencies on restoration, including the long-running Muddy River Restoration Project, which daylights buried sections of the river and improves flood control through the Fens. The parks remain heavily used for recreation, and the Arnold Arboretum and Franklin Park are among Boston's principal green spaces.
