Parapet

A parapet is a barrier that is an upward extension of the wall at the edge of a roof, terrace, balcony, walkway, or other structure. The word comes from the Italian parapetto, from parare ("to cover, defend") and petto ("chest, breast"). Where extending above a roof, a parapet may simply be the portion of an exterior wall that continues above the edge line of the roof surface, or may be a continuation of a vertical feature beneath the roof, such as a fire wall or party wall.

History and military use

Parapets were integral to medieval fortification: a low protective wall on the outer side of a wall walk behind which defenders could shelter while shooting through gaps. When pierced with alternating solid merlons and open crenels, the parapet is described as crenellated or castellated. In early modern fortifications, massive earth-and-masonry parapets protected gun crews and infantry in bastions and trench lines. Earthwork parapets remained standard in siege warfare and in the trenches of the First World War.

In modern construction

In contemporary buildings a parapet serves several purposes: it prevents falls from roofs, conceals rooftop equipment and the edge of roofing materials, reduces wind uplift on roof coverings, and can act as a fire barrier between attached properties. Building codes commonly require parapets of specified height and fire resistance on flat roofs and terraces. Parapets must be properly flashed and capped — coping stones or metal caps protect their top — because they are exposed to weather on both faces and at the top, making them vulnerable to water ingress and frost damage.

On bridges

Bridge parapets are barriers at the edges of a bridge deck designed to prevent vehicles or pedestrians from falling. Modern highway parapets are engineered as vehicle-restraint systems, tested to redirect errant vehicles, while on older stone bridges the parapet may be a solid masonry wall that also stiffens the structure.
