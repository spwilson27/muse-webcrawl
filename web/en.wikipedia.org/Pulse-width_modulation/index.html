Pulse-width modulation

Pulse-width modulation (PWM), also known as pulse-duration modulation (PDM), is a method of controlling the average power delivered to an electrical load by rapidly switching the supply between fully on and fully off. The average voltage (or current) fed to the load is controlled by turning the switch on and off at a fast rate compared with the load's response time. The fraction of one period in which the signal is on is called the duty cycle.

Principle

A PWM signal has a fixed frequency and a variable duty cycle between 0 and 100 percent. A duty cycle of 50 percent delivers, on average, half the full supply voltage to a resistive load. Because the switching device (usually a transistor) is either fully on, where voltage across it is near zero, or fully off, where current through it is zero, very little power is dissipated in the switch itself. This makes PWM far more energy-efficient than linear regulation for controlling power.

Applications

PWM is used in motor speed control, where the inertia of the motor smooths the pulses into an effectively steady rotation; in dimming of LED and incandescent lighting; in class-D audio amplifiers; in switched-mode power supplies and voltage regulators; in servo motor control, where pulse width encodes a commanded position; and in telecommunications and heating control. Periods from microseconds to seconds are chosen according to the load.

Generation

PWM can be generated by comparing a signal with a sawtooth or triangle carrier wave (analog method), or digitally by counters and timers in microcontrollers, which offer PWM peripherals as standard. Variants include pulse-density modulation and space-vector modulation used in three-phase motor drives.
