Frank Billings Kellogg (December 22, 1856 – December 21, 1937) was an American lawyer, politician, and statesman who served as the 45th United States Secretary of State from 1925 to 1929 under President Calvin Coolidge. He co-authored the Kellogg–Briand Pact, for which he received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1929.

Early life and career

Kellogg was born in Potsdam, New York, and moved with his family to Minnesota as a boy. Largely self-educated, he studied law and was admitted to the bar, building a prominent practice in St. Paul. As a federal prosecutor he gained national attention in antitrust cases, including the successful prosecution of the paper trust and work on the government's case against Standard Oil. He served as president of the American Bar Association in 1912–1913.

Senate and diplomacy

Kellogg represented Minnesota in the United States Senate from 1917 to 1923. After losing re-election, he was appointed Ambassador to Great Britain (1924–1925), and then Secretary of State. His best-known achievement was the Kellogg–Briand Pact of 1928, negotiated with French foreign minister Aristide Briand, by which signatory nations renounced war as an instrument of national policy. Although the pact lacked enforcement mechanisms, it became a foundation stone of modern international law and was later invoked at the Nuremberg trials.

Later life

After leaving the State Department, Kellogg served as a judge on the Permanent Court of International Justice at The Hague from 1930 to 1935. He died in St. Paul in 1937, one day before his 81st birthday.
