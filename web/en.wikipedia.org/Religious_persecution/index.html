Religious persecution

Religious persecution

Believers have been persecuted throughout recorded history

DefinitionSystematic mistreatment of individuals or groups because of their religious beliefs
Related conceptsReligious discrimination, religious intolerance, freedom of religion
ProtectionUniversal Declaration of Human Rights, Article 18

Religious persecution is the systematic mistreatment of an individual or a group of individuals as a response to their religious beliefs or affiliations or their lack thereof. The tendency of societies or groups within societies to alienate or repress subcultures is a recurrent theme in human history. Religious persecution may be triggered by religious or anti-religious stances, or occur when a state favors one religion over others, or when a state is hostile to religion altogether.

Historical examples

In the ancient world, the Roman Empire persecuted early Christians in waves, most severely under Diocletian (303–311). After Christianity became the state religion, pagan practices and heretical Christian groups were themselves suppressed. In medieval Europe, Jews faced expulsions, forced conversions, and massacres, including during the Crusades; the Spanish Inquisition targeted converted Jews and Muslims suspected of secret practice of their former faiths, and the Alhambra Decree of 1492 expelled Jews from Spain. The Wars of Religion in 16th- and 17th-century Europe saw persecution of Protestants and Catholics depending on the territory.

Modern era

The 20th century saw state atheist regimes, notably the Soviet Union and Maoist China, suppress religious practice, close places of worship, and imprison or kill clergy. The Holocaust murdered six million Jews. In the contemporary world, groups reported to face severe persecution include the Uyghur Muslims in China, Rohingya Muslims in Myanmar, Christians in parts of the Middle East, Ahmadi Muslims in Pakistan, Baháʼís in Iran, and Jehovah's Witnesses in Russia, among others.

Legal protections

Freedom of religion is enshrined in Article 18 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948) and the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights. Many national constitutions, such as the First Amendment to the United States Constitution, protect free exercise of religion and bar religious establishments. Despite these protections, monitoring organizations report that government restrictions and social hostilities involving religion remain widespread.
