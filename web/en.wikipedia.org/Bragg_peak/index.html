Bragg peak


Depth–dose curve for a proton beam, showing the Bragg peak

The Bragg peak is a pronounced peak on the Bragg curve, which plots the energy loss of ionizing radiation during its travel through matter. For protons, alpha particles, and other heavy charged particles, the peak occurs immediately before the particles come to rest; it was discovered in 1903 by William Henry Bragg during studies of alpha radiation.

Physics

Charged particles lose energy to ionization at a rate (described approximately by the Bethe formula) that increases as the particle slows down, roughly as the inverse square of velocity. Consequently a fast particle deposits relatively little energy along most of its track, then releases a dense burst of ionization in the last millimetres before stopping. The Bragg peak is therefore a sharp maximum in absorbed dose at a depth determined by the particle's initial energy, beyond which the dose falls almost to zero.

Medical application

The Bragg peak is exploited in proton therapy and carbon-ion therapy for cancer: by tuning the beam energy, clinicians place the peak inside a tumour, sparing tissue beyond it almost entirely and reducing dose to tissue in front of it compared with X-rays. In practice, a spread-out Bragg peak (SOBP) is created by superimposing beams of many energies to cover the tumour volume.
