Combinatorial auction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A combinatorial auction is a type of auction in which participants can place bids on combinations of items, or "packages", rather than just individual items. That is, bidders can express complementarity between items — for example, valuing two adjacent radio spectrum licences more together than the sum of their separate values — and avoid the exposure problem of winning only part of a desired bundle.

Winner determination

The auctioneer must choose an allocation maximising total bid value subject to each item being sold at most once. This winner determination problem is a weighted set-packing problem and is NP-hard in general, so practical auctions rely on integer programming solvers, restricted bidding languages (such as XOR bids, in which a bidder wins at most one of several packages), and problem structure to keep computation tractable.

Design issues

Combinatorial auctions raise several mechanism-design challenges: pricing winning packages so that incentives are acceptable (core-selecting payment rules are common), eliciting preferences without requiring bidders to enumerate exponentially many bundles, and running iterative formats — such as combinatorial clock auctions — that let bidders revise packages as prices evolve. Truthful mechanisms such as the Vickrey–Clarke–Groves (VCG) mechanism generally cannot be applied directly at scale because of computational and revenue problems.

Applications

  • Spectrum auctions — combinatorial clock auctions were used for major mobile spectrum sales in the UK, Canada, Australia, and elsewhere.
  • Transport and logistics — procurement of trucking and delivery contracts as bundles of routes.
  • Electricity and airport slots — allocation problems with strong complementarities.
  • Course allocation and kidney exchange design share related combinatorial methods.
