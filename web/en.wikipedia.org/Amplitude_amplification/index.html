Amplitude amplification

Amplitude amplification is a technique in quantum computing that generalizes the idea behind Grover's algorithm. It allows the amplitude of desired ("good") components of a quantum state to be boosted, so that measuring the state yields a good outcome with high probability. Developed by Gilles Brassard, Peter Høyer, Michele Mosca, and Alain Tapp, it applies to any quantum algorithm that produces a desired result with some probability, increasing that probability quadratically faster than classical repetition.

Overview

Suppose a quantum procedure prepares a state that is a superposition of good and bad components, where the good component has squared amplitude (probability) a. Classically, repeating the procedure would need about 1/a trials to find a good outcome. Amplitude amplification achieves the same with on the order of 1/√a iterations, by alternately applying the preparation procedure (and its inverse) and reflections that mark the good states.

The algorithm

Each iteration applies an operator built from the algorithm and its inverse combined with reflections about the initial state and about the subspace of good states — analogous to the Grover iterate's rotation in the two-dimensional plane spanned by the good and bad components. After roughly π/(4θ) iterations, where sin²θ = a, the good amplitude is close to one. Variants known as amplitude estimation use related ideas to estimate the value of a itself, a subroutine used in quantum algorithms for Monte Carlo simulation and finance.

Applications

  • Grover's algorithm for unstructured search
  • Quantum counting and amplitude estimation
  • Speeding up heuristic and randomized algorithms
