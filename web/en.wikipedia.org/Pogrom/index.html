Pogrom

A pogrom is a violent riot incited with the aim of massacring or expelling an ethnic or religious group, particularly Jews. The term entered English from Russian (погром), where it was used to describe the waves of anti-Jewish violence in the Russian Empire in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. Pogroms are typically characterized by mob violence, destruction of homes, businesses, and houses of worship, and the indifference or complicity of authorities.

Etymology

The word derives from the Russian verb gromit' (громить), "to destroy, wreak havoc." It was first widely used in English after the anti-Jewish riots that followed the assassination of Tsar Alexander II in 1881.

History

Anti-Jewish riots occurred in many eras, including the Hep-Hep riots in Germany (1819). The classic pogroms of the Russian Empire came in waves: 1881–1882, 1903–1906 (including the Kishinev pogrom of 1903 and the Odessa pogrom of 1905), and during the Russian Civil War (1918–1921), when pogroms by various armies killed tens of thousands of Jews in Ukraine. In Nazi Germany, Kristallnacht (November 1938) was a state-organized pogrom against Jews. The Farhud in Baghdad (1941) targeted Iraqi Jews. After the Second World War, the Kielce pogrom in Poland (1946) killed 42 Jewish survivors.

The term has since been extended by historians and journalists to analogous mob attacks on other groups, though its application beyond the Jewish context is sometimes debated.

Consequences

The pogroms of the Russian Empire spurred mass Jewish emigration to the United States, Western Europe, and Ottoman Palestine, and stimulated the growth of Zionism and Jewish self-defense organizations. They left a lasting imprint on Jewish collective memory and on the vocabulary of ethnic violence.
