Northern Paiute people

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Northern Paiute people are a Native American tribe of the Great Basin cultural area, inhabiting parts of the present-day US states of Nevada, California, Oregon, and Idaho. They speak the Northern Paiute language, a member of the Western Numic branch of the Uto-Aztecan language family. The Northern Paiute call themselves Numa (or Nimi), meaning "the people."

Traditionally, the Northern Paiute were organized into small, mobile bands that moved seasonally across the high desert of the Great Basin, harvesting pine nuts, seeds, roots, and small game. Unlike their close relatives the Shoshone, with whom they are sometimes grouped under the historical name "Paiute," the Northern Paiute developed a distinct identity shaped by the marshlands, lakes, and valleys of western Nevada and eastern California and Oregon.

History

Archaeological evidence suggests Numic-speaking peoples spread through the Great Basin over roughly the last thousand years. The Northern Paiute established communities around water sources such as Pyramid Lake, Walker Lake, and the Humboldt River, as well as in California's Owens Valley.

European-American contact intensified in the mid-19th century with the California Gold Rush and the opening of emigrant trails such as the California Trail through Paiute territory. Competition over resources led to conflict, including the Pyramid Lake War of 1860. During this era the Paiute prophet Wovoka (Jack Wilson), who grew up near Walker Lake, founded the Ghost Dance movement of 1890, which spread to many Native nations across the American West.

Federal reservations were established for Paiute bands, including the Pyramid Lake Indian Reservation, the Walker River Indian Reservation, the Duck Valley Indian Reservation (shared with Shoshone), and the Burns Paiute Reservation in Oregon. Many other Northern Paiute communities live in small federally recognized colonies and rancherias in Nevada and California.

Culture

Traditional Northern Paiute life revolved around a seasonal round of hunting and gathering. Pine nuts from the singleleaf pinyon were a staple food, harvested communally in the autumn and cached for winter. Other foods included cattail pollen, wild seeds, jackrabbits — hunted in communal drives using long nets — and fish in the desert lakes.

The Northern Paiute are renowned for fine basketry, producing tightly woven willow baskets, seed beaters, and cradleboards. Housing typically consisted of conical brush shelters called wickiups. Social organization was based on extended families and local bands led by headmen whose authority rested on persuasion rather than coercion.

Language

The Northern Paiute language is closely related to Mono and more distantly to Shoshone, Comanche, and other Numic languages. Dialects vary considerably across the wide geographic range of the people. Today the language is endangered, but revitalization programs at several reservations and colonies work to teach it to younger generations.
