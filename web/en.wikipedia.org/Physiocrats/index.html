Physiocrats

Era
18th century, France
Key figures
François Quesnay, Anne Robert Jacques Turgot, Victor de Riqueti, marquis de Mirabeau

The Physiocrats were a school of French economists of the 18th century who believed that the wealth of nations derived solely from the value of agriculture and land development. Their name comes from the Greek for "government of nature," reflecting their view that economic life follows natural laws that governments should not obstruct.

Doctrine

Led by François Quesnay, physician to Louis XV, the Physiocrats argued that only agricultural labour produced a surplus — the produit net — while manufacturing and commerce merely transformed or moved wealth. Quesnay's Tableau économique (1758) modelled the circulation of income between landowners, farmers, and the "sterile" class, and is regarded as a precursor of modern input-output analysis and national accounting.

Policy ideas

The Physiocrats advocated free trade, the removal of internal tolls and guild restrictions, and a single tax on land (the impôt unique). The phrase laissez faire, laissez passer is associated with their circle. Anne Robert Jacques Turgot attempted Physiocratic reforms as finance minister in 1774–76.

Influence

Physiocracy influenced Adam Smith, who met Quesnay in Paris, and later economists including Henry George. Although its claim that only agriculture is productive was rejected, its emphasis on natural order, free markets, and the circular flow of income made it the first systematic school of economics.
