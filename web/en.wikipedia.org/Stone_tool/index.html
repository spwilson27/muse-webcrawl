Stone tool

A stone tool is any tool made partly or entirely out of stone, produced by the controlled fracturing of rock — a craft called flintknapping or lithic reduction. Stone tools were used throughout prehistory and define the Stone Age; their study (lithic analysis) is a central part of archaeology because they survive where organic materials decay.

Chronology

The earliest known stone tools, from Lomekwi in Kenya, date to about 3.3 million years ago, predating the genus Homo. The Oldowan industry (from about 2.6 million years ago) produced simple choppers and flakes; the Acheulean followed with symmetrical hand axes; the Mousterian of the Neanderthals used prepared-core (Levallois) techniques; and Upper Paleolithic industries produced fine blades, burins, and projectile points. Ground and polished stone tools appeared in the Neolithic alongside agriculture.

Materials and manufacture

Toolmakers favored rocks with conchoidal fracture — flint, chert, obsidian, basalt, and quartzite — striking a core with a hammerstone or antler billet to detach sharp flakes. Retouching shaped edges for cutting, scraping, piercing, or hafting into composite tools.
