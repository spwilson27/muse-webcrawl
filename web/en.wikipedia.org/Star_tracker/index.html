Star tracker

TypeSpacecraft attitude sensor
FunctionDetermines spacecraft orientation from star positions
AccuracyArcseconds (typical modern units)
ComponentsLens, CCD/CMOS detector, star catalogue, processor

A star tracker is an optical device that measures the positions of stars in order to determine the orientation (attitude) of the spacecraft or vehicle carrying it. Star trackers are the most accurate attitude sensors in routine use aboard satellites, interplanetary probes and some aircraft and missiles.

Operation

The tracker images a field of stars with a camera, often a CCD or CMOS detector behind a baffle that blocks sunlight and stray light. An onboard processor extracts star centroids, matches the observed pattern against a stored catalogue of several thousand stars, and computes the vehicle's attitude quaternion relative to an inertial frame such as J2000. Modern "lost in space" algorithms can identify the star field from a cold start without prior attitude knowledge, and units update at rates of several hertz with accuracies of a few arcseconds.

History

Star trackers were developed for ballistic missiles and early spaceflight, where gyroscopes alone drifted too much. Early devices used photomultiplier tubes and mechanical scanning; solid-state detectors in the 1970s and 1980s made compact autonomous trackers practical. Today they fly on nearly every Earth-orbiting observatory and deep-space mission.

Limitations

Trackers must avoid looking near the Sun, Moon or bright Earth limb, and their fields of view can be dazzled by glinting debris; missions therefore often carry two tracker heads in different orientations for redundancy.

Astrophotography

The term is also used for motorised equatorial mounts ("star trackers") that rotate a camera to follow the night sky for long-exposure astrophotography — an unrelated consumer application sharing the name.
