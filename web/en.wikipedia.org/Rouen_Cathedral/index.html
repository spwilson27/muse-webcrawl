Rouen Cathedral (French: Cathédrale primatiale Notre-Dame de l'Assomption de Rouen) is a Roman Catholic church in Rouen, Normandy, seat of the Archbishop of Rouen. A masterpiece of Gothic architecture spanning the 12th to the 16th centuries, it is famous for its ornate Flamboyant west front, its Butter Tower (Tour de Beurre), and for being the subject of more than thirty paintings by Claude Monet.

History

A church stood on the site from late Roman times, and a Romanesque cathedral was begun in the 11th century, of which the crypt survives. The Gothic cathedral was begun around 1145; its Saint-Romain tower was the first part completed. The choir and nave were built in the 13th century, while the celebrated west façade with its two dissimilar towers was enriched over centuries. The central spire, rebuilt in cast iron in the 19th century, made the cathedral briefly the tallest building in the world (1876–1880). The cathedral holds a tomb said to contain the heart of Richard the Lionheart.

Architecture

The cathedral is notable for the Tour Saint-Romain (12th century), the Flamboyant Tour de Beurre (late 15th century) — said to have been funded by Lenten donations for butter — the richly carved Booksellers' Portal (Portail des Libraires), and stained glass spanning the 13th to 16th centuries. Its lantern tower and choir reflect the evolution of Norman Gothic.

Monet series

Between 1892 and 1894 Claude Monet painted over thirty canvases of the west front from nearby viewpoints at different times of day and in different weather, one of the most famous of his "series paintings" exploring light and atmosphere.
