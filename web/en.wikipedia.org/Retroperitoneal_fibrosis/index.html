Retroperitoneal fibrosis (RPF), also known as Ormond's disease, is a rare condition characterised by the development of a fibro-inflammatory mass in the retroperitoneum, typically surrounding the abdominal aorta and iliac arteries and often encasing the ureters. It was described in the English literature by John Ormond in 1948.

Causes and associations

Most cases are idiopathic. A substantial subset is now recognised as part of IgG4-related disease, a systemic fibro-inflammatory condition. Secondary retroperitoneal fibrosis can follow malignancy, infections, radiotherapy, surgery, and certain drugs — classically the ergot derivatives such as methysergide. It is also associated with inflammatory abdominal aortic aneurysm and belongs to the broader concept of chronic periaortitis. Smoking is a risk factor, and an association with asbestos exposure has been reported.

Signs and symptoms

Onset is insidious. Patients commonly report dull, poorly localised back, flank, or abdominal pain, fatigue, low-grade fever, weight loss, and raised inflammatory markers. As the mass encases the ureters, progressive obstruction causes hydronephrosis and, if untreated, renal failure; entrapment of veins and lymphatics can cause leg swelling, deep vein thrombosis, and scrotal swelling.

Diagnosis

Contrast-enhanced CT or MRI shows the characteristic smooth periaortic soft-tissue mantle, typically extending from the renal arteries to the iliac bifurcation, drawing the ureters medially. Biopsy — often laparoscopic — is used to exclude malignancy and confirm IgG4-related disease; blood tests may show raised ESR, CRP, and in some cases serum IgG4.

Treatment

Urgent relief of urinary obstruction is achieved with ureteric stents or nephrostomy. The fibro-inflammatory process usually responds to high-dose glucocorticoids, tapered slowly; steroid-sparing agents include tamoxifen, mycophenolate mofetil, and rituximab, the latter especially in IgG4-related disease. Definitive surgery (ureterolysis, freeing the ureters and wrapping them in omentum) is reserved for refractory cases. Relapses are common, so long-term imaging and renal-function monitoring are required.
