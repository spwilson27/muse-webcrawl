The northern elephant seal (Mirounga angustirostris) is one of two species of elephant seal (the other being the southern elephant seal). It is a member of the family Phocidae (true seals). Elephant seals derive their name from their great size and from the male's large proboscis, which is used in making extraordinarily loud roaring noises, especially during the mating season. Sexual dimorphism is extreme: male elephant seals, called bulls, can weigh up to 2,300 kilograms (5,100 lb) and reach 4 to 5 metres in length, while females are much smaller at around 600 to 900 kilograms.

Distribution and habitat

Northern elephant seals range over the eastern Pacific Ocean, from Alaska and British Columbia south to the coasts of California and Baja California. They spend most of their lives at sea — as much as 90 percent of the year — foraging in the deep waters of the North Pacific, where they dive to depths of 300 to 800 metres (occasionally beyond 1,500 metres) in search of squid, fish, rays and small sharks. They come ashore twice a year: once for the winter breeding season (December to March) and once for the annual moult in late spring and summer. Major rookeries include Año Nuevo State Park and Piedras Blancas in California, and Race Rocks in British Columbia.

Breeding

During the breeding season, dominant bulls establish harems of up to fifty females on sandy beaches and fight rivals, rearing up and slamming their chests together in contests that can leave both bloodied. Males fast throughout the roughly three-month season, losing up to a third of their body weight. Females give birth to a single pup, usually within a few days of arriving, and nurse it for about a month on extremely rich milk, during which time the pup's weight roughly quadruples. Pups are then abruptly weaned when their mothers return to the sea.

Conservation

Northern elephant seals were hunted nearly to extinction for their blubber oil during the nineteenth century; by the 1880s the species was considered extinct, and only a tiny remnant population — perhaps as few as 20 to 100 individuals — survived on Guadalupe Island off Mexico. Protected first by Mexico (1922) and then by the United States, the population recovered spectacularly and now numbers well over 200,000. However, the severe population bottleneck left the species with very low genetic diversity, a factor that continues to concern biologists. The species is listed as Least Concern by the IUCN and is protected in the United States under the Marine Mammal Protection Act.
