French Army Mutinies (1917)

The French Army mutinies of 1917 were a series of collective acts of disobedience by French soldiers on the Western Front during the First World War. They followed the failure of the Nivelle Offensive in April 1917, which had promised a decisive breakthrough but instead produced heavy casualties for little gain. At their height in May and June 1917, the mutinies affected roughly half of the French infantry divisions on the front.

Background

By 1917 French soldiers had endured nearly three years of trench warfare, including the slaughter at Verdun in 1916. General Robert Nivelle promised that his spring offensive on the Chemin des Dames would end the war within 48 hours. The attack, launched on 16 April 1917, failed with well over 100,000 casualties in its first days. Disillusionment spread rapidly through the ranks.

The mutinies

Beginning in late April, soldiers in unit after unit refused orders to return to the attack. Most mutineers were willing to hold defensive positions but refused to participate in further futile offensives. Incidents ranged from passive refusal and drunken disorder to, in a few cases, attempts to march on Paris. Demands included peace, better food, more leave, and an end to wasteful attacks.

Suppression and reform

General Philippe Pétain replaced Nivelle as commander-in-chief on 15 May 1917. Pétain combined repression with reform: courts-martial sentenced thousands, and official figures record about 49 executions, though historians debate the totals. At the same time Pétain improved leave, rest, and food, visited units personally, and promised no more reckless offensives until the arrival of American troops and tanks. Discipline was restored by the autumn, and the French Army recovered sufficiently to play a major role in the Allied victories of 1918. The mutinies were kept secret at the time, and French official records were only fully opened to researchers decades later.
