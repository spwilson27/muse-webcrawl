Line infantry was the type of infantry that composed the basis of European land armies from the late 17th century to the mid-19th century. Armed with smoothbore muskets and bayonets, line infantry fought in tight linear formations, delivering massed volley fire before closing with the bayonet. In the British Army the term survives as the collective name for the ordinary infantry regiments of the line, as distinct from guards, rifles and other specialist corps.

Tactics

Because the smoothbore musket was inaccurate beyond short ranges but quick to reload in trained hands, tactics maximised simultaneous firepower: battalions deployed in lines two or three ranks deep so that every musket could bear, firing by platoon or battalion volleys on command. Drill and discipline kept the formation intact under fire and enabled manoeuvres — marching in column, deploying into line, and forming square against cavalry. Soldiers were trained to load and fire mechanically, often three or more rounds a minute, and to advance steadily without breaking ranks.

Development and decline

The system built on the reforms of Maurice of Nassau and Gustavus Adolphus and the replacement of the pike by the bayonet around 1700, which made every musketeer his own pikeman. It reached its height in the Seven Years' War and the Napoleonic Wars. The spread of rifled muskets firing the Minié ball in the 1850s, then breech-loading rifles and machine guns, made dense linear formations prohibitively vulnerable; the American Civil War and the Franco-Prussian War demonstrated this transition, and by the later 19th century open-order tactics had replaced the line.

Legacy

Many modern infantry regiments retain "of the line" status and traditions — colours, battle honours and marching pace — descended from the line infantry era, even though their battlefield role has entirely changed.
