Overgrazing

Causes: Excessive livestock density, lack of rotation

Effects: Soil erosion, desertification, biodiversity loss

Overgrazing occurs when plants are exposed to intensive grazing for extended periods, without sufficient recovery time for the vegetation to regrow. It can be caused by livestock in poorly managed agricultural settings, or by excessive populations of native or introduced wild herbivores. Overgrazing reduces the usefulness, productivity and biodiversity of the land and is one of the causes of desertification and erosion.

Mechanism

Repeated defoliation depletes plants' carbohydrate reserves and root systems, reducing their ability to regrow. Palatable species are grazed out first and replaced by unpalatable weeds and shrubs — or by bare ground. Trampling by hooves compacts the soil, reducing water infiltration, increasing runoff and erosion, and damaging soil crusts and stream banks. Once vegetation cover falls below a threshold, wind and water erosion accelerate and the system may shift to a degraded state that is difficult to reverse even after grazing pressure is removed.

Causes

Overgrazing is classically associated with the "tragedy of the commons", in which herders sharing common pasture each gain from adding animals while the costs of degradation are shared by all. It also arises where pastoralists are confined to smaller areas by fencing, agriculture, settlement or conservation enclosures, preventing traditional mobility and seasonal rest of pastures; where stocking rates are kept artificially high by water development or subsidies; and around water points, where sacrifice zones of bare ground radiate outward (piospheres). Wild herbivores can overgraze when predators are removed — famously, introduced deer, rabbits and goats on islands have devastated native vegetation.

Examples

Overgrazing contributed to the Dust Bowl of the 1930s in the North American Great Plains, to severe degradation of rangelands in the Sahel and Central Asia, and to the loss of native grasslands in Australia and South America. In Britain and Ireland, heavy sheep grazing of uplands has suppressed woodland regeneration and converted diverse moorland to species-poor grass.

Management

Prevention rests on matching stocking rate to forage growth, rotational and rest-rotation grazing, allowing recovery periods, distributing water and salt to spread grazing pressure, and destocking during drought. Approaches such as holistic planned grazing claim that carefully timed intensive grazing can restore land, though their benefits remain debated among range scientists. Restoration of already degraded land may require reseeding, erosion control works and long-term exclusion of grazing.
