Isma'ilism is a branch of Shia Islam whose followers, the Isma'ilis, accept Isma'il ibn Ja'far as the appointed successor (Imam) to Ja'far al-Sadiq, rather than his younger brother Musa al-Kazim, whom the Twelvers follow. Isma'ilis form the second-largest Shia community, with populations across Central and South Asia, East Africa, the Middle East and a worldwide diaspora.

History

The succession dispute arose after Ja'far al-Sadiq's death in 765. Isma'ili missionaries (da'is) spread the movement across the Islamic world, and in 909 it achieved political power with the Fatimid Caliphate, founded in North Africa, which conquered Egypt, built Cairo and al-Azhar University, and ruled a major empire until 1171.

Following the death of the Fatimid caliph al-Mustansir in 1094, the movement split into Nizari and Musta'li branches. The Nizaris, led from Alamut in Persia, became famous in crusader-era legend as the "Assassins"; after Alamut fell to the Mongols in 1256 their Imams lived in obscurity until emerging in Persia and then India. The Musta'lis later divided into Tayyibi communities — the Dawoodi, Sulaymani and Alavi Bohras — centred on Yemen and Gujarat.

Beliefs and practice

Isma'ilism emphasises the distinction between the exoteric (zahir) and esoteric (batin) meanings of scripture, the necessity of a living, divinely guided Imam, and ta'wil (allegorical interpretation). The largest branch today, the Nizaris, follows the Aga Khan as their hereditary Imam and is known for its development work through the Aga Khan Development Network.
