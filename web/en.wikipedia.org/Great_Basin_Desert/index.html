Great Basin Desert

Area~409,000 km² (158,000 sq mi)
LocationNevada, Utah, and parts of California, Oregon, Idaho, Wyoming (western U.S.)
ClimateCold desert (BWk)

The Great Basin Desert is the largest desert in the United States, covering about 409,000 km² (158,000 sq mi) of the Great Basin hydrographic region. It occupies most of Nevada, more than half of Utah, and portions of California, Oregon, Idaho and Wyoming. It is a temperate cold desert, created by the rain shadow of the Sierra Nevada to the west.

Geography and climate

The desert consists of hundreds of north–south trending mountain ranges separated by broad basins — the classic Basin and Range topography. Elevations are high, mostly 1,200–1,800 m in the valleys, so winters are cold and much of the precipitation falls as snow. Annual rainfall is typically 15–30 cm. Unlike the hot deserts to the south, the Great Basin Desert lacks creosote bush and is dominated by sagebrush.

Flora and fauna

Big sagebrush (Artemisia tridentata) and shadscale dominate the shrub steppe; at higher elevations grow pinyon pine and juniper woodlands, and on the highest ranges bristlecone pine, among the oldest living organisms on Earth. Wildlife includes pronghorn, mule deer, jackrabbits, sage-grouse, kit foxes and wild horses. During the Pleistocene, much of the basin held great pluvial lakes, including Lake Bonneville, whose remnant is the Great Salt Lake.

Human history

Indigenous peoples, including the Shoshone, Paiute and Ute, lived in the region for millennia. European-American settlement intensified after 1847 with Mormon pioneers in Utah, followed by mining booms, ranching and the transcontinental railroad. Great Basin National Park, established in Nevada in 1986, protects a representative sample of the desert's ecosystems.
