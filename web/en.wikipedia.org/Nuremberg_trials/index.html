The Nuremberg trials were a series of trials held after World War II to prosecute prominent leaders of Nazi Germany for war crimes, crimes against peace, and crimes against humanity. The first and best known of these trials was that of the major war criminals before the International Military Tribunal (IMT), held between 20 November 1945 and 1 October 1946 in the city of Nuremberg, in the American occupation zone of Germany. Twenty-four leading Nazis were indicted; the tribunal sentenced twelve to death, imposed prison terms on seven, and acquitted three. Twelve subsequent trials before American military tribunals (the "subsequent Nuremberg trials") prosecuted another 177 defendants, including doctors, judges, industrialists, and Einsatzgruppen leaders.

The International Military Tribunal

The IMT was established by the London Charter of August 1945, agreed by the United States, the United Kingdom, the Soviet Union, and France, each of which appointed judges and prosecutors; the chief US prosecutor was Supreme Court Justice Robert H. Jackson. Charges were organised under four counts: conspiracy, crimes against peace, war crimes, and crimes against humanity. Proceedings were conducted with simultaneous interpretation (a pioneering use of the technology) and rested heavily on the Nazis' own documents and film evidence. Hermann Göring, Joachim von Ribbentrop, Wilhelm Keitel, Alfred Jodl and others were sentenced to hang; Göring killed himself with a cyanide capsule the night before his execution (16 October 1946). Rudolf Hess, Erich Raeder and Walther Funk received life sentences; Albert Speer and Baldur von Schirach received 20 years; Hans Fritzsche, Franz von Papen and Hjalmar Schacht were acquitted.

Subsequent trials

Under Control Council Law No. 10, the United States conducted twelve further trials at Nuremberg between December 1946 and April 1949, including the Doctors' Trial (which produced the Nuremberg Code of medical ethics), the Judges' Trial, the Industrialists trials (Flick, IG Farben, Krupp), the Hostages Trial, and the Einsatzgruppen Trial — the largest murder trial in history, concerning the mobile killing squads that murdered over a million people in the occupied Soviet Union.

Legacy

Nuremberg established the principles that individuals — including heads of state and those "following orders" — bear personal criminal responsibility under international law. The Nuremberg Principles were affirmed by the UN General Assembly in 1946 and fed directly into the Genocide Convention (1948), the Geneva Conventions (1949), the ad hoc tribunals for Yugoslavia and Rwanda in the 1990s, and the permanent International Criminal Court (2002). Critics at the time and since have debated charges of victors' justice and retroactive law, but the trials remain the foundational precedent of modern international criminal law.
