Region

In geography, regions are areas or divisions of land or space that share identifiable characteristics — physical, cultural, political, or functional — that distinguish them from neighbouring areas. Regions exist at every scale, from neighbourhoods and administrative districts to continents and global zones such as the tropics.

Regions partition space according to chosen criteria.

Types of region

  • Formal (uniform) regions are defined by a shared characteristic, such as a common language, climate, crop, or legal jurisdiction — for example, the Corn Belt or the French-speaking region of Belgium.
  • Functional (nodal) regions are organised around a node or focal point, such as a city and its commuter hinterland, a newspaper's circulation area, or a river's drainage basin.
  • Vernacular (perceptual) regions exist in people's minds, like "the South" in the United States or "the North" of England, with fuzzy boundaries that vary between individuals.

Administrative regions

Many states organise government through named regions: France is divided into 18 régions, Italy into 20 regioni, and the Philippines into administrative regions that coordinate national agencies. Statistical bodies define standard regions for reporting, such as the EU's NUTS classification and the United Nations geoscheme of world regions.

Regional geography

The study of regions was the dominant paradigm of geography in the first half of the 20th century (regional geography, or chorology, championed by Richard Hartshorne), before the quantitative revolution shifted the discipline toward spatial analysis and general laws. Interest in place and region revived with humanistic and critical geography, treating regions as historically produced and socially constructed entities.
