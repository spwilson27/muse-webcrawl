Ms. (honorific)

Ms. (pronounced miz) is an English-language honorific used before the surname or full name of a woman, which — unlike Miss and Mrs. — does not indicate her marital status. It is the female counterpart to the marital-status-neutral Mr. for men and predates, though it was popularised by, the feminist movement of the 20th century.

History

The form emerged in the 17th century as an abbreviation of "Mistress", the original title from which both Miss and Mrs. also derive. It was revived in 1901 in an American newspaper as a proposed solution for addressing a woman whose marital status was unknown. Feminist activists, notably Sheila Michaels, promoted it from the 1960s, and it gained wide currency after the founding of Ms. magazine by Gloria Steinem in 1971–72. The New York Times long resisted the term but adopted it in 1986. Government forms, businesses and style guides now generally accept Ms. as the default title for women unless another is specified.

Usage

Ms. is appropriate when a woman's marital status is unknown or irrelevant, or when it is her stated preference. It is written "Ms." in American English and usually "Ms" without a full stop in British English, and it takes a surname or full name, never a husband's name in place of her own.
