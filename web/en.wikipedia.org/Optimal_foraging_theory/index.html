Optimal foraging theory

Optimal foraging theory (OFT) is a behavioural ecology model that helps predict how an animal behaves when searching for food. It treats foraging as an optimisation problem shaped by natural selection: animals are expected to forage in ways that maximise net energy intake per unit time, subject to constraints such as predation risk and handling time.

FieldBehavioural ecology
Key worksMacArthur & Pianka (1966); Emlen (1966)
Core modelsDiet-breadth model; marginal value theorem

Models

The diet-breadth (prey choice) model predicts whether a predator should include a less profitable prey type in its diet based on encounter rates with more profitable prey, handling times and energy values. It predicts that specialists arise when profitable prey are abundant, and generalists when they are scarce. The marginal value theorem, proposed by Eric Charnov in 1976, predicts how long a forager should stay in a depleting patch before moving on: it should leave when its intake rate drops to the average rate for the habitat.

Evidence and applications

Classic tests include studies of great tits choosing mealworms of different sizes, oystercatchers selecting mussels, and bees visiting flowers. OFT has been applied in anthropology to human hunter-gatherer subsistence, in conservation, and in understanding pollinator behaviour, though real animals often deviate from simple optima because of nutrient balancing, predation, learning and information limits.

Criticisms

Critics note that maximising energy is not always the relevant currency, that animals may lack the information the models assume, and that predictions can be hard to falsify. Modern approaches incorporate state-dependent behaviour, risk sensitivity and multiple currencies.
