Tax bracket

Tax brackets are the divisions at which tax rates change in a progressive tax system. Essentially, tax brackets are the cutoff values for taxable income — income past a certain point is taxed at a higher rate. A taxpayer's marginal tax rate is the rate of the highest bracket reached, while the effective rate is the total tax divided by total income, which is always lower than the marginal rate under a progressive system.

How brackets work

Under a bracketed system, only the income within each band is taxed at that band's rate. For example, if the first bracket taxes income up to $10,000 at 10% and the next taxes income from $10,000 to $40,000 at 12%, a person earning $40,000 pays 10% on the first $10,000 and 12% on the remaining $30,000 — not 12% on the whole amount. The widespread misconception that moving into a higher bracket can reduce take-home pay is therefore generally false.

United States

The U.S. federal income tax has seven brackets, with rates from 10% to 37%, thresholds varying by filing status and adjusted annually for inflation. The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 revised the brackets and rates. Many states operate their own bracket systems, while some levy a flat tax and others have no income tax.

Bracket creep

When thresholds are not indexed to inflation, nominal income growth pushes taxpayers into higher brackets even without real gains in purchasing power — an effect called bracket creep or fiscal drag. Indexing thresholds, as the United States has done since the 1980s, mitigates this.
