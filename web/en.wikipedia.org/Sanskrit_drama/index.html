Sanskrit drama

OriginsIndia, by the early centuries CE
Key theoristBharata Muni (Natya Shastra)
Major playwrightsKālidāsa, Bhāsa, Śūdraka, Bhavabhūti
LanguagesSanskrit and Prakrits

Sanskrit drama refers to the tradition of theatre and dramatic literature composed in Sanskrit in ancient and medieval India. Flourishing at royal courts between roughly the 1st and 10th centuries CE, it is governed by the aesthetic theory of the Natya Shastra, attributed to Bharata Muni, which defines plot structure, acting, music and the doctrine of rasa — the emotional flavour savoured by the audience.

Conventions

Classical plays mix verse and prose and several languages: gods, kings and learned men speak Sanskrit, while women, servants and comic characters speak regional Prakrits. A play opens with a benediction and a prologue in which the stage manager introduces the piece. Death, violence and other inauspicious events are not shown directly, and tragedies are essentially absent — even painful stories end in reunion or reconciliation. Conventional figures include the noble hero (nāyaka), the heroine, and the vidūṣaka, a Brahmin jester who provides comedy.

Major works

The greatest name is Kālidāsa (c. 4th–5th century), author of Abhijñānaśākuntalam (The Recognition of Shakuntala), Vikramorvaśīyam and Mālavikāgnimitram. Śūdraka's Mṛcchakaṭika (The Little Clay Cart) is an unusually social, urban play, while Bhavabhūti's Uttararāmacarita and the thirteen plays attributed to Bhāsa — including Svapnavāsavadattam — complete the core canon. Viśākhadatta's Mudrārākṣasa is a rare political intrigue drawn from the age of Chandragupta.

Survival

After the 12th century the courtly tradition waned, but it survives in performance in Kerala's temple theatres of Kutiyattam, inscribed by UNESCO as a Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity, and continues to influence modern Indian theatre.
