Buchenwald (German: Konzentrationslager Buchenwald) was one of the largest concentration camps established by Nazi Germany. Opened in July 1937 on the Ettersberg hill near Weimar, it operated until its liberation in April 1945. Its gate bore the cynical motto Jedem das Seine — "to each his own", or "everyone gets what he deserves" — legible from inside the camp.

Prisoners

About 280,000 people from more than 30 countries were imprisoned in Buchenwald and its 139 subcamps. The prisoners included political opponents of the Nazi regime, Jews (especially after Kristallnacht in 1938 and in massive transports from Hungary and Poland late in the war), Roma, Jehovah's Witnesses, homosexuals, criminals, prisoners of war, and forced labourers. Prisoners worked in armaments factories for firms including Gustloff and, at the Dora-Mittelbau subcamp system, on V-weapon production.

Conditions and deaths

An estimated 56,545 prisoners died at Buchenwald from starvation, disease, medical experiments, beatings, executions, and the death marches of early 1945. The camp was not a dedicated extermination camp like those in occupied Poland, but mass killings occurred there, including of Soviet prisoners of war shot in a purpose-built facility. Notable prisoners included the theologian Paul Schneider, the writer Elie Wiesel, and the politician Léon Blum.

Liberation and after

As US forces approached, the SS forced some 28,000 prisoners onto evacuation marches; an underground prisoner resistance seized control of the camp on 11 April 1945 shortly before American troops arrived. The US Army's liberation photographs and films — and the display of camp atrocities to Weimar citizens and the press — became iconic evidence of Nazi crimes. From 1945 to 1950 the Soviet occupation used the site as Special Camp No. 2 for interned Germans, in which over 7,000 more died. Buchenwald is today a major memorial site.
