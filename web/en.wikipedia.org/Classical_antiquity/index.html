Classical antiquity

Classical antiquity, also known as the classical era, classical period, or Greco-Roman world, is the period of cultural history between the 8th century BC and the 5th century AD centred on the Mediterranean Sea, comprising the intertwining civilisations of ancient Greece and ancient Rome known together as the Greco-Roman world. It spans the rise of Greek city-states, the conquests of Alexander the Great, the Roman Republic and Empire, and the eventual fall of the Western Roman Empire.

Archaic and Classical Greece

The Greek world emerged from the Iron Age with the rise of the polis, or city-state. Colonisation spread Greek culture around the Mediterranean and Black Sea coasts. Athens developed democracy in the 5th century BC, while Sparta maintained a militarised oligarchy. Greek victories in the Greco-Persian Wars were followed by the Golden Age of Athens, which produced philosophy, drama, historiography and monumental architecture such as the Parthenon. The Peloponnesian War weakened the city-states, opening the way for Macedonian dominance under Philip II and Alexander the Great, whose conquests inaugurated the Hellenistic Age.

The Roman Republic and Empire

Rome grew from a small Italian city-state into the dominant Mediterranean power through the Punic Wars against Carthage and expansion into the Hellenistic east. Civil wars in the 1st century BC ended the Republic and produced the Principate of Augustus, the first Roman emperor. Under the Empire, Roman law, engineering, roads, aqueducts and urban culture spread from Britain to Mesopotamia. The Pax Romana brought two centuries of relative stability, after which the Empire was divided into eastern and western halves.

Culture and legacy

Classical antiquity produced foundational works of literature — the epics of Homer and Virgil, Greek tragedy, Roman satire — as well as philosophy from Plato and Aristotle to the Stoics and Epicureans. Its art, architecture, political concepts and languages (Latin and Greek) profoundly shaped later European civilisation through the Renaissance and beyond. The period is conventionally said to end with the deposition of the last western emperor in AD 476, though the Eastern Roman (Byzantine) Empire continued for nearly another millennium.
