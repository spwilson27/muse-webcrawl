The Twelve Olympians (Greek: Δωδεκάθεον, Dōdekatheon, "twelve gods") were the principal deities of the Greek pantheon, residing atop Mount Olympus. They gained their supremacy after Zeus led his siblings to victory in the Titanomachy, the war against the Titans. The canonical twelve were Zeus, Hera, Poseidon, Demeter, Athena, Apollo, Artemis, Ares, Aphrodite, Hephaestus, Hermes, and either Hestia or Dionysus.

The twelve

  • Zeus — king of the gods, god of the sky and thunder.
  • Hera — queen of the gods, goddess of marriage and family.
  • Poseidon — god of the sea, earthquakes, and horses.
  • Demeter — goddess of the harvest and agriculture.
  • Athena — goddess of wisdom, warfare strategy, and crafts.
  • Apollo — god of prophecy, music, healing, and archery.
  • Artemis — goddess of the hunt, the wilderness, and the moon.
  • Ares — god of war and bloodshed.
  • Aphrodite — goddess of love and beauty.
  • Hephaestus — god of fire, metalworking, and craftsmen.
  • Hermes — messenger of the gods, god of travel, trade, and thieves.
  • Hestia — goddess of the hearth (in some lists replaced by Dionysus, god of wine and ecstasy).

Variation in the list

Ancient sources do not agree on a single list. Herodotus names twelve including Heracles; other lists include Hades, who was usually excluded because his realm was the underworld rather than Olympus. The Romans adopted the group as the Dii Consentes, with Roman counterparts Juno, Neptune, Minerva, Mars, Venus, Vulcan, Mercury, Ceres, Vesta/Diana and others paired with Greek originals.

Cult and art

The twelve were honoured collectively at Olympia and in Athens, where an altar of the Twelve Gods stood in the Agora. They appear throughout Greek and Roman art, from the Parthenon frieze to Renaissance painting, and remain the best-known figures of Greek mythology.
