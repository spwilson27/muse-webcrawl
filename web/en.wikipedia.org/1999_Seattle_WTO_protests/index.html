The 1999 Seattle WTO protests, sometimes referred to as the Battle in Seattle, were a series of protests surrounding the World Trade Organization (WTO) Ministerial Conference of 1999, held at the Washington State Convention and Trade Center in Seattle, Washington, beginning on November 30, 1999. The conference was to be the launch of a new millennial round of trade negotiations.

The protests were a landmark event of the anti-globalization movement. An estimated 40,000 to 60,000 demonstrators — including labor union members, environmentalists, students, farmers, religious groups, and anarchists — took to the streets, and nonviolent blockades successfully delayed the conference's opening ceremonies. The scale of the protests surprised even their organizers and overwhelmed the Seattle Police Department; the city declared a state of emergency, imposed a curfew, and established a "no-protest zone" in downtown Seattle, with the National Guard called in to support law enforcement.

Background

Opposition to the WTO had grown through the 1990s among critics who argued that the organization's trade rules undermined labor rights, environmental protections, food safety standards, and the sovereignty of developing nations. Labor unions, including the AFL-CIO, feared job losses to countries with lower wages and weaker protections. Environmental groups objected to rulings that struck down measures such as U.S. protections for sea turtles. Planning for the Seattle demonstrations began months in advance; the Direct Action Network coordinated affinity groups trained in nonviolent civil disobedience, while the AFL-CIO organized a large permitted march.

Protests and tactics

On the morning of Tuesday, November 30, thousands of demonstrators converged on the convention center, forming human chains and locking themselves together at key intersections to prevent delegates from reaching the venue. The opening ceremony was cancelled, and U.S. Secretary of State Madeleine Albright was unable to leave her hotel for a time. A permitted labor march of tens of thousands proceeded through downtown in the afternoon.

A small number of participants — including a black bloc of anarchists — smashed windows and vandalized storefronts of multinational corporations such as Nike, Starbucks, and McDonald's. Police responded with tear gas, pepper spray, rubber bullets, and concussion grenades. Protests continued for the remainder of the week, even as trade talks inside stalled and ultimately collapsed on December 3 without agreement on a new round.

Police response

The Seattle Police Department, under Chief Norm Stamper, was widely criticized both for being unprepared on the first day and for heavy-handed tactics thereafter. Mayor Paul Schell declared a civil emergency; Governor Gary Locke deployed the Washington National Guard and state troopers. More than 600 people were arrested, many of them in the "no-protest zone" on December 1. Subsequent lawsuits resulted in settlements, and a later review faulted the city's planning and the constitutionality of the no-protest zone. Stamper resigned after the conference, later describing the episode as a cautionary example of militarized policing.

Media coverage and Indymedia

The protests were a breakthrough moment for independent media: the first Independent Media Center (Indymedia) was launched in Seattle to cover the demonstrations, publishing open-publishing reports from the street and helping spark a global network of Indymedia collectives.

Aftermath and legacy

The failure of the Seattle conference, combined with disagreements among member states, delayed the launch of a new WTO round until Doha in 2001. The protests energized a worldwide anti-globalization movement that mobilized at subsequent summits in Washington, D.C., Prague, Quebec City, and Genoa. The events were later dramatized in the 2007 film Battle in Seattle. Historians commonly regard Seattle 1999 as a turning point in debates over trade and globalization, and the estimated cost to the city in lost business, overtime, and damage ran into the tens of millions of dollars.
