Elizabeth I, the "Virgin Queen", reigned for 44 years

Elizabeth I (7 September 1533 – 24 March 1603) was Queen of England and Ireland from 17 November 1558 until her death in 1603. She was the last and longest-reigning monarch of the House of Tudor. Born the daughter of Henry VIII and his second wife Anne Boleyn, Elizabeth was declared illegitimate after her mother's execution in 1536, but was later restored to the succession.

Accession and religious settlement

After the deaths of her half-siblings Edward VI and Mary I, Elizabeth succeeded to the throne at twenty-five. Her first parliament established the Elizabethan Religious Settlement (1559): the Act of Supremacy made her Supreme Governor of the Church of England, and the Act of Uniformity imposed a revised Book of Common Prayer — a compromise intended to settle decades of religious upheaval. Her principal adviser for forty years was William Cecil, Lord Burghley.

Reign

Elizabeth never married, despite prolonged courtships and diplomatic marriage negotiations, and became known as the "Virgin Queen". Her reign faced Catholic plots centred on Mary, Queen of Scots, whom Elizabeth eventually executed in 1587 after nearly nineteen years of imprisonment. War with Spain followed; in 1588 the Spanish Armada was defeated by English naval skill, fire ships and storms. Elizabeth's speech to the troops at Tilbury — "I know I have the body of a weak, feeble woman; but I have the heart and stomach of a king" — became famous.

The later years of the reign saw the Nine Years' War in Ireland, the failed Essex rising of 1601, and economic strain, but also the flourishing of English drama and poetry associated with Shakespeare, Marlowe, Spenser and Sidney, and voyages of exploration and privateering by Drake, Raleigh and others.

Death and succession

Elizabeth died on 24 March 1603 and was succeeded peacefully by James VI of Scotland, uniting the crowns of England and Scotland. Her 44-year reign, the Elizabethan era, is remembered as a golden age of English culture and national assertion.
