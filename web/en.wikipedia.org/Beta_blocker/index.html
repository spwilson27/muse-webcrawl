Beta blocker

ClassBeta-adrenergic receptor antagonists
ExamplesPropranolol, metoprolol, atenolol, bisoprolol, carvedilol
UsesHypertension, heart failure, arrhythmia, angina, migraine prophylaxis

Beta blockers (β-blockers, beta-adrenergic blocking agents) are a class of medications that block the effects of the catecholamines adrenaline and noradrenaline at beta-adrenergic receptors. They are widely used in the management of cardiovascular disease and several other conditions.

Mechanism of action

Beta blockers competitively antagonize β1 and β2 adrenergic receptors to varying degrees. Blockade of β1 receptors in the heart reduces heart rate, contractility, and conduction velocity, lowering myocardial oxygen demand and blood pressure. Some agents, such as propranolol, are non-selective; others, such as metoprolol and atenolol, are relatively β1-selective ("cardioselective"). Carvedilol and labetalol also block alpha receptors.

Medical uses

Beta blockers are indicated for hypertension, chronic heart failure with reduced ejection fraction, rate control in atrial fibrillation and other arrhythmias, secondary prevention after myocardial infarction, angina, and prophylaxis of migraine. Propranolol is also used for essential tremor, thyrotoxicosis symptoms, and performance anxiety. Topical beta blockers lower intraocular pressure in glaucoma.

History

The first clinically successful beta blocker, propranolol, was developed by Sir James Black in the late 1950s and early 1960s at ICI; it revolutionized the treatment of angina, and Black shared the 1988 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. Beta blockers replaced many older antihypertensive strategies and remain among the most prescribed drug classes worldwide.

Adverse effects

Common side effects include fatigue, bradycardia, cold extremities, and bronchospasm in susceptible patients; beta blockers can mask the warning symptoms of hypoglycemia in diabetes. Abrupt withdrawal may precipitate rebound tachycardia and ischemia.
