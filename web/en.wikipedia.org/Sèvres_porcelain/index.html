Sèvres porcelain

Sèvres porcelain is porcelain produced by the French national manufactory at Sèvres, near Paris — the Manufacture nationale de Sèvres — one of Europe's most prestigious porcelain factories, renowned for its brilliant ground colours, fine gilding, and miniature-style painted decoration.

History

The factory began at Vincennes in 1740 under the protection of Louis XV and his mistress Madame de Pompadour, moving to purpose-built premises at Sèvres in 1756; the king became sole owner in 1759. Initially it made soft-paste porcelain (pâte tendre), developing famous ground colours such as the dark bleu de roi (1752), the turquoise bleu céleste (1753), and rose Pompadour (1757). After kaolin deposits were found near Limoges, hard-paste porcelain was introduced from about 1770. Prestigious services were made for royal and diplomatic gifts — including a famous service for Catherine the Great — and great artists such as the painter François Boucher supplied designs.

Later history and legacy

The Revolution stripped the factory of royal patronage, but under Alexandre Brongniart (director 1800–1847) Sèvres was reorganised, made technically innovative, and flourished again, supplying Napoleon's court. The factory still operates under the French state, producing both historic reproductions and contemporary works commissioned from modern artists; antique Sèvres is among the most collected of all porcelain, with major holdings in the Louvre, the Wallace Collection, and the Metropolitan Museum of Art.
