Fly-by-wire (FBW) is a system that replaces the conventional manual flight controls of an aircraft with an electronic interface. The movements of flight controls are converted to electronic signals transmitted by wires, and flight control computers determine how to move the actuators at each control surface to provide the aircraft's ordered response. The system may use either a mechanical backup or a fully electronic, redundant architecture.

Fly-by-wire systems allow flight control computers to stabilize an aircraft automatically, enabling designs with relaxed static stability that would be unflyable manually, and can protect the flight envelope by preventing the pilot from exceeding the aircraft's structural and aerodynamic limits.

History

Electrical signalling of control surfaces was explored as early as the 1930s, and servo-controlled systems were used on large aircraft during the Second World War. Analog fly-by-wire was developed for the Avro Canada CF-105 Arrow interceptor in the 1950s. The first aircraft without a mechanical backup to fly was a NASA F-8 Crusader modified under the Digital Fly-By-Wire program in 1972, which used the Apollo guidance computer. The General Dynamics F-16, entering service in 1978, was the first production aircraft designed with fly-by-wire and relaxed static stability. In civil aviation, Concorde used an analogue fly-by-wire system, while the Airbus A320 (1988) was the first airliner with full digital fly-by-wire, introducing side-sticks and hard flight-envelope protection. Boeing adopted fly-by-wire with a conventional control yoke and "soft" protections on the 777.

Operation

The pilot's control inputs are measured by transducers and sent to flight control computers, which compare them with data from air-data and inertial sensors and compute actuator commands according to control laws. Under "normal law" the computers provide full envelope protection — limiting pitch, bank angle, load factor and angle of attack. If sensors or computers fail, the system degrades through alternate and direct laws, progressively returning control authority to the pilot with fewer protections. Multiple redundant computers and power sources, using dissimilar hardware and software, guard against common-mode failures.

Advantages and criticisms

Benefits include reduced weight compared with long runs of cables, pulleys and hydraulic lines, improved handling consistency, automatic turbulence alleviation, and the ability to optimize aerodynamics with unstable designs. Criticisms have centred on pilot awareness of envelope protections, differences between manufacturers' philosophies (Airbus hard limits versus Boeing soft limits that the pilot can override), and the consequences of erroneous sensor data, most tragically in accidents where faulty airspeed readings degraded the control laws.

Variants

Related systems include fly-by-optics, which uses fibre-optic links immune to electromagnetic interference, and power-by-wire actuators that replace central hydraulics with local electrically driven units. Throttle control by similar means is known as FADEC (full authority digital engine control).
