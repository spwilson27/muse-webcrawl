Tree (graph theory)

In graph theory, a tree is an undirected graph in which any two vertices are connected by exactly one path, or equivalently a connected acyclic undirected graph. A disjoint union of trees is called a forest. Trees are among the most studied structures in combinatorics and computer science, where they model hierarchical data.

Equivalent characterisations

For a graph T with n vertices, the following statements are equivalent:

  • T is connected and acyclic.
  • T is connected and has exactly n − 1 edges.
  • T is acyclic and has exactly n − 1 edges.
  • Any two vertices of T are joined by a unique simple path.
  • T is connected, and removing any single edge disconnects it.
  • T is acyclic, and adding any new edge creates exactly one cycle.

A vertex of degree 1 is a leaf; every tree with at least two vertices has at least two leaves. A rooted tree designates one vertex as the root, giving the familiar parent–child structure used in data structures such as binary trees and heaps.

Facts and applications

Cayley's formula states that the number of labelled trees on n vertices is nn−2. Spanning trees of a graph — trees that include every vertex — are central to network design; Kirchhoff's matrix tree theorem counts them. Trees appear in decision theory (decision trees), phylogenetics (evolutionary trees), parsing in compilers, and file-system hierarchies.
