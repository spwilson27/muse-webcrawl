Ethnography

Ethnography is a branch of anthropology and the systematic study of individual cultures. Ethnography explores cultural phenomena from the point of view of the subject of the study. It is also a type of social research that involves examining the behavior of the participants in a given social situation and understanding the group members' own interpretation of such behavior.

As a form of inquiry, ethnography relies heavily on participant observation: the researcher participates in the setting or with the people being studied, at least in some marginal role, and seeks to document, in detail, patterns of social interaction and the perspectives of participants, explaining these patterns and perspectives in their own terms.

History

The term was first used in the early nineteenth century. Ethnography developed alongside anthropology in the colonial era, but the modern discipline is usually traced to the fieldwork of Bronisław Malinowski, whose study of the Trobriand Islands in Argonauts of the Western Pacific (1922) established long-term immersive fieldwork as the standard method. Franz Boas and his students in the United States also played a central role in shaping ethnographic practice.

Methods

Ethnographers typically gather data through fieldnotes, interviews, audio and video recordings, and the collection of artifacts. The dominant approach today emphasizes reflexivity: acknowledging how the researcher's own position affects the study. Ethnographic writing often takes the form of a monograph that gives a "thick description" of a culture, a concept developed by Clifford Geertz.

Forms

Varieties of ethnography include autoethnography, digital or virtual ethnography, institutional ethnography, and multi-sited ethnography. Ethnographic methods are also used in sociology, education, public health, and market research.
