Crossing the Red Sea

The Crossing of the Red Sea is an episode in the biblical narrative of the Exodus, recounted in chapter 14 of the Book of Exodus. Pursued by Pharaoh's army after leaving Egypt, the Israelites are led by Moses to the shore of a body of water called in Hebrew Yam Suph — literally "Sea of Reeds," traditionally rendered "Red Sea." God parts the waters so that the Israelites cross on dry ground; when the Egyptians follow, the waters return and destroy them.

The narrative

According to Exodus, a strong east wind drove the sea back all night, exposing the sea bed, with the water standing as a wall on either side. At dawn Moses stretched out his hand again and the sea returned to its place, drowning Pharaoh's chariots and horsemen. The event is followed by the Song of the Sea in Exodus 15, attributed to Moses and Miriam, which celebrates the deliverance and is among the oldest poems in the Bible.

Interpretation

The crossing is central to Jewish memory of the Exodus and is retold at Passover; it is also frequently referenced in the New Testament and in Christian baptismal imagery. Scholars debate the location of Yam Suph — candidates include the Gulf of Suez, the Gulf of Aqaba, and reed lakes in the eastern Nile Delta — and the historicity of the event, for which there is no direct archaeological corroboration. The episode has been depicted extensively in art, from medieval manuscripts to Nicolas Poussin's painting The Crossing of the Red Sea.
