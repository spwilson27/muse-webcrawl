Telegrapher's equations

The telegrapher's equations (or simply telegraph equations) are a pair of coupled, linear partial differential equations that describe the voltage and current on an electrical transmission line with distance and time. The equations come from Oliver Heaviside, who developed the transmission line model in the 1880s, and were originally applied to telegraph wires, from which the name derives.

The theory can be applied to any transmission line, including radio-frequency conductors such as coaxial cable and twisted pair, audio-frequency lines, and direct-current power lines.

The equations

The model represents the transmission line as an infinite series of two-port elementary components, each consisting of a series resistance R per unit length, a series inductance L per unit length, a shunt conductance G per unit length, and a shunt capacitance C per unit length. Applying Kirchhoff's laws to an infinitesimal section of line of length dx gives:

∂V/∂x = −(R + jωL) I
∂I/∂x = −(G + jωC) V

where V(x) is the voltage across the line at position x, I(x) is the current flowing in the line, and ω is the angular frequency. In the time domain the equations take the form:

∂V/∂x = −L ∂I/∂t − R I
∂I/∂x = −C ∂V/∂t − G V

Lossless case

When the line is lossless, the resistance and conductance per unit length are taken as zero (R = 0 and G = 0). The equations then reduce to a pair of wave equations describing waves travelling at speed v = 1/√(LC). Combining the two equations yields the second-order wave equation for voltage and current separately.

Heaviside condition

Heaviside showed that a line would transmit signals without distortion if the ratio R/L equalled G/C. This result, known as the Heaviside condition, motivated the use of loading coils to artificially increase the inductance of long telegraph and telephone cables so that the condition was approximately satisfied.

Solutions

The general solution consists of waves travelling in both directions along the line. The propagation constant γ = √((R + jωL)(G + jωC)) = α + jβ combines the attenuation constant α and the phase constant β, and the line's characteristic impedance is Z0 = √((R + jωL)/(G + jωC)). Reflections occur where the load impedance differs from the characteristic impedance.

History

The equations arose from the practical problem of signal distortion on long submarine telegraph cables. William Thomson (later Lord Kelvin) had produced a model describing only resistance and capacitance, predicting severe retardation. Heaviside's more complete treatment, adding inductance and leakage, explained pulse propagation far better and guided the design of practical long-distance lines.
