Paparazzi

Paparazzi (singular paparazzo) are freelance photographers who pursue celebrities, politicians and other public figures to take candid, often intrusive photographs, which they sell to tabloid newspapers, gossip magazines and entertainment websites. Their aggressive methods have been widely criticised and have prompted legal restrictions in several countries.

Origin of the term

The word comes from Paparazzo, the surname of the celebrity photographer character in Federico Fellini's 1960 film La Dolce Vita. Fellini is said to have taken the name from an Italian dialect word for a buzzing, bothersome insect — a comparison paparazzi subjects often endorse.

Practice and controversy

Paparazzi monitor celebrities' homes, restaurants, airports and holidays, sometimes engaging in high-speed chases. The 1997 death of Diana, Princess of Wales, in a Paris car crash while pursued by photographers intensified public and legal scrutiny worldwide. Jurisdictions have responded with anti-harassment and anti-stalking statutes — for example California's anti-paparazzi laws restricting photography of celebrities' children — while photographers and publishers invoke press-freedom protections. Courts have balanced these interests differently in different countries.

Economics

Exclusive images of major celebrities can command very large fees, though the rise of social media and celebrities publishing their own candid photos has reduced the market value of many paparazzi shots.
