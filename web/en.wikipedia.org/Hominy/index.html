Hominy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hominy is a food produced from dried maize (corn) kernels that have been treated with an alkali, in a process called nixtamalization (from the Nahuatl nextli, "ashes," and tamalli, "maize dough"). The treatment — soaking and cooking the kernels in a dilute solution of lye or slaked lime — softens and removes the hulls (pericarps) and allows the kernels to swell to roughly twice their dry size. Lye hominy is traditional in the cuisine of the Southern United States, while lime-treated maize is the basis of masa for tortillas and tamales in Mexican and Central American cooking.

History and nutrition

Nixtamalization was developed by Mesoamerican peoples thousands of years ago and spread with maize cultivation. Beyond easing processing, the alkali treatment dramatically improves the nutritional value of maize: it makes niacin (vitamin B3) bioavailable, adds dietary calcium from the lime, and improves the balance and digestibility of amino acids. Maize-dependent populations that adopted the grain without the technique — including poor communities in the American South and parts of Africa and Italy — suffered epidemics of pellagra, the niacin-deficiency disease, a historical lesson in the importance of traditional food processing.

Uses

Whole hominy kernels are served as a side dish, fried in butter or bacon fat, and are an essential ingredient of the Mexican stew pozole. Ground wet hominy produces masa, used for corn tortillas, tamales, and pupusas; ground dried hominy yields grits, the coarse porridge of the American South, and masa harina, the dried flour. Canned hominy, usually made with lye and sold in white and yellow varieties, is widely available in the United States and Mexico. Rockihominy, cracked dried hominy, was a trail and pantry staple in earlier centuries.
