Clipper

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A clipper was a type of mid-19th-century merchant sailing vessel, designed for speed. Clippers were generally narrow for their length, could carry limited bulk freight, had a large total sail area, and a relatively small crew. They were developed in the United States and Britain, and were employed in high-value trades such as the transport of tea from China and passengers and gold-rush freight to California and Australia.

Origins and design

The clipper emerged from earlier fast-sailing designs such as Baltimore clippers. American builders — notably Donald McKay of Boston, whose ships Flying Cloud and Sovereign of the Seas set records — produced the archetypal "extreme clipper" of the early 1850s, with a sharp, hollow bow and long, sleek hull. The California Gold Rush created intense demand for the fastest possible passages around Cape Horn, and clippers routinely made the New York–San Francisco run in under 100 days, roughly half the time of ordinary ships.

Tea clippers and decline

British tea clippers, generally smaller and lighter than the American ships, raced each other annually from China to London with the first tea of the season; the Great Tea Race of 1866, narrowly won by Taeping over Ariel, became famous. The opening of the Suez Canal in 1869, which sailing ships could not easily use, together with improving steamship efficiency, ended the clipper era in most trades, although clippers lingered in the Australian wool trade into the 1880s and 1890s.

Surviving ships

The best-known surviving clipper is the Cutty Sark (1869), preserved at Greenwich, London. Other survivors include the City of Adelaide (1864), transported to Australia in 2014, and the iron clipper James Craig in Sydney. Clippers remain celebrated as among the most beautiful and fastest sailing vessels ever built; the record day's run of 436 nautical miles by Champion of the Seas in 1854 stood for the age of sail.
