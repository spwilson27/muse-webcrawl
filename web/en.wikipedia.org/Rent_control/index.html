Rent control

Rent regulation is most common in large cities with housing shortages.

Rent control is a system of price controls limiting the amount a property owner can charge for renting out a home or other real estate, or otherwise regulating the terms of residential tenancies. Policies range from hard rent freezes and ceilings ("first-generation" controls) to limits on the size of annual increases for sitting tenants ("second-generation" rent stabilisation). Rent control is usually justified as a means of keeping housing affordable and preventing displacement, and often accompanies rules restricting eviction.

History

Modern rent controls were widely introduced during and after the two world wars, when emergency shortages made housing scarce. Many cities retained or reintroduced them in the post-war decades; prominent examples include New York City, where rent regulation still covers roughly a million apartments, and cities in Germany, Sweden, and the Netherlands, each with distinct regulatory models.

Economics

Economists have long debated rent control's effects. Many argue that strict controls reduce the quantity and quality of rental housing over time, discourage new construction and maintenance, and create misallocation as tenants remain in units that no longer suit them. Supporters respond that moderate stabilisation policies protect tenants from sudden displacement with modest efficiency costs, and that empirical studies of newer programmes show mixed results. The debate has intensified with the housing affordability crises of the 2010s and 2020s, which led several jurisdictions to adopt or expand rent stabilisation.
