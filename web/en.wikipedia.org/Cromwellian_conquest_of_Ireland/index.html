Cromwellian conquest of Ireland

The Cromwellian conquest of Ireland (1649–1653) was the reconquest of Ireland by the forces of the English Parliament, led by Oliver Cromwell, during the Wars of the Three Kingdoms. Cromwell landed in Ireland with the New Model Army on behalf of the Rump Parliament; by 1652 most organised Irish Catholic resistance had been crushed, though guerrilla warfare dragged on into 1653.

Background

Ireland had been in upheaval since the Irish Rebellion of 1641 and the ensuing Confederate Wars. The alliance of Irish Confederates and English Royalists, concluded in 1649, threatened to make Ireland a base for a Royalist invasion of England. Parliament resolved to intervene, and Cromwell arrived at Dublin in August 1649 with a large army and siege train.

The campaign

The campaign is remembered above all for the sieges of Drogheda (September 1649) and Wexford (October 1649), at which Cromwell's troops massacred much of the garrison and many civilians after storming the towns. Cromwell defended the killings as retribution for 1641 and as a means of discouraging further resistance. The sieges of Waterford, Clonmel and Limerick followed; Cromwell's army suffered heavily from disease, and at Clonmel the New Model Army met its bloodiest repulse of the war. Cromwell left Ireland in May 1650; his successors Henry Ireton and Charles Fleetwood completed the conquest, taking Galway in 1652.

Consequences

The conquest was followed by a massive transfer of land: under the Act for the Settlement of Ireland 1652, most land owned by Irish Catholics was confiscated and granted to English soldiers and investors, and many Catholic landowners were transplanted to Connacht — giving rise to the later phrase "to hell or Connacht". The war, together with plague and famine, caused a catastrophic fall in Ireland's population. Cromwell's name remains deeply controversial in Ireland, where the campaign is remembered as a byword for brutality, while historians debate the extent to which his conduct exceeded the harsh conventions of seventeenth-century siege warfare.
