Dodona

LocationEpirus, Greece (near Ioannina)
TypeSanctuary and oracle
DeitiesZeus, Dione
Founded2nd millennium BC (cult); major buildings 4th century BC

Dodona (Greek: Δωδώνη, Dōdṓnē) in Epirus, in northwestern Greece, was the site of the oldest Hellenic oracle, second in prestige only to Delphi. Dedicated to Zeus and the mother goddess Dione, the sanctuary centred on a sacred oak tree, whose rustling leaves and associated doves and bronze cauldrons priestesses and priests interpreted as the god's responses. Worship is attested from the Late Bronze Age.

The oracle

Consultants inscribed questions on small lead tablets — thousands survive, preserved in museum collections — asking about marriage, travel, children, health and property. The oracle is mentioned in Homer's Iliad and Odyssey, and Herodotus recounts origin stories linking Dodona with a black dove from Egyptian Thebes. The priests, called Selloi, were said to sleep on the ground and never wash their feet.

History and buildings

Under the Molossian kings, especially Pyrrhus in the early 3rd century BC, the sanctuary was monumentalised: a temple of Zeus, a bouleuterion, a stadium and a theatre seating some 18,000 — among the largest in Greece — hosted the Naia festival. The site was destroyed by the Aetolians in 219 BC and rebuilt; Roman forces under Aemilius Paullus devastated Epirus in 167 BC, and the sanctuary declined, though consultations continued into late antiquity until the sacred oak was felled in the late 4th century AD.

Excavation

Systematic excavations began under Konstantinos Karapanos in 1875–76 and have continued under the Greek Archaeological Service; the theatre was partly restored and hosts performances.
