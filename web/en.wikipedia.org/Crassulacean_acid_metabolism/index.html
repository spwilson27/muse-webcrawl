Crassulacean acid metabolism

Crassulacean acid metabolism (CAM)
TypePhotosynthetic pathway; carbon fixation adaptation
Named forFamily Crassulaceae (stonecrops)
Key traitStomata open at night; CO₂ stored as malic acid in vacuoles

Crassulacean acid metabolism, also known as CAM photosynthesis, is a carbon fixation pathway that evolved in some plants as an adaptation to arid conditions. CAM plants open their stomata at night, when evaporation is low, fix carbon dioxide into organic acids — chiefly malic acid — stored in their vacuoles, and then close their stomata by day, releasing the CO₂ internally for photosynthesis behind the closed pores. The pathway is named after the stonecrop family, Crassulaceae, in which it was first studied.

CAM separates CO₂ uptake (night) from the Calvin cycle (day) in time, conserving water.

Mechanism

At night, the enzyme PEP carboxylase fixes CO₂ (as bicarbonate) onto phosphoenolpyruvate to form oxaloacetate, which is reduced to malate and pumped into the vacuole; the leaf tissues become measurably more acidic by dawn. During the day, malate leaves the vacuole and is decarboxylated, and the released CO₂ is refixed by RuBisCO in the Calvin cycle while the stomata remain shut. CAM thus separates carbon uptake from carbon reduction in time, whereas C4 photosynthesis separates them in space between cell types.

Distribution and significance

CAM has evolved independently many times and occurs in over 16,000 species across about 40 families — roughly 6–7% of plant species — including most cacti, agaves, pineapple, many orchids and bromeliads, and succulents such as jade plants. Some species are facultative, switching between C3 and CAM depending on water or salt stress. CAM gives extraordinary water-use efficiency but slow growth, so CAM crops matter agriculturally mainly where water is limiting — pineapple, prickly pear, sisal and agave for fibre and biofuel are prominent examples — and CAM is a target for engineering drought resilience into other crops.
