Black-Scholes model

[Image placeholder: option pricing curve]

The Black-Scholes model (also known as the Black-Scholes-Merton model) is a mathematical model for the dynamics of a financial market containing derivative investment instruments. From the partial differential equation in the model, known as the Black-Scholes equation, one can deduce the Black-Scholes formula, which gives a theoretical estimate of the price of European-style options and shows that the option has a unique price given the risk of the security and its expected return.

The model was introduced in 1973 by economists Fischer Black and Myron Scholes in their paper "The Pricing of Options and Corporate Liabilities"; Robert C. Merton published a companion paper expanding the mathematical understanding and coined the name. Scholes and Merton received the 1997 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences for their work (Black had died in 1995). The model revolutionized finance and remains a standard tool for quoting and hedging options, typically through the implied volatility it defines.

The formula

For a European call option on a non-dividend-paying stock, the price is:

C = S·N(d1) − K·e−rT·N(d2)

where S is the current price of the underlying asset, K is the strike price, r is the risk-free interest rate, T is the time to expiration, and N(·) is the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal distribution. The quantities d1 and d2 depend on S, K, r, T, and the asset's volatility σ — the only input not directly observable, which market participants back out of traded prices as implied volatility.

Assumptions

The classical model assumes that the underlying price follows geometric Brownian motion with constant drift and volatility, that markets are frictionless (no transaction costs or taxes), that trading is continuous, that the risk-free rate is constant and known, and that there are no arbitrage opportunities. In practice, observed option prices deviate from the model, most famously in the volatility smile or skew, in which implied volatility varies with strike price — an effect that became pronounced after the stock market crash of 1987.

Applications and criticism

Extensions of the model handle dividends, American-style early exercise (approximately), and options on currencies and futures. The model and its hedging argument influenced the growth of derivatives markets and the development of risk-neutral valuation throughout mathematical finance, though critics have argued that reliance on its assumptions contributed to failures such as the collapse of Long-Term Capital Management in 1998.
