Grana Padano

A wheel of Grana Padano, showing its grainy texture

Grana Padano is an Italian hard, slow-ripened, semi-fat cheese from the Po River Valley, produced in the regions of Piedmont, Lombardy, Emilia-Romagna, Veneto, and Trentino-Alto Adige. It is one of the world's best-known cheeses and has held Protected Designation of Origin (PDO, in Italian Denominazione di Origine Protetta) status since 1996.

History

Grana Padano was created by Cistercian monks of Chiaravalle Abbey in the 12th century as a way to preserve surplus milk. The name combines grana, referring to its distinctively grainy texture, and Padano, denoting the Pianura Padana (Po Valley). Its production method became the model for grana-style cheeses across northern Italy.

Production

The cheese is made from raw cow's milk — partially skimmed by natural creaming — from cows fed mainly on forage from the production area. The curd is cooked, molded into large wheels of 24 to 40 kilograms, brined, and aged for a minimum of nine months. Wheels are graded and fire-branded with the Grana Padano mark only after inspection. Longer-aged versions (over 20 months) are sold as Riserva.

Characteristics

Grana Padano has a pale straw color, a dense grainy paste that breaks into flakes, and a sweet, savory flavor that intensifies with age. It is used grated over pasta and risotto, shaved into salads, and eaten in chunks. Compared with Parmigiano-Reggiano, it is generally milder, less expensive, and produced over a wider area with somewhat less restrictive rules on milk and aging.
