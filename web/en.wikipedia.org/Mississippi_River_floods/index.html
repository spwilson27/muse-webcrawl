Mississippi River floods

The Mississippi River and its tributaries have repeatedly flooded the surrounding floodplains of the central United States, producing some of the largest and most destructive river floods in American history. Because the river drains about 40 percent of the continental United States, prolonged basin-wide rainfall can send enormous volumes of water down its course.

Major floods

Great Mississippi Flood of 1927

The flood of 1927 was the most destructive river flood in U.S. history up to that time. Levee breaks inundated some 27,000 square miles across seven states, displaced roughly 700,000 people, and caused hundreds of deaths. The disaster led to the Flood Control Act of 1928, which committed the federal government, through the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, to a comprehensive system of levees, floodways, and reservoirs known as the Mississippi River and Tributaries Project.

Great Flood of 1993

From April to October 1993, persistent rain across the Upper Midwest caused record flooding on the upper Mississippi and Missouri rivers. At St. Louis the river crested at a record 49.47 feet. More than 1,000 levees failed or were overtopped, damage was estimated at $12–16 billion, and dozens of people died. The event prompted new attention to floodplain buyouts and wetland restoration.

Other notable floods

  • 1882, 1913, and 1937 — earlier basin-wide floods that devastated river communities, the 1937 flood centering on the Ohio River confluence region.
  • 1973 — a long-duration flood that forced the first-ever opening of the Morganza Spillway.
  • 2011 — a near-record flood wave; the Corps opened the Morganza Spillway for only the second time and blew a levee at Birds Point to save Cairo, Illinois.
  • 2019 — the longest recorded flood fight on the lower river, with stations at flood stage for months and the Bonnet Carré Spillway opened twice in one year for the first time.

Flood control

Flood management combines levees, floodways and spillways (such as the Bonnet Carré and Morganza), tributary reservoirs, and the Old River Control Structure, which prevents the Mississippi from shifting its course into the Atchafalaya River.
