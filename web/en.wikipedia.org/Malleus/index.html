Malleus

Part ofMiddle ear; auditory ossicles
ArticulationsIncus
IdentifiersLatin: malleus

The malleus, or hammer, is a small hammer-shaped bone in the middle ear of mammals. Together with the incus (anvil) and the stapes (stirrup), it forms the chain of three auditory ossicles that transmit vibrations from the tympanic membrane (eardrum) to the inner ear, where they are converted into nerve impulses interpreted as sound.

Anatomy

The malleus is the largest of the three ossicles, measuring about 8 mm in length in humans. It consists of a head, a neck, and a handle (manubrium), along with lateral and anterior processes. The rounded head sits in the epitympanic recess of the middle ear cavity and articulates with the body of the incus at the incudomalleolar joint. The manubrium is embedded in, and firmly attached to, the medial surface of the eardrum, ending at the umbo; when the eardrum vibrates, the malleus moves with it.

Two small muscles influence the ossicular chain: the tensor tympani attaches to the manubrium of the malleus and tenses the eardrum, while the stapedius acts on the stapes. The chorda tympani, a branch of the facial nerve, crosses the neck of the malleus on its way through the middle ear.

Function

Sound waves collected by the outer ear set the eardrum vibrating. The malleus, attached to the eardrum, passes these vibrations to the incus and then to the stapes, whose footplate sits in the oval window of the cochlea. The ossicular chain acts as an impedance-matching lever system: because the eardrum's area is much larger than that of the oval window, and because of the lever action of the malleus and incus, pressure is amplified roughly twentyfold, allowing airborne sound to drive the fluid of the inner ear efficiently.

Development and evolution

The malleus develops from the first pharyngeal arch, from Meckel's cartilage. Evolutionarily, it is homologous to the articular bone of the lower jaw in reptiles and other non-mammalian vertebrates; during mammalian evolution, bones of the ancestral jaw joint were repurposed into the middle ear, an adaptation documented both in the fossil record and in embryonic development.

Clinical significance

Diseases of the middle ear, such as otitis media, cholesteatoma, and otosclerosis, can erode, fix, or dislocate the malleus and other ossicles, producing conductive hearing loss. Surgical repair (ossiculoplasty) may reposition the bone or replace it with a prosthesis.
