Tetraethyl lead (commonly styled tetraethyllead), abbreviated TEL, is an organolead compound with the formula Pb(C2H5)4. Beginning in the 1920s it was widely used as an antiknock additive in petrol ("leaded gasoline"), raising the fuel's octane rating and allowing higher engine compression. Its phase-out during the late twentieth century, driven by the neurotoxicity of lead and its poisoning of catalytic converters, is regarded as one of the great public-health achievements of the era; the final country, Algeria, ended sales of leaded road fuel in 2021.

History

TEL was discovered as an antiknock agent by Thomas Midgley Jr. at General Motors in 1921 and marketed from 1923 by the Ethyl Corporation. Its dangers were apparent early: production workers died of lead poisoning in 1924, prompting a temporary US suspension and a Surgeon General's conference in 1925, after which production resumed. Geochemist Clair Cameron Patterson's research in the 1960s demonstrated the extent of industrial lead contamination of the environment, and epidemiologists such as Herbert Needleman documented harm to children's cognitive development from low-level exposure.

Toxicity

TEL is a potent neurotoxin, readily absorbed through the skin and by inhalation. In the engine it is converted to lead oxides that were emitted in exhaust; population blood-lead levels fell dramatically in countries that banned leaded petrol, and studies have linked the phase-out to gains in average IQ and declines in violent crime.

Phase-out

The United States progressively restricted TEL from the 1970s and banned it in road vehicles in 1996; the European Union banned leaded petrol in 2000. TEL remains in use in avgas (aviation gasoline) for piston-engined aircraft, for which substitutes are still being developed.
