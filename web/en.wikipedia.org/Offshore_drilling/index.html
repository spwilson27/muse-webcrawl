Offshore drilling is the process of extracting petroleum and natural gas from reservoirs beneath the seabed. It began in the late nineteenth century with wells drilled from piers over the water and has developed into a major global industry operating in water depths exceeding 3,000 metres.

History

Some of the first offshore wells were drilled from wharves at Summerland, California, in the 1890s. In 1947 a well drilled out of sight of land in the Gulf of Mexico by Kerr-McGee is usually regarded as the start of the modern offshore industry. Production expanded into the North Sea in the 1960s–70s, and technological advances — dynamic positioning, subsea completions and floating production systems — progressively opened deeper and harsher waters, including Brazil's pre-salt fields and deepwater West Africa.

Methods and structures

In shallow water, fixed steel or concrete platforms and self-elevating jack-up rigs are used. In deeper water, operators employ semi-submersible platforms, drillships held on station by dynamic positioning, tension-leg platforms, spar platforms, and FPSO vessels (floating production, storage and offloading). Wells are drilled directionally from a platform or by subsea wellheads tied back to a host facility. Offshore production accounts for roughly a third of world oil output.

Environmental concerns and accidents

Offshore operations carry risks of blowouts and oil spills. Major disasters include the Piper Alpha platform fire in the North Sea (1988, 167 deaths) and the Deepwater Horizon blowout in the Gulf of Mexico (2010), which killed 11 workers and caused the largest marine oil spill in U.S. history. These events led to tightened regulation, new safety regimes, and continuing public debate over offshore leasing, Arctic drilling and the industry's role in climate change.
