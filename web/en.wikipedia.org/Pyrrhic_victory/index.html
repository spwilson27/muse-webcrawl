Pyrrhic victory

Pyrrhus after the Battle of Asculum (279 BC)

A Pyrrhic victory is a victory won at such a devastating cost to the victor that it is tantamount to a defeat. Such a victory negates any true sense of achievement or damages long-term progress, and the phrase is often used metaphorically in business, politics, sports, and law for any win that comes at an unsustainable price.

Origin

The term is named after Pyrrhus of Epirus, whose army suffered irreplaceable casualties in defeating the Romans at the Battle of Heraclea in 280 BC and the Battle of Asculum in 279 BC during the Pyrrhic War. After Asculum, Plutarch reported that Pyrrhus said to one of his officers: "If we are victorious in one more battle with the Romans, we shall be utterly ruined" — because so many of his officers and best soldiers had been killed, and unlike the Romans he had no reserves of manpower from which to replace them.

Usage

The expression is used of any success achieved at excessive cost: a company that wins a price war but destroys its profits, a litigant who wins a case whose legal fees exceed the award, or a military force that takes ground it cannot hold. Related concepts include the tactical victory but strategic defeat, and the saying "winning the battle but losing the war."
