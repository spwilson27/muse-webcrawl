Conjunction (astronomy)

In astronomy, a conjunction occurs when two astronomical objects or spacecraft have either the same right ascension or the same ecliptic longitude, usually as observed from Earth. The astronomical symbol for conjunction is ☌.

When two objects always appear close to the ecliptic — such as two planets, the Moon and a planet, or the Sun and a planet — the fact implies an apparent close approach between the objects as seen in the sky. A related word, appulse, is the minimum apparent separation in the sky of two astronomical objects.

Passing close

More generally, in the particular case of two planets, conjunction means that they merely have the same right ascension and so appear close in the sky. A striking example was the "great conjunction" of Jupiter and Saturn in December 2020, when the two planets appeared only about a tenth of a degree apart.

Superior and inferior conjunction

A planet that is interior to Earth's orbit (Mercury or Venus) is in inferior conjunction when it lies between Earth and the Sun, and in superior conjunction when it is on the far side of the Sun. The Moon is in conjunction with the Sun at new moon. When a planet is in conjunction with the Sun, it is generally lost in the Sun's glare and is at its least observable.

Quasi-conjunctions

A quasi-conjunction occurs when a planet in retrograde motion appears to approach another planet but does not quite reach the same longitude before resuming direct motion, producing a series of near passes.
