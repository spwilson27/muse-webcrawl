Tiger

Tiger
Scientific name: Panthera tigris
Family: Felidae
Conservation status: Endangered (IUCN)
Habitat: Asia — forests, mangroves, grasslands

The tiger (Panthera tigris) is the largest living member of the cat family (Felidae) and one of the most recognisable animals in the world, distinguished by its orange coat with black stripes — a pattern unique to each individual. Native to Asia, tigers once ranged from Turkey and the Caspian region across southern and eastern Asia to the Russian Far East and the Sunda Islands; they have lost over 90% of this historical range. Adult males can exceed 3 metres in total length and weigh over 250 kg (the Siberian or Amur tiger being the largest population), yet tigers are apex predators under threat, listed as Endangered on the IUCN Red List.

Biology and behaviour

Tigers are solitary, territorial predators that hunt mainly at night, preying on large ungulates such as deer and wild boar. Unlike most cats they are strong swimmers and often bathe. Their stripes provide camouflage in dappled forest light; white tigers, with a recessive leucistic colour variant, occur occasionally among Bengal tigers. Females raise litters of two to four cubs alone for roughly two years. Tigers communicate through roars (audible over 3 km), scent marking, and visual signals.

Subspecies and populations

Modern classifications usually recognise two subspecies: the mainland P. t. tigris (including Bengal, Siberian/Amur, Indochinese, Malayan, and South China populations) and the Sunda Islands P. t. sondaica (Sumatran, and the extinct Javan and Balinese tigers). The Caspian tiger also became extinct in the 20th century. Wild numbers fell to about 3,200 around 2010 but recovered to roughly 5,500 by the early 2020s, with India holding about three-quarters of the global population — the first increase in a century, credited to protected areas and anti-poaching work under efforts such as the 2010 Global Tiger Initiative's "TX2" goal.

Threats and culture

The main threats are habitat loss and fragmentation, poaching for skins and body parts used in traditional medicine, depletion of prey, and conflict with humans and livestock. Tigers are culturally prominent across Asia — national animal of India, Bangladesh, Malaysia, and South Korea; a Chinese zodiac sign; the Hindu goddess Durga's mount — and in world literature from William Blake's "The Tyger" to Yann Martel's Life of Pi. Captive tigers, especially in the United States, may outnumber wild ones.
