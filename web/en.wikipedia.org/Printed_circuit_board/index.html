A printed circuit board (PCB), or printed wiring board (PWB), is a laminated sandwich structure of conductive and insulating layers used to mechanically support and electrically connect electronic components. Conductive pathways, called traces, are etched from copper sheets laminated onto a non-conductive substrate such as FR-4 fibreglass-reinforced epoxy. PCBs are used in virtually all electronic products, from simple toys to supercomputers.

History

Early methods of interconnecting components used point-to-point wiring. The printed circuit process was developed in the 1930s–40s, notably by the Austrian engineer Paul Eisler, who produced a working printed circuit radio while in London during the Second World War. The United States military adopted and standardised the technology after the war, and the release of the process to industry in 1948, together with the invention of automatic assembly and later surface-mount technology, made PCBs the dominant construction method.

Design and manufacture

Boards are designed with electronic design automation software, in which components are placed and traces routed — increasingly by autorouters — and exported as Gerber files for fabrication. Multilayer boards stack several etched cores bonded with prepreg, connected by plated holes called vias. After fabrication, boards receive a protective solder mask and a silkscreen legend, and components are attached by wave or reflow soldering.

Types

PCBs range from single-sided boards for simple devices to high-density interconnect (HDI) boards with microvias used in smartphones, and include flexible circuits on polyimide film and rigid-flex combinations used in medical and aerospace equipment.
