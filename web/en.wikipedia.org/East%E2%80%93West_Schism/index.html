East–West Schism

Date1054 (conventional)
Also known asGreat Schism
PartiesWestern (Roman Catholic) Church; Eastern (Orthodox) Church

The East–West Schism, also known as the Great Schism, is the break of communion which occurred in 1054 between the Roman Catholic Church and the Eastern Orthodox Church. The schism was the culmination of theological and political disputes that had developed over the preceding centuries between the Latin West and the Greek East.

Causes

Disagreements included the source of the Holy Spirit (the Filioque clause added in the West to the Nicene Creed), the use of unleavened bread in the Eucharist, clerical celibacy, and above all the extent of the jurisdiction and authority of the Bishop of Rome. Cultural, linguistic, and political estrangement between the two halves of the former Roman Empire deepened the divide.

Events of 1054

In 1054 a papal legation led by Cardinal Humbert of Silva Candida excommunicated the Ecumenical Patriarch Michael I Cerularius in Hagia Sophia, and Cerularius responded by excommunicating the legates. Although the excommunications applied to individuals rather than whole churches, communion was not restored, and the breach was hardened by later events, notably the sack of Constantinople by Latin crusaders in 1204.

Aftermath

Attempts at reunion, at the councils of Lyons (1274) and Florence (1439), failed. In 1965 Pope Paul VI and Ecumenical Patriarch Athenagoras lifted the mutual excommunications of 1054, and ecumenical dialogue continues, but the churches remain divided.
