Graphology

Graphology is the analysis of handwriting in an attempt to determine the writer's personality traits. The term is sometimes confused with questioned document examination, the forensic discipline of authenticating documents; graphology in the personality sense has no scientific evidence supporting its validity and is generally considered a pseudoscience.

History

Interest in character from handwriting dates to the seventeenth century, when the Italian physician Camillo Baldi published a treatise on the subject in 1622. In the nineteenth century the French cleric Jean-Hippolyte Michon coined the term graphologie and systematized the analysis of individual graphic signs. Ludwig Klages and Max Pulver developed the field in Germany and Switzerland, adding ideas such as form level and symbolic interpretation of space on the page.

Methods

Graphologists examine features such as the slant, size, pressure, speed, rhythm and spacing of writing, the shape of individual letters, margins, baselines and signature. From these they infer traits such as energy, emotional responsiveness, sociability and honesty. In some countries, particularly France, graphology has been used by employers in personnel selection.

Scientific status

Controlled studies and meta-analyses have repeatedly found that graphologists perform no better than non-graphologists, and no better than chance, at predicting personality or job performance from handwriting. Critics attribute positive impressions to the Barnum effect and other cognitive biases. The British Psychological Society has ranked graphology alongside astrology as a measure of zero validity, although forensic handwriting comparison for authorship remains a separate, accepted discipline.
