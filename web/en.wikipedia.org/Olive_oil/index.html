Olive oil

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Olive oil is a liquid fat obtained by pressing whole olives, the fruit of Olea europaea, a traditional tree crop of the Mediterranean Basin. It is commonly used in cooking for frying and salad dressings, and historically also in cosmetics, pharmaceuticals, soaps and as fuel for lamps. Spain is the world's largest producer, followed by Italy, Greece and Tunisia.

History and production

Olives have been cultivated since the Neolithic, and olive oil was a cornerstone of ancient Greek and Roman commerce, stored and shipped in amphorae. Production involves harvesting, washing, crushing the fruit into a paste (traditionally by millstone, now usually by steel crushers), malaxation, and separating the oil by pressing or centrifugation. Oil extracted by mechanical means alone, with acidity below 0.8%, may be graded extra virgin, the highest category under International Olive Council standards; lower grades include virgin olive oil and refined blends.

Composition and health

Olive oil is composed mainly of oleic acid, a monounsaturated fatty acid, with smaller amounts of palmitic acid and linoleic acid, plus phenolic compounds such as hydroxytyrosol. It is a central fat in the Mediterranean diet, which epidemiological studies associate with cardiovascular benefits, though specific health claims are regulated and contested. Adulteration — blending with cheaper oils — is a persistent problem in the trade.

Uses

Beyond food, olive oil is used in skincare, traditional medicine and religious ritual, including anointing. See also olive, Mediterranean diet and vegetable oil.
