Council of Chalcedon

The Council of Chalcedon was the fourth ecumenical council of the Christian Church, convoked by the Roman emperor Marcian in 451 AD and held in Chalcedon, a city of Bithynia in Asia Minor, across the Bosphorus from Constantinople. The council repudiated the Eutychian doctrine of monophysitism and declared that Christ has two natures — divine and human — in one person, a position set out in the Chalcedonian Definition.

Background

The council was convened in the aftermath of controversy over the teaching of Eutyches, an archimandrite of Constantinople, who held that Christ's human nature was absorbed into the divine. Eutyches had been condemned at a synod in Constantinople in 448 but reinstated by the Second Council of Ephesus in 449 — a council later branded the "Robber Council" by Pope Leo I, whose Tome setting out the two-natures doctrine had been ignored there. The new emperor Marcian and Empress Pulcheria called a fresh council to settle the question.

Proceedings and definition

The council met from 8 October to 1 November 451, with several hundred bishops attending, overwhelmingly from the East; it is generally considered the largest and best-documented of the early councils. The bishops acclaimed Leo's Tome and produced the Chalcedonian Definition, confessing Christ to be "acknowledged in two natures, without confusion, without change, without division, without separation", the two natures concurring in one person and one subsistence. The council also deposed Dioscorus of Alexandria and issued 27 disciplinary canons.

Aftermath

The council's decisions were rejected by many Christians in Egypt and Syria, who adhered to miaphysitism; their communities developed into the Oriental Orthodox Churches, separate from the Chalcedonian churches. The rejection of Chalcedon produced a lasting schism within Christianity. The Eastern Orthodox Church and the Roman Catholic Church both accept Chalcedon as an ecumenical council, and its definition remained normative for most of Western and Byzantine Christianity.
