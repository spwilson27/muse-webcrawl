Poutine

Poutine (Quebec French: [put͡sɪn]) is a dish of french fries and cheese curds topped with a brown gravy. It emerged in late 1950s Quebec, in the Centre-du-Québec region, though its exact origins are widely debated. It has long been associated with Quebec cuisine, and has become an iconic symbol of Québécois culture and, more broadly, of Canada.

Composition

Basic poutine consists of three components: french fries, fresh cheese curds, and hot gravy. The fries are traditionally cut to a medium thickness and are sometimes double-fried. The cheese curds should be fresh, soft, and produce a characteristic squeak when bitten. The gravy is typically a light, peppery chicken or veal stock-based sauce, poured over the curds so that they melt slightly while retaining their shape.

History

The dish originated in rural Quebec in the late 1950s. Several local communities claim to be its birthplace, including Drummondville, Princeville and Warwick. A frequently cited account credits Fernand Lachance of Warwick, who in 1957 reportedly combined fries and cheese curds at a customer's request; a restaurant owner in Drummondville, Jean-Paul Roy, is also said to have added gravy to the combination around 1964. The word poutine is believed to derive from Quebec slang meaning "mess", or from the English word "pudding".

Spread and variations

Once confined to snack bars (casse-croûtes) in Quebec, poutine spread across Canada in the 1990s and 2000s, eventually appearing in fast-food chains and upscale restaurants alike. Numerous variations exist, substituting or adding ingredients such as pulled pork, smoked meat, butter chicken, or lobster. An annual poutine festival, La Poutine Week, is held in Montreal and other cities. The dish has also gained popularity in the United States and internationally.
