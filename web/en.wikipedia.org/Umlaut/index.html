Umlaut

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In linguistics, an umlaut (from German Um- "around/changed" + Laut "sound") is a sound change in which a vowel is pronounced more like a following vowel or semivowel. The term was coined by Jacob Grimm in the 19th century. Most commonly, a back vowel is fronted when the following syllable contains a front vowel or the palatal approximant /j/, a process known as i-umlaut or i-mutation, which is fundamental to the history of the Germanic languages.

I-umlaut in Germanic

In Old English and Old High German, i-umlaut affected many words and left durable traces in modern languages: it is responsible for irregular plurals such as English foot/feet and man/men, and for related word pairs such as long/length, strong/strength, and German Gast/Gäste ("guest/guests") and Mann/Männer ("man/men"). The conditioning vowel that originally caused the change was often later lost or reduced, leaving the vowel alternation itself as a grammatical marker.

The diacritic

The word "umlaut" is also used for the two-dot diacritic (¨) placed over a vowel in German and several other languages to indicate the umlauted sound, as in ä, ö, and ü. The mark originated as a small superscript "e" written above the letter, which over time was reduced to two strokes and then two dots. The identical-looking mark is called a diaeresis when it serves the different function of indicating that two adjacent vowels are pronounced separately, as in naïve or coöperate.

Other languages

Umlaut processes are important in German, Dutch, the Scandinavian languages, Turkish (vowel harmony is a related phenomenon), and many others. In German spelling, when the umlaut mark cannot be used, it is conventionally replaced by the digraphs ae, oe, and ue. In popular culture, the decorative "heavy metal umlaut" appears in band names such as Motörhead and Mötley Crüe.
