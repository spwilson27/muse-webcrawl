p-value

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In null-hypothesis significance testing, the p-value is the probability of obtaining test results at least as extreme as the result actually observed, under the assumption that the null hypothesis is correct. A very small p-value means that such an extreme observed outcome would be very unlikely under the null hypothesis, and is therefore taken as evidence against it. Reporting of p-values of statistical tests is common practice in academic publications of many quantitative fields.

Definition

Formally, for a test statistic whose large values are evidence against the null hypothesis, the p-value is the probability, computed under the null model, of obtaining a statistic at least as large as the one observed. It depends on the data, the chosen test statistic, and the specification of what counts as "more extreme". A result is conventionally called "statistically significant" when p is below a pre-set significance level, commonly 0.05.

Interpretation and misinterpretation

The p-value is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true, nor the probability that the observed result occurred by chance alone, nor the probability of a false positive in the sense often assumed. Because misinterpretations are so common, the American Statistical Association issued a formal statement in 2016 setting out six principles, including that p-values do not measure the size of an effect or the importance of a result, and that scientific conclusions should not be based only on whether a p-value passes a specific threshold.

History

Calculations resembling p-values appear in the work of John Arbuthnot (1710) and Pierre-Simon Laplace, and were formalised in the early twentieth century by Karl Pearson. R. A. Fisher popularised the p-value and the 0.05 convention in the 1920s, within a framework of significance testing that differs from the hypothesis-testing framework of Jerzy Neyman and Egon Pearson; modern practice is largely a hybrid of the two.

Criticism

Critics associate the routine use of p-value thresholds with publication bias, p-hacking, and the replication crisis in psychology and other fields. Proposed responses include reporting effect sizes and confidence intervals, pre-registration of analyses, lowering the default threshold to 0.005, or abandoning dichotomous significance claims altogether in favour of interpreting results as continuous evidence.
