SystemSkeletal

FunctionBone resorption

An osteoclast (from the Greek words osteon, bone, and klastos, broken) is a type of bone cell that breaks down bone tissue. This function is critical in the maintenance, repair, and remodelling of bones of the vertebral skeleton. The osteoclast disassembles and digests the composite of hydrated protein and mineral at a molecular level by secreting acid and a collagenase, a process known as bone resorption. This process also helps regulate the level of blood calcium.

Structure

Osteoclasts are large multinucleate cells, typically 150–200 μm in diameter, formed by the fusion of precursor cells of the monocyte/macrophage lineage. They contain between 5 and 20 nuclei. When active, an osteoclast sits in a shallow depression on the bone surface called a resorption bay or Howship's lacuna. The cell membrane facing the bone forms a highly folded "ruffled border", around which a clear actin-rich "sealing zone" isolates the resorption compartment.

Function

Within the sealed compartment, a vacuolar H+-ATPase pumps protons to lower the pH to about 4.5, dissolving the hydroxyapatite mineral of bone. The enzyme cathepsin K and other proteases then degrade the collagenous matrix. The products of resorption are endocytosed and released from the cell. Osteoclast activity is balanced by bone formation from osteoblasts; signalling molecules such as RANKL and its decoy receptor osteoprotegerin regulate osteoclast formation.

Clinical significance

Excessive osteoclast activity contributes to osteoporosis, Paget's disease of bone and the bone loss of multiple myeloma. Defective osteoclasts cause osteopetrosis, in which bone becomes abnormally dense and brittle. Bisphosphonates and the anti-RANKL antibody denosumab are used clinically to suppress osteoclast-mediated resorption.
