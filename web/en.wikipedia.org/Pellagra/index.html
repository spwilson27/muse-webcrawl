Pellagra

CauseDeficiency of niacin (vitamin B3) or tryptophan
SymptomsDermatitis, diarrhoea, dementia ("the three Ds")
TreatmentNiacin supplementation, balanced diet
First described1735, by Gaspar Casal in Spain

Pellagra is a disease caused by a deficiency of niacin (vitamin B3) in the diet, or of its amino-acid precursor tryptophan. It is classically characterised by "the three Ds": dermatitis, diarrhoea and dementia; untreated, a fourth D — death — may follow.

Signs and symptoms

The hallmark skin sign is a symmetric, sun-sensitive dermatitis on exposed areas — the face, neck (where it forms the characteristic "Casal collar"), hands and forearms. Gastrointestinal symptoms include loss of appetite, abdominal pain, inflammation of the mouth and tongue, and diarrhoea. Neurological and psychiatric features range from apathy, depression and memory loss to confusion, hallucinations and dementia.

History

Pellagra was first described in 1735 by the Spanish physician Gaspar Casal among poor peasants in Asturias whose diet was dominated by maize. The name comes from the Italian pelle agra ("rough skin"), coined in the 1770s when the disease was widespread in northern Italy. In the early twentieth century, pellagra reached epidemic proportions in the American South, afflicting millions and killing tens of thousands. US Public Health Service physician Joseph Goldberger demonstrated through dietary studies and experiments in the 1910s–1920s that the disease was caused by a dietary deficiency rather than infection. In 1937 Conrad Elvehjem identified nicotinic acid as the curative factor, and subsequent enrichment of flour with niacin virtually eliminated the disease in developed countries.

Causes and treatment

Primary pellagra arises from diets poor in niacin and tryptophan — notably diets based on untreated maize, in which niacin is chemically bound. Traditional nixtamalization of maize with alkali releases the vitamin, explaining the disease's rarity in Mesoamerica. Secondary pellagra can result from malabsorption, chronic alcoholism, or certain drugs. Treatment is oral niacin or nicotinamide with a protein-rich diet, and symptoms usually resolve rapidly.
