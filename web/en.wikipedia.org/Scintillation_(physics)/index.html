Scintillation (physics)

In physics, scintillation is the process in which a material, called a scintillator, emits brief flashes of light (photons) when it absorbs ionizing radiation. The flashes can be detected and counted with a photomultiplier tube or photodiode, forming the basis of the scintillation counter, one of the most widely used radiation detectors.

Mechanism

When a charged particle or gamma ray deposits energy in a scintillator, it excites atoms or molecules in the material. As these return to their ground state, they emit photons, typically in the visible or ultraviolet range. The amount of light produced is roughly proportional to the energy deposited, allowing both counting and spectroscopy of radiation.

Materials

Common scintillators include inorganic crystals such as sodium iodide doped with thallium (NaI(Tl)), caesium iodide and bismuth germanate, as well as organic crystals, liquids and plastics. Liquid scintillation counting is widely used to measure weak beta emitters such as tritium and carbon-14.

Applications

Scintillation detectors are used in particle physics, nuclear medicine (including gamma cameras and PET scanners), radiation monitoring, security screening and oil-well logging.
