Biostratigraphy

[Image placeholder: index fossils]

Biostratigraphy is the branch of stratigraphy that focuses on correlating and assigning relative ages of rock strata by using the fossil assemblages contained within them. It relies on the observation — formalized by William Smith in the early 19th century as the principle of faunal succession — that fossil organisms succeed one another in a definite, irreversible order through geologic time, so that strata containing the same fossils are of the same age, even when widely separated geographically.

Biozones

The fundamental unit of biostratigraphy is the biozone, a body of rock defined by its fossil content. Types include range zones (the total stratigraphic range of a taxon), interval zones (between two biohorizons such as first or last appearances), assemblage zones (characterized by a distinctive association of fossils), and abundance zones. Fossils especially useful for correlation are called index fossils: they should be widespread, abundant, distinctive, and limited to a short interval of time. Ammonites, trilobites, and graptolites are classic index fossils for the Paleozoic and Mesozoic; in subsurface work, microscopic fossils — foraminifera, calcareous nannofossils, and palynomorphs (pollen and spores) — are routinely used to date drill cuttings and cores.

Applications

Biostratigraphy is an essential tool in petroleum exploration, where microfossils recovered from boreholes help geologists correlate rock units between wells and identify reservoir horizons. It also underpins the construction of the geologic time scale: the boundaries of most Phanerozoic stages are defined, directly or indirectly, by fossil first appearances at Global Boundary Stratotype Sections and Points (GSSPs).

History

The approach was pioneered in the 1790s–1810s by William Smith, who used fossils to map strata across England. Georges Cuvier and Alexandre Brongniart applied the method in the Paris Basin, and Albert Oppel refined zonal schemes in the Jurassic in the 1850s. Charles Darwin's theory of evolution later provided the theoretical explanation for faunal succession.
