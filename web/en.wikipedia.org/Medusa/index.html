Medusa

In Greek mythology, Medusa (Greek: Μέδουσα, "guardian, protectress"), also called Gorgo, was one of the three Gorgons, three monstrous sisters with snakes for hair whose gaze turned onlookers to stone. Unlike her immortal sisters Stheno and Euryale, Medusa was mortal, and she was beheaded by the hero Perseus, who then used her head — which retained its petrifying power — as a weapon before giving it to the goddess Athena to place on her aegis.

Mythology

In the version told by the Roman poet Ovid, Medusa was originally a beautiful maiden and priestess of Athena who was transformed into a monster after being violated by Poseidon in Athena's temple; Athena punished Medusa by turning her hair into serpents. In earlier Greek accounts, including Hesiod's Theogony, the Gorgons were simply born monstrous, daughters of the sea deities Phorcys and Ceto. From Medusa's severed neck sprang the winged horse Pegasus and the giant Chrysaor.

Cultural legacy

The image of Medusa's head, the gorgoneion, was widely used in ancient Greek art as a protective symbol to ward off evil. Medusa has remained a powerful figure in art and culture, depicted by sculptors such as Benvenuto Cellini and painters such as Caravaggio, and reinterpreted in modern feminist literature as a symbol of female rage and survival.
