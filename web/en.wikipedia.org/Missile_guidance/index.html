Missile guidance

Missile guidance refers to a variety of methods of guiding a missile or guided bomb to its intended target. The missile's target accuracy is a critical factor for its effectiveness. Guidance systems improve accuracy by means of a "seeking" device and a control system that steers the weapon in flight.

Categories of guidance

Guidance systems are commonly divided by whether the missile tracks the target itself (homing), follows commands from an external platform, or navigates by itself to a predetermined point.

Homing guidance

  • Active homing — the missile carries its own transmitter and receiver, usually a radar, to illuminate and track the target (e.g., the AIM-120 AMRAAM in its terminal phase).
  • Semi-active homing — the launching platform illuminates the target and the missile homes on the reflected energy, as in semi-active radar homing (SARH) or laser-guided bombs.
  • Passive homing — the missile tracks emissions from the target itself, such as infrared heat (infrared homing air-to-air missiles) or radio transmissions (anti-radiation missiles).

Other methods

  • Command guidance — an external operator or computer tracks both missile and target and commands corrections, as in early surface-to-air missiles and wire-guided anti-tank missiles (MCLOS and SACLOS).
  • Beam riding — the missile rides along a radar or laser beam aimed at the target.
  • Inertial guidance — gyroscopes and accelerometers track the missile's own motion; ballistic missiles rely on it, often refined by stellar or satellite updates.
  • Presets and scene matching — cruise missiles use terrain-contour matching (TERCOM) and digital scene matching (DSMAC), and modern weapons add GPS navigation.

Guidance laws

The classic steering algorithm is proportional navigation, which commands acceleration proportional to the change in line-of-sight angle to the target, producing an efficient collision course. Variants such as augmented proportional navigation compensate for target maneuvers and gravity.
