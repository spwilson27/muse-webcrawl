Pyruvic acid is an organic acid, a ketone, and the simplest of the alpha-keto acids, with the chemical formula CH₃COCOOH. Its anion, called pyruvate, is a key intermediate in several metabolic pathways throughout the cell and is often described as occupying a crossroads of carbohydrate, fat, and protein metabolism.

Chemistry

Pyruvic acid is a colorless liquid with an odor similar to acetic acid. It is miscible with water and can be prepared in the laboratory by heating tartaric acid with potassium bisulfate. Jöns Jacob Berzelius first isolated it in 1835 by distilling tartaric acid. Pyruvic acid readily converts to pyruvate at physiological pH and can be reduced to lactic acid or decarboxylated to acetaldehyde.

Role in metabolism

Pyruvate is the end product of glycolysis, in which one molecule of glucose yields two molecules of pyruvate. From there it has several fates. Under aerobic conditions it is transported into mitochondria and oxidatively decarboxylated by the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex to acetyl-CoA, which enters the citric acid cycle. Under anaerobic conditions, or in rapidly exercising muscle, it is reduced to lactate to regenerate NAD⁺ (lactic acid fermentation). Pyruvate also participates in gluconeogenesis, transamination to the amino acid alanine, and carboxylation to oxaloacetate.

Uses

Pyruvic acid is used commercially in chemical synthesis, in biochemical research, and has been sold as a dietary supplement ("pyruvate") with claims of fat loss that clinical evidence supports only weakly.
