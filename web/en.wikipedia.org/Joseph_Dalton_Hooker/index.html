Joseph Dalton Hooker

Born: June 30, 1817, Halesworth, Suffolk, England
Died: December 10, 1911 (aged 94), Sunningdale, Berkshire, England
Fields: Botany, plant geography
Known for: Director of Kew Gardens; supporter of Darwin

Sir Joseph Dalton Hooker (30 June 1817 – 10 December 1911) was a British botanist and explorer in the 19th century. He was a founder of geographical botany and Charles Darwin's closest friend. For 20 years he served as director of the Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew, succeeding his father, William Jackson Hooker. He was awarded the highest honours of British science.

Early life

Hooker was born in Halesworth, Suffolk, the second son of the botanist William Jackson Hooker. He studied medicine at the University of Glasgow, graduating MD in 1839, and grew up assisting his father with the herbarium that would become the nucleus of Kew.

The Ross expedition

From 1839 to 1843 Hooker sailed as assistant surgeon and botanist aboard HMS Erebus on James Clark Ross's Antarctic expedition to the south magnetic pole. The voyage yielded his six-volume Flora Antarctica and established his reputation. The plants he collected on Kerguelen Island, Tasmania, and New Zealand founded his lifelong study of the geographical distribution of plants.

Himalayan travels

From 1847 to 1851 Hooker explored the Himalayas, travelling through Sikkim and Nepal and collecting some 7,000 species, including the rhododendrons that transformed British gardens. Briefly imprisoned by the Dewan of Sikkim, he published his journals as Himalayan Journals (1854) and produced the monumental Flora of British India (1875–1897).

Darwin and evolution

Hooker was among the first scientists to whom Darwin confided his theory of evolution by natural selection, and in 1858 he and Charles Lyell presented Darwin's and Alfred Russel Wallace's papers jointly to the Linnean Society. At the famous 1860 Oxford evolution debate he defended Darwin against Bishop Samuel Wilberforce. His 1859 introductory essay to the Flora Tasmaniae publicly endorsed evolution, making him one of the first prominent botanists to do so.

Kew and later life

Hooker became assistant director of Kew in 1855 and director in 1865, a post he held for twenty years, greatly expanding the gardens' collections and scientific role. He later travelled in Syria, Morocco, and the western United States. He served as president of the Royal Society from 1873 to 1878 and received the Copley Medal and the Darwin Medal. He died in 1911 and was buried at St Anne's Church, Kew.
