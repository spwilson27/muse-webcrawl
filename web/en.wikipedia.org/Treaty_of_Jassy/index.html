The Treaty of Jassy was a peace treaty between the Russian Empire and the Ottoman Empire signed on 9 January 1792 at Jassy (now Iași, Romania), then the capital of Moldavia. It ended the Russo-Turkish War of 1787–1792 and confirmed Russia's annexation of the Crimean Khanate while extending the Russian frontier to the Dniester River.

Background

The war had begun when the Ottomans, alarmed by Catherine the Great's annexation of Crimea in 1783 and her "Greek Project" ambitions, demanded the peninsula's return. Russia, allied with Austria until 1791, won major victories under Suvorov at Focșani and Rymnik (1789) and captured the fortress of Izmail in a bloody assault in December 1790. Austria left the war by the Treaty of Sistova (1791), and after the Russian naval victory at Cape Kaliakra, the Ottomans — also threatened by events in revolutionary France reshaping European politics — agreed to negotiate at Jassy. Grigory Potemkin, Catherine's favourite and commander in the south, led the Russian side until his death in October 1791, after which Alexander Bezborodko concluded the treaty.

Terms

The treaty confirmed the 1783 annexation of Crimea and the Kuban region to Russia, definitively legitimising what had been contested since the war began. The Russo-Ottoman frontier in Europe was moved west from the Southern Bug to the Dniester, giving Russia the Yedisan territory between the two rivers, including the fortress of Ochakov (Özi) at the Dnieper estuary. Russia returned its other conquests, including Bessarabia and the Danubian principalities of Moldavia and Wallachia, which remained under Ottoman suzerainty with strengthened autonomy guarantees. The treaty also reaffirmed earlier agreements protecting Orthodox Christians in the Ottoman Empire, clauses Russia would repeatedly invoke in the following century.

Aftermath

On the newly acquired Black Sea coast, Russia founded Odessa in 1794, which grew into the empire's great southern port. The Dniester frontier established at Jassy lasted until 1812, when the Treaty of Bucharest after the next Russo-Turkish war moved the border to the Prut and annexed Bessarabia. The settlement thus marked another step in the long Ottoman retreat from the northern Black Sea steppe, a region Catherine's colonisation programme — "New Russia" — was rapidly transforming.
