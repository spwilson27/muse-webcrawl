Xanthan gum

E numberE415
Produced byXanthomonas campestris
FunctionThickener, stabilizer, emulsifier
Discovered1960s, USDA research

Xanthan gum is a polysaccharide widely used as a food additive and rheology modifier. It is produced by the fermentation of sugars by the bacterium Xanthomonas campestris — the same bacterium responsible for black rot on cruciferous vegetables — and is sold as a fine cream-coloured powder.

Properties

Xanthan gum is highly effective at low concentrations: even one percent can greatly increase the viscosity of a liquid. Its solutions are strongly shear-thinning — they flow when stirred or poured but regain viscosity at rest — and their viscosity is stable over wide ranges of temperature, pH, and salt concentration.

Uses

In foods, xanthan gum thickens salad dressings, sauces, dairy products, and beverages, prevents ice-crystal formation in frozen goods, and is a staple of gluten-free baking, where it mimics the binding qualities of gluten. Outside the kitchen it is used in cosmetics, toothpaste, and in oil drilling fluids. In the European Union it is listed as food additive E415.

History

The gum was discovered by Allene Rosalind Jeanes and her team at the United States Department of Agriculture in the 1960s and was commercialized by the Kelco company under the trade name Kelzan in the early 1960s.
