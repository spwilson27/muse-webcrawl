Joseph Campbell

Born: Joseph John Campbell, March 26, 1904, White Plains, New York, U.S.
Died: October 30, 1987 (aged 83), Honolulu, Hawaii, U.S.
Occupation: Author, professor of literature
Alma mater: Dartmouth College; Columbia University (BA, MA)
Known for: The monomyth; The Hero with a Thousand Faces

Joseph John Campbell (March 26, 1904 – October 30, 1987) was an American writer and professor of literature at Sarah Lawrence College who worked in comparative mythology and comparative religion. His work covers many aspects of the human experience. Campbell's best-known work is his 1949 book The Hero with a Thousand Faces, in which he discusses his theory of the journey of the archetypal hero shared by world mythologies, termed the monomyth.

Early life and education

Campbell was born in White Plains, New York, to a middle-class Irish Catholic family. A childhood trip to see Buffalo Bill's Wild West show sparked his lifelong interest in Native American cultures. He studied at Dartmouth College before transferring to Columbia University, where he earned a BA in English literature in 1925 and an MA in medieval literature in 1927. A fellowship took him to the University of Paris and the University of Munich, where he encountered the works of Sigmund Freud and Carl Jung, as well as modernist artists and writers such as James Joyce.

Career

Returning to the United States during the Great Depression, Campbell spent several years reading on the Monterey Peninsula in California, where he became friends with John Steinbeck. In 1934 he joined the faculty of Sarah Lawrence College, where he taught comparative literature for 38 years. With Henry Morton Robinson he co-authored A Skeleton Key to Finnegans Wake (1944), the first major study of Joyce's novel.

The monomyth

In The Hero with a Thousand Faces (1949), Campbell argued that classic hero myths from around the world follow a common pattern he called the monomyth: "A hero ventures forth from the world of common day into a region of supernatural wonder: fabulous forces are there encountered and a decisive victory is won: the hero comes back from this mysterious adventure with the power to bestow boons on his fellow man." The pattern unfolds in three acts — departure, initiation, and return. The book influenced generations of storytellers; George Lucas credited it as an inspiration for Star Wars.

Later work and popular fame

Campbell's four-volume The Masks of God (1959–1968) surveyed world mythology, and his unfinished Historical Atlas of World Mythology followed. Widely known for the phrase "follow your bliss," he reached his largest audience posthumously through Joseph Campbell and the Power of Myth (1988), a PBS television series of interviews with journalist Bill Moyers filmed at George Lucas's Skywalker Ranch.

Criticism

Scholars have criticized Campbell's work as overly universalizing, arguing that the monomyth flattens cultural differences and that his readings sometimes privilege archetype over historical evidence. His remarks on Judaism and race in some writings and diaries have also drawn criticism. Nevertheless his books remain bestsellers and continue to shape screenwriting and popular understandings of myth.
