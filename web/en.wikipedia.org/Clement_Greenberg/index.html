Clement Greenberg

Clement Greenberg (January 16, 1909 – May 7, 1994), sometimes credited with the pen name K. Hardesh, was an American essayist known mainly as a very influential visual art critic of the mid-20th century and the curator of post-painterly abstraction. He is best remembered for his promotion of the abstract expressionist painters, notably Jackson Pollock, and for his theory of modernism, according to which each art should purify itself of everything not proper to its medium.

Career

Born in the Bronx to Jewish immigrant parents, Greenberg graduated from Syracuse University in 1930 and worked in his father's dry-goods business and as a customs clerk before turning to writing. He joined the circle of the Partisan Review, where he published his breakthrough essay "Avant-Garde and Kitsch" (1939), which contrasted genuine avant-garde art with the debased mass culture he called kitsch. From 1940 he was art critic for The Nation and later wrote for Commentary and Art International.

Theory and influence

In essays such as "Towards a Newer Laocoön" (1940), "'American-Type' Painting" (1955), and "Modernist Painting" (1960), Greenberg argued that modernist art, following Kantian self-criticism, progressively acknowledged the flatness of the picture plane and the optical nature of painting. He championed Pollock, Arshile Gorky, Willem de Kooning, Hans Hofmann, Barnett Newman, and Clyfford Still, and later the colour-field painters Helen Frankenthaler, Kenneth Noland, and Morris Louis, whose work he gathered in the 1964 exhibition Post-Painterly Abstraction. His taste and criticism were decisive in shifting the centre of the art world from Paris to New York.

Criticism and legacy

From the 1960s Greenberg's formalism was attacked by a new generation of critics and artists—minimalists, pop artists, and theorists associated with October magazine—who charged his account with narrowness and dogmatism. His editorial interventions in the work of sculptor Anthony Caro and others also drew controversy. Nevertheless his writings remain central documents of modern art history, and the debate over his legacy continues to structure arguments about modernism.
