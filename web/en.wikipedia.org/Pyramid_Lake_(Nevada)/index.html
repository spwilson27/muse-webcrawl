Pyramid Lake is the largest remnant of ancient Lake Lahontan, a vast Pleistocene lake that covered much of northwestern Nevada. It is a terminal (endorheic) desert lake in Washoe County, about 64 km northeast of Reno, fed by the Truckee River flowing from Lake Tahoe. The lake lies entirely within the Pyramid Lake Paiute Tribe Reservation and takes its name from a distinctive pyramid-shaped tufa rock formation near its eastern shore.

Geography and geology

Pyramid Lake covers about 487 square kilometres and reaches a maximum depth of about 109 metres, making it one of the largest natural lakes in Nevada. Its shores and islands, including Anaho Island, are studded with tufa formations — calcium carbonate deposits formed when the lake stood at higher levels. Anaho Island is a National Wildlife Refuge protecting one of the largest American white pelican colonies in the United States.

Ecology

The lake is famous for the Lahontan cutthroat trout, once thought nearly extinct from the lake after water diversions lowered its level; a strain rediscovered in a Utah creek was reintroduced, and Pyramid Lake again produces trophy-sized fish. The lake is also home to the cui-ui, an endangered sucker fish found nowhere else, sacred to the Paiute people. In 1903 the Derby Dam diverted Truckee River water to the Newlands Project, causing the lake to fall roughly 24 metres and contributing to the drying of neighboring Winnemucca Lake; legal settlements in the late twentieth century restored some flows.

History

The Northern Paiute people, who call themselves Kooyooe Tukadu ("cui-ui eaters"), have lived around the lake for centuries. Explorer John C. Frémont was the first European American to map it, in 1844. Two 1860 battles between settlers and Paiute warriors nearby gave the Pyramid Lake War its name. Today the lake is managed by the Pyramid Lake Paiute Tribe and is a noted destination for fishing and recreation.
