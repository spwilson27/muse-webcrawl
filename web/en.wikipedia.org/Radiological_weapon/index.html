Radiological weapon

A radiological weapon is any weapon designed to spread radioactive material with the intent to kill, injure or cause disruption. The best-known type is the radiological dispersal device (RDD), often called a dirty bomb, which combines conventional explosives with radioactive material so the explosion scatters contamination over an area.

Effects

Unlike a nuclear weapon, a dirty bomb involves no nuclear explosion. Casualties from the blast itself would likely exceed those from radiation, but the contamination could render buildings and land unusable until decontaminated, cause public panic, and impose large economic costs — leading analysts to call RDDs "weapons of mass disruption" rather than mass destruction. Another type, the radiological exposure device, hides a strong source where victims pass nearby.

History and concerns

Radiological warfare was studied during the Second World War and the early Cold War but never used in combat. Modern concern focuses on terrorist acquisition of radioactive sources from medicine, industry and research — such as caesium-137, cobalt-60 and iridium-192 — and international programmes track and secure such sources. The 1987 Goiânia accident in Brazil, though not a weapon, illustrated the social disruption caused by scattered caesium contamination.
