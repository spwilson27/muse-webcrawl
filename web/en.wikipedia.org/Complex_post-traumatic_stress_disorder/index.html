Complex post-traumatic stress disorder

Complex post-traumatic stress disorder (CPTSD, or C-PTSD) is a stress-related mental and behavioural disorder generally occurring in response to complex traumas, that is, commonly prolonged or repetitive exposures to a traumatic event or series of traumatic events, from which there is little or no chance to escape.

Diagnosis

CPTSD is recognised as a distinct diagnosis in the ICD-11, published by the World Health Organization, where it is classified alongside PTSD in the grouping of disorders specifically associated with stress. In addition to the core PTSD symptom clusters — re-experiencing the trauma in the present, avoidance, and a persistent sense of current threat — CPTSD requires severe "disturbances in self-organization": problems in affect regulation, a persistently negative self-concept (beliefs of worthlessness, with shame and guilt), and difficulties in sustaining relationships and feeling close to others.

The diagnosis is typically associated with sustained interpersonal trauma such as childhood abuse or neglect, domestic violence, human trafficking, or prolonged exposure to war and captivity. CPTSD has not been adopted as a separate diagnosis in the DSM-5, although the DSM includes a dissociative subtype of PTSD, and debates continue over the boundaries between CPTSD, PTSD, and borderline personality disorder.

History

The concept was first proposed by psychiatrist Judith Herman in her 1992 book Trauma and Recovery, arguing that survivors of prolonged, repeated trauma displayed a syndrome broader than PTSD. Field trials for the DSM-IV considered "disorders of extreme stress not otherwise specified" (DESNOS), and accumulated research evidence, particularly distinguishing the self-organization symptoms, supported the ICD-11's inclusion of CPTSD as a sibling diagnosis to PTSD rather than a subtype.

Treatment

Treatment generally follows phase-based approaches also recommended for severe PTSD: an initial phase of safety, stabilisation, and skills building (including emotion-regulation work), followed by trauma-focused processing such as trauma-focused cognitive behavioural therapy or EMDR, and finally integration and rehabilitation. Medication may target comorbid depression, anxiety, or sleep disturbance. Clinical guidelines emphasise that the additional affective and interpersonal difficulties in CPTSD may require longer or skills-augmented treatment compared with standard PTSD protocols.
