Burgh

A burgh (/ˈbʌrə/) is an autonomous corporate entity in Scotland, a type of town or city granted a charter granting it certain legal privileges and the right to self-government and trade. Burghs were introduced in the 12th century by King David I as part of his programme of reforming Scotland's government and economy, modelled on towns in England and Flanders.

Types of burgh

Royal burghs were created by the Crown and held a monopoly of foreign trade in their area; they sent representatives to the Parliament of Scotland and later returned MPs to Westminster. Burghs of barony and burghs of regality were created by powerful lay or ecclesiastical lords, and from the 19th century police burghs were established for growing industrial towns. Burgesses — the freemen of the burgh — enjoyed exclusive rights to trade and to participate in burgh government, which was run by a provost and council.

History and abolition

The first burghs included Berwick-upon-Tweed, Edinburgh, Roxburgh and Stirling, sited at royal castles and ports. Over the centuries their trading privileges were eroded, and the Burgh Reform Act 1833 opened council elections beyond the burgess class. Burghs ceased to exist as units of local government on 16 May 1975 under the Local Government (Scotland) Act 1973, when Scotland's counties and burghs were replaced by regions and districts. The status survives ceremonially in some places, and "royal burgh" titles are still borne by towns such as Stirling and Inverness.
