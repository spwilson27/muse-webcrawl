Polygraph

A polygraph, often incorrectly referred to as a lie detector test, is a device or procedure that measures and records several physiological indicators—such as blood pressure, pulse, respiration and skin conductivity—while a person is asked a series of questions. The underlying assumption is that deceptive answers produce distinctive physiological responses that can be detected by a trained examiner.

History

Early deception tests were developed in the 1910s–1920s by William Moulton Marston, who promoted the systolic blood-pressure test, and by John Augustus Larson and Leonarde Keeler, who built recording instruments combining multiple physiological channels. The polygraph became widely used by police and government agencies in the United States in the mid-twentieth century, including for employee screening.

Scientific status

The scientific community is broadly sceptical: reviews such as the US National Research Council's 2003 report concluded that polygraph testing lacks a solid theoretical and empirical basis, that accuracy is insufficient for security screening, and that results are vulnerable to countermeasures and false positives. Consequently, polygraph results are inadmissible as evidence in most courts, although they remain in use by some US federal agencies for clearances and investigations, subject to limits such as the Employee Polygraph Protection Act of 1988 for private employers.

Procedure

The most common technique, the comparison question test, contrasts reactions to "relevant" questions with reactions to broader "control" questions. Alternatives include the concealed information (guilty knowledge) test, which checks recognition of details only a knowledgeable person would know and is considered better grounded scientifically.
