Annales school

The Annales school is a group of historians associated with a style of historiography developed by French historians in the 20th century to stress long-term social history. It is named after its scholarly journal, Annales. Histoire, Sciences Sociales, founded in 1929 by Marc Bloch and Lucien Febvre as the Annales d'histoire économique et sociale.

Principles

The school rejected the predominantly political and diplomatic narrative history of events (histoire événementielle) in favour of a "total history" drawing on geography, economics, demography, and the social sciences. Fernand Braudel, the leading figure of the second generation, distinguished three layers of historical time: the almost imperceptible longue durée of structures, the medium-term of conjunctures, and the short-term of events — a framework set out in his masterpiece The Mediterranean and the Mediterranean World in the Age of Philip II (1949).

Generations

The first generation was led by Bloch and Febvre; the second, after the Second World War, by Braudel, who institutionalised the school in the VIe Section of the École pratique des hautes études and later the École des hautes études en sciences sociales. A third generation from the late 1960s, including Emmanuel Le Roy Ladurie and Jacques Le Goff, turned toward the history of mentalities, while a later fragmentation produced microhistory and a renewed interest in politics and narrative.

Influence

The Annales approach reshaped historical practice worldwide, influencing social history, world-systems analysis, and historical sociology far beyond France.
