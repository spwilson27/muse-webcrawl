Minimalist program

The minimalist program is a research program in generative linguistics proposed by Noam Chomsky in the early 1990s, notably in his 1995 book The Minimalist Program. It represents a major development of the principles and parameters framework and of earlier transformational grammar. Rather than being a complete theory, minimalism is a mode of inquiry: it asks how much of the structure of language can be explained by general principles of conceptual and computational economy, given that the language faculty must interface with the systems of thought and of speech perception and production.


Minimalist derivations build structure by successive applications of Merge.

Background

Generative grammar from Chomsky's Syntactic Structures (1957) onward sought a formal characterization of the knowledge that allows speakers to produce and understand an unbounded range of sentences. Successive frameworks — standard theory, government and binding theory (1981), and principles and parameters — progressively reduced the inventory of language-specific rules in favour of universal principles with parametric variation. The minimalist program continues this trajectory, asking whether even the remaining machinery can be derived from optimal design: if language is "a perfect system" meeting interface conditions in an economical way, its apparently arbitrary features should follow from those conditions.

Core ideas

Merge

The central syntactic operation is Merge, which takes two syntactic objects and forms a set containing them, yielding a new object that can itself be merged further. External Merge combines separate items (building phrase structure); internal Merge — the minimalist descendant of movement — takes an object already inside a structure and re-merges it in a new position, accounting for displacement phenomena such as question formation ("What did you see?" where what is understood as the object of see). Derivations proceed in phases, chunks of structure (typically CP and vP) that, once complete, are shipped to the interfaces in a process called Spell-Out.

Interfaces and economy

A derivation is legitimate if it converges at the two interfaces: the conceptual–intentional system (meaning) and the articulatory–perceptual system (sound or sign). Older levels of deep structure and surface structure are eliminated; only the interface representations — roughly, logical form and phonetic form — remain. Feature checking drives operations: uninterpretable features (for example, case and agreement features on verbs) must be valued and deleted before the interfaces, via the operation Agree between a probe and a goal.

Strong minimalist thesis

The strong minimalist thesis holds that language is an optimal solution to interface conditions — that the faculty of language in the narrow sense may comprise little more than recursion, implemented by Merge, plus the lexicon. In later work with Marc Hauser and W. Tecumseh Fitch (2002), Chomsky distinguished the faculty of language in the broad sense (shared with other animals and other cognitive systems) from the narrow faculty, hypothesized to be uniquely human and possibly very small.

Development and influence

Key texts include Chomsky's papers "A Minimalist Program for Linguistic Theory" (1993), The Minimalist Program (1995), "Minimalist Inquiries" (2000), "Derivation by Phase" (2001), and subsequent work on labelling, successive cyclicity, and the role of third-factor principles — general laws of nature, such as computational efficiency, not specific to language. Minimalist assumptions underlie much contemporary theoretical syntax, with substantial literature on the structure of phrases (bare phrase structure), the cartography of functional structure, and minimalist approaches to morphology and semantics.

Criticism

Critics argue that minimalism's core notions — economy, optimality, perfection — are programmatic rather than falsifiable, and that successive versions have reintroduced, under new names, much of the machinery they eliminated. Usage-based linguists and proponents of construction grammar question the assumption of an innate, domain-specific language faculty itself. Others note the difficulty of testing hypotheses about a hypothesized biological system without clearer links to neuroscience. Defenders reply that the program has generated precise, testable analyses of phenomena such as agreement, case, and island constraints, and that simplification of theory is itself an empirical bet.
