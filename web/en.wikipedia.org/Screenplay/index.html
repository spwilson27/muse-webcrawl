A screenplay, or script, is a written work by screenwriters for a film, television program, or video game. These screenplays can be original works or adaptations from existing pieces of writing. In them, the movement, actions, expression and dialogues of the characters are also narrated. A screenplay written for television is also known as a teleplay.

Format and style

The format is structured so that one page equates roughly to one minute of screen time, though this is used only as a rough guideline in practice. The standard screenplay format uses 12-point Courier font, scene headings (sluglines) written in capitals beginning INT. or EXT., action description in the present tense, character names centred (or indented) in capitals above their dialogue, and parentheticals used sparingly. Standard elements are:

  • Scene heading — e.g. "INT. COFFEE SHOP – DAY"
  • Action — description of the scene's visual elements
  • Character — the name of the person speaking
  • Dialogue — the words spoken
  • Parenthetical — a brief direction for delivery
  • Transition — e.g. "CUT TO:", "DISSOLVE TO:"

Types

A spec script is written on speculation, without commission, in hopes of a sale; a commissioned script is written under contract for a producer or studio. The shooting script is the production-ready version, with scene numbers and camera information such as revision marks. Television screenplays may be written for single-camera or multi-camera formats, with act breaks timed to commercial intervals; scripts for continuing series are overseen by a showrunner and a writers' room.

Structure

Most mainstream screenplays follow the three-act structure popularised by Syd Field's Screenplay (1979): Act 1 (the setup), Act 2 (the confrontation) and Act 3 (the resolution), joined by turning points or "plot points". Other influential models include Christopher Vogler's adaptation of the hero's journey and Blake Snyder's beat sheet in Save the Cat! (2005).
