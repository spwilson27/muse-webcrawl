The Battle of Moscow was a military campaign that consisted of two periods of strategically significant fighting on a 600 km sector of the Eastern Front during World War II, between October 1941 and January 1942. The Soviet defensive effort frustrated Nazi Germany's attack on Moscow, the capital of the Soviet Union, and the subsequent Soviet counteroffensive drove the Germans back from the city.

Operation Typhoon

After the encirclements of Kyiv and Smolensk, Hitler directed Army Group Centre, under Fedor von Bock, to resume the drive on Moscow in Operation Typhoon, launched on 2 October 1941. German panzer groups encircled and destroyed huge Soviet forces at Vyazma and Bryansk, taking hundreds of thousands of prisoners. Panic gripped Moscow in mid-October; much of the government evacuated to Kuibyshev, though Stalin remained in the capital.

The defence

General Georgy Zhukov took command of the city's defence, organising new lines manned by reserve armies and divisions transferred from Siberia and the Far East — freed by intelligence confirming Japan would not attack. Autumn rains turned roads to mud, then severe winter cold froze men and machinery, while German supply lines stretched to breaking point. By early December the German advance had stalled within sight of the Kremlin's suburbs.

The Soviet counteroffensive

On 5 December 1941 the Red Army struck back with fresh armies along the whole front north and south of Moscow. The Germans, exhausted and unprepared for winter warfare, were driven back 100–250 km. Moscow was never seriously threatened again. The battle marked the first major defeat of the Wehrmacht in the war and shattered the myth of German invincibility, with combined casualties on both sides well over a million.
