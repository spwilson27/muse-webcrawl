Two and twenty

"Two and twenty" is the traditional hedge fund fee structure.

"Two and twenty" is a fee structure that has long been standard in the hedge fund industry: a management fee of 2% of a fund's assets under management, charged annually, plus a performance (or incentive) fee of 20% of the fund's profits, typically measured above a high-water mark and sometimes a hurdle rate.

History

The performance component is commonly traced to Alfred Winslow Jones, whose pioneering 1949 fund charged investors 20% of profits, reportedly inspired by the share customarily taken by Phoenician merchants on successful voyages. The flat 2% management fee became conventional as the industry grew, and the combined "2 and 20" arrangement came to symbolise hedge fund economics, covering operating costs through the management fee and aligning manager incentives through the profit share.

Criticism and change

The model has been criticised for rewarding managers even in mediocre years (the 2% accrues regardless of performance) and for encouraging risk-taking, since managers share in gains but not losses beyond their own invested capital. Following the 2008 financial crisis and a period of weak returns, many funds reduced fees — arrangements such as "1.5 and 15" or lower became common — while large institutional investors negotiated customised terms. Nonetheless, elite funds with strong records have continued to command fees at or above the traditional level, sometimes through newer models such as pass-through expense structures.

Usage

The phrase is also applied by analogy to similar arrangements in private equity, where management fees and carried interest of around 20% are likewise standard.
