In mathematics, Galois theory, originally introduced by Évariste Galois, provides a connection between field theory and group theory. This connection, the fundamental theorem of Galois theory, allows reducing certain problems in field theory to group theory, which makes them simpler and easier to understand.

Galois introduced the subject to study roots of polynomials. This allowed him to characterize the polynomial equations that are solvable by radicals in terms of properties of the permutation group of their roots — an equation is solvable by radicals if and only if its Galois group is a solvable group. The theory explains, among other things, why there is no formula using only radicals for the roots of a general quintic (fifth-degree) polynomial.

History

Galois wrote his ideas in 1830–1832, shortly before his death in a duel at age 20. His manuscripts were published by Joseph Liouville in 1846, and the theory was developed and popularized by Richard Dedekind, Leopold Kronecker, Emil Artin and others. Artin's reformulation in terms of field extensions and automorphism groups gave the subject its modern form.

Main theorem

The fundamental theorem of Galois theory states that for a finite Galois extension E/F there is a one-to-one correspondence between the intermediate fields of the extension and the subgroups of its Galois group. The correspondence reverses inclusions: larger subgroups correspond to smaller fields. Intermediate fields that are normal extensions correspond precisely to normal subgroups, and the quotient group is then the Galois group of the smaller extension.

Applications

Beyond solvability of polynomials, Galois theory yields elegant proofs of classical geometric impossibility results — trisecting an arbitrary angle and doubling the cube with straightedge and compass are impossible — and underlies much of modern algebraic number theory, including the theory of Galois representations used in the proof of Fermat's Last Theorem.
