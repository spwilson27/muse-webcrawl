Textile

Textile
MaterialsNatural fibres (cotton, wool, silk, flax) and synthetics (polyester, nylon)
ProcessesSpinning, weaving, knitting, dyeing, finishing
ProductsClothing, household fabrics, industrial fabrics

A textile is a flexible material made by creating an interlocking network of yarns or threads, produced by spinning raw fibres — such as cotton, wool, flax, silk, or synthetic polymers — into long strands. Textiles are formed by weaving, knitting, crocheting, knotting, felting, or bonding fibres together, and are used above all for clothing and household goods.

History

Textile production is one of humanity's oldest technologies: dyed flax fibres from about 34,000 years ago have been found in a cave in Georgia, and weaving was established in Neolithic cultures worldwide. The textile trade shaped economies from ancient Egyptian linen and Chinese silk to medieval European wool. Mechanisation of spinning and weaving — the spinning jenny, water frame, and power loom — was the driving force of the early Industrial Revolution in 18th-century Britain.

Production and fibres

Modern textiles begin with fibre production: natural fibres are harvested from plants and animals, while synthetics such as polyester (now the world's most-produced fibre) are extruded from petrochemicals. Fibres are spun into yarn, then woven on looms (interlacing warp and weft) or knitted (interlocking loops), then dyed, printed, and finished. The global industry, concentrated in Asia, is one of the world's largest employers and a significant source of pollution, spurring interest in recycling and circular production.

Uses

Beyond apparel and furnishings, "technical textiles" serve in medicine (implants, bandages), agriculture, geotextiles for civil engineering, automotive and aerospace composites, and protective clothing.
