Pythagorean comma

Interval531441:524288
Size≈ 23.46 cents
Also calledDitonic comma

The Pythagorean comma (also called the ditonic comma) is the small interval existing in Pythagorean tuning between two enharmonically equivalent notes such as C and B♯. It equals the ratio 531441:524288, or about 23.46 cents — roughly a quarter of a semitone.

Derivation

In Pythagorean tuning, notes are generated by stacking pure perfect fifths (ratio 3:2). Twelve fifths give (3/2)12 = 531441/4096, while seven octaves give 27 = 128. Comparing the two totals at the same pitch level, twelve fifths exceed seven octaves by the ratio 531441:524288 — the Pythagorean comma. Equivalently, it is the difference between the Pythagorean apotome and limma (the chromatic and diatonic semitones), and between the two whole tones of the system in certain comparisons.

Consequences for tuning

Because a circle of pure fifths does not close, keyboard and fretted instruments tuned in pure fifths leave one "wolf" interval absorbing the comma. Historical temperaments distribute the comma: quarter-comma meantone eliminates it by flattening each fifth slightly, while equal temperament divides it evenly among all twelve fifths, each narrowed by about 1.96 cents (a twelfth of the comma), so that enharmonic notes coincide.

History

The discrepancy was known in ancient Greek theory and described by followers of Pythagoras's school; it was also noted in Chinese music theory. It exposes the incompatibility of the pure ratios 2:1 and 3:2 as generators of a closed scale — a foundational problem in music theory addressed by all subsequent tuning systems.
