Elizabeth Siddal

Elizabeth Eleanor Siddall (25 July 1829 – 11 February 1862), better known as Elizabeth Siddal, was an English poet and artist's model who became one of the most recognisable faces of Pre-Raphaelite painting. She modelled for John Everett Millais's Ophelia (1851–52) and for many works by Dante Gabriel Rossetti, whom she married in 1860. Siddal was also an artist and poet in her own right, supported by the critic John Ruskin.

Early life and modelling

Siddal was born in Holborn, London, to a cutler's family, and worked in a millinery shop when she was discovered in 1849 by the artist Walter Deverell. She modelled for Deverell, William Holman Hunt and Millais; for Ophelia she posed in a bath of water, an episode often linked to her chronic ill health.

Relationship with Rossetti

From the early 1850s Siddal worked almost exclusively with Rossetti, who produced hundreds of drawings and watercolours of her. They married on 23 May 1860 in Hastings, after a long and troubled engagement. Their daughter was stillborn in 1861.

Art and poetry

Ruskin settled an annuity on Siddal in exchange for her drawings and watercolours, and she exhibited at the Pre-Raphaelite exhibition at Russell Place in 1857, showing works such as The Lady of Shalott and Clerk Saunders. Her poems, often melancholic lyrics of lost love, were published posthumously.

Death and legacy

Depressed and addicted to laudanum, Siddal died of an overdose in February 1862. Rossetti buried a manuscript of his poems in her coffin, later having it exhumed. Her image — pale, red-haired and ethereal — came to define the Pre-Raphaelite ideal, and modern scholarship has restored attention to her own creative work.
