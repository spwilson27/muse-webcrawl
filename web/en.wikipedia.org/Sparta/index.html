Sparta, known in antiquity as Lacedaemon, was a prominent city-state in ancient Greece, situated on the banks of the Eurotas River in Laconia in the south-eastern Peloponnese. Renowned for its military discipline and austere social system, Sparta built the most formidable army in Greece and led the Greek resistance by land in the Persian Wars — most famously in the stand of Leonidas and the 300 at Thermopylae (480 BC) — before defeating Athens in the Peloponnesian War (431–404 BC) and becoming the hegemon of Greece.

Society and government

Spartan society rested on a small class of full citizens, the Spartiates, supported by free non-citizen perioeci and a vast population of state-owned serfs, the helots, whose labour freed citizens for full-time military life. Boys entered the agoge — a harsh communal upbringing — at age seven. Unusually, Sparta had two hereditary kings (from the Agiad and Eurypontid houses), checked by five annually elected ephors, a council of elders (gerousia) and a citizen assembly (apella). Spartan women enjoyed more property rights and public freedom than elsewhere in Greece.

History

Tradition attributed Sparta's constitution to the lawgiver Lycurgus. Sparta conquered Messenia in the 8th–7th centuries BC, organised the Peloponnesian League, and after the Persian Wars rivalled Athens for supremacy of Greece. Victory in 404 BC made Sparta dominant, but overreach, a shrinking citizen body (oliganthropia) and Thebes's victory at Leuctra in 371 BC ended Spartan hegemony; Messenia was liberated and Sparta declined into a second-rank power, later revived briefly under Cleomenes III before absorption into Rome's world in 146 BC.

Legacy

The "Spartan mirage" — admiration for Spartan discipline from Plato and Xenophon to modern popular culture — contrasts with a harsher reality of helot oppression and ritualised violence (the krypteia). The words "spartan" and "laconic" (from Laconia) preserve its reputation for austerity and terse speech.
