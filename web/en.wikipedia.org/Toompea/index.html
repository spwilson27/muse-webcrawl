Toompea (German: Domberg, "Cathedral Hill") is a limestone hill in the central part of Tallinn, the capital of Estonia. The hill, standing about 20–30 metres above the surrounding city, forms the historic upper town of Tallinn and has been the seat of power in Estonia for centuries: today it houses the Parliament of Estonia in Toompea Castle and the Government of Estonia in Stenbock House.

History

According to Estonian mythology, the hill is the burial mound of the legendary hero Kalev. An Estonian stronghold stood on Toompea by the early Middle Ages; in 1219 the Danish king Valdemar II captured the site and founded a castle, and the settlement that became Tallinn — in Estonian tradition "the Danish town" — grew around it. The castle and the cathedral chapter developed separately from the merchant Lower Town (the present Old Town), and in the medieval and early modern periods Toompea was governed by the nobility and the cathedral authorities rather than by the city council.

Toompea Castle was repeatedly rebuilt, most prominently under Swedish rule and again in the 18th century under Catherine II of Russia, when its pink Baroque palace front was constructed. In 1870 the hill's separate administration was abolished and Toompea was merged into Tallinn, though it remains physically distinct — a walled precinct reached from the Lower Town by the Long Leg and Short Leg gate passages.

Main sights

Landmarks include Toompea Castle with its 46-metre Pikk Hermann tower, from which the Estonian flag is raised daily; St Mary's Cathedral (Toomkirik), the oldest church in mainland Estonia, founded in the 13th century; the Russian Orthodox Alexander Nevsky Cathedral, completed in 1900; the Stenbock House, seat of the Government; and the Kiek in de Kök artillery tower and bastion tunnels at the hill's southwestern edge. Viewing platforms on the hill offer panoramic views over the Old Town and the Gulf of Finland.
