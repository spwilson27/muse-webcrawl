Ergodic theory

FieldMathematics (dynamical systems, probability)
Key resultBirkhoff's ergodic theorem (1931)
FoundersLudwig Boltzmann, George David Birkhoff, John von Neumann, Andrey Kolmogorov
A trajectory in an ergodic system eventually visits every region of its phase space.

Ergodic theory is a branch of mathematics that studies statistical properties of deterministic dynamical systems. In a broad sense it asks whether the long-run time average of an observable along a typical trajectory equals its average over the whole space of states. Systems with this property are called ergodic.

History

The subject grew out of statistical mechanics. Ludwig Boltzmann introduced the ergodic hypothesis in the 1870s while attempting to justify the use of ensemble averages in thermodynamics: he proposed that a gas system's trajectory passes through every point of its constant-energy surface. In 1931 George David Birkhoff proved the pointwise ergodic theorem, and John von Neumann proved a related mean ergodic theorem in Hilbert space, putting the subject on a firm measure-theoretic footing.

Basic concepts

The standard setup is a measure-preserving transformation T on a probability space (X, Σ, μ). The transformation is ergodic if every invariant set has measure 0 or 1. Birkhoff's theorem states that for any integrable function f, the time averages (1/n) Σ f(Tkx) converge for almost every x to the space average ∫ f dμ whenever T is ergodic.

Examples

Irrational rotations of the circle are ergodic with respect to Lebesgue measure. The doubling map on the unit interval, geodesic flows on negatively curved manifolds, and the "baker's transformation" are classical ergodic — indeed mixing — systems. Ergodicity is the first rung in a hierarchy that continues through weak mixing and strong mixing to K-systems and Bernoulli systems.

Applications

Ergodic theory underlies parts of number theory (such as proofs related to Szemerédi's theorem via Furstenberg's correspondence principle), statistical physics, information theory, and the theory of stochastic processes. See also dynamical system, measure theory, and mixing.
