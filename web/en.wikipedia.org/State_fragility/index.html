State fragility

State fragility describes the extent to which a state is vulnerable to internal or external shocks and unable to deliver core functions to its population, including security, public services, economic management, and the rule of law. Fragile states sit at one end of a spectrum whose other end is marked by resilient, well-governed states; the extreme case is the failed state.

Measurement

Fragility is commonly assessed through composite indices. The best known is the Fragile States Index (formerly the Failed States Index), published annually by the Fund for Peace in partnership with Foreign Policy, which scores countries on twelve social, economic, and political-military indicators such as demographic pressures, group grievance, state legitimacy, public services, and the security apparatus. The OECD also maintains a multidimensional fragility framework covering economic, environmental, political, security, and societal dimensions.

Causes and consequences

Drivers of fragility include armed conflict and civil war, corruption, weak institutions, poverty, inequality, rapid urbanization, and vulnerability to natural disasters and climate change. Fragile states tend to host a disproportionate share of the world's extreme poverty and displacement, and fragility can spill across borders through refugee flows, organized crime, and regional instability. International development policy increasingly treats fragility as a central focus, with aid agencies targeting the prevention of conflict and the strengthening of institutions in fragile and conflict-affected situations.
