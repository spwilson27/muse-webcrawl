Bilevel car

A bilevel car (also called a double-decker coach or double-decker train car) is a type of railway passenger car that has two levels of seating, as opposed to a single level. Bilevel cars roughly double the passenger capacity of a conventional coach without increasing train length, making them especially popular for commuter rail services where platform length and train paths are limited.

Design

Designs vary by loading gauge. In North America, where clearances are generous, cars such as the Bombardier BiLevel and the Pullman "gallery" cars used in Chicago and San Francisco place a main lower deck, an upper deck, and often mezzanine seating over the bogies, with entrances at an intermediate level. Gallery cars have an open upper level overlooking the lower aisle so conductors can collect tickets from both decks. In Europe, lower clearances lead to designs in which the lower deck is sunk between the bogies and doors are located over the bogies or at the ends; examples include the German Doppelstockwagen and double-deck stock used in France, the Netherlands, and Switzerland.

History

Double-deck passenger vehicles appeared in the 19th century, including early double-decker tramcars and French railway coaches of the 1870s. The concept matured after the Second World War as suburban traffic grew: the Chicago-area railroads introduced gallery cars in the 1950s, German railways built large fleets of bilevel coaches from the 1950s onward, and double-deck trains became a hallmark of Sydney's suburban network in Australia. India's railways and several other systems also operate double-decker express trains.

Advantages and disadvantages

Bilevel cars increase capacity per train path and per metre of platform, and can lower the cost per seat. Their drawbacks include longer dwell times at stations because passengers must climb stairs, reduced luggage and bicycle space, accessibility challenges for passengers with limited mobility, and incompatibility with routes whose tunnels and bridges lack clearance. High-speed double-deck trains, such as France's TGV Duplex, show that the concept can also work for intercity services.
