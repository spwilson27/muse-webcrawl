Hard problem of consciousness

In the philosophy of mind, the hard problem of consciousness is to explain why and how humans and other organisms have qualia, phenomenal consciousness, or subjective experiences. The term was coined by philosopher David Chalmers in 1995, contrasting it with the comparatively "easy problems" of explaining cognitive functions such as discrimination, integration of information, reportability, and the control of behaviour.

Formulation

Even if every neural and functional process associated with consciousness were fully explained, Chalmers argues, it would remain unexplained why those processes are accompanied by experience at all—why there is "something it is like" to be an organism. The problem relates to Thomas Nagel's 1974 essay "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" and to Frank Jackson's thought experiment of Mary's room, in which a scientist who knows every physical fact about colour vision still seems to learn something new when she first sees red.

Responses

Philosophers and scientists are divided. Physicalists such as Daniel Dennett and Patricia Churchland hold that the problem dissolves once the functional facts are understood, or that intuitions about qualia are misleading. Dualists and property dualists argue that experience is a fundamental feature of reality not reducible to physical processes. Others propose panpsychism, integrated information theory, or higher-order theories as routes to a solution, while "mysterians" such as Colin McGinn doubt that human cognition can solve the problem at all.

Influence

The hard problem has become a central organising concept in consciousness studies, prompting dedicated research centres and conferences. Chalmers later proposed a "meta-problem": explaining why we think there is a hard problem, which he suggests may be more tractable scientifically.
