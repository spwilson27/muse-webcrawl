Primitivism

Primitivism is a mode of aesthetic idealization that either emulates or aspires to recreate "primitive" experience. In Western art, primitivism typically has borrowed motifs from non-Western or prehistoric peoples that were perceived as "primitive", such as Paul Gauguin's inclusion of Tahitian motifs in paintings and ceramics.

In art

Primitivism in modern art emerged in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, influencing movements such as Fauvism, Cubism, and Expressionism. Artists including Pablo Picasso, Henri Matisse, and the German group Die Brücke drew inspiration from African, Oceanic, and Native American art, which they encountered in ethnographic museums and colonial exhibitions.

In philosophy and politics

Anarcho-primitivism is a political ideology that advocates a return to pre-industrial ways of life. Thinkers associated with primitivist ideas include Jean-Jacques Rousseau, whose concept of the "noble savage" idealized life in a state of nature, and later writers such as John Zerzan.

Criticism

Critics argue that primitivism often exoticizes non-Western cultures and rests on colonial assumptions about "primitive" peoples, projecting Western fantasies onto them rather than engaging with their actual traditions.
