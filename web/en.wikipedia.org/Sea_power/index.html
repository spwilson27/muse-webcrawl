Sea power

Sea power, also spelled seapower, is a nation's ability to project power by naval and maritime means, encompassing its navy, merchant marine, ports, shipbuilding industry and maritime trade. A state possessing strong sea power is often called a maritime power.

Theory

The concept was systematically developed by the American naval officer Alfred Thayer Mahan in The Influence of Sea Power upon History, 1660–1783 (1890), which argued that control of maritime trade routes and victory in decisive fleet battles were key determinants of national greatness. Mahan listed geographical position, physical conformation, extent of territory, population, national character and government policy as the principal conditions affecting sea power.

Elements

Sea power includes the capacity to protect one's own shipping, deny the use of the sea to an enemy, project military force from the sea and sustain maritime commerce. Strategists distinguish between command of the sea — the ability to use a sea area for one's own purposes — and sea denial.

History

Historically great sea powers include ancient Athens, Carthage and Rome, early modern Portugal, Spain and the Netherlands, and later Britain, whose Royal Navy underpinned global trade in the 19th century, followed by the United States in the 20th.
