Sustainable design

Sustainable design (also called environmental design or environmentally conscious design) is the philosophy of designing physical objects, the built environment and services to comply with the principles of ecological sustainability and to improve the health and comfort of occupants. Its objectives include reducing consumption of non-renewable resources, minimising waste, and creating healthy, productive environments.

Principles

Widely cited frameworks include the Hannover Principles, articulated by William McDonough and Michael Braungart in 1992, which call for eliminating the concept of waste, relying on natural energy flows, and respecting the interdependence of design with nature. Life-cycle assessment is used to evaluate the environmental impact of a product or building from raw-material extraction through manufacture, use and disposal. The cradle-to-cradle approach advocates designing products whose materials circulate in closed biological or technical cycles.

Applications

In architecture, sustainable design includes passive heating and cooling, daylighting, efficient insulation, renewable energy systems, water conservation and the use of recycled or low-impact materials; rating systems such as LEED and BREEAM certify performance. In product design it informs eco-design regulations on energy efficiency, repairability and recyclability. Sustainable urban design addresses compact development, public transport, green infrastructure and mixed land use.

Criticism

Critics point to "greenwashing" — marketing claims of sustainability without substantive change — and to trade-offs between environmental goals and affordability or function. Some argue that efficiency gains can be offset by rebound effects, in which lower operating costs encourage greater consumption.
