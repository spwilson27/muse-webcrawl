Hairy ball theorem

The hairy ball theorem of algebraic topology states that there is no nonvanishing continuous tangent vector field on even-dimensional n-spheres. For the ordinary sphere (the 2-sphere), the theorem says that if every point of a sphere is assigned a tangent vector varying continuously over the sphere, then at least one point must be assigned the zero vector.

The theorem takes its informal name from the image of trying to comb flat the hair on a hairy ball: any attempt leaves at least one tuft standing up, or a cowlick, at the point where the vector vanishes.

Statement and history

More precisely, for every continuous tangent vector field on the sphere S2 there exists at least one point where the field is zero. The result follows from the fact that the Euler characteristic of the 2-sphere is 2, not 0. Henri Poincaré proved results in this direction in 1885 for a class of fields, and the general theorem was proved by Heinz Hopf in 1926 in connection with his work on the Euler characteristic and vector fields. L. E. J. Brouwer also proved the case of the sphere in 1912.

Consequences and applications

An often-cited meteorological corollary is that, given wind as a continuous tangent vector field on the Earth's surface, there is always at least one point where the wind is perfectly calm (the centre of a cyclone or anticyclone). The theorem also shows that the 2-sphere is not parallelizable, in contrast to the circle S1 and the torus, which admit nonvanishing fields because their Euler characteristic is 0.

In computer graphics, the theorem implies that a continuous function assigning a direction in a tangent plane to every point of a sphere cannot exist, which affects algorithms for texture mapping and orientation fields.
