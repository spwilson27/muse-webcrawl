Tyrian purple

Tyrian purple dye and the murex snails from which it was extracted.

Tyrian purple (Ancient Greek: πορφύρα, porphýra; Latin: purpura), also known as royal purple, imperial purple, or imperial dye, is a dye extracted from the sea snails of the family Muricidae, principally Bolinus brandaris, Hexaplex trunculus, and Stramonita haemastoma. It was produced by the Phoenicians of the ancient city of Tyre (in modern Lebanon), from which it takes its name, and by other peoples around the Mediterranean and beyond.

The dye was extraordinarily expensive: thousands of snails were required to produce a single gram of dye, and the process involved a foul-smelling fermentation of the snails' hypobranchial gland secretions. The colour, a reddish-purple that becomes brighter with exposure to sunlight, does not fade appreciably and was prized for prestige garments in the ancient world.

History

Production of purple dye may date to the Minoans of Crete in the second millennium BCE, but it was the Phoenician cities — above all Tyre and Sidon — that industrialised it and traded purple cloth throughout the Mediterranean. Enormous shell middens still mark ancient production sites. According to legend, the dye was discovered by Heracles (or his dog), whose mouth was stained purple after eating a snail on the beach.

In classical antiquity the dye became a status symbol of the highest order. Alexander the Great and the kings of the Hellenistic world wore it; in the Roman Republic, senators wore a Tyrian purple stripe on their togas, and generals celebrating triumphs wore purple-and-gold tunics. Under the Empire, purple became increasingly reserved to the emperor; in Byzantium the association was so strong that children born to reigning emperors were called porphyrogennetos — "born in the purple" — after the purple-draped chamber of the imperial palace. Sumptuary laws in several periods restricted its use.

Production

The pigment's main chemical constituent is 6,6′-dibromoindigo, closely related to indigo. The snails were collected in baited traps, the glands extracted and fermented in vats with salt for several days, then heated in a slow process described by Pliny the Elder in his Natural History. Wool was dipped multiple times to achieve the desired depth of colour. Estimates suggest that tens of thousands of snails were needed to dye a single garment, which explains accounts of the dye being worth more than its weight in silver or gold.

Decline and rediscovery

Production contracted with the fall of Constantinople in 1453, and the art was largely lost as cheaper purple dyes and later synthetic dyes became available. In 1856 William Henry Perkin accidentally synthesised mauveine, the first synthetic dye, partly inspired by the search for affordable purples. The chemical structure of Tyrian purple was identified by Paul Friedländer in 1909, after processing thousands of snails to obtain a small sample of dye. Small-scale artisanal production has been revived in a few places, and the dye remains a potent symbol of royalty and rarity in language — "born to the purple" and "purple prose" among them.

Other purple-producing snails

Independent purple-dye industries existed in pre-Columbian Mexico and Central America, where Plicopurpura pansa snails were "milked" non-lethally for dye used in traditional textiles, a practice that survives in Oaxaca. Similar dyes were produced in East Asia from other muricid species.
