Chuppah

A chuppah (also spelled chupah or huppah; Hebrew: חופה) is a canopy under which a Jewish couple stands during their wedding ceremony. It consists of a cloth or sheet, sometimes a tallit, stretched or supported over four poles, or sometimes manually held up by attendants to the ceremony.

Symbolism

The chuppah symbolizes the home that the couple will build together. Its open sides are said to recall the tent of Abraham, open to guests on all four sides in hospitality, and the lack of a top covering in some traditions signifies that the home's foundation is faith. The cloth canopy has biblical associations: the word appears in the Hebrew Bible in contexts of the bridal chamber and divine protection, as in Psalm 19 and Joel 2.

History

In the Talmudic period, the chuppah referred to the bridal chamber where the marriage was consummated rather than a canopy. By the Middle Ages in Ashkenazi communities, the ceremony itself came to be performed under a canopy, and the term transferred to it. Sephardic and other communities adopted varying forms, including the use of a tallit held over the couple.

Customs

The couple enters the chuppah at the beginning of the ceremony, often accompanied by their parents. Many couples use a family heirloom cloth, a tallit of a parent or grandparent, or a canopy decorated with flowers. In modern ceremonies the poles may be fixed in a frame or held by close friends and family members.
