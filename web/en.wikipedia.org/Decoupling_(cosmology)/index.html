Decoupling (cosmology)


The cosmic microwave background, released at photon decoupling

In cosmology, decoupling refers to the period in the early universe when types of particles fell out of thermal equilibrium with one another as the universe expanded and cooled. Interaction rates dropped below the expansion rate of the universe, so the particles thereafter evolved independently.

Photon decoupling

The most famous decoupling is that of photons, which occurred around 380,000 years after the Big Bang, at a redshift of about 1100, when the universe cooled enough for free electrons and protons to combine into neutral hydrogen in the era known as recombination. The universe became transparent, and the released radiation is observed today as the cosmic microwave background.

Neutrino decoupling

Neutrinos decoupled much earlier, about one second after the Big Bang, when weak interactions became too slow to keep them in equilibrium. The resulting cosmic neutrino background has not yet been directly detected but its existence is inferred from its effects on the cosmic microwave background and on the abundances of light elements formed in Big Bang nucleosynthesis.
