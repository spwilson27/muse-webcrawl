Curry–Howard correspondence

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In programming language theory and proof theory, the Curry–Howard correspondence (also known as the Curry–Howard isomorphism or equivalence, or the proofs-as-programs and propositions- or formulae-as-types interpretation) is the direct relationship between computer programs and mathematical proofs. It is a generalisation of a syntactic analogy first noticed by Haskell Curry and William Alvin Howard.

The correspondence

In general, the correspondence states that a formal proof of a proposition can be represented as a program whose type is the proposition, and that checking the proof corresponds to type-checking the program:

Under the correspondence, the simply typed lambda calculus corresponds to intuitionistic propositional logic; extending the picture to dependent types yields the calculus of constructions, where types can express arbitrary first-order and higher-order statements.

History and impact

Curry observed in the 1930s that the types of combinators resembled the axioms of intuitionistic implication; Howard made the correspondence for natural deduction explicit in a 1969 manuscript published in 1980. The idea underlies modern proof assistants such as Coq, Agda and Lean, in which proving a theorem and writing a well-typed program are literally the same activity, and programs can be extracted from constructive proofs.
