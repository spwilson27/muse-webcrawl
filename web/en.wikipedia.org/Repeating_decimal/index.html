Repeating decimal

A repeating decimal or recurring decimal is a decimal representation of a number whose digits are periodic (repeating its values at regular intervals) and the infinitely repeated portion is not zero. Examples include 1/3 = 0.333…, 1/7 = 0.142857142857…, and 3227/555 = 5.8144144144…. The repeating block is called the repetend and is often written with a vinculum (overline) or dots above the digits.

Which fractions repeat?

A rational number in lowest terms p/q has a terminating decimal exactly when q has no prime factors other than 2 and 5. Otherwise its decimal repeats, with a pre-period related to the factors 2 and 5 and a period equal to the multiplicative order of 10 modulo the remaining part of q. Fractions with prime denominator p (not 2 or 5) have periods dividing p − 1; denominators achieving the full period p − 1, such as 7, 17, 19, and 23, are called full reptend primes.

Converting to fractions

Every repeating decimal represents a rational number. To convert 0.142857142857…, multiply by 106 (the period length), subtract the original, and solve: 999999x = 142857, so x = 142857/999999 = 1/7. A classic consequence is 0.999… = 1.

General properties

Repeating expansions occur in any positional base, with the role of 10 played by the base. In binary, for example, 1/3 = 0.010101… Irrational numbers have decimal expansions that neither terminate nor repeat.
