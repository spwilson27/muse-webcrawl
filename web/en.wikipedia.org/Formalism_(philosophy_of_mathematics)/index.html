Formalism (philosophy of mathematics)

Formalism is a school of thought in the philosophy of mathematics holding that the statements of mathematics can be regarded as statements about the rules of manipulating symbols—like the moves in a game played with meaningless marks—rather than as descriptions of abstract objects or truths about an independent mathematical realm.

Hilbert's programme

The most influential version was David Hilbert's "formalism" of the 1920s, which sought to secure mathematics by encoding theories as formal systems of axioms and proving their consistency by finitary means acceptable to intuitionists—an effort known as Hilbert's programme. Kurt Gödel's incompleteness theorems (1931) showed that any sufficiently strong consistent formal system cannot prove its own consistency, undermining the original programme, though Hilbert's methods founded modern proof theory.

Varieties

Modern formalists differ in strength: "game formalism" treats mathematics as symbol manipulation without meaning; other versions grant symbols meaning from their use within a system. Formalism contrasts with Platonism (mathematical realism), which takes mathematical objects to exist independently, and with intuitionism, which grounds mathematics in mental construction.
