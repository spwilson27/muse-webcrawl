Vestibulo–ocular reflex

The vestibulo–ocular reflex (VOR) is a reflex that stabilises images on the retina during head movement by producing eye movements in the direction opposite to the head movement, thus preserving the image on the centre of the visual field. For example, when the head moves to the right, the eyes move to the left, keeping gaze fixed in space. The reflex is driven by the vestibular system of the inner ear and is among the fastest reflexes in the body, with a latency of only about 7–10 milliseconds.

Mechanism

Rotation of the head is sensed by the semicircular canals; linear acceleration is sensed by the otolith organs (utricle and saccule). Signals travel via the vestibular nerve to the vestibular nuclei in the brainstem, and from there through a short three-neuron arc to the motor nuclei of the extraocular muscles (cranial nerves III, IV and VI), largely via the medial longitudinal fasciculus. A compensatory slow movement of the eyes is periodically reset by a fast movement in the opposite direction, producing the pattern known as nystagmus.

Gain and calibration

The "gain" of the VOR is the ratio of eye velocity to head velocity; ideally it is close to 1.0. The cerebellum, especially the flocculus, continuously calibrates the gain using visual error signals (retinal slip). Wearing magnifying or reversing spectacles can drive adaptive changes in gain over days — a classic model system for motor learning.

Clinical significance

Loss of the VOR, for example from bilateral vestibular damage due to ototoxic drugs such as gentamicin, causes oscillopsia: the visual world appears to bounce with each step, and reading while walking becomes difficult. The head impulse test, in which the examiner rapidly rotates the patient's head while watching for a corrective saccade, is a standard bedside test of VOR function. The reflex is also used in assessing brainstem function in coma, via the oculocephalic ("doll's head") manoeuvre and caloric testing.
