Polymer science

Polymer science (or macromolecular science) is the interdisciplinary study of polymers — large molecules (macromolecules) composed of repeating subunits called monomers. It spans chemistry, physics, and engineering, addressing the synthesis, structure, properties, characterization, and applications of polymeric materials such as plastics, rubbers, fibers, and biological macromolecules.

A polymer chain consists of many repeating monomer units.

History

Natural polymers — rubber, cellulose, silk, and proteins — were used for millennia, and nineteenth-century chemists modified them into materials like vulcanized rubber (Goodyear, 1839) and celluloid. The modern field began when Hermann Staudinger proposed in the 1920s that polymers are long covalent chains, a once-controversial idea for which he received the 1953 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Wallace Carothers at DuPont developed nylon and neoprene in the 1930s; Karl Ziegler and Giulio Natta won the 1963 Nobel Prize for catalytic polymerization, and Paul Flory (1974) and Pierre-Gilles de Gennes (1991) advanced polymer physics.

Subdisciplines

Polymer chemistry studies synthesis via chain-growth and step-growth polymerization and copolymer architecture. Polymer physics examines chain conformations, the glass transition, crystallinity, viscoelasticity, and rheology. Characterization uses techniques such as gel permeation chromatography for molecular weight, NMR and infrared spectroscopy for structure, and DSC and TGA for thermal behavior. Polymer engineering applies this knowledge to processing — extrusion, injection molding, and fiber spinning — and to design of materials from commodity plastics to biopolymers and conductive polymers.
