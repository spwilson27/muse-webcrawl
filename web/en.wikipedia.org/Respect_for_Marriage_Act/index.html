The Respect for Marriage Act is a United States federal law enacted in 2022 that requires the federal government and all U.S. states to recognise the validity of marriages performed in any state, regardless of the spouses' sex, race, ethnicity or national origin. It repealed the Defense of Marriage Act (DOMA) and provided statutory protection for same-sex and interracial marriages against the possibility that the Supreme Court might revisit its rulings in Obergefell v. Hodges (2015) and Loving v. Virginia (1967).

Background

Versions of the bill had been introduced in Congress since 2009. Its passage became urgent for supporters after the Supreme Court's 2022 decision in Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization overturned Roe v. Wade, and Justice Clarence Thomas's concurrence suggested reconsidering other substantive due process precedents, including Obergefell. The bill passed the House in July 2022, then, after an amendment adding religious-liberty protections — affirming that religious organisations need not perform or celebrate marriages and cannot lose tax-exempt status for declining — passed the Senate 61–36 with bipartisan support, the House 258–169, and was signed by President Joe Biden on 13 December 2022 at a large White House ceremony.

Provisions

The Act requires every state to give full faith and credit to marriages validly performed in another state, and defines marriage for federal purposes by the law of the state where it was celebrated. It does not require states to license same-sex marriages themselves should Obergefell be overturned, nor does it protect polygamous marriages. It also codifies protections for interracial marriage, though those rest on the firmer constitutional ground of the Equal Protection Clause.

Reception

The Act was praised by LGBT advocacy groups as a crucial backstop safeguarding hundreds of thousands of existing marriages, and criticised by some religious conservatives; some supporters of full marriage equality noted its limits, since residents of a state that banned same-sex marriage would have to travel to marry elsewhere. It marked the culmination of a quarter-century reversal of federal policy from DOMA's 1996 restrictions.
