Royal forest

A royal forest, occasionally known as a king's forest, is an area of land with different definitions in England, Wales and Scotland in the Middle Ages. The term did not necessarily denote woodland: a "forest" in law was a territory placed under forest law, reserved for the monarch's hunting, and might include heath, moor, pasture and villages as well as trees.

Forest law

Introduced to England by the Norman kings, forest law protected the "venison" (game animals, principally deer) and the "vert" (the vegetation that sheltered them). Within a royal forest it was an offence to hunt deer, clear land (assart), fell timber or graze certain animals without licence; offences were punished by special courts presided over by forest justices and administered locally by wardens, verderers and agisters. At its greatest extent under Henry II, perhaps a third of England lay under forest law, a major source of resentment: the Charter of the Forest (1217), issued alongside Magna Carta, reduced penalties, disafforested areas and confirmed commoners' rights of pasture and pannage.

Surviving forests

Over the centuries forests were sold, granted away or disafforested, and timber extraction for shipbuilding transformed many. The New Forest (created by William the Conqueror about 1079) and the Forest of Dean remain the best-known examples of former royal forests retaining distinctive institutions such as verderers' courts and commoning rights. Related institutions existed elsewhere in medieval Europe, including the Danish and French royal hunting reserves.
