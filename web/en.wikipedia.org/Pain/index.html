TypesNociceptive, neuropathic, nociplastic

Pain is a distressing feeling often caused by intense or damaging stimuli. The International Association for the Study of Pain defines it as "an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience associated with, or resembling that associated with, actual or potential tissue damage." Pain motivates withdrawal from damaging situations and avoidance of similar experiences in the future.

Classification

Pain is commonly classified by mechanism. Nociceptive pain results from activation of nociceptors by noxious stimuli acting on body tissue, as in a burn or a sprained ankle; it may be somatic or visceral. Neuropathic pain arises from damage or disease of the somatosensory nervous system itself, as in diabetic neuropathy or postherpetic neuralgia. Nociplastic pain, recognized more recently, arises from altered nociception without clear tissue or nerve damage, as in fibromyalgia. Pain is also described by duration: acute pain resolves with healing, while chronic pain persists beyond three months and affects roughly a fifth of adults worldwide.

Physiology

Nociceptors — free nerve endings responding to mechanical, thermal, and chemical extremes — send signals along thinly myelinated Aδ fibres (fast, sharp pain) and unmyelinated C fibres (slow, dull pain) to the dorsal horn of the spinal cord, and from there to the thalamus and cortex. Descending pathways from the brainstem can amplify or suppress these signals, the basis of the gate control theory of pain proposed by Melzack and Wall in 1965. Measurement in clinical practice relies largely on self-report scales, such as the numeric rating scale from 0 to 10.
