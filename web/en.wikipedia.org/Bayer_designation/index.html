Bayer designation

A Bayer designation is a stellar designation in which a specific star is identified by a Greek letter (or, less commonly, a Latin letter) followed by the genitive form of its parent constellation's Latin name. The system was introduced by the German astronomer Johann Bayer in his star atlas Uranometria of 1603, and Bayer designations remain the most familiar names for the brighter stars: for example Alpha Centauri, Beta Ursae Majoris and Gamma Cassiopeiae.

Bayer's Uranometria (1603) introduced the Greek-letter system.

The system

Bayer generally assigned the letters in order of apparent brightness: alpha to the brightest star in a constellation, beta to the second, and so on through the twenty-four letters of the Greek alphabet. When these were exhausted he continued with Latin letters. To the designation is added the genitive of the constellation name, as listed by convention (e.g. Ursae Majoris, "of the Great Bear").

Exceptions

Bayer's ordering was not exact. He sometimes assigned letters by position within the constellation rather than brightness — the stars of the Plough in Ursa Major are lettered from east to west — and sometimes classed stars by magnitude bands, so that several stars of similar brightness received letters in positional order. As a result the alpha star is not always the brightest: in Orion, for instance, Rigel (Beta Orionis) outshines Betelgeuse (Alpha Orionis). Variable stars and later refinements produced further irregularities, and many faint stars have no Bayer designation at all, being known instead by Flamsteed numbers or catalogue identifiers.

Usage

Bayer designations are used throughout professional and amateur astronomy, in star atlases, and in the names of many exoplanet host stars. Bayer letters also extend to some other objects: Bayer applied letters to stars within constellations he depicted, and the system was later adopted for stars in constellations delineated after 1603, including the southern constellations charted by later astronomers.
