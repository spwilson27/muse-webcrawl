The First Council of Nicaea was a council of Christian bishops convened by the Roman Emperor Constantine I in Nicaea, Bithynia, in AD 325 — the first ecumenical council of the Christian Church. Its principal achievement was the settlement of the Arian controversy over the nature of Christ, expressed in the original Nicene Creed: the council affirmed that Jesus, the Son of God, is "of one substance" (homoousios) with the Father, and condemned the teaching of Arius that the Son was a created being.

Background

Arius, a presbyter of Alexandria, taught that the Father alone is truly eternal God and that the Son was created "out of nothing" — "there was when he was not". His bishop, Alexander, condemned the doctrine, and the dispute spread through the eastern churches. Constantine, fresh from unifying the empire, wanted a unified church and summoned bishops at imperial expense; some 300, mostly from the Greek-speaking East, attended, among them the young deacon Athanasius, later Arius's great opponent, and the historians Eusebius of Caesarea and Eusebius of Nicomedia.

Decisions

The council issued a creed — the ancestor of the Nicene Creed still recited in churches — with anathemas against Arian propositions. It also produced twenty canons on church order, settled the Meletian schism, and fixed the date of Easter independently of the Jewish calendar, to be calculated on the first Sunday after the first full moon on or after the spring equinox. Arius and two dissenting bishops were exiled.

Aftermath

The settlement did not end the controversy: Arian and semi-Arian factions dominated imperial policy for much of the fourth century, Athanasius was exiled five times, and the Nicene position was only firmly restored at the Council of Constantinople in 381. Nicaea nonetheless set the pattern of emperor-convoked ecumenical councils and remains a touchstone of orthodox Christology for Catholic, Orthodox and most Protestant churches.
