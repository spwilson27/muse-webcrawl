Galvanic anode

Other namesSacrificial anode
FunctionCathodic protection
Common materialsZinc, magnesium, aluminium alloys
ApplicationsShip hulls, pipelines, water heaters, offshore structures

A galvanic anode, or sacrificial anode, is the main component of a galvanic cathodic protection system used to protect buried or submerged metal structures from corrosion. It is made from a metal alloy with a more "active" (more negative) reduction potential than the metal of the structure it protects. The difference in potential causes the anode material to corrode preferentially — to be "sacrificed" — while the structure remains protected.

How it works

When two dissimilar metals are electrically connected in an electrolyte such as seawater or moist soil, the more active metal becomes the anode of a galvanic cell and oxidizes, driving the protected structure to act as the cathode, where corrosion is suppressed. The anode is consumed over time and must be sized to last the design life of the installation, or replaced periodically.

Materials

Zinc anodes are standard for seawater service; magnesium anodes, with higher driving voltage, suit buried pipelines and domestic water heater tanks; aluminium alloy anodes activated with indium or zinc are widely used on offshore platforms. In water heaters, a magnesium or aluminium "anode rod" extends tank life by corroding in place of the steel lining.
