Shinran (親鸞, 1173–1263) was a Japanese Buddhist monk of the Kamakura period and the founder of Jōdo Shinshū ("True Pure Land"), today the largest school of Buddhism in Japan. A disciple of Hōnen, Shinran radicalised Pure Land teaching: salvation comes not from any practice or merit of one's own ("self power") but solely from the "other power" (tariki) of Amida Buddha's Primal Vow, entrusted through sincere faith and expressed in the recitation of the nembutsu, "Namu Amida Butsu".

Life

Born Matsuwaka-maro of the Hino family in 1173, Shinran entered the Tendai monastery on Mount Hiei at age nine and spent twenty years in study and ascetic practice without attaining certainty of liberation. In 1201 he descended the mountain to study with Hōnen, who taught exclusive reliance on the nembutsu. In 1207 the nembutsu movement was suppressed: Hōnen was exiled and Shinran, defrocked and given the secular name Fujii Yoshizane, was banished to Echigo on the Sea of Japan coast. There he married Eshinni — an extraordinary step for a monk — and began calling himself "neither monk nor layman", the "foolish, bald-headed one" (gutoku). Pardoned in 1211, he moved to the Kantō region, where he preached to peasants, fishermen, and warriors for two decades, then retired to Kyoto, where he completed his masterwork, the Kyōgyōshinshō, and died in 1263 at the age of 89, attended by his daughter Kakushinni.

Teaching

Shinran taught that in the age of mappō (the Dharma's decline) human beings are incapable of the practices that lead to enlightenment; even the desire to recite the nembutsu must be given by Amida. Faith (shinjin) is therefore not a human achievement but Amida's own mind awakened in the devotee, guaranteeing birth in the Pure Land at death — indeed, in the moment of true entrusting. His teaching that "even the good person is born in the Pure Land, how much more the evil person" (akunin shōki) scandalised contemporaries and became the hallmark of his thought. His devotional hymns (wasan) and the letters collected by his followers made his ideas accessible beyond the clergy.

Legacy

Kakushinni established Shinran's mausoleum, which grew into the Hongan-ji temple organisation; under the eighth monshu Rennyo (1415–1499), Jōdo Shinshū expanded into the most widespread Buddhist movement in Japan, centred on the Nishi and Higashi Hongan-ji temples in Kyoto. Shinran's uncompromising doctrine of grace has often been compared with Protestant ideas of justification by faith, and he remains one of the most studied figures in Japanese religious history.
