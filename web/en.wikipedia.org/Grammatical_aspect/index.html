Grammatical aspect

Aspect is a grammatical category that expresses how an action, event, or state, denoted by a verb, extends over time. Whereas tense locates an event in time relative to the moment of speaking, aspect concerns the event's internal temporal structure — whether it is viewed as completed, ongoing, repeated, or bounded. A language may mark aspect through verb morphology, auxiliary verbs, or particles.

Perfective and imperfective

The most fundamental aspectual contrast is between the perfective, which views an event as a single, bounded whole from the outside, and the imperfective, which views it from within, as ongoing or unbounded. This distinction is grammaticalised in Slavic languages, where most verbs exist in perfective/imperfective pairs, and in Ancient Greek and Sanskrit verb systems. The imperfective subdivides into the progressive (an event in progress, as in English "I was reading") and the habitual (events recurring regularly).

Aspect in English

English marks aspect mainly with auxiliary constructions: the progressive ("is going"), the perfect ("has gone"), and combinations of the two ("has been going"). The English perfect is often analysed as an aspect rather than a tense, since it relates a prior event to a later reference point. Habitual aspect appears in "used to go" and, in African American Vernacular English, in invariant "be" ("he be working").

Aspect versus Aktionsart

Linguists distinguish grammatical aspect — the speaker's chosen viewpoint — from Aktionsart or lexical aspect, the inherent temporal character of the verb's meaning itself: states ("know"), activities ("run"), accomplishments ("build a house"), and achievements ("arrive"), in Zeno Vendler's influential classification. The interaction of the two determines how aspectual markers can apply.
