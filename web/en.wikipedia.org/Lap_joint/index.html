Lap joint

A lap joint or overlap joint is a joint in which the members overlap one another. Lap joints may be used in wood, plastic, or metal. A lap joint may be a full lap or half lap. In a full lap, no material is removed from either of the members to be joined, resulting in a joint whose combined thickness is the sum of the thicknesses of the two members. In a half lap joint (also known as a halving joint), material is removed from both members so that the resulting joint is the thickness of the thickest member.

Half lap joints in woodworking

Half lap joints are among the most basic and widely used woodworking joints. Material is removed from each piece — typically half its thickness — so that the two pieces sit flush when overlapped and fastened with glue, screws, nails, or dowels. Variants include:

  • End lap (corner lap) — the basic form used in frame construction, where two members meet at their ends at right angles.
  • Cross lap — the two pieces cross in the middle rather than at the ends.
  • Tee lap — one member ends at the middle of another; the end of the horizontal member is notched into the vertical member.
  • Dovetail lap — the notch is cut at an angle resembling a dovetail, giving mechanical resistance to pulling apart.
  • Mitered half lap — combines a mitered corner appearance with a half lap for strength.

Applications in metal and engineering

In metalworking and structural engineering, lap joints are commonly formed by welding, riveting, bolting, or adhesive bonding of overlapping plates or sheets. In welded construction, a lap joint is filled with a fillet weld along the edge of the overlapping member. In aviation and sheet-metal work, riveted lap joints join skin panels; engineers must account for stress concentrations and for "pillowing" and corrosion between the sheets. In electronics and electrical work, a lap joint (or lap splice) may describe conductors twisted or soldered in parallel overlap.

Strength

The strength of a glued half lap joint depends largely on the quality of the mating surfaces and the long-grain glue area; a well-made half lap is considerably stronger than a simple butt joint but weaker than a mortise and tenon of comparable size. In metal lap joints, loads are transferred in shear across the fasteners or weld, and eccentric loading can introduce bending that designers must consider.
