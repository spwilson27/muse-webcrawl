Fluorodeoxyglucose (18F)

[18F]Fluorodeoxyglucose, usually abbreviated as 18F-FDG, is a radiopharmaceutical and glucose analogue in which the hydroxyl group on the 2-position carbon of glucose is replaced by the positron-emitting radionuclide fluorine-18. It is the most commonly used imaging agent in positron emission tomography (PET), particularly in oncology.

Mechanism

Like glucose, FDG is taken up into cells by glucose transporters and phosphorylated by hexokinase to FDG-6-phosphate. Unlike glucose-6-phosphate, it cannot be processed further through glycolysis and, having been deprotonated, remains trapped in the cell. Tissues with high glucose metabolism — including most malignant tumours, the brain and the myocardium — therefore accumulate FDG, and the annihilation radiation from fluorine-18 decay (half-life about 110 minutes) reveals their distribution in a PET scan.

Medical uses

FDG PET/CT is widely used for detecting cancers, staging disease, distinguishing malignant from benign masses, assessing response to chemotherapy, and detecting recurrence. In cardiology it assesses myocardial viability; in neurology it is used in the evaluation of dementia and epilepsy. Because fluorine-18 has a half-life of only about 110 minutes, FDG must be produced in a cyclotron near the point of use and delivered under tight time constraints.

History

FDG was first synthesized in the 1970s, with the fluorine-18 compound developed by Tatsuo Ido and colleagues at Brookhaven National Laboratory in 1976; the first administration to human volunteers took place at the University of Pennsylvania, and PET imaging with FDG was pioneered by groups including Abass Alavi and Michael Reivich. It received FDA approval in the 1990s and became the workhorse tracer that made clinical PET practical.
