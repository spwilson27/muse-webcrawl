The Thracians were an Indo-European speaking people who inhabited a large area of Southeast Europe, centred on the region of Thrace in present-day Bulgaria, northeastern Greece and European Turkey. Herodotus described them as the most numerous people in the world after the Indians, potentially the most powerful if united.

History

Thracian tribes appear in Homer's Iliad as allies of Troy. For much of their history they were divided among many tribes, including the Odrysae, Getae, Triballi and Bessi. In the 5th century BC the Odrysian kingdom unified much of Thrace under kings such as Teres and Sitalces. The region was conquered by Philip II of Macedon in the 4th century BC, later ruled by Lysimachus and by Celtic invaders, before Rome annexed it as a province in AD 46 under Claudius.

Culture

The Thracians were famed as warriors and mercenaries, serving in Greek, Persians' and Roman armies; the light-armed peltast was a characteristic Thracian soldier. In Greek religion, figures such as Orpheus, Dionysus and the goddess Bendis were associated with Thrace. Thracian craftsmen produced spectacular gold and silver work, exemplified by the Panagyurishte and Rogozen treasures.

Legacy

Romanised Thracians rose to prominence in the empire; the emperor Maximinus Thrax was of Thracian origin, and the gladiator Spartacus was said to be Thracian. The Thracian language died out by late antiquity, leaving few inscriptions and glosses, but Thracian place names and personal names survive across the southern Balkans.
