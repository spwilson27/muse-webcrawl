Duchy of Brabant

Map of the Duchy of Brabant around 1350 (placeholder image)

The Duchy of Brabant was a state of the Holy Roman Empire established in 1183. It was a traditionally rich and politically important duchy located in the heart of the Low Countries, divided today between the Netherlands and Belgium, with its capitals at Leuven and later Brussels.

History

The duchy grew out of the landgraviate of Brabant, held by the counts of Leuven. In 1183–1184 Emperor Frederick Barbarossa formally created the duchy for Henry I. The dukes expanded their territory, acquiring Limburg by inheritance in 1283 after the Battle of Worringen. Brabant's cities — Leuven, Brussels, Antwerp, 's-Hertogenbosch — prospered on the cloth trade and river commerce, and the duchy was famous for its chartered liberties, notably the Joyous Entry of 1356, a constitutional charter sworn by each new duke.

Burgundian and Habsburg periods

In 1430 the duchy passed to Philip the Good of Burgundy. Under Burgundian and later Habsburg rule, Brabant became part of the Seventeen Provinces. During the Dutch Revolt, the duchy was split: the northern part (States Brabant) came under the Dutch Republic while the south remained Spanish, a division confirmed by the Peace of Westphalia in 1648 and reflected in today's provinces of North Brabant (Netherlands) and Flemish/Walloon Brabant (Belgium).

Legacy

The name survives in three modern provinces and in the title of the heir to the Belgian throne, who is styled Duke or Duchess of Brabant.
