Bronze Age Britain is the period of British history spanning roughly 2500 to 800 BC, between the Neolithic and the Iron Age. It began with the arrival of the Beaker culture from continental Europe, which brought metalworking, individual burials and new pottery styles, and ended with the gradual adoption of iron.

Early Bronze Age

Around 2500 BC, Beaker-using groups reached Britain. Genetic studies indicate substantial population replacement over the following centuries. Copper and gold were worked first, then bronze — an alloy of copper and tin, for which Cornwall became an important tin source. The rich burials of the Wessex culture, such as the Bush Barrow grave near Stonehenge, contained gold ornaments, daggers and amber, indicating trade links with Europe. Monument building continued: the later phases of Stonehenge and Avebury belong to this period.

Middle and Late Bronze Age

From around 1500 BC, round barrow burials gave way to cremation cemeteries associated with the Deverel–Rimbury culture. Settlement evidence grows richer, with roundhouses, field systems and enclosures. Great quantities of bronze tools and weapons were deposited in hoards and in rivers, a practice interpreted as votive offering. The Dover Bronze Age boat, dating to about 1550 BC, shows sophisticated sewn-plank boatbuilding and cross-Channel trade.

End of the period

Ironworking reached Britain by about 800 BC, and bronze was gradually displaced for tools and weapons, although it continued in use for ornaments and vessels. The transition marks the start of the Iron Age.
