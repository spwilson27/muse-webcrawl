The Séré de Rivières system was an ensemble of fortifications built across France in the late 19th century, after the Franco-Prussian War of 1870–71. Named for their designer, General Raymond Adolphe Séré de Rivières, the works replaced the old bastioned city walls with rings of detached polygonal forts placed several kilometres outside the cities they protected, out of range of contemporary artillery.

Design and construction

Following the loss of Alsace-Lorraine, France fortified its new eastern frontier with four great entrenched camps — Verdun, Toul, Épinal and Belfort — linked by a curtain of forts along the Hauts de Meuse and the Moselle, as well as defences for Paris, Lille, Maubeuge, and Alpine and coastal positions. The forts, initially of masonry with earth cover, used caponiers and moats defended by flanking fire. From 1885, the "torpedo shell crisis" — the advent of high-explosive melinite shells — forced costly modernisation with special concrete, steel turrets (including retractable Mougin and Bussière turrets) and Bourges casemates.

First World War

Several forts fell quickly in 1914 (Liège-style attacks hit Maubeuge, and Fort de Manonviller was smashed by German 420 mm howitzers), leading France to strip many forts of guns in 1915. The folly of that policy was exposed at Verdun in 1916, where Fort Douaumont — the largest of the Verdun ring — fell almost undefended in February but the remaining forts, above all Fort Vaux, mounted famous resistance. Lessons from the system informed the Maginot Line built in the 1930s.
