Epistolary novel

An epistolary novel is told through letters and other documents

An epistolary novel is a novel written as a series of letters. The term is derived from the Latin word epistola, meaning a letter. The form may also include diary entries, newspaper clippings, and other documents. Epistolary fiction allows the author to present the story from multiple viewpoints and to reproduce the immediacy of events being recorded as they happen, without the mediation of an omniscient narrator.

History

The epistolary novel rose to prominence in the 18th century. Samuel Richardson's Pamela (1740) and Clarissa (1748) established the popularity of the form in English. Other notable examples include Montesquieu's Persian Letters (1721), Tobias Smollett's The Expedition of Humphry Clinker (1771), Johann Wolfgang von Goethe's The Sorrows of Young Werther (1774), Pierre Choderlos de Laclos's Les Liaisons dangereuses (1782), and Mary Shelley's Frankenstein (1818), which is framed by letters from Captain Robert Walton to his sister.

Characteristics

Letters allow the writer to develop character through voice and to create dramatic irony, since characters record their experiences without knowledge of later events. Novels written entirely as monologic letters (from one character) differ from dialogic exchanges between two or more correspondents, and from novels in which letters play only a part. The form declined in the 19th century as critics came to regard it as contrived, but it has been revived in modern works using diaries, emails, transcripts, and other documents — examples include Bram Stoker's Dracula (1897), Alice Walker's The Color Purple (1982), and Stephen Chbosky's The Perks of Being a Wallflower (1999).
