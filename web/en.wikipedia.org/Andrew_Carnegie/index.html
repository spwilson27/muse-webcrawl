Andrew Carnegie

Andrew Carnegie (November 25, 1835 – August 11, 1919) was a Scottish-American industrialist and philanthropist. He led the enormous expansion of the American steel industry in the late 19th century and is often identified as one of the richest Americans of all time. In 1901 he sold the Carnegie Steel Company to J. P. Morgan for $303,450,000, forming the basis of the U.S. Steel Corporation. Carnegie devoted the remainder of his life to large-scale philanthropy, donating about $350 million (roughly 90% of his fortune) to charities, foundations and universities, and funding the construction of more than 2,500 public libraries worldwide.

Early life

Carnegie was born in Dunfermline, Scotland, to a handloom weaver father and a mother who helped support the family by binding shoes. As mechanisation destroyed the handloom trade, the family emigrated to Allegheny, Pennsylvania, in 1848 when Andrew was twelve. He worked first as a bobbin boy in a cotton factory for $1.20 a week, then as a telegraph messenger boy — a position in which he taught himself to translate telegraph signals by sound — and rose to become a telegraph operator and then superintendent of the Pittsburgh division of the Pennsylvania Railroad under Thomas A. Scott.

Business career

Using savings and borrowed money, Carnegie invested shrewdly in railroad sleeping cars, oil wells and iron works during and after the American Civil War, in which he served organising military transportation. In 1873 he opened his first steel plant, the Edgar Thomson Steel Works near Pittsburgh, taking advantage of the new Bessemer process and relentless cost accounting to undercut competitors. His firms were consolidated in 1892 as the Carnegie Steel Company, by then the largest and most profitable steel enterprise in the world. His hard-driving management brought labour conflict, most notoriously the Homestead Strike of 1892, which damaged his reputation.

Philanthropy

In his 1889 essay "The Gospel of Wealth", Carnegie argued that the rich have a moral obligation to distribute their surplus wealth for the public good during their lifetimes. He funded 2,509 Carnegie libraries worldwide, founded the Carnegie Institute of Technology (now part of Carnegie Mellon University), the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace, the Carnegie Foundation for the Advancement of Teaching, and built Carnegie Hall in New York City (1891). He also endowed the Peace Palace at The Hague and established "hero funds" honouring civilians who performed acts of bravery.

Death and legacy

Carnegie died of bronchial pneumonia in 1919 at his estate in Lenox, Massachusetts, and is buried in Sleepy Hollow Cemetery, New York. He is remembered both as a titan of American industry and as the father of modern philanthropy; his doctrine that "the man who dies thus rich dies disgraced" influenced later benefactors including Bill Gates and Warren Buffett.
