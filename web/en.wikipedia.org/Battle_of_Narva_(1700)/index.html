The Battle of Narva on 30 November 1700 was an early battle of the Great Northern War. A Russian army of some 35,000–40,000 men had besieged the Swedish fortress town of Narva in Estonia. The eighteen-year-old King Charles XII, having first knocked Denmark out of the war at Humlebæk, landed in Estonia with a relief army and, attacking in a blinding snowstorm with only about 8,000–10,000 men, utterly routed the much larger Russian force in one of the most lopsided victories of the century.

Background

Russia, Saxony-Poland and Denmark-Norway had formed a coalition to partition Sweden's Baltic empire, expecting an easy contest with the young king. Tsar Peter I declared war in August 1700 and laid siege to Narva, whose garrison under Henning Rudolf Horn numbered only about 1,800 men. Peter left the army shortly before the battle to meet reinforcements, leaving command to the Saxon field marshal Charles Eugène, Duke de Croÿ, whose authority the Russian generals barely acknowledged.

The battle

On 30 November the Swedish army approached the Russian siege lines — an extensive circumvallation of earthworks studded with artillery — from the west. At about two o'clock in the afternoon a violent snowstorm began blowing directly into the Russians' faces, and under its cover the Swedes stormed the works in two columns. Fighting with characteristic aggression at close quarters ("Gå på" tactics — marching through fire to deliver a single volley and charge with cold steel), the Swedes broke into the camp; the Russian line disintegrated, regiments panicked, and thousands drowned when a bridge over the Narva River collapsed under fleeing troops. De Croÿ and many senior officers surrendered to the Swedes to avoid being killed by their own mutinous soldiers. Only the Guards regiments (Preobrazhensky and Semyonovsky) and some units on the flank withdrew in order.

Aftermath

The Russians lost nearly all their artillery and siege equipment and perhaps 8,000–18,000 men killed, drowned or captured; most prisoners were released for lack of means to feed them. Swedish losses were under 2,000. Europe, which had dismissed Charles as a boy, was stunned. Peter, however, treated the disaster as a lesson: he rebuilt his army on modern lines over the following years, while Charles turned south against Poland-Saxony — a decision that gave Russia time to recover and to conquer Ingria and found Saint Petersburg. The two kings met again at Poltava in 1709, with a very different outcome. Narva itself fell to the Russians in 1704.
