Rabbit-proof fence

State Barrier Fence of Western Australia
Original name: Rabbit-Proof Fence
Built: 1901–1907
Total length: 3,256 km (three fences)
Purpose: Pest-exclusion barrier

The rabbit-proof fence, now known as the State Barrier Fence of Western Australia, is a pest-exclusion fence built between 1901 and 1907 to keep rabbits and other agricultural pests of eastern Australia out of Western Australia's farmland. At 1,833 kilometres, the No. 1 fence was, at completion, the longest unbroken fence in the world.

Construction

European rabbits, introduced to Australia in 1859, spread across the continent with devastating effects on pasture and native vegetation. Western Australia responded with three fences: No. 1 ran from the south coast near Ravensthorpe north to the Eighty Mile Beach on the north-west coast; No. 2 and No. 3 fences added further lines to the west. By the time the fences were finished, rabbits had already breached them, and the barriers were later adapted against emus and dingoes as the State Barrier Fence.

Cultural significance

The fence is famous worldwide through Doris Pilkington Garimara's 1996 book Follow the Rabbit-Proof Fence, which recounts how her mother Molly Craig, a girl of the Stolen Generations, escaped from the Moore River Native Settlement in 1931 and walked some 1,600 kilometres home to Jigalong by following the fence. The story was filmed in 2002 as Rabbit-Proof Fence.
