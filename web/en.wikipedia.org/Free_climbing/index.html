Free climbing

Free climbing is a form of rock climbing in which the climber uses only their hands, feet, and body against natural features of the rock to ascend, while ropes, harnesses, and hardware are used solely as protection against falls — never to pull upward or bear the climber's weight. It contrasts with aid climbing, in which equipment is used directly to make progress.

Terminology and styles

"Free climbing" is frequently confused by the public with free soloing — climbing without any rope or protection — which is a specialised and extremely dangerous subset of free climbing. Most free climbing is done with a belayer and protection: traditional ("trad") climbing places removable gear in cracks, while sport climbing clips pre-placed bolts. A free ascent completed without falls or rests on the rope on the first try after practice is called a redpoint; an unrehearsed first-try ascent is an onsight or flash.

History

Early twentieth-century climbers in Saxony and the Lake District developed strong free-climbing ethics, and climbers such as Paul Preuss advocated for unaided ascent before 1914. In Yosemite in the 1960s and 1970s, climbers including Royal Robbins and later Lynn Hill pushed standards; Hill's free ascent of the Nose of El Capitan in 1993 was a landmark. Modern free climbing spans single-pitch crags, bouldering, and multi-day big-wall ascents, with difficulty graded on scales such as the Yosemite Decimal System.
