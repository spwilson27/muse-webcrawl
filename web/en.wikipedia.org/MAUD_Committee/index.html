The MAUD Committee was a British scientific working group established in April 1940 to investigate whether an atomic bomb was feasible during the Second World War. Its conclusions — that a uranium bomb could be built within the war's timescale — were decisive in launching the British Tube Alloys programme and in galvanising the American Manhattan Project.

Background

In March 1940 the émigré physicists Otto Frisch and Rudolf Peierls at Birmingham calculated that the critical mass of uranium-235 was far smaller than previously believed — on the order of kilograms rather than tons — making a deliverable weapon conceivable. Their memorandum prompted the formation of the committee under George Thomson. The name "MAUD" came from a telegram phrase about "Maud Ray" of Kent, misread as an anagram; it was not an acronym.

Report and legacy

The MAUD Report of July 1941 concluded that a bomb using about 12 kg of uranium-235, with an effect equivalent to some 1,800 tons of TNT, could be ready by 1943, and recommended pursuing it as a matter of urgency. Transmitted to the United States, the report spurred President Roosevelt to authorise an all-out effort. The British work was folded into the Tube Alloys directorate and, after the 1943 Quebec Agreement, into the Manhattan Project.
