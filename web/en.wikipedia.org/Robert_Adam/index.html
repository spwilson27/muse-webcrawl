Robert Adam (3 July 1728 – 3 March 1792) was a Scottish neoclassical architect, interior designer, and furniture designer. He was a son of the architect William Adam and, with his brother James Adam, developed the Adam style, a light, elegant interpretation of classical architecture that integrated architecture, interiors, and furnishings into a unified scheme. Adam was one of the most successful and fashionable architects in Britain in the second half of the eighteenth century.

Early life and the Grand Tour

Born in Kirkcaldy, Fife, Adam was educated at the Royal High School and the University of Edinburgh. After his father's death in 1748 he worked in the family firm with his brothers. From 1754 to 1758 he undertook a Grand Tour of France and Italy, studying classical ruins, drawing with Charles-Louis Clérisseau, and meeting Giovanni Battista Piranesi in Rome — formative experiences for his mature style.

Career

Setting up practice in London in 1758, Adam quickly won aristocratic clients. He became architect of the King's Works in 1761 (jointly with William Chambers) and a member of Parliament for Kinross-shire in 1768. With James he published The Works in Architecture of Robert and James Adam (1773–79), which publicized their style across Britain and North America.

Works

Major works include the interiors of Syon House and Osterley Park near London; Kedleston Hall in Derbyshire; Harewood House in Yorkshire; Kenwood House in Hampstead; Culzean Castle in Ayrshire; and in Edinburgh, the Old College of the University of Edinburgh, Register House, and Charlotte Square (completed by others). The Adelphi development in London, undertaken with his brothers, was a celebrated but financially perilous speculative scheme rescued in part by a lottery authorized by Parliament.

The Adam style

Reacting against the heavier Palladianism then dominant, the Adam style favored slender proportions, delicate plasterwork ornament drawing on Roman, Etruscan, and Renaissance grotesque motifs, painted decoration, and rooms of varied shape — oval, circular, apsidal — arranged in sequences that Adam called "movement." He designed everything down to furniture, carpets, and door handles so that each interior formed a complete work of art. The style strongly influenced the Federal style in the early United States.

Death and legacy

Adam died in London in 1792 and was buried in Westminster Abbey. His drawings are preserved in Sir John Soane's Museum, London. Though taste shifted toward the Greek Revival after his death, the Adam style was revived in the later nineteenth and twentieth centuries and remains synonymous with refined Georgian interiors.
