The Elements (Ancient Greek: Στοιχεῖα, Stoicheia) is a mathematical treatise attributed to the Greek mathematician Euclid, who wrote it in Alexandria around 300 BC. Consisting of thirteen books, it covers plane and solid geometry, number theory, and the theory of proportions, and is the most influential textbook ever written: it was used as the basic text on geometry throughout the Western world for more than two thousand years.

Contents

Books I–IV treat plane geometry: congruence, area, the Pythagorean theorem, circles, and regular polygons. Book V presents Eudoxus' theory of proportion; Book VI applies it to similar figures. Books VII–IX cover number theory, including the Euclidean algorithm and a proof that there are infinitely many prime numbers. Book X classifies incommensurable magnitudes. Books XI–XIII treat solid geometry, ending with the construction and classification of the five regular (Platonic) solids.

Method

The Elements proceeds axiomatically from definitions, postulates, and common notions, deriving 465 propositions by rigorous proof. The fifth postulate — the parallel postulate — resisted all attempts at proof from the others and eventually led to the discovery of non-Euclidean geometries in the 19th century.

Influence

Translated into Arabic in the 9th century and into Latin in the 12th, the Elements was first printed in Venice in 1482 and has since appeared in more than a thousand editions. Abraham Lincoln famously studied it to learn what it means to "demonstrate"; its logical structure shaped philosophy from Spinoza to Newton, whose Principia imitated its form.
