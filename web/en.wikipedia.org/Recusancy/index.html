Recusancy

Recusant households sheltered missionary priests in concealed hiding places known as priest holes.

Recusancy (from Latin recusare, "to refuse") was the state of those who remained loyal to the Catholic Church and refused to attend services of the Church of England after the English Reformation. Such persons were termed recusants. The term, which originated in Elizabethan England, initially denoted Catholics who declined to attend Anglican worship, but it was later extended to Protestant nonconformists and dissenters who likewise refused to conform, notably in the penal-law regimes applied in England, Wales and Ireland.

Penal laws under Elizabeth and the Stuarts

The Act of Uniformity 1559 imposed a fine of twelve pence for absence from church, but repression intensified after the papal bull Regnans in Excelsis (1570) excommunicated Elizabeth I. The Recusancy Act of 1581 raised the fine to the ruinous sum of £20 per month and made it treason to reconcile subjects to Rome; further statutes under Elizabeth and James I confiscated two-thirds of a convicted recusant's lands in default of payment, restricted their movement, and excluded them from offices, universities and the legal profession. Missionary priests arriving from seminaries such as Douai and the English College in Rome were hunted by priest-catchers, and harbouring one was a felony; over a hundred priests and many laypeople were executed.

Recusant society

Recusancy survived most strongly among sections of the gentry and nobility, especially in the north of England, Lancashire and the Welsh Marches, where households protected chaplains and hosted secret Masses. Recusant families commissioned hiding places — priest holes, many built by the Jesuit lay brother Nicholas Owen — and sustained a clandestine literature. Some Catholics became "church papists", outwardly conforming while privately remaining Catholic, a stance the authorities struggled to police. The Gunpowder Plot of 1605, hatched by a small group of recusant extremists, provoked a fresh wave of penal legislation.

Relief and emancipation

Enforcement waxed and waned over two centuries, and recusancy fines were often compounded for payments rather than exacted in full. Relief began with the Papists Act 1778 and the Roman Catholic Relief Act 1791, which legalized Catholic worship for those taking an oath, and culminated in the Roman Catholic Relief Act 1829 ("Catholic emancipation"), which removed most remaining disabilities. The term "recusant" survives in history and in fine arts and family history to describe the recusant families and their houses, chapels and literature.
