Infrared sensing in snakes

GroupsPit vipers (Crotalinae), some boas and pythons
OrgansPit organs; labial pits
Receptor moleculeTRPA1 ion channel
SensitivityTemperature differences of ~0.003 °C at the membrane

Some snakes possess the ability to detect infrared thermal radiation, allowing them to sense the body heat of warm-blooded prey even in complete darkness. This "heat vision" is best developed in the pit vipers (subfamily Crotalinae — rattlesnakes, copperheads, and lanceheads), which bear a pair of deep pit organs between the eye and the nostril, and occurs in a simpler form as labial pits in many boas and pythons.

Anatomy and mechanism

The pit organ contains a thin, richly innervated membrane suspended across an air-filled cavity; the cavity acts like a pinhole camera, projecting a crude thermal image onto the membrane. Infrared radiation warms the membrane, and heat-sensitive nerve endings respond. In 2010 researchers showed that the molecular sensor is the ion channel TRPA1, expressed at high levels in the trigeminal nerve fibres supplying the pit — a striking case of convergent evolution, since vampire bats independently use a modified TRPA1 for the same purpose.

Function

Infrared input is integrated with vision in the optic tectum of the brain, producing a combined image that guides precise strikes at endothermic prey. Experiments show that pit vipers can strike accurately at warm targets while blindfolded, and infrared sensing also helps snakes select cool refuges and detect predators. Pythons and boas, whose labial pits lack the suspended membrane, have a less sharply focused version of the sense.
