Louis Francis Albert Victor Nicholas Mountbatten, 1st Earl Mountbatten of Burma (25 June 1900 – 27 August 1979), commonly known as Lord Mountbatten, was a British statesman, naval officer and close relative of the British royal family. A great-grandson of Queen Victoria, he served as Supreme Allied Commander, South East Asia Command during the Second World War, and as the last Viceroy of India and its first Governor-General after independence. He was assassinated by the Provisional Irish Republican Army in 1979.

Early life

Mountbatten was born at Frogmore House, Windsor, the fourth child and second son of Prince Louis of Battenberg and Princess Victoria of Hesse and by Rhine. His maternal grandmother was Princess Alice, second daughter of Queen Victoria. During the First World War, anti-German sentiment led the family to anglicise its name from Battenberg to Mountbatten; his father resigned as First Sea Lord in 1914 and later became Marquess of Milford Haven. Mountbatten was educated at Lockers Park School, the Royal Naval College, Osborne, and Christ's College, Cambridge.

Naval career

Mountbatten joined the Royal Navy as a cadet in 1913 and served at sea in the closing stages of the First World War. Between the wars he specialised in signals and wireless communications, earning a reputation as a technically minded officer. In 1939 he took command of the destroyer HMS Kelly, which was sunk during the Battle of Crete in 1941 with heavy loss of life — an episode later dramatised in Noël Coward's film In Which We Serve (1942).

In 1942 Winston Churchill appointed him Chief of Combined Operations, overseeing commando raids including the costly Dieppe Raid of August 1942. In October 1943 he became Supreme Allied Commander, South East Asia Command, directing the campaign that recaptured Burma from Japanese forces, working closely with General William Slim's Fourteenth Army. He accepted the Japanese surrender at Singapore in September 1945.

Viceroy of India

In March 1947 Mountbatten succeeded Lord Wavell as Viceroy of India, charged with overseeing Britain's withdrawal. Concluding that a united India was unachievable, he advanced the timetable for independence to August 1947 and endorsed the partition of British India into the dominions of India and Pakistan, formalised in the Mountbatten Plan of 3 June 1947. The partition displaced between ten and twenty million people and was accompanied by mass violence in which hundreds of thousands — possibly over a million — died. Mountbatten remained as Governor-General of the Dominion of India until June 1948, when he was succeeded by Chakravarti Rajagopalachari. Jawaharlal Nehru, India's first prime minister, became a close personal friend.

Later career

Returning to naval service, Mountbatten commanded in the Mediterranean, served as Fourth Sea Lord, and in 1955 became First Sea Lord, the professional head of the Royal Navy — the post his father had been forced to leave in 1914. From 1959 to 1965 he was Chief of the Defence Staff, pressing for unified defence organisation. He was created Earl Mountbatten of Burma in 1947 and was made a Knight of the Garter; Charles, Prince of Wales (later Charles III), regarded him as a mentor and called him his "honorary grandfather."

Assassination

On 27 August 1979, while on holiday at Classiebawn Castle in County Sligo, Ireland, Mountbatten went lobster fishing aboard his boat Shadow V off Mullaghmore. A radio-controlled bomb planted by the Provisional IRA exploded, killing Mountbatten, his 14-year-old grandson Nicholas Knatchbull, and Paul Maxwell, a 15-year-old boat boy; the Dowager Lady Brabourne died of her injuries the following day. The attack, carried out the same day as the Warrenpoint ambush of British soldiers, drew worldwide condemnation. Mountbatten received a ceremonial funeral at Westminster Abbey and was buried at Romsey Abbey in Hampshire.
