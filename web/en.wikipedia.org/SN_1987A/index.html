SN 1987A

SN 1987A was a type II supernova in the Large Magellanic Cloud, a dwarf satellite galaxy of the Milky Way, approximately 168,000 light-years from Earth. Discovered on 23 February 1987 by Ian Shelton at Las Campanas Observatory in Chile, it was the first supernova visible to the naked eye since Kepler's Supernova of 1604 and remains the best-studied supernova of the modern era.

The triple-ring system surrounding the SN 1987A remnant, seen by Hubble.

Discovery and neutrinos

About three hours before the first light arrived, neutrino detectors at Kamiokande II, IMB and Baksan recorded a burst of some two dozen neutrinos — the first (and so far only) neutrinos detected from a supernova. The observation confirmed models of core collapse, in which roughly 99% of the gravitational energy of the collapsing core escapes as neutrinos. The progenitor star was identified as Sanduleak −69 202, a blue supergiant of about 18 solar masses, surprising astronomers who expected type II progenitors to be red supergiants.

The remnant

Around the supernova lies a striking triple-ring nebula, ejected by the progenitor some 20,000 years before the explosion; the rings lit up as they were ionised and later struck by the expanding debris. The ejecta, rich in radioactive nickel-56 whose decay powered the light curve, have expanded into a complex remnant studied by Hubble, Chandra, ALMA and the James Webb Space Telescope. In 2024, JWST observations found emission lines best explained by ionising radiation from a newborn neutron star at the centre — the long-sought compact remnant.
