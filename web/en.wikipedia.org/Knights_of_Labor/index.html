Knights of Labor

The emblem of the Noble and Holy Order of the Knights of Labor.

The Knights of Labor (officially the Noble and Holy Order of the Knights of Labor) was an American labor federation founded in Philadelphia in 1869 by Uriah Smith Stephens and fellow garment cutters. It was the first organisation to attempt to organise all workers — skilled and unskilled, men and women, Black and white — into one great union, and at its peak in 1886 it counted some 700,000 members, making it the largest labor body in the United States.

Growth under Powderly

Originally a secret society with elaborate rituals, the Knights became a public, mass organisation under Grand Master Workman Terence V. Powderly, who led it from 1879. Membership was open to all "producers" — excluding only bankers, speculators, lawyers, liquor dealers and gamblers — and local assemblies organised by trade or by locality. The Knights demanded an eight-hour day, the abolition of child and convict labor, equal pay for equal work, and ultimately a cooperative commonwealth in place of the wage system. A victorious strike against Jay Gould's Wabash Railroad in 1885 brought a surge of new members.

Decline

A failed strike against Gould's southwestern lines in 1886, the fallout of the Haymarket affair the same year — with which the Knights were unfairly associated — internal factionalism and the rise of the rival American Federation of Labor, committed to craft unionism, caused a rapid collapse; by the mid-1890s the Knights had dwindled to a remnant, though the order lingered until 1949.
