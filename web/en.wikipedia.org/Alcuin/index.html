Alcuin of York (Latin: Flaccus Albinus; c. 735 – 19 May 804) was an English scholar, cleric, poet and teacher from York, Northumbria. He became the foremost scholar and teacher at the court of Charlemagne and a central figure of the Carolingian Renaissance, the revival of learning in early medieval Europe.

Life

Alcuin was educated at the cathedral school of York under Archbishop Ecgbert and his teacher Aelbert, whom he succeeded as head of the school. In 781, returning from a mission to Rome, he met Charlemagne at Parma and accepted the king's invitation to lead the palace school at Aachen. There he taught the royal family and the sons of the Frankish nobility, and advised the king on education and church reform. In 796 he retired as abbot of St Martin's Abbey at Tours, where he died in 804.

Works and influence

Alcuin wrote textbooks on grammar, rhetoric, dialectic and arithmetic — including the famous collection of mathematical puzzles Propositiones ad acuendos iuvenes, which contains the classic river-crossing problem of the wolf, goat and cabbage. He revised the Latin Vulgate Bible, composed liturgical works, saints' lives and some 300 letters that illuminate the intellectual life of the age. His promotion of Caroline minuscule script and of standardised Latin helped shape medieval scholarship for centuries.
