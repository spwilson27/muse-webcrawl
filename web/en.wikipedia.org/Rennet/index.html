Rennet

Rennet (/ˈrɛnɪt/) is a complex set of enzymes produced in the stomachs of ruminant mammals. Chymosin, its key component, is a protease enzyme that curdles the casein in milk. In addition to chymosin, rennet contains other enzymes, such as pepsin and lipase. Rennet is used to separate milk into solid curds (for cheesemaking) and liquid whey, and it has been essential to cheese production for thousands of years.

Rennet curdles milk, the first step in making most hard cheeses.

Action and sources

Chymosin cleaves κ-casein at a specific bond, destabilizing the casein micelles in milk so that they aggregate into a gel, trapping fat and other milk solids. Traditional animal rennet is extracted from the abomasum (fourth stomach) of young calves. Because of cost, supply limits, and vegetarian demand, much of the cheese industry now uses alternatives: microbial coagulants from fungi such as Rhizomucor miehei, vegetable coagulants from plants such as the cardoon thistle (used in some Portuguese and Spanish cheeses), and fermentation-produced chymosin, in which the calf chymosin gene is expressed in yeast or fungi. Fermentation-produced chymosin, approved by the US Food and Drug Administration in 1990, was one of the first genetically engineered food additives in commercial use.

Uses beyond cheese

Rennet is also used to make junket, a milk pudding, and historically was employed in some traditional medicines. The choice of coagulant affects the flavour and texture of the finished cheese, since the accompanying enzymes contribute to proteolysis during ripening.
