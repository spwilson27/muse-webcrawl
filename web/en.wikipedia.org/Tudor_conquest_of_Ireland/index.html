Tudor conquest of Ireland

Tudor conquest of Ireland
Datec. 1536 – 1603
LocationIreland
ResultEnglish control extended over the whole island; Gaelic political order destroyed

The Tudor conquest (or reconquest) of Ireland took place under the Tudor dynasty, which held the Kingdom of England during the sixteenth century. Following a failed rebellion by the Earl of Kildare in the 1530s, the English Crown set about extending its control over the whole of Ireland, which had previously been confined largely to the Pale around Dublin. The conquest was completed in 1603, at the end of the Nine Years' War, and was followed within a few years by the Flight of the Earls (1607), marking the end of the old Gaelic order.

Background

Since the Norman invasion of 1169, English authority in Ireland had waxed and waned; by 1500 the great Anglo-Irish and Gaelic lordships were effectively autonomous and Gaelic culture had resurged. The Tudor monarchs, facing rebellion at home and war with Catholic Spain, regarded Ireland as a strategic vulnerability and a candidate for religious reformation and administrative centralisation.

Course of the conquest

Henry VIII had himself declared King of Ireland in 1541 and pursued "surrender and regrant", under which Gaelic lords submitted and received their lands back under English law. Under his successors policy hardened: rebellions by the O'Neills and others were crushed, "plantations" of English settlers were established in Laois and Offaly and later in Munster, and the Desmond Rebellions of 1569–1583 devastated the south. The final and greatest challenge, the Nine Years' War (1594–1603) led by Hugh O'Neill and Hugh Roe O'Donnell, drew Spanish support but ended in defeat after the Battle of Kinsale (1601) and the Treaty of Mellifont (1603).

Consequences

The conquest destroyed the Gaelic political and social system, imposed English law and the established (Protestant) church on a largely Catholic population, and opened the way to the much larger Ulster Plantation under James I. It is regarded as a foundational episode in the history of Anglo-Irish relations.
