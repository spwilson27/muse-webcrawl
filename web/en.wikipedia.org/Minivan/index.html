Minivan

A minivan (North American terminology) or multi-purpose vehicle (MPV) is a car classification for vehicles designed to transport passengers in the rear seating row(s), with reconfigurable seats in two or three rows. The typical minivan has a one-box or two-box body, a high roof, a flat floor, sliding doors for rear passengers and a tall rear hatch. Most are front-wheel drive and based on car platforms, distinguishing them from larger, truck-based full-size vans.

History

Precursors included the Volkswagen Type 2 (1950) and various American station wagons and compact vans. The modern minivan segment was created in late 1983 when Chrysler launched the Plymouth Voyager and Dodge Caravan for the 1984 model year. Designed under Hal Sperlich and championed by Lee Iacocca, they packaged front-wheel drive, a car-like driving position and a "magic wagon" interior in a vehicle small enough to fit in a standard garage. The concept was an immediate success and was imitated worldwide: Renault's Espace (1984) pioneered the European MPV, and Japanese makers followed with models such as the Toyota Previa.

Characteristics

Minivans typically seat six to eight people in three rows. Signature features include dual sliding rear doors, removable or fold-flat seating (Chrysler's Stow 'n Go seats fold into floor wells), a low step-in height and family-oriented equipment such as multiple cup holders, rear entertainment systems and conversation mirrors. Engines are usually V6 petrol units, with hybrid versions offered by Toyota and Chrysler in recent years.

Market evolution

The segment peaked in North America around 2000, when about 1.3 million minivans were sold annually in the United States. Thereafter families increasingly chose SUVs and crossovers, and many manufacturers withdrew; by the 2020s the North American market consisted mainly of the Chrysler Pacifica, Honda Odyssey, Toyota Sienna and Kia Carnival. In Asia, luxury MPVs such as the Toyota Alphard remain prestigious chauffeur vehicles, and compact MPVs continue to sell in Europe and emerging markets, increasingly in electric form.
