Stigmata

Stigmata (singular stigma) are bodily wounds, scars, or sensations of pain in locations corresponding to the crucifixion wounds of Jesus Christ — the hands, wrists, feet, side, and sometimes the head (from the crown of thorns) or shoulders. The phenomenon is reported mainly within the Roman Catholic tradition, and those who bear them are called stigmatics or stigmatists.

History

The first recorded case is that of Saint Francis of Assisi, who was said to have received the wounds in 1224 during a vision of a seraph on Mount La Verna. Since then several hundred cases have been reported, including Saint Catherine of Siena, Saint Gemma Galgani, and Padre Pio of Pietrelcina, whose stigmata were widely publicized in the twentieth century. The term derives from Saint Paul's words in Galatians 6:17: "I bear on my body the marks (stigmata) of Jesus."

Views and investigation

The Catholic Church has neither required belief in stigmata nor formally explained them; individual cases connected with canonized saints are treated as possible miraculous signs. Medical and psychological explanations proposed include psychosomatic phenomena, self-infliction (deliberate or in altered states), and fraud; some documented cases have admitted to self-infliction. Cases differ in whether wounds are visible or "invisible" (pain without marks) and whether they bleed continuously or periodically.
