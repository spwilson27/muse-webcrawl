Appalachian music

Cultural origins18th–19th century Appalachia; British, Irish, African, and German influences
Typical instrumentsFiddle, banjo, guitar, mandolin, dulcimer, upright bass
Derivative formsBluegrass, country music, old-time music

Appalachian music is the traditional music of Appalachia in the eastern United States, formed from the intermingling of British and Irish ballads and fiddle tunes, African American banjo traditions and blues, German hymnody, and shape-note singing. Preserved in relative isolation in the mountains, it was "discovered" by collectors and the recording industry in the early twentieth century and became a foundation of country music, bluegrass, and the American folk revival.

Origins

Settlers from the British Isles brought unaccompanied ballads — songs of murder, love, and the supernatural traceable to Child ballads — and a vigorous fiddle tradition. The banjo, derived from West African lutes, entered the mountains through enslaved and free Black musicians, and the fiddle–banjo duet became the core of the mountain string band. The Appalachian dulcimer, adapted from German zithers, and sacred traditions such as lining-out hymns and Sacred Harp singing added further strands. Cecil Sharp, collecting in the mountains in 1916–1918, famously found Elizabethan-era songs surviving in oral tradition.

Commercial era and the Bristol Sessions

Record companies began recording mountain musicians in the 1920s. The 1927 Bristol Sessions, organised by Ralph Peer in Bristol, Tennessee/Virginia, launched the Carter Family — whose "Carter scratch" guitar style and Maybelle Carter's arrangements shaped country music — and Jimmie Rodgers. Fiddlin' John Carson, Gid Tanner and the Skillet Lickers, and Uncle Dave Macon were other early stars; the Stanley Brothers and Bill Monroe later distilled mountain string-band music into bluegrass.

Styles and revival

Key genres include old-time music (dance-oriented string-band playing), bluegrass (virtuosic, high-energy, and built around Scruggs-style banjo and close harmony), ballad singing, gospel, and the clawhammer banjo style. The folk revival of the 1950s and 1960s carried performers such as Jean Ritchie, Doc Watson, and Roscoe Holcomb to national audiences. Today festivals like the Galax Old Fiddlers' Convention (founded 1935) and Clifftop sustain the tradition, and old-time music enjoys a global community of players.
