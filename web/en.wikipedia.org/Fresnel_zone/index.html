Fresnel zone

A Fresnel zone (pronounced "fray-NEL") is one of a series of concentric ellipsoidal regions of space between and around a transmitter and a receiver, named after the physicist Augustin-Jean Fresnel. Radio waves travelling between the two points take many paths, and whether they arrive in phase or out of phase depends on the path length relative to the direct path. The geometry of the Fresnel zones is used to predict losses from obstruction and reflections in radio links, particularly microwave links.

Definition

The n-th Fresnel zone is the locus of points for which a reflected or diffracted ray path exceeds the direct path by n half-wavelengths. The boundary of the first Fresnel zone is an ellipsoid with the transmitter and receiver at its foci. The radius of the first Fresnel zone at a point along the path is largest at the midpoint and shrinks toward the antennas. For a path of total length D, the first-zone radius at the midpoint is approximately the square root of (wavelength × D / 4).

Clearance

Objects intruding into the first Fresnel zone cause diffraction losses and can put the received signal partially out of phase. A common rule of thumb in link planning is that at least 60 percent of the first Fresnel zone should be clear of obstructions for the path to behave approximately as free space. Fresnel zone clearance is a major consideration in siting microwave towers, point-to-point wireless bridges, and satellite earth stations.
