The Gregorian Reform (also called the Papal Reform) was a series of reforms initiated by Pope Gregory VII and the circle he formed in the papal curia from about 1050, directed against abuses in the Western Church. Its chief goals were to free the Church from secular control (libertas ecclesiae), to end lay investiture of bishops and abbots, to eliminate simony (the purchase of church offices) and clerical marriage (Nicolaitism), and to assert the authority of the papacy over both clergy and rulers.

Background and measures

Reform drew on earlier monastic movements such as Cluny and on the reforming papacy of Leo IX, who from 1049 held synods condemning simony and clerical marriage. In 1059 the papal election decree In nomine Domini assigned the election of popes to the cardinal-bishops, reducing imperial and Roman aristocratic influence, and the Lateran synods legislated against simoniac ordinations. Gregory VII's Dictatus papae (1075) listed sweeping claims of papal authority.

Investiture Controversy

The reform's confrontation with Emperor Henry IV over investiture produced the dramatic penance at Canossa in 1077, renewed conflict, and Gregory's death in exile at Salerno in 1085 ("I have loved justice and hated iniquity, therefore I die in exile"). The controversy was partly settled by the Concordat of Worms (1122), which distinguished spiritual investiture (reserved to the Church) from temporal investiture by the ruler.

Legacy

The reforms strengthened papal monarchy, promoted canon law (culminating in Gratian's Decretum), enforced clerical celibacy in the Latin Church, and contributed to the emergence of the Church as a separate corporate order in medieval society — changes some historians describe as a "papal revolution".
