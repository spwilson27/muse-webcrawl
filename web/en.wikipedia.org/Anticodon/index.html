Anticodon

An anticodon is a sequence of three nucleotides forming a unit of genetic code in a transfer RNA (tRNA) molecule, corresponding to a complementary sequence of three nucleotides (a codon) in messenger RNA (mRNA). During translation at the ribosome, the anticodon pairs with the mRNA codon through hydrogen bonding, ensuring that the amino acid carried by the tRNA is inserted into the growing polypeptide chain in the correct position.

Structure and function

The anticodon occupies positions 34–36 of the tRNA, at the end of the anticodon stem-loop. Because the genetic code is degenerate, several codons may specify the same amino acid; the "wobble" hypothesis, proposed by Francis Crick in 1966, explains how the first base of the anticodon can pair non-standardly with the third base of the codon, so fewer tRNA species are needed than there are codons. Modified bases such as inosine in the wobble position broaden pairing capacity.

Significance

Accurate codon–anticodon recognition is essential to the fidelity of protein synthesis. Mutations in tRNA genes, including anticodon mutations that convert a tRNA to recognize a different codon, can act as suppressor mutations in genetic experiments and, in humans, are associated with certain mitochondrial and neurological diseases.
