Ratites are a group of flightless birds united by a flat breastbone (sternum) lacking the keel (carina) that anchors the flight muscles of flying birds — the name derives from the Latin ratis, "raft," for the keel-less, raft-like sternum. Living ratites include the ostriches of Africa, the rheas of South America, the emu and cassowaries of Australia and New Guinea, and the kiwis of New Zealand; extinct members include the giant moas of New Zealand and the elephant birds of Madagascar, among the largest birds ever known.

Evolution

Ratites were long Exhibit A for Gondwanan vicariance: their distribution across southern continents was taken to show that flightless ancestors rafted apart on the fragments of Gondwana. Molecular studies have overturned the simple version of this story. Phylogenies place the flying tinamous of South America inside the ratite radiation and group elephant birds with kiwis, implying that ratite ancestors could fly and dispersed between continents, losing flight independently several times — a striking case of convergent evolution. Ratites and tinamous together form the Palaeognathae, the sister group of all other living birds.

Characteristics

Beyond the keel-less sternum, ratites share a simple palate structure and feathers lacking the interlocking barbules that stiffen flight feathers, giving their plumage a loose, hair-like texture. Sizes range from the kiwi, about the size of a chicken, to the common ostrich at up to 2.8 m tall and 150 kg — the largest living bird, and the fastest runner among birds. Several ratites are farmed (ostrich, emu, rhea) for meat, leather, feathers and oil, while many island forms were exterminated soon after human arrival.
