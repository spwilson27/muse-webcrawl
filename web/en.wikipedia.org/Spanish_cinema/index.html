Spanish cinema refers to the filmmaking of Spain, a tradition internationally recognised for figures such as Luis Buñuel and Pedro Almodóvar and for a distinctive blend of surrealism, social realism and melodrama.

Early years and Buñuel

Cinema arrived in Spain in 1896, and Segundo de Chomón pioneered special effects in the silent era. Luis Buñuel, working with Salvador Dalí, made the surrealist classics Un Chien Andalou (1929) and L'Age d'Or (1930) before a long exile career in Mexico and France.

Franco era

Under Franco, cinema was censored and the state promoted historical epics, yet directors such as Juan Antonio Bardem and Luis García Berlanga smuggled social critique into comedies and dramas; Buñuel returned to make Viridiana (1961), which won the Palme d'Or and was promptly banned in Spain. Carlos Saura emerged in the 1960s–70s with allegorical works such as Cría Cuervos.

Democracy and Almodóvar

After 1975, censorship ended and regional cinemas (Catalan, Basque, Galician) developed. Pedro Almodóvar, a figurehead of Madrid's Movida counterculture, achieved global success with films including Women on the Verge of a Nervous Breakdown, All About My Mother and Talk to Her. Víctor Erice's The Spirit of the Beehive (1973) is widely ranked among the greatest Spanish films. Recent decades have added directors such as Alejandro Amenábar, Guillermo del Toro (for the Spanish-Mexican Pan's Labyrinth) and Carla Simón.
