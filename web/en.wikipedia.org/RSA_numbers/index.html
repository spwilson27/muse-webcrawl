RSA numbers

RSA numbers are large semiprimes offered as factoring challenges.

In mathematics, the RSA numbers are a set of large semiprimes (numbers with exactly two prime factors) that were part of the RSA Factoring Challenge. The challenge was to find the prime factors of each number; it was created by RSA Laboratories in March 1991 to encourage research into computational number theory and the practical difficulty of factoring large integers. The challenge was declared inactive in 2007.

When the challenge ended, cash prizes had been offered for the factorisation of numbers from RSA-100 (100 decimal digits) up to RSA-2048. Many of the smaller numbers were factored by distributed efforts using the general number field sieve, while the largest numbers remain unfactored and are expected to stay so for the foreseeable future with classical computers.

Notable factorisations

RSA-100 was factored in April 1991, shortly after the challenge began. RSA-129, mentioned in Martin Gardner's 1977 Scientific American column about the RSA cryptosystem, was factored in 1994 by a team coordinated over the Internet — the decrypted message read "The Magic Words are Squeamish Ossifrage". RSA-155 (512 bits) fell in 1999, demonstrating that 512-bit RSA keys no longer offered meaningful security. RSA-250 (829 bits) was factored in February 2020 by Fabrice Boudot, Pierrick Gaudry, Aurore Guillevic, Nadia Heninger, Emmanuel Thomé and Paul Zimmermann, using roughly 2,700 CPU-years of computation.

Significance

The security of the RSA cryptosystem rests on the presumed difficulty of factoring the product of two large primes. The progressive factorisation of the RSA numbers tracks improvements in algorithms and hardware, and has directly informed recommendations for minimum key sizes in public-key cryptography standards.
