Sverdrup balance

The Sverdrup balance, or Sverdrup relation, is a theoretical relationship in physical oceanography between the wind stress curl applied to the surface of the open ocean and the resulting meridional (north–south) transport of water. It was derived in 1947 by the Norwegian oceanographer Harald Sverdrup and forms the basis of the modern theory of wind-driven ocean circulation and subtropical gyres.

Theory

Considering a steady, linear, depth-integrated flow on a beta plane — in which the Coriolis parameter varies with latitude — the vorticity equation reduces to a balance in which the advection of planetary vorticity by the meridional flow is driven by the curl of the wind stress. In symbols, βV = (1/ρ) curlz τ, where V is the depth-integrated meridional mass transport, β is the rate of change of the Coriolis parameter with latitude, ρ is density, and τ is the wind stress. Thus the direction and magnitude of the interior ocean transport are determined directly by the local wind stress curl: in the subtropical gyres, where the wind curl is negative in the Northern Hemisphere, the transport is equatorward.

Applications and extensions

Integrating the Sverdrup transport from the eastern boundary gives the streamfunction throughout the gyre interior. The balance cannot hold at the western boundary, where friction becomes important; Henry Stommel (1948) and Walter Munk (1950) added bottom and lateral friction to explain the narrow, intense western boundary currents such as the Gulf Stream and Kuroshio that return the Sverdrup transport poleward. The unit of volume transport in oceanography, the sverdrup (Sv), equal to 106 m³/s, is named after Harald Sverdrup.
