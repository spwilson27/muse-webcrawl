Limerick (poetry)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A limerick is a form of verse, usually humorous and frequently rude, in five-line anapestic meter with a strict rhyme scheme of AABBA, in which the first, second, and fifth lines rhyme and are longer, while the third and fourth lines rhyme and are shorter. The form appeared in England in the early years of the 18th century and was popularized in the 19th century by Edward Lear.

Form

The standard limerick has lines of seven to ten syllables, with the shorter middle lines of five to seven syllables. The rhythm is anapestic (da-da-DUM) and the last line often repeats — or mildly varies — the first in Lear's examples, whereas later limericks typically end with a punchline. The first line traditionally introduces a person and a place, often a city, e.g. "There was an Old Man of Nantucket..."

There was an Old Man with a beard,
Who said, "It is just as I feared!—
Two Owls and a Hen,
Four Larks and a Wren,
Have all built their nests in my beard!"
— Edward Lear, A Book of Nonsense (1846)

History

Five-line anapestic verses appear in print from the 1820s in collections of comic verses such as The History of Sixteen Wonderful Old Women (1820) and Anecdotes and Adventures of Fifteen Gentlemen (1822). Edward Lear's A Book of Nonsense (1846, expanded 1861) made the form famous as nonsense verse for children. In the same decades the form was adopted for (frequently bawdy) oral humour, and the name "limerick" — a reference, by uncertain pathways, to the Irish city or county of Limerick — became attached to it by the 1890s, possibly via a parlour game or soldiers' song with the refrain "Will you come up to Limerick?"

Later use

Limerick contests were held by magazines from the late 19th century onward (one London magazine's competition famously drew thousands of entries). Notable later writers include Dixon Lanier Merritt, whose "pelican" limerick (1910) is among the most quoted in the form, and Morris Bishop; the form is also used for mnemonics, mathematical verse, and extended humorous compositions, and there exists a tradition of "anti-limericks" that subvert the expected meter or rhyme.
