Transparency International (TI) is an international non-governmental organisation founded in 1993 and based in Berlin, Germany. Its non-profit purpose is to take action to combat global corruption with civil societal anti-corruption measures and to prevent criminal activities arising from corruption. It is best known for publishing the Corruption Perceptions Index (CPI), an annual ranking of countries by their perceived levels of public-sector corruption.

History

Transparency International was founded in May 1993 by Peter Eigen, a former regional director for the World Bank, together with a group of associates including Hansjörg Elshorst, Joe Githongo, Fritz Heimann, Michael Hershman, Kamal Hossain, Dolores L. Español, George Moody Stuart, Jerry Parfitt, Jeremy Pope and Frank Vogl. Eigen served as the organisation's first chair. The founding followed growing frustration that corruption was routinely ignored in international development practice, and that development institutions treated it as a taboo subject.

From a small secretariat in Berlin, TI grew into a global movement with more than 100 national chapters and contact points around the world. In its early years it helped push corruption onto the international agenda, contributing to the momentum behind the OECD Anti-Bribery Convention of 1997 and the United Nations Convention against Corruption of 2003. The organisation held its first annual membership meeting in 1994, and the Corruption Perceptions Index was first published in 1995, initially covering 41 countries.

Corruption Perceptions Index

The Corruption Perceptions Index, published annually since 1995, ranks countries and territories by how corrupt their public sector is perceived to be, as determined by expert assessments and opinion surveys. Since 2012 the index has used a scale from 0 (highly corrupt) to 100 (very clean). The index draws on data sources from institutions including the World Bank, the World Economic Forum, and private risk and consulting companies. Denmark, Finland, New Zealand and Singapore have consistently appeared near the top of the ranking, while countries affected by conflict and weak institutions, such as Somalia, Syria, South Sudan and Venezuela, have appeared at the bottom.

The CPI has been criticised for measuring perceptions rather than actual corruption, for potentially reinforcing stereotypes about developing countries, and for relying on surveys of business people and experts. Transparency International responds that perceptions matter because they reflect the assessments of those who interact with public institutions, and that no single measure can capture corruption in full.

Other work

TI also publishes the Global Corruption Barometer, based on public opinion surveys about people's experiences of bribery, and the Bribe Payers Index, which ranked major exporting countries by the propensity of their firms to bribe abroad. Through its national chapters, the organisation runs advocacy and legal advice centres that support victims and witnesses of corruption, campaigns for whistleblower protection, monitors public procurement, and works on issues such as money laundering, shell companies, and the recovery of stolen assets. TI also helped develop the Business Principles for Countering Bribery and has worked with companies on anti-corruption programmes.

Structure and funding

Transparency International is a membership association governed by an international board of directors elected by its chapters. The international secretariat in Berlin coordinates the movement and produces global research. Funding comes from government development agencies, foundations, and private donors; TI states that it does not accept funding that could compromise its independence and publishes its audited accounts and major donors. The organisation has occasionally faced internal disputes, including controversies over governance and the accreditation of chapters.
