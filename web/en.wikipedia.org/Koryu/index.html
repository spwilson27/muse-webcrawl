Koryū (古流, "old school") is a Japanese term for martial arts schools (ryūha) founded before the Meiji Restoration of 1868, in contrast to modern martial arts (gendai budō) such as judo, karate and aikido. Koryū schools — also called kobudō in this older sense — were originally systems of military training for the samurai class.

Characteristics

Koryū training is centred on kata, pre-arranged forms practised with a partner, often using wooden weapons or blunted steel, rather than free sparring. Many schools are sōgō bujutsu, comprehensive systems combining swordsmanship (kenjutsu), spear, staff, grappling (jūjutsu) and auxiliary skills. Transmission is governed by licences such as menkyo kaiden and traditionally by documents and, historically, blood oaths (keppan).

History

The oldest surviving schools date to the Muromachi period; famous examples include Tenshin Shōden Katori Shintō-ryū (founded c. 1447) and Kashima Shintō-ryū. With the abolition of the samurai class in the 1870s, many schools declined, but some survived by preserving their curricula as cultural heritage. Today koryū are practised in Japan and abroad, and umbrella organisations such as the Nihon Kobudō Kyōkai oversee demonstrations.

Versus gendai budō

Unlike modern budō, which emphasise sport or self-development, koryū claim direct, documentable lineage to battlefield traditions and generally do not hold competitions; ranking by dan grades is uncommon, with teaching licences used instead.
