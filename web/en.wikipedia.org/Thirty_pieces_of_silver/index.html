Thirty pieces of silver

Thirty pieces of silver was the price for which Judas Iscariot betrayed Jesus, according to an account in the Gospel of Matthew 26:15 in the New Testament. Before the Last Supper, Judas is said to have gone to the chief priests and agreed to hand over Jesus in exchange for thirty silver coins. After the arrest of Jesus, overcome by remorse, Judas attempted to return the money to the chief priests and then hanged himself.

Biblical account

Matthew's Gospel is the only one that specifies the amount. When Judas returned the coins, the chief priests decided that they could not put the "blood money" into the temple treasury, so they used it to buy the Potter's Field as a burial place for foreigners — which, Matthew says, fulfilled a prophecy attributed to Jeremiah but closely matching Zechariah 11:12–13, where thirty pieces of silver are thrown to the potter in the house of the Lord. The Book of Acts gives a different account in which Judas himself bought the field and died there, falling headlong.

The coins

The Greek word used in Matthew is argyria ("silver coins"), which does not specify a denomination. Candidates proposed include Tyrian shekels (tetradrachms), which were the coins used for the Temple tax and are about 14 grams of high-purity silver, Antioch staters, Ptolemaic tetradrachms, or Roman denarii. Thirty Tyrian shekels would amount to roughly 420 grams of silver; in modern reconstructions the sum is often compared to several months' wages for a labourer. In Exodus 21:32, thirty shekels is also the price set for a slave gored by an ox, a parallel frequently noted by commentators.

Cultural use

The phrase "thirty pieces of silver" has become a proverb in many languages for a bribe paid for an act of betrayal or treachery, and is used of anyone who betrays a cause or a friend for personal gain. It appears widely in literature, political rhetoric and song.
