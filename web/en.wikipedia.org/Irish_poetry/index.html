Irish poetry

Languages: Irish, English, Latin

Earliest verse: 6th century AD

Irish poetry is poetry written by Irish poets, whether in the Irish language, in English, or in Latin. Irish poetry is among the oldest vernacular literatures in Europe, with written examples dating from the sixth century. For centuries the dominant tradition was in Irish, maintained by professional bardic poets attached to noble families; from the nineteenth century, poetry in English became dominant.

Early and bardic poetry

Early monastic poets produced nature lyrics and religious verse of striking directness. From about 1200 to 1650 the bardic order composed elaborate praise poetry in strict syllabic metres (dán díreach) for Gaelic and Norman lords. The collapse of the Gaelic order after the Flight of the Earls (1607) and the Cromwellian conquest ended the bardic schools; eighteenth-century poets such as Aogán Ó Rathaille and Eibhlín Dubh Ní Chonaill, composer of the lament Caoineadh Airt Uí Laoghaire, wrote in looser song metres.

Poetry in English

Jonathan Swift and Oliver Goldsmith wrote in the Anglo-Irish tradition of the eighteenth century. The Irish Literary Revival made poetry central to the national movement: W. B. Yeats, the towering figure of twentieth-century Irish verse and a Nobel laureate, founded the Irish Literary Theatre and drew on Celtic mythology. Later generations include Patrick Kavanagh, Louis MacNeice, Seamus Heaney — who won the Nobel Prize in 1995 — Eavan Boland, and Paul Muldoon. A parallel tradition in Irish continued with Máirtín Ó Direáin, Seán Ó Ríordáin and Nuala Ní Dhomhnaill.
