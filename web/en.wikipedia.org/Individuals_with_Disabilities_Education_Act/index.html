The Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA) is a United States federal law ensuring that children with disabilities receive a free appropriate public education tailored to their individual needs. It was first enacted in 1975 as the Education for All Handicapped Children Act and renamed IDEA in 1990. The most recent comprehensive reauthorization was the Individuals with Disabilities Education Improvement Act of 2004.

Structure

The act is divided into parts. Part B, the largest, provides grants to states for the education of children aged 3 to 21 with disabilities in thirteen eligibility categories, including autism, specific learning disabilities, intellectual disability, and speech or language impairments. Part C covers early intervention services for infants and toddlers, and Part D funds national activities such as personnel preparation and research.

Core requirements

IDEA guarantees each eligible child a free appropriate public education (FAPE) delivered through an Individualized Education Program (IEP) developed by a team including parents and educators. Children must be educated in the least restrictive environment (LRE), states must identify and evaluate all children with disabilities (Child Find), and parents are afforded procedural safeguards including due process hearings. Key Supreme Court decisions interpreting the statute include Board of Education v. Rowley (1982) and Endrew F. v. Douglas County School District (2017), the latter holding that schools must offer an education reasonably calculated to enable progress appropriate in light of the child's circumstances.

Funding

Congress originally authorized federal funding of up to 40% of the excess cost of special education, but appropriations have historically fallen well short of that level, leaving states and local districts to bear most of the cost.
