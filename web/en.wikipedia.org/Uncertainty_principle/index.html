Uncertainty principle

The uncertainty principle, also known as Heisenberg's uncertainty principle, is a fundamental concept in quantum mechanics. It states that there is a limit to the precision with which certain pairs of physical properties of a particle — its complementary variables — can be predicted from initial conditions, the best-known pair being position and momentum. Introduced by German physicist Werner Heisenberg in 1927, it is usually expressed as:

Δx Δp ≥ ħ/2

where Δx and Δp are the standard deviations (uncertainties) of position and momentum, and ħ is the reduced Planck constant.

Overview

The principle is not merely a statement about the clumsiness of measuring instruments. In the modern view it reflects the wave nature of quantum objects: a particle is described by a wave packet, and a wave packet narrowly localized in space (small Δx) necessarily contains a broad spread of wavelengths and hence momenta (large Δp), and vice versa. The relation follows mathematically from the non-commutation of the position and momentum operators, [x̂, p̂] = iħ.

History

Heisenberg published the principle in 1927, illustrating it with the gamma-ray microscope thought experiment, in which observing an electron with short-wavelength light disturbs its momentum. Niels Bohr's principle of complementarity provided a broader philosophical frame. Later, Earle Hesse Kennard and Hermann Weyl derived the formal inequality, and Howard Percy Robertson generalized it to any pair of non-commuting observables.

Other uncertainty relations

Analogous relations hold for angular momentum components, for energy and time (with subtleties, since time is not an operator in standard quantum mechanics), and for many other observable pairs. Entropic uncertainty relations, formulated in terms of Shannon entropy, give stronger bounds in some settings.

Interpretation and criticism

Albert Einstein objected to the probabilistic character the principle implied — "God does not play dice" — and devised thought experiments, including the EPR paradox (1935) with Podolsky and Rosen, to argue that quantum mechanics was incomplete. Experiments testing Bell's inequalities have since supported the quantum-mechanical predictions. Within the Copenhagen interpretation, the uncertainty is ontic — an indeterminacy in nature itself — while other interpretations attribute it to hidden variables or measurement contexts.

Applications

The principle underlies the stability of atoms (preventing electrons from collapsing into the nucleus), zero-point energy, quantum tunnelling rates, the natural linewidth of spectral lines, and limits on the precision of quantum metrology; squeezed states can redistribute uncertainty between quadratures to beat the standard quantum limit in one variable.
