The Colombian conflict is a low-intensity asymmetric war in Colombia that began in the mid-1960s between the Colombian government and far-left guerrilla groups such as the Revolutionary Armed Forces of Colombia (FARC) and the National Liberation Army (ELN), with right-wing paramilitary groups and drug cartels also becoming major actors. Rooted in land inequality and political exclusion, it is commonly dated from the period of La Violencia and the founding of the guerrilla movements in 1964.

Background

The assassination of populist leader Jorge Eliécer Gaitán in 1948 triggered La Violencia, a decade of partisan bloodshed in the countryside. The National Front power-sharing pact that followed excluded other political forces, and rural self-defence groups radicalised into the FARC under Manuel Marulanda, while the ELN formed in 1964 inspired by the Cuban Revolution.

Escalation

From the 1980s the cocaine trade transformed the conflict's economics: guerrillas taxed coca production, cartels such as the Medellín and Cali organisations built private armies, and paramilitary groups — later federated as the AUC — waged brutal campaigns against suspected guerrilla sympathisers. Massacres, kidnapping, and forced displacement reached their peak around the turn of the century, when the FARC fielded an estimated 18,000 fighters.

Peace processes

The AUC demobilised between 2003 and 2006 under President Álvaro Uribe, whose military offensive sharply reduced guerrilla capacity. In 2016, after four years of talks in Havana, the government of Juan Manuel Santos signed a peace accord with the FARC; despite its narrow rejection in a plebiscite, a revised agreement was ratified by Congress, the FARC demobilised some 13,000 combatants, and Santos received the Nobel Peace Prize. Implementation has been uneven, and dissident FARC factions, the ELN, and criminal groups continue armed activity in remote regions, with further negotiations pursued under President Gustavo Petro's "Total Peace" policy.

Human cost

Colombia's National Center for Historical Memory estimated more than 260,000 deaths between 1958 and 2018, the great majority civilians, and over seven million people registered as displaced or otherwise victimised — one of the world's largest internally displaced populations.
