The French New Wave (French: La Nouvelle Vague) was a film movement that emerged in France in the late 1950s, in which a group of young critic-filmmakers rejected the polished, literary "Tradition of Quality" of mainstream French cinema in favour of personal expression, location shooting, and formal experimentation. It profoundly reshaped world cinema and popularized the idea of the director as auteur.

Origins

The movement grew from the film journal Cahiers du Cinéma, founded in 1951, where critics François Truffaut, Jean-Luc Godard, Éric Rohmer, Claude Chabrol, and Jacques Rivette championed directors such as Hitchcock and Hawks and attacked stagy studio filmmaking. Alexandre Astruc's 1948 essay on the caméra-stylo ("camera-pen") supplied the theoretical call for directors to write with the camera. Cheap cameras, fast film stock, and lightweight sound equipment let them shoot on the streets of Paris with small crews.

Characteristics and key works

New Wave films feature jump cuts, improvised dialogue, long takes, direct address, self-reflexive references to cinema, and narratives of drifting young protagonists. Landmark works include Chabrol's Le Beau Serge (1958), Truffaut's The 400 Blows (1959), Godard's Breathless (1960), and, from the related Left Bank group, Alain Resnais's Hiroshima mon amour (1959) and Agnès Varda's Cléo from 5 to 7 (1962).

Influence

The New Wave inspired parallel movements worldwide — including the New Hollywood of the late 1960s and 1970s — and permanently changed film grammar, criticism, and production economics.
