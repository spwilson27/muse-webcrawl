Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party

Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party
Founded: 1964
State: Mississippi, United States
Context: Civil rights movement; Freedom Summer

The Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party (MFDP), also known as the Freedom Democratic Party, was an American political party created in Mississippi in 1964 during the civil rights movement. It was founded by Black Mississippians and white allies, with support from the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee (SNCC) and the Council of Federated Organizations (COFO), as an alternative to the segregationist regular Mississippi Democratic Party, from which Black voters were systematically excluded.

The 1964 convention challenge

During Freedom Summer 1964, the MFDP organised its own precinct meetings, county conventions, and a state convention open to all races, electing a delegation of 68 people to the Democratic National Convention in Atlantic City. There it challenged the seating of the all-white regular delegation, arguing that the regulars had been elected in violation of the national party's own rules and the US Constitution. Activist Fannie Lou Hamer, a sharecropper who had been evicted from her home for attempting to register to vote, gave televised testimony before the credentials committee describing the violence she had suffered, including a brutal beating in the Winona jail.

Outcome and legacy

Under pressure from President Lyndon B. Johnson, the convention offered a compromise: two at-large seats for the MFDP and a promise that future delegations would be integrated. The MFDP refused the compromise, but the challenge helped spur the Voting Rights Act of 1965 and reforms that transformed the Democratic Party's delegate selection. In 1968 an integrated "Loyalist" delegation from Mississippi was seated in place of the regulars.
