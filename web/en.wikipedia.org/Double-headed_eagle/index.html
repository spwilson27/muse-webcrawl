Double-headed eagle

The double-headed eagle is a motif and heraldic charge found in the cultures of many peoples from antiquity onward. It depicts an eagle with two heads, each facing outward, symbolising dominion, empire, and vigilance in two directions — often interpreted as rule over both East and West. It is most famously associated with the Byzantine Empire, the Holy Roman Empire, and Russia, and it remains in the coats of arms of several modern states, including Russia, Albania, Serbia, and Montenegro.

Origins

Two-headed eagles appear in Hittite art of the second millennium BC, carved on monuments in Anatolia, and the motif recurs in ancient Near Eastern and early medieval art. It passed into wider use through Sasanian and early Islamic textiles and metalwork, reaching both Byzantium and Western Europe.

Byzantine and Holy Roman use

In the Byzantine Empire the double-headed eagle became the emblem of the ruling Palaiologos dynasty and of the empire itself, the two heads variously explained as looking to Europe and Asia or to the authority of emperor and patriarch. In the West, the Holy Roman Emperors adopted the eagle: a single-headed eagle initially, and from the 15th century the double-headed "imperial eagle" (Reichsadler), which later passed into the arms of the Austrian Empire and other German states.

Russia and the Balkans

Grand Prince Ivan III of Moscow adopted the double-headed eagle in the late 15th century, after his marriage to the Byzantine princess Sophia Palaiologina, as a symbol of Moscow's imperial claims; it remained the emblem of the Russian Empire until 1917 and was restored as the coat of arms of the Russian Federation in 1993. The black double-headed eagle on a red field is the national emblem of Albania, derived from the banner of the 15th-century hero Skanderbeg, and double eagles also appear in the arms of Serbia and Montenegro.
