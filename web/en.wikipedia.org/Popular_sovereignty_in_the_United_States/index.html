Popular sovereignty in the United States

Popular sovereignty is the principle that the authority of a government is created and maintained by the consent of its people, who are the source of all political power. In the United States the doctrine is foundational: the Constitution opens with the words "We the People," and the Declaration of Independence grounded legitimate government in "the consent of the governed." The idea drew on Enlightenment thinkers, above all John Locke's social contract theory, and on the colonial experience of self-governing assemblies.

Constitutional foundations

The framers institutionalised popular sovereignty through a written constitution ratified by conventions of the people, regular elections, and the amendment process. Federalism divided power between the states and the union, but in the dominant American theory ultimate sovereignty resided in the people rather than in any government. The Ninth and Tenth Amendments, reserving undelegated powers to the states and the people, are often read as textual expressions of the principle.

The slavery controversy

In the nineteenth century "popular sovereignty" acquired a narrower, more contentious meaning: the proposal, championed by Senator Lewis Cass and then Stephen A. Douglas, that the settlers of a federal territory should decide for themselves whether to permit slavery. Douglas built the Kansas–Nebraska Act of 1854 on the doctrine, repealing the Missouri Compromise. The result was violent conflict in "Bleeding Kansas," the fracturing of the Democratic Party, and — after the Supreme Court held in Dred Scott (1857) that Congress could not empower a territory to ban slavery — the doctrine's collapse as a compromise formula. Abraham Lincoln's rebuttal, that popular sovereignty ignored the moral question of slavery itself, helped carry him to the presidency and the nation toward civil war.
