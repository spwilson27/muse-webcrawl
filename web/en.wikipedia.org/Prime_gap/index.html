Prime gap

Prime gaps grow on average as numbers increase, though they fluctuate irregularly.

A prime gap is the difference between two successive prime numbers. The n-th prime gap, denoted gn, is the difference between the (n+1)-th and the n-th primes: gn = pn+1 − pn. The first prime gap is 1, between 2 and 3; there are no other odd prime gaps, because after 2 every prime is odd and the difference between two odd numbers is even. Prime gaps of length 2 are counted between twin primes.

Simple results

Prime gaps can be arbitrarily long: for any n, the n consecutive integers (n+1)! + 2, …, (n+1)! + n + 1 are all composite, giving a prime gap of at least n. The prime number theorem implies that the average gap around a number x is about ln x. The maximal prime gap below x is conjectured to be of order (ln x)2, a statement known as Cramér's conjecture.

Numerical results

A prime gap is said to be a record, or maximal, if it is larger than all preceding gaps. The first record gaps were found by hand and later by computer search. As of the 2020s, the largest known prime gaps exceed 1,500 in width and occur below 1019. Notable milestones include the gap of 906 found below 231 and gaps of more than 1,000 discovered by volunteer computing projects such as PrimeGrid.

Further results

In 2013, Yitang Zhang proved that there are infinitely many prime gaps of bounded size (initially below 70 million); the Polymath Project and work by James Maynard and Terence Tao reduced the bound to 246. In the opposite direction, Kevin Ford, Ben Green, Sergei Konyagin, Maynard, and Tao established in 2014–2016 that maximal prime gaps near x can be as large as a constant times (ln x)(ln ln x)(ln ln ln ln x)/(ln ln ln x), resolving a long-standing problem of Paul Erdős.
