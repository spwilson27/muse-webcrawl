Angle trisection

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Angle trisection is a classical problem in geometry: the challenge of dividing a given angle into three equal smaller angles using only an unmarked straightedge and a compass. It is one of the three famous construction problems of antiquity, alongside squaring the circle and doubling the cube.

Impossibility

In 1837 Pierre Wantzel proved that trisecting an arbitrary angle with straightedge and compass is impossible. The proof uses field theory: a compass-and-straightedge construction can only produce lengths obtainable by successive square roots, whereas trisecting a 60° angle would require constructing cos 20°, which satisfies the irreducible cubic equation 8x3 − 6x − 1 = 0 and so cannot be reached by such constructions. Some specific angles, such as 90°, can be trisected, but no general method exists.

Other methods

Trisection is possible with additional tools. A marked ruler (the neusis construction, used by Archimedes), a tomahawk, origami (paper folding, by the Huzita–Hatori axioms), or curves such as the quadratrix of Hippias all allow exact trisection. Approximate trisections can also be drawn to any desired accuracy with straightedge and compass alone.

History

The problem dates to ancient Greek mathematics. Despite Wantzel's proof, amateur mathematicians still regularly submit purported trisections to universities and publishers, making angle trisection a classic example of a mathematical crank topic.
