Norm (philosophy)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In philosophy, a norm is a standard, rule, or principle that prescribes how things ought to be, as opposed to a description of how they are. Norms are the subject matter of normative disciplines such as ethics, aesthetics, and logic, and propositions expressing norms are called normative statements — for example, "one ought not to lie" — in contrast to descriptive statements such as "most people disapprove of lying".

Types

Philosophers distinguish norms of morality, rationality, etiquette, grammar, and law. A central distinction, drawn by Kant, is between categorical imperatives, which bind unconditionally, and hypothetical imperatives, which bind only given some desired end. Georg Henrik von Wright's Norm and Action (1963) helped found deontic logic, the formal study of obligation, permission, and prohibition.

Normativity

The "normativity" of meaning and content — the idea that words and thoughts can be correct or incorrect in their application — is a major theme in the philosophy of language following Kripke's reading of Wittgenstein. Explaining how normative facts fit into a naturalistic worldview, and whether norms can be reduced to facts, remains a contested issue; the distinction between "is" and "ought" traces to Hume.
