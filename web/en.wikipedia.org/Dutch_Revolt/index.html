Dutch Revolt

The Dutch Revolt (1566/1568–1648), also called the Eighty Years' War, was the revolt of the Seventeen Provinces of the Habsburg Netherlands against Philip II of Spain. It resulted in the independence of the northern Dutch Republic, recognised by Spain in the Peace of Münster (1648), while the southern provinces remained Spanish.

Causes

Grievances included Spanish centralisation, heavy taxation, the Inquisition's persecution of Protestants, and resentment of Spanish troops. Calvinism spread rapidly; the iconoclastic fury (Beeldenstorm) of 1566 prompted Philip to send the Duke of Alba, whose Council of Troubles (1567) executed thousands, including Counts Egmont and Horn.

Course

William of Orange led resistance from exile. Sea Beggars captured Brielle in 1572; Holland and Zeeland endured sieges at Haarlem, Alkmaar and Leiden (relieved 1574). The Spanish Fury at Antwerp (1576) drove the provinces to the Pacification of Ghent, but they split into the Union of Arras (south) and Union of Utrecht (north, 1579). The Act of Abjuration (1581) deposed Philip. William was assassinated in 1584; Maurice of Nassau and Johan van Oldenbarnevelt rebuilt the Dutch army and state. A Twelve Years' Truce (1609–1621) preceded final campaigns ending in 1648.

Legacy

The Dutch Republic became a commercial and maritime great power — the Dutch Golden Age — while the war reshaped European warfare and state formation.
