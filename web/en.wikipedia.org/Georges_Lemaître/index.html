Georges Henri Joseph Édouard Lemaître (17 July 1894 – 20 June 1966) was a Belgian Catholic priest, theoretical physicist, mathematician, astronomer, and professor of physics at the Catholic University of Leuven. He was the first to theorise that the recession of nearby galaxies could be explained by an expanding universe, and he proposed what became known as the Big Bang theory of the origin of the universe, calling it the "hypothesis of the primeval atom".

Work on cosmic expansion

After serving in the First World War, Lemaître studied at Cambridge under Arthur Eddington and at MIT and Harvard. In his 1927 paper "A homogeneous Universe of constant mass and increasing radius", published in French, he derived an expanding-universe solution of Einstein's equations and estimated the expansion rate later measured by Edwin Hubble, whose observations of galactic redshifts were published in 1929. The relation is now officially known as the Hubble–Lemaître law. In 1931 Lemaître proposed that the universe began as a single quantum — the "primeval atom" — whose disintegration produced all matter and space; Fred Hoyle later derisively dubbed the idea the "Big Bang", and the name stuck.

Faith and later life

Lemaître insisted on keeping his scientific work separate from theology, resisting suggestions that the primeval atom amounted to a scientific proof of creation. He served as president of the Pontifical Academy of Sciences from 1960 until his death. He learned of the discovery of the cosmic microwave background radiation shortly before he died in 1966.
