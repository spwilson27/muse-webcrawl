Kakawin are long narrative poems composed in Old Javanese (also called Kawi), written in metres borrowed from Indian Sanskrit kāvya poetry. Flourishing between roughly the 9th and 16th centuries at the Hindu-Buddhist courts of Java, and preserved afterwards in Bali, kakawin form the classical literature of Java.

Form

The word kakawin derives from kawi, meaning "poet" or "skilled in language." Each canto of a kakawin is written in a different Indian quantitative metre, with stanzas of four lines, each line having a fixed number of syllables and a pattern of long and short syllables. Poets often signed their works with chronograms and wordplay in the opening or closing cantos, which invoke a deity — Shiva, Vishnu, or the Buddha — as the poet's divine inspiration and patron king's counterpart.

Major works

The earliest surviving kakawin is the Kakawin Ramayana (c. 9th century), a Javanese retelling of the Sanskrit epic. Other major works include the Arjunawiwaha by Mpu Kanwa, celebrating King Airlangga; the Bharatayuddha (1157) by Mpu Sedah and Mpu Panuluh; the Sutasoma by Mpu Tantular, source of Indonesia's national motto Bhinneka Tunggal Ika ("Unity in Diversity"); and the Nagarakretagama (1365) by Mpu Prapanca, a eulogy of King Hayam Wuruk of Majapahit that is a major historical source.

Themes and transmission

Kakawin retell Indian epic stories — the Ramayana and Mahabharata — in Javanese landscapes and court settings, blending Shaivite and Buddhist themes. After Islamisation ended court patronage in Java, the tradition was maintained in Bali, where kakawin are still copied on palm-leaf lontar manuscripts and sung in communal readings.
