Midterm election

United States midterm elections
TimingNovember, two years after a presidential election
OfficesAll 435 House seats, one-third of the Senate, most governorships
PatternPresident's party usually loses House seats

Midterm elections in the United States are the general elections held near the midpoint of a president's four-year term of office, on Election Day — the Tuesday after the first Monday in November — of even-numbered years not divisible by four. Federal offices contested include all 435 seats in the House of Representatives and whichever third of the Senate's seats are up for election, along with many state and local offices, including most governorships.

The midterm penalty

Historically, the president's party almost always loses seats in the House at the midterms — a pattern political scientists attribute to a check on the incumbent, lower turnout among the president's supporters, and exposure of marginal seats won in the presidential year. Notable exceptions include 1934, 1998 and 2002, when the president's party gained seats. Large midterm losses, as in 1994 and 2010, have repeatedly shifted control of Congress and reshaped the remainder of a presidency.

Turnout

Turnout in midterms is substantially lower than in presidential elections — typically around 40 per cent of the eligible electorate in recent decades, compared with 60 per cent or more in presidential years — though the 2018 midterms saw the highest midterm turnout in a century, followed by another high-turnout cycle in 2022.

Significance

Midterms determine control of Congress for the second half of a president's term, affecting legislation, oversight and investigations, and they elect the governors and state legislatures who administer elections and draw congressional maps after each census.
