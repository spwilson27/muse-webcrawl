Antiviral drug

Antiviral drugs are a class of medication used for treating viral infections. Unlike most antibiotics, which destroy bacteria, antivirals do not kill their target pathogen; instead they inhibit its development. Most antivirals are specific to particular viruses, in contrast to broad-spectrum antibiotics.

Mechanisms

Antivirals interfere with specific steps of the viral life cycle: attachment and entry into host cells, uncoating, genome replication, assembly, and release. Examples include acyclovir, a nucleoside analogue used against herpesviruses; oseltamivir, a neuraminidase inhibitor used against influenza; sofosbuvir, which inhibits the hepatitis C virus NS5B polymerase and can cure HCV infection in combination regimens; and nirmatrelvir/ritonavir, a protease inhibitor combination for COVID-19.

History

Because viruses replicate inside host cells, antiviral development lagged behind antibiotics. Early agents such as idoxuridine (1963) and acyclovir (approved 1982), developed by Gertrude Elion and colleagues, showed that selective toxicity was achievable. The HIV epidemic from the 1980s drove a major expansion of antiviral research.

Resistance

As with antibiotics, viruses can evolve resistance, especially during prolonged therapy in immunocompromised patients; combination therapy is used to limit resistance, notably for HIV and HCV.
