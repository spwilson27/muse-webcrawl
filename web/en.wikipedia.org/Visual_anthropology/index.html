Visual anthropology

Visual anthropology is a subfield of social anthropology concerned, in part, with the study and production of ethnographic photography, film, and, since the mid-1990s, new media. More recently it has been used by historians of science and visual culture. Although theories are increasingly instigated from works in other disciplines like film studies, visual anthropology as a discipline remains focused on anthropological problems.

History

Photography was used by anthropologists from the 19th century (e.g. in the work of Franz Boas and Bronisław Malinowski), and Robert Flaherty's Nanook of the North (1922) established ethnographic film. Margaret Mead and Gregory Bateson's photographic study Balinese Character (1942) and their film work promoted visual methods, though they remained marginal. Jean Rouch's "cinéma vérité" and ethnofiction, and the film programmes founded in the 1960s–80s (at Harvard, Manchester, and elsewhere), consolidated the field, along with institutions such as the Society for Visual Anthropology (founded 1984) and the journal Visual Anthropology (1987).

Scope and debates

Visual anthropologists study visual systems of other cultures, make films and photographs as research outputs, and analyse images reflexively — asking how media represent cultures and how subjects participate in their own representation. Issues of ethics, power, and collaboration, and the turn to sensory ethnography and digital media, are central contemporary concerns.
