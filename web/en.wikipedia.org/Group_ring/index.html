Group ring

Group ring
FieldAbstract algebra (representation theory)
NotationR[G], RG

Multiplication in a group ring extends the group operation by convolution

In algebra, a group ring is a free module and a ring constructed in a natural way from a given ring and a given group. As a free module, its elements are finite formal sums of elements of the group weighted by coefficients in the ring; addition is componentwise, and multiplication extends the group operation linearly, using a form of convolution.

Definition

Let G be a group and R a ring. The group ring R[G] (also written RG) is the set of formal sums Σg∈G rg g with almost all coefficients zero. Addition is Σ rg g + Σ sg g = Σ (rg + sg) g, and multiplication is determined by (Σ rg g)(Σ sh h) = Σ rg sh (gh), extended by distributivity. The identity element of G serves as the multiplicative identity, and R embeds via scalars. If G is finite of order n, then R[G] is a free R-module of rank n.

Examples

If G is the infinite cyclic group, R[G] is the ring of Laurent polynomials in one variable. The rational group algebra of a cyclic group of order n decomposes into cyclotomic fields. The group algebra ℂ[G] of a finite group is semisimple by Maschke's theorem, decomposing as a direct sum of matrix algebras—one for each irreducible representation—which makes group rings central to representation theory.

Properties and problems

Commutativity of R[G] is equivalent to commutativity of both R and G. Famous problems concern units and zero divisors: Kaplansky's unit conjecture (disproved in 2021 for group rings over the field with two elements) and the zero-divisor conjecture for torsion-free groups over fields remain influential in geometric group theory, with connections to the Novikov conjecture and L²-Betti numbers.
