Direct Cinema is a documentary genre that emerged in North America in the late 1950s and 1960s, made possible by the development of lightweight, portable 16mm film equipment with synchronized sound. Practitioners sought to record events as they unfolded, with the camera as a quiet observer — the "fly on the wall" ideal — avoiding voice-over narration, staged interviews, and intervention in the events filmed.

Origins

The movement coalesced around producer Robert Drew and his associates at Drew Associates, including Richard Leacock, D. A. Pennebaker, and Terence Macartney-Filgate, working for Time-Life Broadcast. Their landmark film Primary (1960) followed presidential candidates John F. Kennedy and Hubert Humphrey through the Wisconsin primary, using mobile cameras that could follow subjects through doors and crowds for the first time.

Characteristics and relationship to cinéma vérité

Direct Cinema is commonly contrasted with the contemporary French cinéma vérité of Jean Rouch: where vérité filmmakers (such as Rouch in Chronicle of a Summer) acknowledged and even provoked with the camera, Direct Cinema pursued observational detachment. Major works include Pennebaker's Dont Look Back (1967), the Maysles brothers' Salesman (1969) and Gimme Shelter (1970), and Frederick Wiseman's institutional studies beginning with Titicut Follies (1967).
