The Turpan water system is the karez (also spelled kariz) irrigation system of the Turpan Depression in Xinjiang, China. A karez is a gently sloping underground channel that taps groundwater at the base of mountains and delivers it by gravity to an oasis, minimizing evaporation in one of the world's driest and hottest places. The system has operated for more than two thousand years.

History

The oldest karez in Turpan are believed to date to the Han dynasty, over two thousand years ago, possibly introduced from Persia (where the same technology is known as qanat). At their peak, more than 1,000 karez chains with some 5,000 kilometres of underground channels irrigated the oasis cities of Turpan, the second-lowest place on earth outside the Dead Sea basin.

How a karez works

A karez begins with a "mother well" sunk into the water table at the foot of the Tian Shan. From it, a tunnel with a gradient of about 1:1000 runs toward the oasis, ventilated by a series of vertical shafts every 20–30 metres. Because the water flows underground, almost none is lost to evaporation. The karez supplies villages, the famous Turpan grape vineyards, and the flaming-red cotton fields of the depression.

Heritage status

The Turpan karez are considered one of the three great engineering projects of ancient China, alongside the Great Wall and the Grand Canal. Many have run dry in recent decades due to falling water tables and competing modern wells, and preservation efforts are ongoing.
