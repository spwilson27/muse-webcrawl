Admiralty law

Admiralty law or maritime law is a body of law that governs nautical issues and private maritime disputes. Admiralty law consists of both domestic law on maritime activities, and private international law governing the relationships between private parties operating or using ocean-going ships. It covers matters including marine commerce, navigation, shipping, salvage, seafarers' rights, and the carriage of goods and passengers by sea.

History

Maritime law is among the oldest bodies of law: the Rhodian Sea Law influenced Roman and Byzantine practice, and medieval compilations such as the Rolls of Oléron, the Consolato del Mare, and the laws of Wisby governed European seafaring. In England the Lord High Admiral's court developed a distinct admiralty jurisdiction, and in the United States admiralty jurisdiction is vested in the federal courts under the Constitution, with cases heard without a jury.

Key features

Distinctive features include maritime liens against vessels, the ability to proceed in rem against a ship itself, limitation of shipowners' liability, general average, salvage awards, and maintenance and cure for injured seamen. International conventions — including SOLAS, MARPOL, the Hague-Visby Rules, and the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea — supply much of the modern framework, though UNCLOS principally addresses public international law of the sea.
