Forced induction

Forced induction is the process of delivering air at an increased pressure to the intake of an internal combustion engine. A forced induction engine uses a compressor — either a turbocharger or a supercharger — to increase the pressure or density of the air supplied to the engine. With more oxygen available per cycle, more fuel can be burned and power output rises without increasing engine displacement.

Types

A turbocharger is driven by the engine's exhaust gases, recovering energy that would otherwise be wasted, but may exhibit a delay in boost response known as "turbo lag". A supercharger is mechanically driven by the engine, typically by a belt, providing immediate response at the cost of parasitic power draw. Some engines use both in sequence (twincharging), or pair a compressor with an electric motor. Compressed air may be cooled by an intercooler to increase density further and reduce the risk of detonation (knocking).

Applications

Forced induction is universal in modern diesel engines, dominant in downsized petrol engines designed for efficiency, and standard in high-performance and racing applications. It also compensates for the thinner air at altitude in piston-engine aircraft, maintaining sea-level power. Naturally aspirated engines, by contrast, rely solely on atmospheric pressure.

History

Experiments with supercharging date to the late 19th century; Gottlieb Daimler patented a forced-induction design in 1885. Alfred Büchi patented the turbocharger in 1905, and turbocharging saw wide use in military aircraft during the Second World War before spreading to production cars in the 1960s and 1970s.
