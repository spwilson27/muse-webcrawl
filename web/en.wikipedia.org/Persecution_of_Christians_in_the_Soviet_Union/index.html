Persecution of Christians in the Soviet Union

The Cathedral of Christ the Saviour in Moscow, demolished in 1931 and rebuilt in the 1990s.

Throughout the history of the Soviet Union (1917–1991), the official ideology of Marxism–Leninism, which mandated state atheism, was accompanied by varying degrees of persecution of Christians and religious believers. Soviet policy sought the elimination of religion and, at its most aggressive in the 1920s and 1930s, the eradication of religious institutions themselves.

Revolution and terror

Following the October Revolution, the 1918 Decree on Separation of Church and State disestablished the Russian Orthodox Church and confiscated church property. During the Civil War and the 1921–22 famine-relief confiscation campaign, clergy and believers were arrested and killed; Patriarch Tikhon was placed under house arrest and died in 1925. The League of Militant Atheists promoted atheist propaganda, churches, mosques and synagogues were closed by the tens of thousands, and in the Great Terror of 1937–38 clergy and active believers were executed in large numbers or sent to the Gulag. By 1941 only a few hundred Orthodox churches remained open in the USSR, down from more than 50,000 in 1914.

Wartime thaw and later campaigns

Stalin's government relaxed repression during the Second World War to mobilise patriotic support: the Patriarchate was restored in 1943 and thousands of churches reopened, though under tight state supervision through councils for religious affairs. Persecution renewed under Nikita Khrushchev's anti-religious campaign of 1959–1964, which closed about half of the reopened churches and targeted baptisms, monasteries and religious education. Under Brezhnev, control rather than elimination predominated: registered congregations were monitored, while unregistered Baptists, Pentecostals, Jehovah's Witnesses, catacomb Orthodox and other dissenting believers faced fines, imprisonment and psychiatric confinement. Only with Mikhail Gorbachev's glasnost reforms in the late 1980s — marked by the 1988 millennium of the Baptism of Rus' — did religious freedom substantially return.
