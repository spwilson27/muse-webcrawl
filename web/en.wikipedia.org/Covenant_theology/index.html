Covenant theology

Tradition: Reformed Christianity
Key covenants: Works, Grace, Redemption
Key figures: Johannes Cocceius, Herman Witsius, John Owen

Covenant theology (also known as covenantalism or federal theology) is a framework within Reformed theology that interprets the whole of Scripture through the concept of covenant — a binding relationship between God and humanity. It provides the theological structure underlying historic Presbyterian, Dutch Reformed, and many Reformed Baptist confessions, such as the Westminster Confession of Faith.

The three covenants

Classical covenant theology organizes redemptive history around three overarching covenants. The covenant of redemption is an eternal agreement within the Trinity concerning the salvation of the elect. The covenant of works was made with Adam before the Fall, promising life for obedience. The covenant of grace, instituted after the Fall and administered in various forms through Abraham, Moses, David, and finally Christ, promises salvation by grace through faith.

Development

The system developed among Reformed theologians of the 16th and 17th centuries, including Heinrich Bullinger, Zacharias Ursinus, Johannes Cocceius, and Herman Witsius, whose Economy of the Covenants became a standard treatment. It stands in contrast to dispensationalism, which divides history into distinct eras of God's dealing with humanity, and to Lutheran law–gospel frameworks.

Implications

Covenant theology undergirds practices such as infant baptism, which its adherents defend by appealing to the continuity of the covenant of grace and the inclusion of believers' children in the covenant community, and it shapes the Reformed reading of Israel and the church as essentially one people of God across both Testaments.
