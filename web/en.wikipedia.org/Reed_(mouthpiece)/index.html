Reed (mouthpiece)

A reed is a thin strip of material that vibrates to produce sound in a musical instrument. Reeds are used in woodwind instruments such as the clarinet, saxophone, oboe, and bassoon, as well as in free-reed instruments such as the harmonica, accordion, and reed organ. As air passes over or through the aperture the reed controls, its vibration chops the airflow into pulses, generating a tone.

Cane single reeds for clarinet and saxophone.

Single reeds

Single-reed instruments use one reed pressed against a mouthpiece with a table and window: clarinets, saxophones, and their relatives. The player blows across the reed, whose vibration against the mouthpiece produces the sound. Reeds are traditionally made from cane (Arundo donax) grown mainly in southern France and are graded by strength (hardness), with players choosing a strength suited to their mouthpiece and embouchure; synthetic reeds made from polymers are increasingly common.

Double reeds

Oboes, bassoons, English horns, and many folk instruments (shawms, crumhorns, bagpipe chanters) use a double reed: two pieces of cane bound together that vibrate against each other, held directly between the lips without a mouthpiece. Double reeds are usually handmade by the player and require constant adjustment.

Free reeds

In free-reed instruments, a metal (or cane) tongue riveted over a slot vibrates freely through the opening, driven in one direction of airflow or both. This family includes harmonicas, accordions, concertinas, harmoniums, and the melodica.

Care

Cane reeds are hygroscopic and warp or wear with use; players rotate several reeds, store them flat in reed cases, and replace them as they soften or chip. Reed adjustment — scraping and balancing — is a traditional skill among advanced players.
