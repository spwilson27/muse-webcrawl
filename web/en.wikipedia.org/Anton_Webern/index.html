Anton Webern

Born3 December 1883, Vienna, Austria-Hungary
Died15 September 1945 (aged 61), Mittersill, Austria
OccupationComposer, conductor
MovementSecond Viennese School; twelve-tone technique; serialism

Anton Webern (3 December 1883 – 15 September 1945) was an Austrian composer and conductor whose music was among the most radical and concentrated of the twentieth century. A pupil of Arnold Schoenberg and, with Schoenberg and Alban Berg, a member of the Second Viennese School, he moved from late-Romantic tonality through free atonality to the twelve-tone method, which he refined into structures of extreme brevity, symmetry, and economy that profoundly influenced post-war serialism.

Life

Born Anton von Webern in Vienna (he dropped the nobiliary particle in 1919), he studied musicology at the University of Vienna under Guido Adler and took a doctorate in 1906 with a dissertation on Heinrich Isaac. From 1904 to 1908 he studied composition privately with Schoenberg, forming a lifelong devotion to his teacher. He worked as a theatre conductor in Danzig, Stettin, Prague, and Vienna, and after the First World War conducted workers' choral societies and taught composition. His works were banned as "degenerate" after the Nazi annexation of Austria in 1938, and he withdrew to internal exile. On 15 September 1945, shortly after the war's end, he was accidentally shot and killed by an American soldier during a curfew raid in Mittersill.

Music

Webern's output is small — 31 opus-numbered works, most of them very short. The early Passacaglia, Op. 1 (1908) is still late-Romantic in style; the Five Movements for String Quartet, Op. 5 (1909) and Six Bagatelles, Op. 9 (1913) compress expression into aphorisms lasting seconds. From the Three Little Pieces for Cello and Piano, Op. 11, to the songs and cantatas, he explored Klangfarbenmelodie — melody distributed among instruments by timbre. Adopting Schoenberg's twelve-tone method in the mid-1920s, Webern built rows with internal symmetries, as in the Concerto, Op. 24, whose row consists of four versions of the same three-note cell, and the Symphony, Op. 21, with its canonic double mirror structures. His last works include the Variations for Piano, Op. 27, and two cantatas, Opp. 29 and 31.

Legacy

Neglected during his lifetime, Webern became the central model for the post-war avant-garde: Pierre Boulez, Karlheinz Stockhausen, and Luigi Nono took his serial rigour and pointillistic textures as the starting point for total serialism, and performers such as Robert Craft championed his recordings. His complete works can be heard in about three hours — a disproportion between scale and influence unmatched among major composers.
