Active site

The active site of an enzyme is the region that binds substrate molecules and directly participates in catalysing the chemical reaction. It is typically a pocket or cleft formed by amino acid residues that may be far apart in the primary sequence but are brought together by the protein's three-dimensional folding. Only a small fraction of an enzyme's residues constitute the active site; the rest of the protein provides the scaffold and the dynamics that make catalysis possible.

Structure

The active site contains two functional groupings: the binding site, whose residues recognise and orient the substrate through non-covalent interactions, and the catalytic site, whose residues perform the chemistry — acid–base catalysis, covalent catalysis, or metal-ion catalysis. Many enzymes also require cofactors (such as metal ions or coenzymes like NAD⁺) positioned within the active site.

Models of binding

Emil Fischer's "lock-and-key" model (1894) proposed that the active site is rigid and precisely complementary to the substrate. Daniel Koshland's "induced fit" model (1958) refined this: the active site moulds itself around the substrate, and this conformational change strains the substrate toward the transition state. Enzymes achieve their enormous rate enhancements largely by binding the transition state more tightly than the substrate itself, lowering the activation energy.

Specificity and inhibition

The geometry and chemistry of the active site determine enzyme specificity. Molecules resembling the substrate can act as competitive inhibitors by occupying the active site without reacting — a principle exploited by many drugs, such as statins (which inhibit the active site of HMG-CoA reductase) and HIV protease inhibitors.
