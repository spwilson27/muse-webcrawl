Electromagnetic interference

Electromagnetic interference (EMI), called radio-frequency interference (RFI) in the radio frequency context, is a disturbance generated by an external source that affects an electrical or electronic circuit by electromagnetic induction, electrostatic coupling, or conduction. The disturbance may degrade performance, corrupt data, or cause complete malfunction.

Sources

EMI sources include electric motors, ignition systems, power electronics and switching power supplies, fluorescent lamps, radio transmitters, lightning, solar storms, and electrostatic discharge. Interference may be conducted along power or signal lines, or radiated through space; it may be narrowband (from intentional transmitters) or broadband (from arcing or switching).

Effects and history

From the earliest days of radio, interference was a practical problem — operators of spark-gap transmitters blanketed wide bands. Famous incidents include consumer electronics disrupting aviation and medical equipment, leading to restrictions on in-flight device use, and the 1990s problem of GSM phone "buzz" in hearing aids and audio equipment. Intentional EMI is a form of electronic warfare (jamming).

Mitigation and regulation

Electromagnetic compatibility (EMC) engineering counters EMI with shielding (Faraday cages, shielded cables), filtering, grounding, twisted-pair wiring, spread-spectrum techniques, and good circuit-board layout. Regulators such as the US Federal Communications Commission (FCC Part 15) and international standards (CISPR, the EU EMC Directive) limit emissions from devices and set immunity requirements.
