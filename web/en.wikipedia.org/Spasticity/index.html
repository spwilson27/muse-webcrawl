Spasticity

Spasticity is a feature of altered skeletal muscle performance with a combination of paralysis, increased tendon reflex activity, and hypertonia. It is also colloquially referred to as an unusual "tightness", stiffness, or "pull" of muscles. Clinically, spasticity results from the loss of inhibition of motor neurons, causing excessive velocity-dependent muscle contraction. This ultimately leads to hyperreflexia, an exaggerated deep tendon reflex.

Definition

The classic definition, due to Lance (1980), is that spasticity is a motor disorder characterised by a velocity-dependent increase in tonic stretch reflexes ("muscle tone") with exaggerated tendon jerks, resulting from hyperexcitability of the stretch reflex, as one component of the upper motor neuron syndrome. The faster a muscle is stretched, the greater the resistance offered. A related phenomenon is the "clasp-knife response", in which resistance builds then suddenly gives way.

Causes

Spasticity is associated with damage to the corticospinal (pyramidal) tracts and other descending pathways that normally inhibit spinal reflexes. Common causes include cerebral palsy, multiple sclerosis, stroke, traumatic brain injury, spinal cord injury, and hereditary spastic paraplegia. Severity is commonly graded with the Modified Ashworth Scale.

Treatment

Management is multidisciplinary. Physical and occupational therapy, stretching, splinting and orthotics are first-line measures. Medications include oral baclofen, tizanidine, dantrolene, and benzodiazepines; focal spasticity is often treated with botulinum toxin injections or phenol nerve blocks. Severe generalised spasticity may be managed with intrathecal baclofen delivered by an implanted pump, and selected cases with surgical procedures such as selective dorsal rhizotomy. Treatment aims to improve function, ease care, relieve pain and prevent contractures, rather than to abolish tone entirely, since some patients rely on their stiffness for standing and transfers.
