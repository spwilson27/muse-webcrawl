The Hitler Diaries (Hitler-Tagebücher) were a series of sixty-two volumes of purported diaries written by Adolf Hitler, forged by Konrad Kujau and bought by the West German magazine Stern in 1983 for 9.3 million Deutsche Marks (about US$3.7 million at the time). Stern announced the sensational discovery on 25 April 1983 and sold serialisation rights to newspapers worldwide, including The Sunday Times in Britain and Newsweek in the United States. Within days, forensic examination by the West German Federal Archives showed the diaries to be crude forgeries written on post-war paper with modern inks and materials. The scandal forced resignations at Stern, ended the career of its reporter's editors, and became a byword for journalistic gullibility and the dangers of chequebook journalism.

Background

The story was built around a kernel of truth: in April 1945 a transport plane carrying Hitler's effects from Berlin crashed near Börnersdorf in Saxony, and some documents were recovered. Stern reporter Gerd Heidemann, an obsessive collector of Nazi memorabilia who had bought Hermann Göring's yacht Carin II, claimed that peasant rescuers had salvaged the diaries and that they had been smuggled out of East Germany by a general. His source was in fact the Stuttgart forger and militaria dealer Konrad Kujau, who fabricated the volumes—filling school exercise books bought in East Berlin with entries derived largely from published chronologies of Hitler's activities, such as Max Domarus's compilation of Hitler's speeches and proclamations. Kujau even misread the Gothic-letter monogram on the covers, rendering what was supposed to be "AH" as "FH".

Authentication failures

Stern's parent company, Gruner + Jahr, pressed ahead in secrecy to protect the scoop. Handwriting experts compared the diaries with genuine Hitler samples—some of which were themselves Kujau forgeries that had entered the market earlier—and pronounced them consistent. Historian Hugh Trevor-Roper (Lord Dacre), a director of Times Newspapers, examined the diaries in a Zurich bank vault and publicly vouched for their authenticity, though he later expressed doubts as the story's inconsistencies grew. The Sunday Times nonetheless published the first instalment on 24 April 1983, the day before Stern's announcement.

Exposure and trial

The West German Bundesarchiv's forensic tests quickly established that the paper contained optical brighteners not used until after the war, that the inks were modern and of differing recent ages, that the typewriter work was post-war, and that the content contained factual errors and verbatim copying—including errors—from Domarus. On 6 May 1983 the interior ministry announced the diaries were forgeries. Stern's editors Peter Koch and Felix Schmidt resigned. Kujau surrendered after a manhunt and confessed; Heidemann was arrested, and it emerged that much of the money paid by Stern had never reached Kujau—Heidemann had embezzled millions. In 1985 both men were convicted of fraud and sentenced to prison terms of about four and a half years.

Aftermath and cultural impact

The affair badly damaged Stern's credibility and stands as a classic case study in media ethics and the failure of verification under commercial pressure. It inspired the German comedy film Schtonk! (1992) and Robert Harris's book Selling Hitler (1986), later adapted as a television miniseries. Kujau, after his release, traded openly on his fame as a forger, selling "genuine Kujau forgeries" until his death in 2000. The diaries themselves are held by the German Federal Archives as a cautionary artefact.
