Camille Guérin

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Jean-Marie Camille Guérin (22 December 1872 – 9 June 1961) was a French veterinarian and bacteriologist who, together with physician Albert Calmette, developed the Bacillus Calmette–Guérin (BCG) vaccine against tuberculosis. The vaccine remains one of the most widely administered in the world.

Early life and career

Guérin was born in Poitiers, France. He studied veterinary medicine at the École nationale vétérinaire d'Alfort, graduating in 1896, and joined the newly founded Pasteur Institute of Lille, where he became head of a laboratory and later director of services. His early work concerned snake venom and the preparation of antivenoms, as well as rabies vaccination.

Development of BCG

Working with Calmette from 1908, Guérin helped culture a virulent strain of Mycobacterium bovis on a glycerine–bile–potato medium. Through roughly 230 subcultures over thirteen years, the team progressively attenuated the bacillus until it no longer caused disease while retaining its immunogenicity. The resulting strain was first used in a human infant in 1921, orally administered to a newborn whose mother had died of tuberculosis; the child remained healthy, and the vaccine was adopted across France and internationally.

Later life

Guérin continued at the Pasteur Institute in Paris, working on the production and standardisation of BCG and on veterinary vaccines. He died in Paris in 1961. The BCG vaccine that bears his and Calmette's names has been given to billions of people and is also used in immunotherapy for bladder cancer.
