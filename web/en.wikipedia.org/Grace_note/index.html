Grace note

A grace note is a kind of music notation denoting several kinds of musical ornamentation. It is usually printed in smaller type than the principal note and takes its rhythmic value from the note that follows (or occasionally the note that precedes), rather than counting within the measure's meter. Grace notes are typically written with a slash through the stem and flag.

Types

The two main historical categories are the appoggiatura ("leaning note"), which takes half the value of the following note (or two-thirds if the note is dotted), and the acciaccatura ("crushing note"), played extremely quickly before the beat. In Baroque and Classical practice, grace note interpretation varied by convention; in modern notation the acciaccatura is the more common form. Other ornaments related to grace notes include slides, mordents, and crushed notes in jazz and folk idioms.

Usage

Grace notes are central to several musical traditions. In Scottish bagpipe music, a repertoire of gracings—such as the G grace note—articulates repeated notes and ornaments melodies on an instrument with continuous airflow. In Baroque keyboard music, composers such as Couperin and Bach used grace notes extensively, and their realisation remains a subject of performance-practice scholarship. Drummers use "grace notes" (flams and drags) as quiet strokes immediately preceding a main stroke.

Notation

In modern engraving, a single grace note resembles a small quaver (eighth note) with a diagonal slash; groups of grace notes are beamed together in small type. Composers sometimes write out ornaments in full-size notes to remove ambiguity about their rhythmic placement.
