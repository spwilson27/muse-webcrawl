Latitude

In geography, latitude is a coordinate that specifies the north–south position of a point on the surface of the Earth or another celestial body. Latitude is given as an angle (symbol φ) ranging from 0° at the Equator to +90° at the North Pole and −90° at the South Pole. Lines of constant latitude, or parallels, run east–west as circles parallel to the equator. Together with longitude, latitude gives an exact position on the globe.

Named latitudes

Important parallels include the Equator (0°), the Tropic of Cancer (about 23°26′ N) and Tropic of Capricorn (23°26′ S), which mark the limits of the Sun's overhead passage, and the Arctic and Antarctic Circles (about 66°34′), beyond which the Sun can remain above or below the horizon for a full day. Latitude strongly influences climate: solar heating per unit area decreases from the equator toward the poles.

Definitions on the ellipsoid

Because the Earth is an oblate spheroid rather than a perfect sphere, several definitions are used. Geodetic latitude — the latitude of maps and GPS — is the angle between the equatorial plane and a line perpendicular to the reference ellipsoid's surface. Geocentric latitude is measured to the centre of the Earth; the two differ by up to about 11.5 minutes of arc. One degree of latitude corresponds to roughly 111 kilometres of surface distance, varying slightly from about 110.6 km at the equator to 111.7 km near the poles.

Determination

Latitude can be found astronomically from the altitude of the celestial pole or of the Sun at noon. Historically it was the easier coordinate to measure at sea, using instruments such as the astrolabe, quadrant, and sextant, whereas longitude required accurate timekeeping.
