Transalpine Gaul

Transalpine Gaul (Latin: Gallia Transalpina) was the Roman term for the part of Gaul lying "beyond the Alps" from the perspective of Italy, in contrast to Cisalpine Gaul (Gallia Cisalpina) on the Italian side. The term came to refer in particular to the Roman province in southern Gaul, later known as Gallia Narbonensis, extending along the Mediterranean coast from the Pyrenees to the Maritime Alps.

History

Rome became involved in Transalpine Gaul in the late 2nd century BC, intervening at the request of its ally Massilia (Marseille) against neighbouring Celtic and Ligurian tribes. After the defeat of the Allobroges and Arverni in 121 BC, Rome organised the province, sometimes simply called Provincia ("the Province" — the origin of the modern name Provence).

The province secured the land route between Italy and Hispania and was organised around the Via Domitia, the Roman road built from the Rhône to the Pyrenees in about 118 BC. The colony of Narbo Martius (Narbonne) was founded in the same period and gave the province its later name, Gallia Narbonensis.

Later usage

After Julius Caesar's conquest of the rest of Gaul in the Gallic Wars (58–50 BC), "Transalpine Gaul" could be used more broadly for all of Gaul beyond the Alps, including the "long-haired Gaul" (Gallia Comata) organised into the Three Gauls — Aquitania, Lugdunensis and Belgica. Under Augustus and his successors the terminology shifted, and Narbonensis remained the formal name for the original southern province.
