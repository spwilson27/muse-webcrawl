Decimal

The decimal numeral system, also called base ten or denary, is the standard system for denoting integer and non-integer numbers. It is the most widely used number system in the world, employing ten digits, 0 through 9, with the value of each digit determined by its position.

Decimal notation

In decimal notation a number is written as a sequence of digits in which each position represents a power of ten. A decimal point separates the integer part from the fractional part: in 247.35, the digits represent 2×10² + 4×10¹ + 7×10⁰ + 3×10⁻¹ + 5×10⁻². Fractions whose denominators have only the prime factors 2 and 5 have terminating decimal expansions; all other rational numbers produce repeating decimals, such as 1/3 = 0.333…

History

Counting in tens likely arose from the ten fingers on two hands. Positional decimal notation with a symbol for zero was developed in India and transmitted through Arabic mathematics to Europe, which is why the digits are often called Hindu–Arabic numerals. Decimal fractions were systematically described by Simon Stevin in 1585, and the system gradually displaced Roman numerals and fractional systems based on sixtieths for everyday calculation.

Use

Decimal is used in nearly all modern measurement: the metric system is built on decimal scaling, and currencies are generally divided into hundredths. Computers, however, use binary internally, which is why some simple decimal fractions, such as 0.1, cannot be represented exactly in floating-point arithmetic.
