Final girl

The final girl is a trope in horror films (especially slasher films). It refers to the last girl or woman alive to confront the killer, ostensibly the one left to tell the story. The final girl has been observed in many films, including The Texas Chain Saw Massacre, Halloween, Alien, Friday the 13th, A Nightmare on Elm Street, and Scream.

[Image placeholder: A lone heroine confronting the killer in the climax of a slasher film]

The term was coined by Carol J. Clover in her 1992 book Men, Women, and Chainsaws: Gender in the Modern Horror Film. Clover argued that the final girl is typically resourceful, intelligent, and watchful; she is often the character with the clearest moral sense, and she frequently survives by adopting traditionally "masculine" traits and weapons, defeating the killer herself or being rescued at the last moment.

Characteristics

Clover identified several recurring characteristics of the final girl. She is often sexually unavailable or virginal compared with her more sexually active friends, who are killed over the course of the film. She is intelligent, resourceful, and mechanically adept; she notices the danger before the other characters do and is the one who investigates. In the climax she is chased, cornered, wounded, and whimpering in fear, but she ultimately rises to defeat the killer, frequently with an improvised weapon or the killer's own weapon.

The final girl is also the character with whom the audience is most encouraged to identify. Clover noted that much of the slasher audience is young and male, and argued that the final girl's androgynous or boyish qualities — including names such as Laurie, Sidney, or Ripley — facilitate cross-gender identification.

Notable examples

  • Sally Hardesty in The Texas Chain Saw Massacre (1974)
  • Laurie Strode (Jamie Lee Curtis) in Halloween (1978)
  • Ellen Ripley (Sigourney Weaver) in Alien (1979)
  • Alice Hardy in Friday the 13th (1980)
  • Nancy Thompson (Heather Langenkamp) in A Nightmare on Elm Street (1984)
  • Sidney Prescott (Neve Campbell) in Scream (1996)
  • Erin Harson in You're Next (2011)

Evolution of the trope

Later films have complicated or subverted the trope. Heroines such as Sidney Prescott survive across entire franchises and develop over multiple films, becoming experienced combatants rather than terrified survivors. Other films invert the formula entirely, presenting the "final girl" from the killer's perspective or revealing that the heroine is herself dangerous. Scholars continue to debate whether the trope empowers female characters or reinforces restrictive norms about female sexuality and survival.
