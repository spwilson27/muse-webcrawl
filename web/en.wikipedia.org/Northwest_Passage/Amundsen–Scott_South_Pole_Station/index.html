The Amundsen–Scott South Pole Station is a United States scientific research station at the geographic South Pole, the southernmost point on Earth. It is named in honour of Roald Amundsen of Norway, who reached the pole in December 1911, and Robert Falcon Scott of the United Kingdom, whose party arrived a month later and perished on the return journey.

History

The original station was built by the U.S. Navy in 1956–57 for the International Geophysical Year and has been continuously occupied ever since. It was replaced in 1975 by a geodesic dome under which buildings sheltered from drifting snow; accumulating snow eventually buried the dome. The present elevated station, designed to let wind scour snow from beneath it, was progressively occupied from 1999 and formally dedicated on 12 January 2008.

Research

The station supports research in astronomy, astrophysics, glaciology, geophysics, meteorology, and atmospheric science. Its high, dry, stable atmosphere makes it an exceptional observing site: instruments there include the South Pole Telescope, the BICEP/Keck cosmic microwave background experiments, and the IceCube Neutrino Observatory, a cubic-kilometre detector buried in the ice that announced the first detection of high-energy astrophysical neutrinos in 2013.

Life at the pole

The population reaches about 150 in the austral summer and falls to roughly 40–50 winter-over staff during the months of darkness, when the station is completely isolated. All personnel and cargo arrive by ski-equipped LC-130 aircraft from McMurdo Station, and the average annual temperature is about −49 °C. See also Antarctica.
