Antarctica is Earth's southernmost and least-populated continent, containing the geographic South Pole. Almost entirely south of the Antarctic Circle and surrounded by the Southern Ocean, it is the coldest, driest, and windiest continent, and is covered by an ice sheet that holds about 61 percent of the world's fresh water and roughly 90 percent of its ice.

History

The continent was first sighted in 1820 by expeditions under Fabian Gottlieb von Bellingshausen, Edward Bransfield, and Nathaniel Palmer. Landings and exploration followed through the 19th century, culminating in the Heroic Age of Antarctic Exploration: Roald Amundsen's party reached the South Pole in December 1911, a month ahead of Robert Falcon Scott's ill-fated expedition. Seven nations made territorial claims in the 20th century, but the Antarctic Treaty of 1959 set the continent aside for peaceful scientific research.

Geography and climate

The Transantarctic Mountains divide the continent into the larger East Antarctica, a high ice-covered plateau, and the smaller West Antarctica, partly occupied by marine-based ice vulnerable to collapse. Antarctica is the coldest place on Earth: the lowest natural temperature ever recorded, −89.2 °C, was measured at Vostok Station in 1983. Precipitation is so low that the interior qualifies as a polar desert.

Wildlife and research

Terrestrial life is limited mainly to mosses, lichens, algae, and invertebrates such as mites and midges, while the surrounding ocean supports krill, penguins, seals, and whales. Around 70 research stations operated by some 30 countries host scientists studying glaciology, climate, astronomy, and biology. The ozone hole, discovered over Antarctica in 1985, led to the Montreal Protocol banning ozone-depleting substances.
