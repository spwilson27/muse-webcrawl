South Pole

The South Pole, also known as the Geographic South Pole or Terrestrial South Pole, is the point in the Southern Hemisphere where the Earth's axis of rotation meets its surface. It is located on the continent of Antarctica, at an elevation of about 2,835 metres (9,301 ft) above sea level, on a nearly featureless, windswept plateau of ice nearly 2,700 metres thick.

Exploration

The race for the pole between Norwegian Roald Amundsen and Briton Robert Falcon Scott was a defining episode of the Heroic Age of Antarctic Exploration. Amundsen's party of five, using skis and dog sleds and a base at the Bay of Whales, reached the pole on 14 December 1911 and returned safely. Scott's party reached the pole on 17–18 January 1912, finding Amundsen's tent, and all five men perished on the return journey. The pole was next visited in 1929, when Richard E. Byrd made the first flight over it, and was not reached overland again until 1956, when the Amundsen–Scott station was established.

Geography and climate

The Geographic South Pole is marked by a small sign and a marker repositioned each New Year's Day, because the ice sheet moves about 10 metres per year. A separate ceremonial pole, surrounded by the flags of Antarctic Treaty signatories, is maintained for photographs. The pole experiences one sunrise and one sunset per year, with six months of continuous daylight and six months of polar night. Temperatures range from about −12 °C in summer to below −70 °C in winter. Other "south poles" include the South Magnetic Pole, the geomagnetic pole, the Southern Pole of Cold, and the southern pole of inaccessibility, the point on Antarctica farthest from any coastline.

Research

The Amundsen–Scott South Pole Station supports research in astronomy and astrophysics — including the South Pole Telescope and the IceCube Neutrino Observatory — as well as glaciology, seismology and atmospheric science, taking advantage of the exceptionally cold, dry and stable air above the plateau.
