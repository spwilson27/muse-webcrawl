Northwest Passage

The Northwest Passage is the sea route between the Atlantic and Pacific oceans through the Arctic Ocean, along the northern coast of North America via waterways through the Canadian Arctic Archipelago. For centuries, European explorers sought such a route as a possible shorter trade passage to Asia. The first complete transit was made by Roald Amundsen in 1903–1906 in the small ship Gjøa.

Exploration

Early attempts included voyages by Martin Frobisher (1570s), John Davis, Henry Hudson, and William Baffin. The search intensified after 1818 under John Barrow of the Admiralty. Sir John Franklin's 1845 expedition with HMS Erebus and HMS Terror ended in disaster, with all 129 men lost; dozens of rescue expeditions followed. Robert McClure traversed the passage in 1850–1854 partly by ship and partly by sledge. Amundsen's 1903–1906 voyage in Gjøa was the first single-vessel transit.

Routes

Several routes wind through the islands of the Arctic Archipelago, including via Lancaster Sound, Barrow Strait, and either Prince of Wales Strait or James Ross Strait and King William Island. Depths vary; some channels are too shallow for deep-draught shipping.

Modern use and climate change

Declining Arctic sea ice has made the passage increasingly navigable in summer. In 2007 the European Space Agency reported the passage fully navigable for the first time in satellite records. Transits by cargo vessels, cruise ships and pleasure craft have grown. Canada claims the waters as internal waters; the United States and others regard them as an international strait — a sovereignty dispute that gains importance as traffic increases.
