The North Pole, also known as the geographic North Pole or terrestrial North Pole, is the point in the Northern Hemisphere where the Earth's axis of rotation meets its surface. It is the northernmost point on Earth and lies diametrically opposite the South Pole. Unlike the South Pole, which rests on a continental landmass, the North Pole sits in the middle of the Arctic Ocean amid drifting sea ice.

Exploration

Claims by Frederick Cook (1908) and Robert Peary (1909) to have reached the pole on foot remain disputed. The first undisputed sighting was in 1926, when Roald Amundsen, Umberto Nobile, and Lincoln Ellsworth overflew the pole in the airship Norge. The nuclear submarine USS Nautilus passed beneath the pole in 1958, and USS Skate surfaced there in 1959. The first confirmed surface expedition to reach the pole was Ralph Plaisted's snowmobile party in 1968; the first undisputed dogsled journey was Wally Herbert's in 1969.

Environment

At the pole the sun rises and sets only once a year, producing six months of continuous daylight and six months of polar night. Sea ice thickness typically ranges from two to three metres, though open leads occur, and Arctic warming has thinned the ice pack in recent decades. The seabed beneath is crossed by the Lomonosov Ridge, and Russia, Denmark (via Greenland), and Canada have submitted claims to extended continental shelf rights encompassing the pole.

Definitions

The geographic pole is distinct from the north magnetic pole, to which compasses point, and from the north geomagnetic pole. The axial pole itself shifts by several metres per year owing to polar motion.
