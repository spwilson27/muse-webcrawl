Postfeminism

Postfeminism is a term used to describe a range of cultural, media and theoretical positions that react to, revise, or declare superseded the feminism of the second wave. The term has been used in contradictory ways since the early 1980s: in popular journalism it often implied that feminism's goals had been achieved and were no longer needed, while in academic media studies — notably in the work of scholars such as Angela McRobbie and Rosalind Gill — it describes a cultural sensibility in which feminist ideas are simultaneously taken for granted and repudiated.

Meanings and uses

Media scholar Rosalind Gill characterised the postfeminist sensibility by an emphasis on individualism, choice and empowerment; the treatment of femininity as a bodily property to be worked on; a shift from objectification to "sexual subjectification"; intensive self-surveillance; and irony and knowingness. Angela McRobbie described postfeminism as a "double entanglement", in which neo-liberal values and feminist vocabulary coexist: young women are addressed as empowered subjects precisely as collective feminist politics is dismantled.

History

The term appeared in print as early as 1919, and was revived in the 1980s, for example in Susan Faludi's account of a media "backlash" against feminism in Backlash: The Undeclared War Against American Women (1991). In 1990s popular culture, postfeminist themes were identified in television series and films centred on independent professional women, and in the "girl power" rhetoric of groups such as the Spice Girls. Some theorists instead link postfeminism with third-wave feminism and postmodern critiques of essentialism, treating the "post" as a challenge to feminism from within rather than its end.

Criticism

Critics argue that postfeminism depoliticises gender inequality by recasting it as personal choice, and that its celebration of consumer empowerment is unevenly available across class and race. Others defend aspects of the term as a useful description of contemporary media culture or as a genuine reworking of feminist ideas for new conditions.
