Dried fruit

Dried fruit is fruit from which the majority of the original water content has been removed, either naturally through sun drying, freeze-drying, or using specialised dryers or dehydrators. Drying preserves fruit far beyond its fresh shelf life and concentrates its sugars and flavour.

History

Dried fruit has been produced in the Middle East and Mediterranean since the fourth millennium BC; dates, figs, and grapes were staples of trade. Raisins (dried grapes), currants, and dried plums (prunes) were valued in Roman cuisine, and dried fruits spread along trade routes across Asia and Europe. Traditional dried fruits include raisins, dates, prunes, figs, and apricots; many other fruits — mango, pineapple, cranberries, apples, and bananas — are commonly dried today, sometimes with added sugar or sulfur dioxide as a preservative.

Nutrition

Dried fruit is energy-dense and rich in fibre, potassium, and various micronutrients, but also high in natural sugars by weight. Most of the vitamin C of fresh fruit is lost in drying. Dried fruit is used in cooking, baking (such as fruitcake and granola), trail mix, and as a snack.

Production

Traditional sun drying remains common for grapes and figs, while industrial production uses tunnel dehydrators that control temperature and airflow. Freeze-drying preserves colour and nutrients better but is more expensive. Global production is concentrated in the United States (California raisins), Turkey (apricots, figs, raisins), and Iran (dates, raisins, pistachio-adjacent trade).
