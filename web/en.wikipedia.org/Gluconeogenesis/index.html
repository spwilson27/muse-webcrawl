Gluconeogenesis

Gluconeogenesis (GNG) is a metabolic pathway that results in the generation of glucose from certain non-carbohydrate carbon substrates. It is a ubiquitous process, present in plants, animals, fungi, bacteria, and other microorganisms. In vertebrates it takes place mainly in the liver and, to a lesser extent, in the cortex of the kidneys, and it is essential for keeping blood glucose levels up during fasting, starvation, low-carbohydrate diets, and intense exercise.

Substrates and pathway

The main substrates are lactate (recycled from anaerobic glycolysis in the Cori cycle), glucogenic amino acids such as alanine and glutamine, glycerol from fat breakdown, and propionate. Most steps of gluconeogenesis are the reverse of glycolysis, but the three irreversible glycolytic steps are bypassed by distinct enzymes: pyruvate carboxylase and PEPCK (bypassing pyruvate kinase), fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase (bypassing phosphofructokinase), and glucose-6-phosphatase (bypassing hexokinase), the last being found only in liver and kidney, which allows those organs to release free glucose into the blood.

Regulation

Gluconeogenesis is energetically expensive, costing six high-energy phosphate bonds per glucose. It is stimulated by glucagon and cortisol during fasting and inhibited by insulin and by the drug metformin. Reciprocal regulation with glycolysis — for example through fructose 2,6-bisphosphate — prevents futile cycling. Excessive hepatic glucose output via gluconeogenesis is a major contributor to high blood sugar in type 2 diabetes.
