Uterine rupture is a tear through the full thickness of the uterine wall, most often through a previous caesarean scar.

Uterine rupture is when the muscular wall of the uterus tears during pregnancy or childbirth. It is a serious obstetric emergency associated with substantial maternal and perinatal mortality and morbidity. Rupture most commonly occurs along a scar from a previous caesarean section during labour, but it can also occur in an unscarred uterus, typically in the setting of obstructed labour, uterine overdistension, trauma, or injudicious use of uterotonic drugs.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms may include severe abdominal pain that persists between contractions, abnormal vaginal bleeding, cessation of contractions, recession of the presenting part of the fetus, and signs of haemorrhagic shock in the mother (rapid pulse, falling blood pressure). The most common fetal sign is an abnormal fetal heart rate pattern, particularly prolonged bradycardia. In a complete rupture the fetus and placenta may be expelled into the abdominal cavity.

Risk factors

  • Previous caesarean delivery, especially with a classical (vertical) incision or a short interpregnancy interval;
  • Previous myomectomy or other uterine surgery, or previous rupture;
  • Trial of labour after caesarean (TOLAC), particularly with prostaglandin induction or high-dose oxytocin augmentation;
  • Obstructed labour, malpresentation, and grand multiparity — the leading causes in low-resource settings;
  • Abdominal trauma and uterine anomalies.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is usually clinical and made at emergency laparotomy; there is no reliable screening test. Scar dehiscence — separation of a previous scar with intact overlying serosa — is a related but less dangerous finding, often discovered incidentally at repeat caesarean section.

Treatment

Management requires immediate resuscitation with intravenous fluids and blood transfusion, broad-spectrum antibiotics, and emergency laparotomy. The baby is delivered, and the uterus is either repaired — in most cases where the tear is limited and the mother stable — or removed (hysterectomy) when the rupture is extensive, uncontrollable bleeding occurs, or the bladder is involved. Speed of intervention is the main determinant of fetal survival.

Epidemiology and prognosis

In high-income countries, rupture of an unscarred uterus occurs in roughly 1 in 8,000–17,000 deliveries, while rupture complicates about 0.5–1 percent of trials of labour after one previous low-transverse caesarean. Rates are much higher where obstructed labour is common and access to caesarean delivery is delayed. Perinatal mortality remains significant even with prompt care; maternal death is uncommon in well-resourced settings but remains a leading cause of obstetric mortality worldwide.
