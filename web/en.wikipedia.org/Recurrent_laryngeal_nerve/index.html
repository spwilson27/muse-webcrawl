The recurrent laryngeal nerve (RLN) is a branch of the vagus nerve (cranial nerve X) that supplies all the intrinsic muscles of the larynx, with the exception of the cricothyroid muscles. There are two recurrent laryngeal nerves, right and left, which take strikingly different and famously circuitous courses through the body.

The left nerve loops under the aortic arch; the right loops under the subclavian artery.

Course

The nerves branch from the vagus in the chest. The right recurrent laryngeal nerve loops under the right subclavian artery and ascends in the tracheoesophageal groove. The left nerve travels farther: it descends into the thorax, loops under the arch of the aorta at the ligamentum arteriosum, and then ascends to the larynx. This asymmetry reflects the embryological development of the aortic arch system.

Evolutionary significance

The detour is often cited as evidence of evolution by descent with modification. In fish, the nerve passes directly behind the gill arches; in tetrapods the neck lengthened and the heart descended, dragging the nerve with it. In the giraffe, the left recurrent laryngeal nerve may travel more than 4.5 metres down the neck and back, although its target lies only centimetres from its origin. In some sauropod dinosaurs the equivalent path may have exceeded 25 metres.

Clinical significance

The nerve is vulnerable during thyroid and parathyroid surgery, anterior cervical spine surgery, and mediastinal operations. Injury causes vocal cord paresis or paralysis, producing hoarseness, a weak cough, and aspiration risk; bilateral injury can obstruct the airway. Enlargement of the left atrium or aortic aneurysm can compress the left nerve, causing Ortner's syndrome (cardiovocal syndrome). A rare variant, the non-recurrent laryngeal nerve (about 0.5–1% on the right, associated with an aberrant right subclavian artery), runs directly from the vagus to the larynx and is at particular risk in neck surgery.
