Precipitation hardening

Age hardening strengthens alloys by forming fine precipitate particles that impede dislocation motion.

Precipitation hardening, also called age hardening or particle hardening, is a heat treatment technique used to increase the yield strength of malleable materials, including most structural alloys of aluminium, magnesium, nickel, titanium, and some steels and stainless steels. It relies on changes in solid solubility with temperature to produce fine particles of an impurity phase that impede the movement of dislocations, the primary mechanism of plastic deformation in metals.

Process

The technique involves three main steps. First, solution treatment heats the alloy to a high temperature so that alloying elements dissolve into a single-phase solid solution. Second, rapid quenching traps these elements in a supersaturated solid solution at room temperature. Third, ageing at a moderate temperature allows the dissolved elements to diffuse and form finely dispersed precipitate particles — often coherent intermetallic phases such as GP zones and the θ′ phase in aluminium–copper alloys.

History and applications

Age hardening was discovered accidentally in 1906 by Alfred Wilm, who found that an aluminium alloy containing copper, magnesium and manganese (later marketed as Duralumin) grew harder over several days after quenching. The phenomenon was explained in 1919 by Paul Merica and co-workers in terms of precipitation from supersaturated solution. Precipitation-hardened alloys are now essential in aerospace structures (e.g. 2024 and 7075 aluminium), nickel-based superalloys for turbine blades (strengthened by the γ′ phase, Ni3(Al,Ti)), and precipitation-hardening stainless steels such as 17-4 PH.
