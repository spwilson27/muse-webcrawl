The Chinese classics or canonical texts (經書, jīngshū) are the works of Chinese literature authored prior to the establishment of the imperial state in 221 BC, principally during the Zhou dynasty, and traditionally associated with the sage Confucius and the school of Confucianism. After Confucius's death, Confucian scholars organised his teachings and the associated texts into canons, and along with the curriculum known as the Four Books and Five Classics, these formed the basis of education and the imperial examination system for over two thousand years.

Five Classics

The Five Classics (五經, Wǔjīng) traditionally attributed to Confucius as editor or author are:

  • Classic of Poetry (詩經, Shījīng) — an anthology of 305 poems dating from the 11th to 7th centuries BC.
  • Book of Documents (書經, Shūjīng) — a collection of speeches and records attributed to rulers and ministers of early China.
  • Book of Rites (禮經, Lǐjīng) — describing social forms, administration, and ceremonial rites of the Zhou dynasty.
  • I Ching or Book of Changes (易經, Yìjīng) — a divination manual built around 64 hexagrams.
  • Spring and Autumn Annals (春秋, Chūnqiū) — a terse chronicle of the state of Lu from 722 to 481 BC.

A sixth work, the Classic of Music (樂經), is traditionally counted but was lost by the Han dynasty.

Four Books

The Four Books (四書, Sìshū), selected by the Neo-Confucian philosopher Zhu Xi in the 12th century, are the Great Learning, the Doctrine of the Mean, the Analects of Confucius, and the Mencius. From 1313 until 1905 they served as the core curriculum of the civil service examinations.

Expanded canons

Over time the canon grew: the Han dynasty recognised five classics; the Tang dynasty expanded the list to nine; and by the Song dynasty there were Thirteen Classics (十三經), adding works such as the Erya dictionary, the Classic of Filial Piety, and the commentaries on the Spring and Autumn Annals.

Influence

The classics shaped education, law, and political thought across East Asia, including Korea, Japan, and Vietnam. Mastery of the texts was the principal route to government office in imperial China, and their ethical teachings remain foundational to Confucian culture.
