Balancing selection

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
TypeNatural selection
EffectMaintains genetic polymorphism
MechanismsHeterozygote advantage; negative frequency-dependent selection

Balancing selection refers to a number of selective processes by which multiple alleles (different versions of a gene) are actively maintained in the gene pool of a population at frequencies larger than expected from genetic drift alone.

Mechanisms

The best-known mechanism is heterozygote advantage (overdominance), in which individuals carrying two different alleles have higher fitness than either homozygote. A classic example is the sickle-cell allele, maintained in malaria-endemic regions because heterozygotes resist malaria while avoiding severe sickle-cell disease.

Negative frequency-dependent selection, in which an allele's fitness declines as it becomes more common, also maintains diversity; it operates in host–parasite interactions and in the self-incompatibility systems of flowering plants.

Examples

The highly polymorphic major histocompatibility complex (MHC) genes of vertebrates are thought to be maintained by balancing selection, as are ABO blood group polymorphisms, which in some lineages predate the species themselves (trans-species polymorphism).
