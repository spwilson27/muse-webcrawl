Endomorphism

In mathematics, an endomorphism is a morphism from a mathematical object to itself. An endomorphism that is also an isomorphism is an automorphism. For example, an endomorphism of a vector space V is a linear operator f : V → V; an endomorphism of a group G is a group homomorphism h : G → G. One can compose endomorphisms of any object, so the endomorphisms of an object form an algebraic structure in their own right.

Algebraic structures

In most categories the set of endomorphisms of an object X, written End(X), can be composed and forms a monoid under composition. In additive settings such as vector spaces, modules, and abelian groups, endomorphisms can also be added, making End(X) a ring — the endomorphism ring. For a finite-dimensional vector space, End(V) is isomorphic to the ring of n × n matrices over the underlying field, once a basis is chosen.

Examples

  • On the vector space of polynomials, differentiation is an endomorphism, as is multiplication by a variable.
  • On a group, conjugation by a fixed element is an endomorphism (indeed an automorphism), called an inner automorphism.
  • The Frobenius endomorphism on algebraic varieties over finite fields raises coordinates to the p-th power.
  • A projection operator, sending each point to its shadow on a subspace, is an idempotent endomorphism.

Operator theory

In functional analysis, endomorphisms of infinite-dimensional spaces are simply linear operators on a space, and their study — eigenvalues, spectra, invariant subspaces — is a central topic. In finite dimensions, the eigenvalues of an endomorphism are the roots of its characteristic polynomial.
