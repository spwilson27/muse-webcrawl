Bell's palsy

Bell's palsy is a type of facial paralysis that results in a temporary inability to control the facial muscles on the affected side of the face. It is the most common cause of one-sided facial muscle weakness, accounting for roughly 70 percent of cases of facial nerve (cranial nerve VII) palsy. The condition is named after the Scottish surgeon Charles Bell, who described the function of the facial nerve in the nineteenth century.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms usually appear suddenly over hours to a few days and affect the entire half of the face, including the forehead — a feature that distinguishes Bell's palsy from the facial weakness of stroke, in which the forehead is relatively spared. Signs include drooping of the eyebrow and mouth, inability to close the eye on the affected side, drooling, loss of taste on the front two-thirds of the tongue, increased sensitivity to sound (hyperacusis), and sometimes pain behind the ear.

Cause

The exact cause is unknown, but inflammation and swelling of the facial nerve within its narrow bony canal (the fallopian canal) is thought to compress the nerve. Reactivation of herpes simplex virus type 1 has been proposed as a trigger, though the evidence is not conclusive. Risk factors include diabetes, pregnancy (particularly the third trimester), obesity, and hypertension.

Treatment and prognosis

Corticosteroids such as prednisolone, started within 72 hours of onset, significantly improve the chance of complete recovery; antiviral agents may add a small benefit in severe cases. Eye protection — lubricating drops, ointment, and taping the eye shut at night — is essential when eye closure is impaired. Most people recover fully: about 70 percent recover completely even without treatment, and with steroids the figure rises to roughly 85–90 percent. A minority have permanent weakness, synkinesis (involuntary linked movements), or crocodile tears (gustatory lacrimation).

Differential diagnosis

Other causes of facial nerve palsy include Lyme disease, Ramsay Hunt syndrome (herpes zoster oticus), stroke, tumours of the parotid gland or cerebellopontine angle, sarcoidosis, and Guillain–Barré syndrome.
