The Baloch people are an Iranian ethnic group native to the Balochistan region of Pakistan, Iran, and Afghanistan, an arid area straddling the borders of the three countries along the northern coast of the Arabian Sea. They speak Balochi, a Northwestern Iranian language closely related to Kurdish. The total Baloch population is estimated at around ten million, with the largest share in Pakistan's Balochistan province, whose capital is Quetta and whose chief port is Gwadar.

History

Baloch origins are debated; their own epic traditions trace migrations from the west, possibly from the vicinity of Aleppo or the Caspian region, into Makran during the early medieval period. By the fifteenth century, under leaders such as Mir Chakar Rind, Baloch tribal confederacies dominated the region. In the seventeenth century the Khanate of Kalat emerged as the leading Baloch state. British expansion in the nineteenth century divided Balochistan among British India, Persia, and Afghanistan — a partition formalised by the Durand Line (1893) and the Goldsmid Line — which Baloch nationalists still resent. In 1948 the Khan of Kalat acceded to Pakistan; insurgencies advocating greater autonomy or independence have recurred in Pakistani Balochistan since.

Culture

Baloch society is traditionally tribal, organised under chiefs (sardars) and bound by a code of honour known as Balochmayar, which prizes hospitality, refuge for the persecuted, and vengeance for wrongs. Balochi culture is rich in oral epic poetry, embroidery, and music featuring instruments such as the sorud fiddle and benju. Traditional dress includes the shalwar kameez with voluminous trousers, turbans for men, and elaborately embroidered long dresses and headscarves for women. Many Baloch were historically nomadic pastoralists herding sheep, goats, and camels; today agriculture, fishing along the Makran coast, mining, and labour migration to the Gulf are major livelihoods.
