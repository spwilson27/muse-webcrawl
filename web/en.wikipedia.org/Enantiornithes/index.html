The Enantiornithes, also known as the enantiornithines, are a group of extinct avialans ("birds" in the broad sense), the most abundant and diverse group known from the Mesozoic era. Almost all retained teeth and clawed fingers on each wing, but otherwise looked much like modern birds.

Name and anatomy

The name means "opposite birds", after the way the shoulder bones articulate: in enantiornithines the coracoid and scapula form a ball-and-socket joint reversed relative to that of modern birds (Euornithes), a sign that the two groups evolved powered flight anatomy in parallel in some respects. They ranged from sandpiper-sized forms to gobipterygiids with wingspans over a metre.

Distribution and ecology

Enantiornithines are known from Cretaceous deposits worldwide, including the Jehol Biota of China, the Las Hoyas and La Huérguina formations of Spain, and amber deposits of Myanmar, where feathered hatchlings have been found preserved entire. Many were arboreal and adapted to perching; others were shorebirds or, like the flightless Patagopteryx (sometimes excluded), secondarily terrestrial. They occupied many of the ecological niches held by modern birds.

Extinction

Despite their success for more than 65 million years, all enantiornithines died out in the Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction event 66 million years ago, along with the hesperornithines and non-avian dinosaurs. Only a few lineages of euornithines survived to give rise to modern birds.
