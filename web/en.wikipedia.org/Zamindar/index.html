Zamindar

A zamindar (from Persian zamīn, "land", and the suffix -dār, "holder") was, in the Indian subcontinent, an autonomous or semi-autonomous ruler and hereditary tax collector of a tract of land under the Mughal Empire and, later, a landowner recognized under British colonial rule. Zamindars collected revenue from the peasantry (ryots) on behalf of the state and, in return, retained a share; great zamindars maintained courts, armies, and estates, functioning almost as local princes.

Under the Mughals

In the Mughal administrative system, zamindars were intermediary landholders who collected land revenue from cultivators and remitted a fixed portion to the imperial treasury. The category ranged from powerful rajas controlling vast territories to petty local chiefs. Unlike the appointed mansabdars, zamindari rights were generally hereditary, and zamindars were expected to maintain order and promote cultivation in their areas.

Under British rule

The East India Company's Permanent Settlement of 1793 in Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa transformed zamindars into proprietors of the land itself: their revenue obligation to the state was fixed in perpetuity, but they could lose their estates by auction if they failed to pay by the due date. Many old families were ruined, and a new class of absentee landlords, often urban merchants and officials, arose. Tenants' rents were not regulated by the settlement, and rack-renting and subinfeudation became widespread. Other regions of British India used alternative systems — the ryotwari settlement directly with cultivators and the mahalwari settlement with villages.

Abolition

After independence, both India and Pakistan moved to abolish the zamindari system as part of land reform. India's state legislatures passed zamindari abolition acts in the early 1950s, with compensation to the former zamindars; the measures survived constitutional challenge after the First Amendment protected them. In East Bengal (later Bangladesh), the State Acquisition and Tenancy Act of 1950 similarly abolished the rent-receiving interests. Abolition ended a centuries-old layer of intermediaries, though its effects on actual cultivation patterns varied widely.
