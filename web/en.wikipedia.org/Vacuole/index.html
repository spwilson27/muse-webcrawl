Vacuole

A vacuole is a membrane-bound organelle present in plant and fungal cells, and in some animal, protist and bacterial cells. Vacuoles are enclosed compartments filled with water containing various inorganic and organic molecules, including enzymes and waste products. They have no set shape or size, changing as the needs of the cell require.

In plant cells

Mature plant cells typically contain one very large central vacuole, bounded by a membrane called the tonoplast, which may occupy 30–80% of the cell's volume. The fluid inside, called cell sap, contains water, salts, sugars, pigments and waste substances. By absorbing water, the vacuole presses the cytoplasm against the cell wall, generating the turgor pressure that keeps the plant rigid and drives cell enlargement during growth. Pigments stored in vacuoles, such as anthocyanins, give many flowers and fruits their colour, and vacuoles can also store defensive compounds and sequester toxins.

In other organisms

Animal cells have smaller and more numerous vacuoles, often involved in storage and in exocytosis and endocytosis. Many freshwater protists possess contractile vacuoles that collect excess water and expel it from the cell, regulating osmotic pressure. Food vacuoles in organisms such as Paramecium and amoebae fuse with lysosomes so that ingested material can be digested. In fungi and yeast, the vacuole is an acidic compartment comparable to the lysosome, important for storage, degradation and pH homeostasis.

Functions

Across cell types, vacuoles serve to isolate harmful materials, maintain internal pressure and pH, store water, ions and nutrients, export unwanted substances, and support growth by allowing cells to expand rapidly without synthesising large amounts of new cytoplasm.
