Québécois people

The fleurdelisé, flag of Quebec

The Québécois people (French: Québécois, feminine Québécoises) are the inhabitants of the Canadian province of Quebec, and more specifically, in common and political usage, the French-speaking majority descended largely from the settlers of New France. The term is used both as a civic identity for all Quebecers and as an ethnocultural identity for French Canadians of Quebec.

Identity and history

French settlement in the St. Lawrence valley began with Quebec City in 1608; the some 10,000 immigrants who arrived under the French regime founded a population that grew mainly by natural increase and survived the British conquest of 1760–1763 as a distinct French-speaking, predominantly Catholic society. In 2006 the Canadian House of Commons recognised "that the Québécois form a nation within a united Canada." Quebec nationalism — expressed in movements ranging from the Quiet Revolution of the 1960s to the sovereignty referendums of 1980 and 1995 — centres on the protection of the French language, institutionalised in the Charter of the French Language (Bill 101, 1977).

Population and culture

Quebec has a population of about 8.5–9 million, of whom the large majority speak French as a first language. Québécois culture is expressed through its French-language media, literature, music, cuisine (such as poutine and tourtière), winter carnival traditions, and institutions such as the National Assembly of Quebec.
