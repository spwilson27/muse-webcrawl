The Three Prisoners problem is a classic paradox in probability theory, closely related to the Monty Hall problem. It appeared in Martin Gardner's "Mathematical Games" column in Scientific American in 1959.

Statement

Three prisoners, A, B and C, are held in separate cells awaiting execution. The governor has randomly selected one of them to be pardoned; the others will be executed. The warden knows who is pardoned but is forbidden to tell a prisoner his own fate. Prisoner A asks the warden to name one of the other two who is to be executed: "Since at least one of B or C will be executed, telling me a name gives away nothing about my own fate." The warden names B. Prisoner A now reasons: "Before, my chance of pardon was 1/3. Now I know B will die, so it is between me and C — my chance must be 1/2." A is wrong: his chance of pardon remains 1/3, while C's chance has risen to 2/3.

Solution

The key is that the warden's answer is not random. If A is pardoned (probability 1/3), the warden may name either B or C; if C is pardoned (probability 1/3), the warden must name B; if B is pardoned (probability 1/3), the warden must name C. So hearing "B" is twice as likely to happen when C is the pardoned one as when A is. Applying Bayes' theorem, A's posterior probability of pardon stays at 1/3 and C's doubles to 2/3 — exactly the structure of Monty Hall, where switching doors wins with probability 2/3.

Discussion

The paradox illuminates the difference between learning an outcome and receiving evidence produced by a protocol: information that is guaranteed to be available regardless of the truth cannot change one's own odds, though it can redistribute probability among the alternatives the informant was free to choose between. Variations of the problem, including Gardner's follow-up correspondence, explore how the answer changes if the warden's choice is biased or unspecified.
