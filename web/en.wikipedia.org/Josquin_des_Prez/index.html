Josquin des Prez (c. 1450–1455 – 27 August 1521), often referred to simply as Josquin, was a composer of the Renaissance, widely regarded as the central figure of the Franco-Flemish school and one of the greatest composers of the era. Martin Luther famously praised him as "the master of the notes".

Life

Little is certain about Josquin's early life. He was probably born in Picardy, near modern Belgium, and served as a singer in Milan for the Sforza family and in the papal chapel in Rome under Popes Innocent VIII and Alexander VI. He later worked for Ercole I d'Este, Duke of Ferrara, for whom he wrote the celebrated Missa Hercules dux Ferrariae, and possibly briefly for Louis XII of France. In 1504 he settled in Condé-sur-l'Escaut, where he served as provost of the collegiate church of Notre-Dame until his death.

Music and style

Josquin composed in all the major genres of his day: around 18 masses, some 50 motets, and about 65 secular works including chansons and frottole. His style is marked by pervasive imitation, paired duets, careful attention to the meaning and declamation of text, and lucid polyphonic textures. Famous works include the motet Ave Maria... virgo serena, the masses Missa Pange lingua and Missa L'homme armé super voces musicales, and the chansons Mille regretz and El grillo.

Legacy and attribution problems

Josquin's fame after his death was so great that publishers attributed numerous works by other composers to him to increase sales — "now that Josquin is dead, he is putting out more works than when he was alive", as the theorist Georg Forster quipped. Modern scholarship has steadily revised the canon of securely attributed works. His music was widely disseminated through the newly invented medium of printed music, beginning with Ottaviano Petrucci's editions of 1502–1503.
