Helmholtz equation

The real part of the resulting field Areal = Re(A) after solving the inhomogeneous Helmholtz equation, here ∇²A = −k²A, in two dimensions.

In mathematics, the Helmholtz equation is the eigenvalue problem for the Laplace operator. It corresponds to the elliptic partial differential equation

∇²f = −k²f,

where ∇² is the Laplace operator, k is the eigenvalue, and f is the (eigen)function. When applied to physical problems, the Helmholtz equation is the time-independent form of the wave equation, obtained by separation of variables when a harmonic time dependence e−iωt is assumed. It is named after the German physicist Hermann von Helmholtz.

Derivation

The wave equation ∇²u = (1/c²) ∂²u/∂t² describes the propagation of waves such as sound and light. Assuming a solution of the form u(x, t) = A(x) eiωt separates the spatial and temporal parts; substituting into the wave equation and dividing through by the time factor gives the Helmholtz equation for the spatial amplitude A(x), with k = ω/c the wavenumber.

Solving the Helmholtz equation

The Helmholtz equation often arises in the study of physical problems involving partial differential equations in both space and time. Solving it can be achieved using separation of variables in a small number of coordinate systems — eleven in three dimensions — in which the equation reduces to ordinary differential equations whose solutions involve special functions such as Bessel functions, Legendre polynomials, and spherical harmonics. Boundary conditions, typically of Dirichlet, Neumann, or radiation type (such as the Sommerfeld radiation condition for scattering problems), determine the admissible solutions.

Applications

The equation governs acoustic waves in enclosures, the modes of vibrating membranes and electromagnetic cavities, optical resonators, and the time-independent Schrödinger equation of quantum mechanics, which has the same form with k² = 2m(E − V)/ħ². In seismology it underlies frequency-domain modelling of wave propagation in the Earth.
