Tartarus

Tartarus, the abyss beneath Hades where the Titans were imprisoned after the Titanomachy.

In Greek mythology, Tartarus (Greek: Τάρταρος, Tártaros) is the deep abyss far beneath the underworld, used as a dungeon of torment and suffering for the wicked and as the prison of the Titans. In Hesiod's Theogony Tartarus is also personified as one of the primordial beings, alongside Chaos, Gaia and Eros.

Description

Hesiod describes Tartarus as lying as far beneath the earth as the earth lies beneath the sky: a bronze anvil falling from heaven would take nine days to reach the earth, and nine more to reach Tartarus. It is surrounded by a bronze fence and triple layers of night, with gates of bronze guarded by the hundred-handed Hecatoncheires. Later writers — Plato, Virgil in the Aeneid — elaborated it into a place of judgment and punishment for the dead, where great sinners suffered fitting torments.

Inhabitants and prisoners

After their defeat in the Titanomachy, Cronus and the Titans were cast into Tartarus by Zeus. Its famous sufferers include Sisyphus, condemned to roll his boulder eternally; Tantalus, tormented by receding water and fruit; Ixion, bound to a fiery wheel; and the Danaïdes, endlessly carrying water in leaking jars. The monstrous Typhon was also hurled there (or beneath Mount Etna in other versions).

Later usage

In the Greek Old Testament (Septuagint) the verb derived from Tartarus describes the fate of fallen beings, and the New Testament's Second Epistle of Peter uses it (Greek tartaroō) for the place where sinning angels are held in chains awaiting judgment. The name survives in literature and popular culture as a byword for a pit of ultimate punishment, and features in works from Dante scholarship to modern fantasy.
