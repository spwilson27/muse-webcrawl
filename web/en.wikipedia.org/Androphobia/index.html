Androphobia

Androphobia is an abnormal and persistent fear of men. The term derives from the Greek anēr (man) and phobos (fear). In clinical usage it denotes a specific phobia in which encounters with men—or the prospect of them—provoke intense anxiety, avoidance, and physiological symptoms such as rapid heartbeat, sweating, or panic. It is distinguished from misandry, which refers to dislike or prejudice rather than a fear response.

Causes

Like other specific phobias, androphobia may develop after traumatic experiences involving men, including abuse or assault, or through learned associations in childhood. Genetic predisposition and temperament can contribute, and the condition frequently co-occurs with post-traumatic stress disorder or generalised social anxiety.

Symptoms and diagnosis

Symptoms include dread of male strangers or of particular settings involving men, avoidance that restricts education, work, or relationships, and marked distress when avoidance is impossible. Clinicians diagnose a specific phobia when the fear is out of proportion to actual danger, persists for six months or more, and significantly impairs daily functioning.

Treatment

Evidence-based treatments parallel those for other phobias: cognitive behavioural therapy, particularly graded exposure therapy that gradually reduces the fear response; trauma-focused therapy where PTSD is present; and, in some cases, short-term medication for acute anxiety. Supportive counselling and self-help techniques for arousal regulation are commonly combined with these approaches.
