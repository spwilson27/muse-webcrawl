Cogito, ergo sum

Cogito, ergo sum — usually translated "I think, therefore I am" — is a philosophical statement by René Descartes, first appearing in French as je pense, donc je suis in his Discourse on the Method (1637). The Latin form appears in his Principles of Philosophy (1644). It is the first principle of Descartes's philosophy: the one belief that survives his method of radical doubt.

Argument

Descartes set out to doubt everything that could possibly be doubted, including the evidence of the senses and even mathematics, supposing an evil demon might deceive him about all of it. But he found he could not doubt that he was thinking, and therefore that he, as a thinking thing, existed: even being deceived requires a deceiver's victim who exists. In the Meditations on First Philosophy (1641) the point is expressed without the "therefore": "I am, I exist" is necessarily true whenever it is uttered or conceived.

Criticism and influence

Critics from Pierre Gassendi onward have objected that the cogito presupposes the "I" it is meant to prove, that it licenses at most "thinking is occurring" (Georg Christoph Lichtenberg's famous remark), or that inferring existence from thought smuggles in the syllogism Descartes disclaimed. Despite these debates, the cogito became a foundational move in modern philosophy, grounding knowledge in the subject's own consciousness.
