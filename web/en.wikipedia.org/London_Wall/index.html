London Wall

A surviving section of the Roman wall, near Tower Hill

The London Wall is a defensive wall built by the Romans around the strategic port town of Londinium in the late 2nd or early 3rd century AD. Built of Kentish ragstone, it enclosed the city on its landward sides, running roughly two miles (about 3.2 km) from the Tower of London in the east to Blackfriars in the west, with a riverside wall added later along the Thames.

Construction

The wall was about 2.5–3 metres thick at its base and at least 6 metres high in places, fronted by a ditch and built with tile bonding courses and a rubble core faced with squared ragstone blocks, much of it barged from quarries near Maidstone in Kent. Forts were incorporated into its circuit, notably at Cripplegate, and a large fort in the north-west defended the city. Bastions were added at intervals, some in the later Roman period and others in medieval times.

Later history

The wall continued to define the boundary of the City of London through the Saxon and medieval periods, maintained and heightened by successive rulers including Alfred the Great and medieval kings. Its city gates — Aldgate, Bishopsgate, Cripplegate, Aldersgate, Newgate, Ludgate, and Moorgate (added in the 15th century) — gave their names to districts and streets. Most of the wall and gates were demolished in the 18th and 19th centuries as the city expanded, but significant fragments survive, including stretches near the Tower of London, in the Barbican area, at Cooper's Row, and within the Museum of London precinct (now London Museum).

"London Wall" is also the name of a modern street in the City that follows part of the line of the northern section of the wall.
