Apolinario Mabini y Maranan (23 July 1864 – 13 May 1903) was a Filipino revolutionary leader, educator, lawyer and statesman who served as the first Prime Minister of the Philippines under the First Philippine Republic and as its first Secretary of Foreign Affairs. Despite being paralysed by polio shortly before the Philippine Revolution, he became its chief political theorist and is remembered as the "Brains of the Revolution" and the "Sublime Paralytic."

Early life

Mabini was born in Tanauan, Batangas, to a poor family. He educated himself largely through borrowed books, working as a houseboy and teacher to fund his studies. He studied at San Juan de Letran and the University of Santo Tomas, where he earned a law degree in 1894. In 1896 he contracted an illness — believed to be polio — that left him paralysed from the waist down.

Role in the revolution

Summoned by Emilio Aguinaldo in 1898, Mabini became his principal adviser, drafting decrees that organised the revolutionary government, including the establishment of municipalities and provinces and the framework that led to the Malolos Congress and the First Philippine Republic. He wrote the "True Decalogue," a moral and political programme for the revolution, and served as President of the Council of Secretaries (prime minister) and Secretary of Foreign Affairs from January 1899. His insistence on constitutional limits to Aguinaldo's power and on reforming the army brought him into conflict with rivals, and he resigned in May 1899.

Exile and death

During the Philippine–American War, Mabini continued to write against American annexation. Captured by American forces in December 1899, he was exiled to Guam in 1901 after refusing to swear allegiance to the United States. He consented to the oath only in 1903, in declining health, and returned to the Philippines, where he died of cholera in Manila on 13 May 1903, aged 38.

Legacy

Mabini is honoured as a national hero of the Philippines; his likeness appeared on the Philippine ten-peso coin and banknote for many years, and numerous schools, streets and a municipality bear his name. His writings remain foundational texts of Filipino political thought.
