Halomonas is a genus of halophilic (salt-loving) proteobacteria first described in 1980. Its members are Gram-negative, rod-shaped, aerobic or facultatively anaerobic bacteria found in hypersaline environments worldwide — salt lakes, salterns, saline soils, salted foods, and marine habitats. The genus contains well over 100 recognised species, making it the largest group in the family Halomonadaceae.

Characteristics

Species of Halomonas tolerate an exceptionally wide range of salinities — many grow from near-freshwater conditions up to salt saturation (above 25% NaCl) — and many are also alkaliphilic, growing at high pH. To survive osmotic stress they accumulate compatible solutes such as ectoine and hydroxyectoine. Most are motile by flagella, oxidase- and catalase-positive chemoorganotrophs that degrade a wide variety of organic compounds, including some pollutants and hydrocarbons.

Biotechnology

Because Halomonas species grow vigorously in high-salt, high-pH conditions that suppress contaminants, they can be cultivated in open, unsterile fermentation, greatly lowering production costs. Halomonas bluephagenesis and related engineered strains are used as industrial chassis organisms for producing polyhydroxyalkanoates (PHAs) — biodegradable plastics — in commercial "next-generation industrial biotechnology" processes. The genus is also exploited for production of ectoine (used in cosmetics and medicine), enzymes active in saline conditions, and for bioremediation of saline wastewaters.

Notable species

  • Halomonas elongata — the type species, a model organism for salt adaptation and ectoine synthesis.
  • Halomonas titanicae — isolated from the wreck of RMS Titanic, associated with rusticle formation and iron corrosion.
  • Halomonas bluephagenesis — engineered production strain for PHA bioplastics.
