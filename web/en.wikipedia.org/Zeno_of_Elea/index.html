Zeno of Elea

Zeno of Elea (c. 490 – c. 430 BC) was a pre-Socratic Greek philosopher from Elea in southern Italy, a student of Parmenides. He is famous for his paradoxes, arguments intended to defend Parmenides' doctrine that motion and plurality are illusions by showing that the contrary assumptions lead to absurdity.

Paradoxes

Aristotle preserved several paradoxes in his Physics. The best known are Achilles and the tortoise, in which the swift Achilles can never overtake a tortoise given a head start, because he must first reach each point the tortoise has passed; the dichotomy paradox, in which covering any distance requires completing infinitely many sub-journeys; the arrow paradox, arguing that a flying arrow is at rest at every instant; and the stadium paradox.

Influence

Zeno's paradoxes have challenged philosophers and mathematicians for over two millennia and played a notable role in the development of rigorous ideas about infinity, limits, and the continuum. Aristotle called him the inventor of dialectic.
