History of the alphabet

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The history of the alphabet begins in the Bronze Age Near East in the second millennium BC. The first true alphabet—writing in which each sign represents a single consonant or vowel sound—probably originated with Semitic-speaking workers in or near Egypt around 1800 BC, who adapted Egyptian hieroglyphic signs by the acrophonic principle, using a picture to stand for the first sound of its name. Nearly all the world's alphabets descend, directly or indirectly, from this single invention, transmitted through the Phoenician alphabet.

Origins: Proto-Sinaitic and Phoenician

The earliest alphabetic inscriptions—Proto-Sinaitic texts from Serabit el-Khadim in the Sinai (c. 1850–1550 BC) and graffiti at Wadi el-Hol in Egypt—show signs derived from hieroglyphs re-purposed to write a Semitic language. By about 1050 BC the Phoenicians, maritime traders of the Levant, had streamlined the script into 22 consonantal letters (an abjad), written right to left. Phoenician trade spread the alphabet around the Mediterranean and eastward; closely related scripts include Hebrew, Aramaic, and the South Arabian scripts.

The Greek adaptation

The decisive innovation came when the Greeks adopted the Phoenician alphabet, probably in the 9th or 8th century BC: they reassigned letters for Semitic consonants that Greek lacked to represent vowels—aleph became A (alpha), he became E, yod became I, ayin became O, waw gave both the consonant digamma and the vowel U—creating the first alphabet that fully represented vowels as well as consonants. The Greeks also, after a period of boustrophedon (alternating direction), settled on left-to-right writing. Homer's epics were preserved in this script, and Greek alphabetic writing spread with colonisation.

Descendants

From Greek came the Etruscan alphabet and hence the Latin alphabet, which, spread by Rome and later by European colonisation and print, became the most widely used script in the world; the English alphabet's 26 letters descend from it, having gained J, U, and W as distinct letters only in the early modern period. Greek also parented the Coptic, Gothic, Armenian, Georgian, and—through the Glagolitic mission of Saints Cyril and Methodius and their successors—the Cyrillic alphabets used across Eastern Europe and Central Asia. Through Aramaic, the alphabet's second great branch, came the Hebrew and Arabic scripts (the latter, technically an abjad, spread with Islam across Africa and Asia), Syriac, and plausibly, though the connection is debated, the Brahmi script of India, ancestor of the Devanagari family and the scripts of Southeast Asia and Tibet. The Korean Hangul (1443) is a featural alphabet possibly inspired by these traditions but largely an original creation of King Sejong's scholars.

Why the alphabet spread

With only twenty-odd signs to learn, alphabetic writing drastically lowered the barrier to literacy compared to syllabaries with hundreds of signs or logographic systems with thousands. Its flexibility—readily adapted to new languages by adding, dropping, or repurposing letters—made it the most successful writing system in history: of the roughly 7,000 living languages, most that are written use an alphabet.
