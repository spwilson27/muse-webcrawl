The Traditionalist School is a group of 20th- and 21st-century thinkers who hold that beneath the varying forms of the world's religions lies a single, timeless, primordial tradition of metaphysical truths. Founded on the thought of the French metaphysician René Guénon (1886–1951), the school elaborates the perennial philosophy and a sharp critique of the modern world.

Key ideas

Traditionalists distinguish between exoteric (outer) religious forms and the esoteric (inner) unity they express. They teach that history moves cyclically through declining ages — the Hindu Kali Yuga being the present dark age — and that modernity represents a "reign of quantity": materialism, individualism, and the loss of sacred knowledge and caste-like functional order.

Principal figures

Ananda Coomaraswamy applied Traditionalist ideas to art history; Frithjof Schuon systematized the "transcendent unity of religions" and founded the Maryamiyya order; Julius Evola gave the school a more political and activist reading; Seyyed Hossein Nasr brought it into academic Islamic studies and environmental thought.

Influence and criticism

The school has influenced comparative religion, art history, and some political movements, drawing criticism for its anti-modernism and, in Evola's case, associations with far-right politics. Academic perennialists in religious studies share some themes but differ in method.
