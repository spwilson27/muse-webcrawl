A bridegroom (often shortened to groom) is a man who is about to be married, or who has just been married, taking part in a wedding ceremony alongside his partner, traditionally the bride. The term derives from Old English brȳdguma, "bride's man", in which the second element guma (man) was later replaced by the more familiar word "groom" (a young man or servant).

Customs

In Western weddings the groom traditionally waits at the front of the venue for the bride's procession, exchanges vows and rings, and is attended by a best man and groomsmen. Pre-wedding customs include the stag party (bachelor party). In many cultures the groom's role involves distinctive ceremonies: in Hindu weddings he arrives in a procession (baraat), often on horseback; in Chinese tradition he may face playful "door games" before fetching the bride.

Attire

Formal Western attire for the groom typically matches the wedding's formality — a morning suit, dinner jacket (tuxedo), or lounge suit — often distinguished from the groomsmen by a boutonnière or details of dress. Military and national dress variants are also common, such as Highland dress in Scotland.

Religious imagery

The image of the bridegroom is prominent in religious symbolism: in Christian theology Christ is described as the bridegroom of the Church, and the Parable of the Ten Virgins in the New Testament turns on waiting for the bridegroom's arrival.
