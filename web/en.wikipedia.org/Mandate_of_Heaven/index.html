Mandate of Heaven

The Mandate of Heaven (Chinese: 天命; pinyin: Tiānmìng) is a Chinese political philosophy that was used in ancient and imperial China to justify the rule of the king or emperor. According to this doctrine, Heaven (天, Tian) — which embodies the natural order and will of the universe — bestows the mandate on a just ruler, the Son of Heaven. If a ruler is overthrown, this is interpreted as an indication that the ruler was unworthy and had lost the mandate.

History

The concept dates to the Zhou dynasty (c. 1046–256 BC), whose founders used it to justify their overthrow of the Shang dynasty: the last Shang king was portrayed as corrupt and cruel, so Heaven had withdrawn its mandate and given it to the virtuous Zhou. The doctrine thus appeared early as a justification for dynastic change.

Principles

Unlike a hereditary divine right of kings, the Mandate of Heaven was conditional and performance-based. It did not require a ruler to be of noble birth — the founders of the Han and Ming dynasties were of common origins — but it did require just and able rule. Natural disasters such as floods, droughts, famines and earthquakes, as well as peasant rebellions, were read as signs that Heaven was displeased and that a dynasty was losing the mandate.

Dynastic cycle

The Mandate underlies the traditional Chinese conception of the dynastic cycle: a vigorous new dynasty restores order and prosperity, gradually decays into corruption and weakness, loses the mandate amid disaster and rebellion, and is replaced by a new dynasty that has gained it. Historians applied the idea retrospectively from the Zhou through the Qing dynasty, and it also influenced political thought in Korea, Vietnam and Japan.
