Variable-frequency drive

A variable-frequency drive (VFD, or adjustable-frequency drive, adjustable-speed drive, variable-speed drive, AC drive, micro drive, or inverter drive) is a type of motor drive used in electro-mechanical drive systems to control AC motor speed and torque by varying motor input frequency and, depending on topology, to control associated voltage or current variation. VFDs are used in applications ranging from small appliances to large compressors.

Operation

A VFD typically has three stages: a rectifier converts incoming AC power to DC; a DC bus (with capacitors) smooths the result; and an inverter using power transistors (typically IGBTs) switching at high frequency converts the DC back to AC with controlled frequency and voltage via pulse-width modulation (PWM). Because an induction motor's speed is set by the supply frequency, varying the frequency varies the speed, while maintaining the volts-to-hertz ratio preserves torque capability.

Benefits

Since about 25% of the world's electrical energy is consumed by electric motors in industrial applications, VFDs offer large energy savings: for centrifugal pumps and fans, power falls with the cube of speed, so modest speed reductions save substantial energy. VFDs also provide soft starting, precise process control, and reduced mechanical wear compared with throttling valves or dampers.

Drawbacks

Potential issues include harmonic distortion injected into the power supply, electromagnetic interference, and bearing currents in motors driven by long cables. Filters, shielding, and inverter-duty motors mitigate these effects.
