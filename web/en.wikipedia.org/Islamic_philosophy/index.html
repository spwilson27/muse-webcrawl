Islamic philosophy is philosophy that emerges from the Islamic tradition, principally in Arabic and Persian, beginning in the 2nd century AH / 9th century AD. It developed as Muslim thinkers engaged with the translated heritage of Greek philosophy — above all Aristotle, Plato, and the Neoplatonists — within the framework of Islam. Its debates shaped, and were transmitted to, medieval Jewish and Christian scholastic thought in Europe.

The translation movement

Under the Abbasid caliphs, Baghdad's translators — notably Hunayn ibn Ishaq and his circle — rendered Greek scientific and philosophical works into Arabic, often via Syriac. Al-Kindi (c. 801–873), the "philosopher of the Arabs", was the first major thinker to fuse this material with Islamic theology.

Falsafa: the Hellenising tradition

Al-Farabi (c. 872–950) systematised logic and political philosophy, earning the title "the Second Teacher" after Aristotle. Avicenna (Ibn Sina, 980–1037) built a vast metaphysical system around the distinction between essence and existence and the concept of the Necessary Existent; his "Floating Man" argument anticipated debates about self-consciousness, and his Canon of Medicine served medicine for centuries. In al-Andalus, Averroes (Ibn Rushd, 1126–1198) wrote commentaries on Aristotle so influential that Latin scholastics called him simply "the Commentator".

Kalam and the critique of philosophy

Kalam (speculative theology) developed rational arguments for doctrines such as God's unity and the createdness of the world, in schools including the Mu'tazila and the Ash'ariyya. The theologian and mystic al-Ghazali (1058–1111) attacked the philosophers in The Incoherence of the Philosophers, charging that on questions like the eternity of the world they exceeded what reason could prove; Averroes replied in The Incoherence of the Incoherence.

Later developments

Philosophy did not end with Averroes, as older narratives claimed. Suhrawardi founded the Illuminationist (Ishraqi) school in Persia, and in Safavid Iran, Mulla Sadra (c. 1571–1640) produced a grand synthesis — "Transcendent Theosophy" — built on the primacy and gradation of existence and the doctrine of substantial motion. Philosophical traditions continue in the seminaries of Iran and Iraq and in modern academic debate.

Influence

Through 12th-century translations in Toledo and Sicily, Arabic philosophy profoundly influenced Latin scholasticism: thinkers such as Albertus Magnus and Thomas Aquinas engaged deeply with Avicenna and Averroes, and terms and arguments from the Arabic tradition entered European philosophy permanently.
