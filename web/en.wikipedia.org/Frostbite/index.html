Frostbite is a skin injury that occurs when someone is exposed to extremely low temperatures, causing the freezing of the skin and underlying tissues. The most vulnerable areas are the extremities — fingers, toes, ears and nose. In severe cases, frostbite can lead to permanent damage and amputation.

Mechanism and stages

When skin is exposed to cold, blood vessels constrict to preserve core temperature. Prolonged exposure leads to ice crystal formation in tissues, cell dehydration, and vascular injury; damage continues during rewarming as inflammation and clotting compromise blood flow. Frostbite is classified by depth: first-degree (frostnip and superficial injury), second-degree (blistering), third-degree (deep blistering and tissue death), and fourth-degree (involvement of muscle and bone).

Signs and treatment

Affected skin becomes cold, numb, waxy and discoloured — white, blue-grey or mottled. After thawing, severe frostbite produces blisters and intense pain. Recommended first aid is rapid rewarming in water at 37–39 °C, avoiding dry heat and avoiding refreezing, which greatly worsens injury. Hospital treatment may include ibuprofen, thrombolytic drugs to restore circulation, and delayed surgery, since the extent of dead tissue takes weeks to declare itself.

History

Frostbite has been a major cause of casualties in winter warfare, famously devastating Napoleon's army during the 1812 retreat from Russia and troops on both sides in the World Wars. It remains a risk for mountaineers, polar workers, and people experiencing homelessness in cold climates.
