Bakelite is an early plastic made from a synthetic polymer of phenol and formaldehyde. Developed by Belgian-American chemist Leo Baekeland in Yonkers, New York, in 1907 and patented in 1909, it is often considered the first truly synthetic plastic.

Synthesis and properties

Bakelite is produced by the condensation reaction of phenol with formaldehyde, catalyzed by acid or base, often with fillers such as wood flour. The resulting thermosetting resin is heat-resistant, electrically non-conductive and chemically stable, though brittle.

Applications

Because of its electrical insulation properties, Bakelite was quickly adopted for radio and telephone casings, electrical switches, billiard balls, jewelry, pipe stems and kitchenware. The Bakelite Corporation was founded in 1922.

Legacy

Bakelite marked the beginning of the modern plastics industry. The American Chemical Society designated its invention a National Historic Chemical Landmark in 1993. The material has largely been superseded by cheaper thermoplastics, though phenolic resins remain in use.
