Nathaniel Hawthorne

Born: 4 July 1804, Salem, Massachusetts, U.S.

Died: 19 May 1864, Plymouth, New Hampshire, U.S.

Occupation: Novelist, short-story writer

Notable works: The Scarlet Letter, The House of the Seven Gables, Twice-Told Tales

Nathaniel Hawthorne (born Nathaniel Hathorne; 4 July 1804 – 19 May 1864) was an American novelist and short-story writer. His works often focus on history, morality, and religion, and are noted for their dark romanticism, psychological depth, and preoccupation with sin, guilt and the inherited consequences of the past. He is regarded as one of the greatest figures of nineteenth-century American literature.

Early life

Hawthorne was born in Salem, Massachusetts, a descendant of early Puritan settlers. His great-great-great-grandfather John Hathorne was one of the judges at the Salem witch trials; Hawthorne is said to have added the "w" to his surname partly to distance himself from that legacy, whose themes of guilt and persecution recur throughout his fiction. His father, a sea captain, died of yellow fever when Hawthorne was four. He graduated from Bowdoin College in 1825, where his classmates included Henry Wadsworth Longfellow and future president Franklin Pierce.

Career

After college Hawthorne spent over a decade writing in near seclusion in Salem, publishing the novel Fanshawe anonymously in 1828 and many tales later collected in Twice-Told Tales (1837). He worked at the Boston Custom House and lived for a time at the transcendentalist community of Brook Farm before marrying Sophia Peabody in 1842. His masterpiece, The Scarlet Letter (1850) — the story of Hester Prynne, condemned to wear a scarlet "A" for adultery in Puritan Boston — was an immediate success. It was followed by The House of the Seven Gables (1851), The Blithedale Romance (1852) and The Marble Faun (1860). Hawthorne's campaign biography of Franklin Pierce helped win him appointment as United States consul at Liverpool (1853–1857).

Death and legacy

Hawthorne died in his sleep on 19 May 1864 while travelling in New Hampshire with Pierce. His unfinished romances were published posthumously. Herman Melville, who dedicated Moby-Dick to him, was among his most ardent admirers, and his exploration of moral ambiguity profoundly influenced later American writers.
