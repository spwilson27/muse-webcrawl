Saint Nicholas

BornTraditionally 15 March 270, Patara, Lycia
DiedTraditionally 6 December 343, Myra
Feast6 December (Western Christianity); 19 December (Eastern, old calendar)
PatronageChildren, sailors, merchants, archers, brewers, pawnbrokers, students

Saint Nicholas of Myra (traditionally 15 March 270 – 6 December 343), also known as Nicholas of Bari, was an early Christian bishop of the ancient Greek maritime city of Myra in Asia Minor (modern-day Demre, Turkey) during the time of the Roman Empire. Because of the many miracles attributed to his intercession, he is also known as Nicholas the Wonderworker.

Life and legends

Little is reliably known of the historical Nicholas. The most famous legend tells how he learned of a poor man who could not afford dowries for his three daughters and so secretly threw three purses of gold coins through the man's window (in later versions, down the chimney) on three successive nights, saving the daughters from being sold into prostitution. This tale is the origin of his reputation as a gift-giver and of the pawnbrokers' symbol of three gold balls.

Other stories describe him calming a storm at sea, saving sailors, rescuing three unjustly condemned officers, and restoring to life three children murdered by a butcher. Tradition holds that he attended the First Council of Nicaea in 325, where he vigorously opposed Arianism.

Veneration and legacy

Nicholas's tomb in Myra became a major pilgrimage site. In 1087, Italian sailors removed most of his relics and carried them to Bari, where the Basilica di San Nicola was built to house them. His feast day, 6 December, is celebrated across Europe with gift-giving to children, notably in the Netherlands as Sinterklaas, whose traditions, carried to North America, developed into the modern figure of Santa Claus. He is one of the most popular saints in both Eastern and Western Christianity, and patron saint of numerous countries, cities, churches, and occupations.
