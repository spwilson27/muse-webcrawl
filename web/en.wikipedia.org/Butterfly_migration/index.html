Butterfly migration

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Butterfly migration is the seasonal, long-distance movement undertaken by many species of butterfly, typically between breeding grounds and overwintering sites. Although insects were long thought incapable of true migration, several hundred butterfly species are now known to migrate, some over thousands of kilometres.

The monarch migration

The best-known case is the eastern North American population of the monarch butterfly, which flies up to 4,800 km each autumn to overwintering groves of oyamel fir in central Mexico; a western population migrates to the California coast. No single individual makes the round trip: the return north takes place over several generations, with the butterflies breeding along the way.

Other migrants

The painted lady undertakes a multi-generational circuit of some 14,500 km between Europe and tropical Africa, one of the longest insect migrations known. The globe skimmer dragonfly is a famous insect migrant, but among butterflies the bogong-like long flights of the Australian bogong moth are rivalled by species such as the long-tailed skipper and the great southern white in the Americas.

Navigation and threats

Migrants navigate using a sun compass corrected by an internal clock, and monarchs also appear to use a magnetic compass. Migratory butterflies face threats from habitat loss at overwintering sites, loss of larval host plants such as milkweed, pesticides and climate change.
