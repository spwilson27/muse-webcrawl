Defibrillation

Defibrillation
SpecialtyEmergency medicine, cardiology
IndicationVentricular fibrillation, pulseless ventricular tachycardia
Energy120–360 J (device-dependent)
DevicesManual defibrillator, AED, ICD

Defibrillation is a treatment for life-threatening cardiac arrhythmias, specifically ventricular fibrillation (VF) and pulseless ventricular tachycardia (VT). It delivers a dose of electric current to the heart through the chest wall (or, in implanted devices, internally). This depolarizes a critical mass of the heart muscle, terminating the chaotic rhythm and allowing the heart's natural pacemaker to re-establish an effective rhythm.

Mechanism

During ventricular fibrillation the myocardium contracts in a disorganized fashion and no effective circulation occurs. A defibrillatory shock simultaneously depolarizes most cardiac cells, after which the sinoatrial node — normally the dominant pacemaker — may resume coordinated control. Defibrillation does not "jump-start" a heart in asystole (flatline); asystole is not a shockable rhythm and is treated with CPR and drugs instead.

Devices

Manual defibrillators in hospitals allow clinicians to select energy and synchronize shocks for cardioversion of rhythms with a pulse. Automated external defibrillators (AEDs) analyse the rhythm and guide lay rescuers by voice prompts; their widespread public placement has significantly improved survival from out-of-hospital cardiac arrest. Implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (ICDs) continuously monitor patients at high risk and deliver shocks internally. Modern external devices use a biphasic waveform, which achieves success at lower energies (typically 120–200 J) than older monophasic devices (360 J).

Prognosis

The probability of successful defibrillation declines rapidly with time — roughly 7–10% per minute without CPR — which is why immediate defibrillation is a key link in the chain of survival.

History

Precursors were demonstrated by Prévost and Batelli in 1899; Claude Beck performed the first successful internal defibrillation on a human patient in 1947, and Paul Zoll achieved external defibrillation in the 1950s. Bernard Lown introduced the direct-current (DC) defibrillator in the 1960s.
