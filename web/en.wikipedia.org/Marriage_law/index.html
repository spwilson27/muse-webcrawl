Marriage law

Marriage law is the body of law governing the formation, effects and dissolution of marriage. It determines who may marry, the formalities required for a valid marriage, the legal rights and duties of spouses during marriage, and the rules for ending a marriage through annulment or divorce.

Requirements for marriage

Most legal systems impose conditions of capacity and consent: a minimum age (commonly 18), the requirement that neither party is already married (bigamy being prohibited), restrictions on marriage between close blood relatives, and free and genuine consent by both parties. Formal requirements usually include a licence or registration and a ceremony before an authorised celebrant, though some jurisdictions recognise common-law marriage formed by cohabitation and repute.

Legal effects

Marriage alters the legal status of the spouses in many areas: property ownership and inheritance rights, tax treatment, pension and social security entitlements, immigration status, medical decision-making, and presumptions about the parentage of children. Legal regimes for marital property range from community of property to complete separation, with courts in many jurisdictions able to redistribute assets on divorce.

Dissolution

A marriage may be annulled if it was void from the outset (for example, for bigamy or lack of capacity) or voidable (for example, for duress). Divorce terminates a valid marriage; modern systems have largely moved to no-fault divorce, often on the sole ground of irretrievable breakdown.

Modern developments

The legal definition of marriage has been a subject of major reform and litigation. From 2001, beginning with the Netherlands, a growing number of countries have extended marriage to same-sex couples. Other themes in contemporary marriage law include the recognition of foreign marriages, the regulation of prenuptial agreements, and the status of religious marriages under civil law.
