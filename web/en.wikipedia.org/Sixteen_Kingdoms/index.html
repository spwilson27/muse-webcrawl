Sixteen Kingdoms

Sixteen Kingdoms Period: 304 – 439 AD
Region: Northern China
Preceded by: Western Jin
Succeeded by: Northern Wei (Northern Dynasties)

The Sixteen Kingdoms, less commonly known as the Sixteen States, was a chaotic period in Chinese history from AD 304 to 439, when northern China fragmented into a series of short-lived dynastic states. Most of these states were founded by rulers from the "Five Barbarians" — the Xiongnu, Jie, Xianbei, Qiang and Di peoples — who had settled inside the Jin frontier, though some, such as Former Liang, were founded by Han Chinese.

Background

The collapse of the Western Jin dynasty followed the War of the Eight Princes, a civil war among Jin princes that exhausted the dynasty, and the consequent uprisings of non-Han peoples. In 304 Li Xiong founded Cheng-Han in Sichuan and Liu Yuan founded Han-Zhao, events conventionally taken as the start of the period. Jin loyalists retreated south of the Yangtze and established the Eastern Jin at Jiankang.

Major states

Among the more durable and powerful of the kingdoms were Han-Zhao, Later Zhao under the Jie ruler Shi Le, Former Qin under the Di Fu clan — which briefly unified the north under Fu Jian before its catastrophic defeat at the Battle of Fei River in 383 — Former Yan, Later Yan and the several Liang states of the north-west. The historian Cui Hong of the Northern Wei compiled the Spring and Autumn Annals of the Sixteen Kingdoms, from which the period takes its name.

Unification

The period ended in 439 when the Northern Wei, founded by the Tuoba Xianbei, conquered Northern Liang and unified northern China, inaugurating the Northern and Southern Dynasties period. The era, though politically fragmented and war-torn, saw the deepening spread of Buddhism in China and growing integration between Han Chinese and the peoples of the steppe frontier.
