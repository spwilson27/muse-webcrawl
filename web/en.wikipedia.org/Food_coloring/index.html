Food coloring

Other namesFood dyes, colour additives
UsesColouring food and drink
Natural sourcesBeetroot, turmeric, annatto, carmine
Synthetic examplesTartrazine, Sunset Yellow, Brilliant Blue

Food coloring, or color additive, is any dye, pigment, or substance that imparts color when it is added to food or drink. It comes in many forms consisting of liquids, powders, gels, and pastes. Food coloring is used in both commercial food production and domestic cooking. Food colorants are also used in a variety of non-food applications, including cosmetics, pharmaceuticals, home craft projects, and medical devices.

History

The addition of colorants to food is thought to have occurred as early as 1500 BC in Egyptian cities, where candy makers added natural extracts and wine to improve the appearance of products. Saffron, paprika, turmeric and cochineal were traditional colorants. The first synthetic dye, mauveine, was discovered by William Henry Perkin in 1856, and by the end of the 19th century coal-tar dyes were widely used in food — sometimes fraudulently or dangerously, prompting the first regulatory lists of permitted colors.

Natural and synthetic colors

Natural colorings are derived from plant, animal or mineral sources: carmine (from cochineal insects), annatto (from achiote seeds), chlorophyll, anthocyanins (from red cabbage and berries), turmeric (curcumin) and beetroot red (betanin). Synthetic colors, many originally derived from petroleum, include tartrazine (Yellow 5), Sunset Yellow FCF, Allura Red AC, Brilliant Blue FCF and erythrosine. Synthetic dyes generally offer greater stability and intensity, but consumer demand has pushed many manufacturers toward natural alternatives.

Regulation and health debate

As a class of food additives, colorings are regulated by authorities such as the FDA in the United States and EFSA in Europe. Permitted lists differ between jurisdictions. A 2007 study from the University of Southampton suggested a possible link between certain artificial colors and hyperactivity in children, leading the EU to require warning labels on six colors; subsequent reviews have remained inconclusive.
