Incubation period

The incubation period (also known as the latent period or latency period) is the time elapsed between exposure to a pathogenic organism, a chemical, or radiation, and when symptoms and signs are first apparent. In a typical infectious disease, the incubation period is the time taken for the multiplying organism to reach a number necessary to produce symptoms in the host.

The incubation period varies between diseases and between individuals; it may be as short as a few hours for staphylococcal food poisoning or as long as decades for some slow infections. During incubation, a person may already be infectious — as in measles, which can be transmitted before symptoms appear.

Examples

DiseaseTypical incubation period
Common cold (rhinovirus)1–3 days
Influenza1–4 days
Measles9–12 days
Chickenpox14–16 days
COVID-192–14 days (median about 5)
Rabies1–3 months (highly variable)
Hepatitis B30–180 days

Related terms

The latent period is sometimes distinguished as the interval between infection and the start of infectiousness (shedding of the pathogen), while the serial interval is the time between symptom onset in successive cases in a chain of transmission. These quantities are central to epidemiology and to designing quarantine and isolation periods.
