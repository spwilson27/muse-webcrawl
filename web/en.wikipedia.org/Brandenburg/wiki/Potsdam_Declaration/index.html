The Potsdam Declaration, formally the "Proclamation Defining Terms for Japanese Surrender", was a statement issued on 26 July 1945 by President Harry S. Truman of the United States, Prime Minister Winston Churchill of the United Kingdom, and Chairman Chiang Kai-shek of the Republic of China. It outlined the terms of surrender for the Empire of Japan in World War II, warning that the alternative was "prompt and utter destruction".

Terms

The declaration demanded the unconditional surrender of all Japanese armed forces. Its thirteen points required the elimination of the authority of those who had led Japan into war, the occupation of Japanese territory, the limitation of Japanese sovereignty to the four main home islands, the disarmament of Japanese forces, war crimes trials, and the establishment of a "peacefully inclined and responsible government" in accordance with the freely expressed will of the Japanese people. It notably made no mention of the fate of Emperor Hirohito, a silence that was the subject of intense debate within the Allied governments.

Japanese response

The Japanese government, divided between a peace faction and military leaders determined to fight on, responded cautiously. Premier Kantarō Suzuki's use of the word mokusatsu ("killing with silence") at a press conference was interpreted by the Allies as rejection of the ultimatum.

Consequences

Following the declaration, the United States dropped atomic bombs on Hiroshima (6 August) and Nagasaki (9 August), and the Soviet Union declared war on Japan and invaded Manchuria. On 14 August the Japanese government accepted the Potsdam terms, and the formal surrender was signed aboard the USS Missouri on 2 September 1945. The declaration remained the legal basis of the Allied occupation of Japan.
