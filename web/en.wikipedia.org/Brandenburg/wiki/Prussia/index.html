Prussia (German: Preußen) was a German state that emerged from the union of the Duchy of Prussia and the Margraviate of Brandenburg. From 1701 as the Kingdom of Prussia, it rose through military strength, administrative efficiency, and reform to become the dominant power in Germany, leading German unification in 1871. It remained the largest state of the German Reich until its formal abolition by the Allies in 1947.

Origins

The name derives from the Old Prussians, a Baltic people conquered and Christianized by the Teutonic Knights in the 13th century. After the secularization of the Order's state in 1525, the Duchy of Prussia passed in 1618 to the Hohenzollern electors of Brandenburg. The Great Elector built a standing army; his son was crowned "King in Prussia" at Königsberg in 1701.

Rise and reform

Frederick William I, the "Soldier King," created a famously drilled army and efficient bureaucracy; his son Frederick the Great seized Silesia, survived the Seven Years' War, and made Prussia a European great power. Crushing defeat by Napoleon at Jena (1806) spurred the Stein–Hardenberg reforms, Scharnhorst's army reform, and the founding of Berlin University. Prussia industrialized rapidly, built the Zollverein customs union, and under Bismarck won wars against Denmark (1864), Austria (1866), and France (1870–71), proclaiming the German Empire at Versailles with the Prussian king as Kaiser.

Decline and abolition

After 1918 the Free State of Prussia was a democratic bulwark of Weimar Germany until Franz von Papen's 1932 coup (Preußenschlag). The Nazis subordinated it, and after 1945 East Prussia was divided between the USSR and Poland, its German population expelled. In 1947 the Allied Control Council declared Prussia abolished as a "bearer of militarism and reaction." Its legacy—discipline, bureaucracy, education, and landmarks such as Potsdam—remains debated.
