Frederick II (German: Friedrich II.; 24 January 1712 – 17 August 1786) was King of Prussia from 1740 until his death in 1786. Known as Frederick the Great (German: Friedrich der Große), he was a leading figure of the Enlightenment and one of the most formidable military commanders of the 18th century. His seizure of Silesia from Austria and Prussia's survival in the Seven Years' War established Prussia as a great power.

Early life

Frederick's upbringing was marked by conflict with his authoritarian father, Frederick William I, the "Soldier King," who despised his son's interest in music, literature, and philosophy and had Frederick's close friend Hans Hermann von Katte executed before the prince's eyes after a failed attempt to flee Prussia in 1730. Reconciled to the court and married to Elisabeth Christine of Brunswick-Wolfenbüttel, Frederick spent the 1730s at Rheinsberg writing (including the Anti-Machiavel, edited by Voltaire) and corresponding with leading philosophes.

Wars

On becoming king, Frederick invaded Habsburg Silesia in 1740, beginning the War of the Austrian Succession, and retained the wealthy province in subsequent settlements. In 1756 he pre-emptively invaded Saxony, opening the Seven Years' War, in which Prussia faced a coalition of Austria, France, Russia, and Sweden. He won celebrated victories at Rossbach and Leuthen (1757) but suffered crushing defeats at Kolín and Kunersdorf (1759). Prussia was saved in 1762 when the death of Empress Elizabeth of Russia brought the pro-Prussian Peter III to the throne — the "Miracle of the House of Brandenburg." Later, Frederick joined Russia and Austria in the First Partition of Poland (1772), acquiring West Prussia.

Domestic rule and legacy

At home, Frederick promoted religious toleration, reformed the judiciary, encouraged immigration and agriculture (including widespread potato cultivation), patronized the arts at Sanssouci palace in Potsdam, composed flute music, and corresponded with Voltaire. He described the ruler as "the first servant of the state." An emblematic figure of enlightened absolutism, his legacy was later contested: celebrated in Prussian and German national mythology, appropriated by the Nazis, and reassessed after 1945; his remains were reburied at Sanssouci in 1991.
