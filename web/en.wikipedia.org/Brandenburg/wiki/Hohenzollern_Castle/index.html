Hohenzollern Castle (German: Burg Hohenzollern) is the ancestral seat of the imperial House of Hohenzollern. The third of three hilltop castles built on the site, it is located atop Mount Hohenzollern, above and south of Hechingen, on the edge of the Swabian Jura of central Baden-Württemberg, Germany. The first fortress on the mountain was constructed in the early 11th century.

History

The first castle on Mount Hohenzollern was built in the early 11th century and was completely destroyed after a ten-month siege in 1423 by the imperial cities of Swabia. A second, larger and sturdier castle was constructed from 1454 to 1461 and served as a refuge for the Swabian Hohenzollerns during wartime, including during the Thirty Years' War. By the end of the 18th century, however, it had lost its strategic importance and fell into disrepair.

The third and present castle was built between 1850 and 1867 under the direction of the Prussian king Frederick William IV, who wished to rebuild the ancestral home of his dynasty. The English Gothic Revival design was executed by the Berlin architect Friedrich August Stüler, working from plans by the officer-engineer Moritz Karl Ernst von Prittwitz. The castle was intended as a dynastic monument rather than a residence, and no Hohenzollern ruler ever lived in it permanently.

Architecture

The castle rises on an almost isolated conical hill 855 metres above sea level. Its skyline of towers, turrets and fortified walls is considered a masterpiece of 19th-century military and Gothic Revival architecture. Within are the Count's Hall (Grafensaal), the library, the King's bedroom, the Blue Salon, the ancestral hall with portraits of the Hohenzollern line, and two chapels — the Protestant Christ Chapel and the Catholic St. Michael's Chapel.

Collections

The castle houses significant artefacts of Prussian history, including the crown of Wilhelm II, part of the Prussian crown jewels, the uniform worn by Frederick the Great, and a letter from George Washington to Baron von Steuben thanking him for his service in the American Revolutionary War. The coffins of Frederick William I and Frederick the Great rested in the castle from 1952 until German reunification, when they were returned to Potsdam.

Today

The castle remains privately owned by the House of Hohenzollern and is open to the public, receiving more than 300,000 visitors a year. It is one of Germany's most visited castles, and its silhouette, often floating above a sea of fog, is a celebrated photographic subject.
