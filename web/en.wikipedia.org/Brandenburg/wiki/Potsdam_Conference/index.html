Potsdam Conference

Potsdam Conference
Date17 July – 2 August 1945
LocationCecilienhof Palace, Potsdam, Germany
ParticipantsJoseph Stalin (USSR); Harry S. Truman (USA); Winston Churchill, then Clement Attlee (UK)
OutcomePotsdam Agreement; Potsdam Declaration
Code nameTerminal

The Potsdam Conference was held at Potsdam in the Soviet occupation zone of Germany from 17 July to 2 August 1945 to allow the three leading Allied powers — the Soviet Union, the United States, and the United Kingdom — to negotiate the postwar settlement of Europe following the end of the Second World War in Europe. It was the last of the wartime "Big Three" conferences, following Tehran (1943) and Yalta (February 1945).

Participants

The Soviet Union was represented by Joseph Stalin, the United States by its new president, Harry S. Truman (Franklin D. Roosevelt having died in April), and Britain initially by Winston Churchill; after the Conservative defeat in the 1945 general election, Churchill was replaced mid-conference by the new prime minister, Clement Attlee. Truman learned during the conference of the successful Trinity atomic bomb test and informed Stalin of a new weapon — which Stalin, informed by espionage, received calmly.

Agreements

The Potsdam Agreement provided for the division of Germany and Austria into four occupation zones, the demilitarization, denazification, democratization, and decentralization of Germany, and the prosecution of war criminals. It confirmed the transfer of German territory east of the Oder–Neisse line to Poland (and northern East Prussia to the USSR), with the "orderly and humane" transfer of German populations from Poland, Czechoslovakia, and Hungary — expulsions that in practice involved great hardship and many deaths. Reparations were settled largely by zonal extraction, an arrangement that prefigured Germany's division. The conference also issued the Potsdam Declaration of 26 July 1945, an ultimatum demanding Japan's unconditional surrender; Japan's rejection was followed by the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki and its surrender.

Aftermath

Relations between the wartime allies deteriorated rapidly after Potsdam, and many of its arrangements — especially over Germany and Poland's western border — became foundations of the Cold War order in Europe; the Oder–Neisse line was not finally recognized by West Germany until 1970 and by a united Germany until 1990.
