Sanssouci is a historical building in Potsdam, near Berlin. Built by Prussian King Frederick the Great as his summer palace in 1745–1747, it is often counted among the German rivals of Versailles. While Sanssouci is in the more intimate Rococo style and is far smaller than its French Baroque counterpart, it is renowned for its gardens and follies within the Sanssouci park. Its name is a French phrase (sans souci, "without worries") emphasising that the palace was a place of relaxation rather than a seat of power.

Design and construction

Frederick sketched the design himself; it was executed by the architect Georg Wenzeslaus von Knobelsdorff. The palace is a single-storey villa of ten principal rooms on top of a terraced vineyard, deliberately modest in scale. The interior includes the Marble Hall, the concert room decorated in Rococo style, the library with cedar panelling, and Frederick's study and bedroom, where he died in 1786.

The park

Sanssouci Park covers nearly 300 hectares. It includes the terraced vineyard laid out in 1744, the Picture Gallery (1755–1764) housing Frederick's collection of paintings, the New Palace built after the Seven Years' War (1763–1769), the Chinese House, the Orangery Palace, Charlottenhof Palace and numerous temples, fountains and statues. The mill legend associated with the Historic Mill of Sanssouci became a celebrated symbol of Prussian justice.

History after Frederick

After Frederick's death the palace remained a Hohenzollern residence; Frederick William IV enlarged it in the 1840s. The palace became a museum after the abolition of the monarchy in 1918. Following the Second World War it lay in the Soviet occupation zone and then in East Germany; the remains of Frederick the Great, removed during the war, were returned to a grave on the palace terrace in 1991, in accordance with his wishes.

World heritage

In 1990 Sanssouci and its extensive gardens were added to UNESCO's World Heritage List as part of the "Palaces and Parks of Potsdam and Berlin". The site is administered by the Prussian Palaces and Gardens Foundation Berlin-Brandenburg.
