François-Marie Arouet (21 November 1694 – 30 May 1778), known by his nom de plume Voltaire, was a French Enlightenment writer, philosopher, dramatist, and historian. Famous for his wit and his criticism of Christianity — especially the Roman Catholic Church — and of slavery, Voltaire was an advocate of freedom of speech, freedom of religion, and the separation of church and state.

Early life and exile

Arouet was born in Paris, the youngest son of a lawyer. His satirical verses against the regent led to imprisonment in the Bastille and exile. After a second imprisonment in 1726 he was exiled to England, where exposure to the ideas of Locke and Newton, and to British parliamentary government, profoundly shaped his thinking. His Lettres philosophiques (1734), which praised English institutions by implicit contrast with France, caused a scandal and forced him to flee Paris again.

Potsdam and Frederick the Great

After years at the château of Cirey with his companion Émilie du Châtelet, Voltaire accepted the long-standing invitation of Frederick the Great of Prussia and lived at the Prussian court in Potsdam from 1750 to 1753, residing at Sanssouci and in Berlin. The friendship soured amid disputes over money and Voltaire's satire of the Berlin Academy's president, and Voltaire left Prussia in 1753, briefly detained at Frankfurt on the king's orders.

Candide and later work

Voltaire eventually settled at Ferney, near Geneva, where he spent the last twenty years of his life. There he wrote his most famous work, the philosophical novella Candide (1759), a satire of Leibnizian optimism, as well as the Dictionnaire philosophique (1764). He campaigned for victims of religious persecution, most famously Jean Calas, a Protestant executed in 1762 whom Voltaire succeeded in having posthumously exonerated.

Death and legacy

Voltaire returned to Paris in 1778 to a triumphant reception and died there that May. In 1791 his remains were transferred to the Panthéon as a hero of the French Revolution. He wrote more than 20,000 letters and some 2,000 books and pamphlets, across every literary form: plays, poems, novels, essays, histories and scientific expositions. His name became synonymous with the Enlightenment itself.
