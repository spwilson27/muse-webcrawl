Harry S. Truman (May 8, 1884 – December 26, 1972) was the 33rd president of the United States, serving from 1945 to 1953. A member of the Democratic Party, he previously served as a United States senator from Missouri (1935–1945) and as the 34th vice president in 1945, succeeding to the presidency on the death of Franklin D. Roosevelt.

Presidency

Truman ended the Second World War in the Pacific, authorising the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki. His foreign policy defined the early Cold War: the Truman Doctrine of containment (1947), the Marshall Plan for European recovery, the Berlin Airlift, the creation of NATO (1949), recognition of Israel (1948), and American intervention in the Korean War (1950), where he controversially dismissed General Douglas MacArthur. At home he desegregated the armed forces (1948), proposed the Fair Deal, and won a famous upset re-election that year over Thomas E. Dewey — memorialised by the erroneous "Dewey Defeats Truman" headline.

Later life and reputation

Retiring to Independence, Missouri, Truman wrote his memoirs and championed his presidential library. Once unpopular on leaving office, he is now consistently ranked among the greatest American presidents by historians.
