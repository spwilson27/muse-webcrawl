Joseph Vissarionovich Stalin (born Ioseb Besarionis dze Jughashvili; 18 December 1878 – 5 March 1953) was a Soviet politician and revolutionary who led the Soviet Union from 1924 until his death in 1953. He held power as General Secretary of the Communist Party and, from 1941, as premier of the Soviet government.

Early life

Stalin was born in Gori, Georgia, then part of the Russian Empire, to a poor family; his father was a cobbler and his mother a laundress. He studied at the Tiflis Theological Seminary, where he was expelled in 1899 after becoming involved in Marxist literature and revolutionary circles. He joined the Russian Social Democratic Labour Party and aligned with Vladimir Lenin's Bolshevik faction, organizing strikes, propaganda, and fund-raising robberies in the Caucasus. He was repeatedly arrested and exiled to Siberia.

Rise to power

After the Russian Revolution of 1917, Stalin rose through the party apparatus, becoming General Secretary in 1922. Following Lenin's death in 1924, he outmanoeuvred rivals — above all Leon Trotsky — by building a loyal network within the bureaucracy, and by the late 1920s he had become the undisputed leader of the Soviet Union.

Industrialization and collectivization

Stalin launched a series of Five-Year Plans that rapidly industrialized the Soviet economy and collectivized agriculture. The forced collectivization of peasant farms, combined with the suppression of the so-called kulaks, contributed to catastrophic famines in 1932–33, including the Holodomor in Ukraine, in which millions died.

Great Purge

In the Great Purge of 1936–1938, Stalin's regime arrested, imprisoned in the Gulag, or executed hundreds of thousands of people — party officials, military officers, intellectuals and ordinary citizens — in a campaign of political terror. Estimates of total excess deaths under Stalin's rule range from several million to around twenty million.

Second World War

Stalin signed the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact with Nazi Germany in 1939, but in June 1941 Germany invaded the Soviet Union. After initial catastrophe, the Red Army turned the tide at Stalingrad and Kursk and advanced to Berlin in 1945. Stalin participated in the Allied conferences at Tehran, Yalta and Potsdam, which shaped the postwar order.

Later years and legacy

After the war Stalin consolidated a sphere of influence in Eastern Europe, tested an atomic bomb in 1949, and supported North Korea in the Korean War. He died of a cerebral haemorrhage in March 1953. In 1956 his successor Nikita Khrushchev denounced his cult of personality in the "Secret Speech", beginning a process of de-Stalinization. Stalin remains a deeply controversial figure: credited by some with industrializing the USSR and defeating Nazism, and condemned for totalitarian rule, mass repression and famine.
