The Potsdam Agreement (German: Potsdamer Abkommen) was the agreement among three of the Allies of World War II — the United Kingdom, the United States, and the Soviet Union — reached at the Potsdam Conference, held at Cecilienhof Palace in Potsdam from 17 July to 2 August 1945. It concerned the military occupation and reconstruction of Germany, its borders, and the entire European theatre of war. It also addressed Germany's demilitarisation, reparations, the prosecution of war criminals and the expulsion of ethnic Germans from former German territories and Eastern Europe.

The conference

The conference was attended by Joseph Stalin, Winston Churchill (replaced partway through by Clement Attlee after the British general election), and the new American president Harry S. Truman, who had succeeded Franklin D. Roosevelt after Roosevelt's death in April 1945. It followed the earlier Yalta Conference of February 1945.

Terms

The protocol established the Allied Control Council to govern Germany as a single economic unit, divided into four occupation zones (American, British, French and Soviet). The "five Ds" guided policy: demilitarization, denazification, democratization, decentralization and deindustrialization. Germany's eastern border was provisionally set along the Oder–Neisse line, placing territories east of it under Polish and Soviet administration pending a final peace settlement. German populations in Poland, Czechoslovakia and Hungary were to be transferred to Germany in an "orderly and humane" manner; in practice the expulsions involved great suffering and many deaths.

Reparations

Each occupying power was to extract reparations principally from its own zone, with the Soviet Union additionally receiving a share of industrial equipment from the western zones. German external assets were divided, and the German merchant fleet and industry were apportioned among the Allies.

Aftermath

Disagreements over the implementation of the agreement — over reparations, the Polish border, and the economic unity of Germany — deepened the rift between the Soviet Union and the Western Allies and contributed to the onset of the Cold War and the eventual division of Germany into two states in 1949. The provisional Oder–Neisse border was confirmed by West Germany in 1970 and definitively recognized by the reunified Germany in the 1990 German–Polish Border Treaty.
