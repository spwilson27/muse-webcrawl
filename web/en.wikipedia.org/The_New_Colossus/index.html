The New Colossus

"The New Colossus" is a sonnet by American poet Emma Lazarus (1849–1887), written in 1883 to raise money for the construction of the pedestal of the Statue of Liberty (Liberty Enlightening the World) in New York Harbor. In 1903, the poem was cast onto a bronze plaque and mounted inside the pedestal's lower level. Its lines "Give me your tired, your poor, / Your huddled masses yearning to breathe free" became among the most famous in American literature and are closely associated with the United States' self-image as a nation of immigrants.

Background

Lazarus was asked to contribute a work to the Art Loan Fund Exhibition in aid of the Bartholdi Pedestal Fund, an auction and exhibition held in 1883 to raise funds for the statue's pedestal. Initially reluctant, she was persuaded by writer Constance Cary Harrison, who suggested she write as though the statue were speaking; Lazarus, sensitized to the plight of Jewish refugees fleeing Russian pogroms, produced the sonnet in a few days. It was read at the exhibition's opening and published in Joseph Pulitzer's New York World.

The poem

The title contrasts the statue with the ancient Colossus of Rhodes, a monument to military might. Where the Greek colossus is a "brazen giant of Greek fame" standing in conquest, the new colossus is "a mighty woman with a torch" — the "Mother of Exiles" — whose welcome is directed not at the storied pomp of old lands but at their outcasts:

"Keep, ancient lands, your storied pomp!" cries she
With silent lips. "Give me your tired, your poor,
Your huddled masses yearning to breathe free,
The wretched refuse of your teeming shore.
Send these, the homeless, tempest-tost to me,
I lift my lamp beside the golden door!"

Later history

The poem was largely forgotten after Lazarus's early death in 1887 until her friend Georgina Schuyler campaigned to memorialize it; in 1903 the sonnet was inscribed on a bronze plaque placed on the inner wall of the pedestal. The plaque is now displayed in the Statue of Liberty Museum. The poem's closing lines have been quoted extensively in debates over American immigration policy.
