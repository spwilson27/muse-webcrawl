Critical illness insurance, otherwise known as critical illness cover or a dread disease policy, is an insurance product in which the insurer is contracted to typically make a lump sum cash payment if the policyholder is diagnosed with one of the specific illnesses on a predetermined list as part of an insurance policy.

History

The product was devised by South African heart surgeon Dr Marius Barnard, brother of Christiaan Barnard, who observed that while medical advances let patients survive serious illnesses, many were financially ruined by the experience. The first policy was launched in South Africa on 6 October 1983, initially covering four conditions: heart attack, coronary artery bypass surgery, cancer and stroke. The concept spread to the United Kingdom, where standardised definitions were later developed by the Association of British Insurers, and then worldwide.

How it works

Unlike health insurance, which reimburses treatment costs, critical illness insurance pays a fixed benefit upon diagnosis of a covered condition meeting the policy definition, regardless of actual expenses or recovery. The money may be used for any purpose — replacing lost income, adapting a home, or funding treatment. Policies typically impose a survival period (often 10–30 days after diagnosis) and exclude pre-existing conditions. Covered condition lists have expanded over time to dozens of illnesses, with partial payments for less severe forms in many modern policies.

Criticism

Policies have been criticised for restrictive definitions that can lead to declined claims where a diagnosis does not precisely match the policy wording, prompting regulators and industry bodies to promote clearer standard definitions and publication of claims-paid statistics.
