Sound system (Jamaican)

In Jamaican popular culture, a sound system is a group of disc jockeys, engineers, and MCs playing ska, rocksteady, reggae, and dancehall music. The concept originates in the ghettos of Kingston, Jamaica, in the 1950s and became the primary way Jamaicans heard new music.

History

Sound system operators such as Tom the Great Sebastian, Clement "Coxsone" Dodd (Downbeat), and Duke Reid (the Trojan) built powerful mobile rigs of amplifiers and huge speaker cabinets to play American rhythm and blues records at outdoor dances. Competition between systems was fierce, driving operators to seek exclusive recordings; Dodd and Reid went on to found the legendary Studio One and Treasure Isle recording studios as the music shifted toward locally produced ska.

Elements

A sound system centrepieces the selector (DJ), who chooses records, and the deejay (MC), who toasts — rhythmic, rhymed chanting over the music — a practice that strongly influenced the development of rap. Exclusive "dubplates" (special recordings praising one's own system) and sound clashes, formal contests between rival systems, remain central to the culture.

Influence

Carried by the Jamaican diaspora, sound system culture shaped British reggae, jungle, dubstep, and grime, and its emphasis on bass, versioning, and the MC influenced hip hop in the Bronx, where Jamaican-born DJ Kool Herc transplanted the Kingston model.
