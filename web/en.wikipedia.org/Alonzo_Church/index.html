Alonzo Church

Born: 14 June 1903, Washington, D.C., US
Died: 11 August 1995, Hudson, Ohio, US
Known for: Lambda calculus, Church–Turing thesis

Alonzo Church (14 June 1903 – 11 August 1995) was an American mathematician, logician, and computer scientist who made fundamental contributions to mathematical logic and theoretical computer science. He is best known for the lambda calculus, the Church–Turing thesis, and for proving the undecidability of the Entscheidungsproblem.

Life

Church was born in Washington, D.C., and educated at Princeton University, receiving his PhD in 1927 under Oswald Veblen. After a National Research Fellowship took him to Harvard, Göttingen, and Amsterdam, he joined the Princeton mathematics faculty in 1929, becoming a full professor in 1939. He edited the Journal of Symbolic Logic for decades and supervised many notable doctoral students, including Alan Turing, Stephen Cole Kleene, J. Barkley Rosser, Leon Henkin, and Dana Scott. In 1967 he moved to the University of California, Los Angeles, where he remained until 1990.

Work

In the 1930s Church developed the lambda calculus, a formal system for defining functions and their application, originally intended as a foundation for mathematics. In 1936 he used it to give the first negative solution to Hilbert's Entscheidungsproblem, showing that no general algorithm can decide the truth of arbitrary statements of first-order logic; Turing independently reached the same conclusion with his Turing machine model. The Church–Turing thesis identifies the intuitively computable functions with those computable by these formal systems. Church also devised the first purely functional programming formalism, making lambda calculus an ancestor of the Lisp and functional programming traditions, and introduced the notions underlying Church numerals and the Church–Rosser theorem.
