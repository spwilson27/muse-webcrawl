Stesichorus (Greek: Στησίχορος; c. 630 – 555 BC) was a Greek lyric poet native to Sicily, associated with the city of Himera. Among the Nine Lyric Poets esteemed by scholars of Hellenistic Alexandria, he was famous for long narrative choral poems treating epic themes — Quintilian said he bore "the burden of epic song on a lyre".

Life

Tradition says his real name was Tisias and that "Stesichorus" ("chorus-arranger") was a professional nickname. He was said to have warned Himera against the tyrant Phalaris with a fable, and to have opposed the Locrians. His lifetime spans the era of the poets Sappho and Alcaeus.

Works

His poems, arranged in 26 books in antiquity, included the Geryoneis (on Heracles' theft of the cattle of Geryon, notable for sympathising with the monster), the Sack of Troy, Helen, the Oresteia, and the Nostoi. Only fragments survive, greatly expanded by papyrus finds in the 20th century.

The Palinode

Legend held that Stesichorus was struck blind for slandering Helen in a poem, and recovered his sight after composing a palinode recanting: Helen never went to Troy — only her eidolon (phantom) did, while the real Helen waited in Egypt. Plato retells the story in the Phaedrus, and Euripides dramatised the version in his Helen.

Influence

Stesichorus' triadic strophic structure (strophe, antistrophe, epode) influenced later choral lyric, especially Pindar. Modern poet Anne Carson's Autobiography of Red (1998) reimagines the Geryoneis.
