Checksum

A checksum is a small-sized block of data derived from another block of digital data for the purpose of detecting errors that may have been introduced during its transmission or storage. By itself it is not proof of authenticity, but a mismatch between a recomputed checksum and the stored value shows that the data has changed. Checksums are the digital analogue of the check digit used on identification numbers.

Algorithms

Simple checksum algorithms include parity bytes, modular sums and position-dependent sums such as Fletcher's checksum and Adler-32. More robust error-detecting codes, notably cyclic redundancy checks (CRCs), treat the data as a polynomial and take its remainder modulo a generator polynomial; CRC-32 is embedded in Ethernet, ZIP archives and PNG images. Cryptographic hash functions such as MD5 and SHA-256 are often used as checksums for file integrity because they are extremely sensitive to small changes, though only the collision-resistant hashes are suitable where deliberate tampering is a concern.

Applications

Checksums appear in network protocols (the Internet checksum in IPv4, TCP and UDP headers), file transfer and storage verification, software distribution (package managers compare published hashes), and error-correction schemes in memory and disk systems. Because simple sums cannot detect reordered or compensating errors, protocol designers choose checksum strength to match the expected error model and performance constraints.
