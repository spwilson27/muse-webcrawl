Linear video editing

Linear video editing is the process of selectively transferring video and audio material from source tapes to a master recording in a sequential, linear fashion. Unlike non-linear editing, which allows random access to any frame and rearrangement of material at any time, linear editing requires that a program be assembled from start to finish: once a segment is recorded onto the master tape, inserting or removing material earlier in the program requires re-recording everything after that point or dubbing to another tape with a loss of quality.

History

Video editing was initially a manual process of physically cutting and splicing 2-inch quadruplex videotape. The introduction of electronic editing controllers in the 1960s and 1970s, and later the Ampex and Sony tape formats such as U-matic, Betacam and 1-inch Type C, made tape-to-tape linear editing the standard method in television production for decades. Edit decision lists (EDLs) were assembled by editors working with timecode, and cuts or dissolves were executed by synchronizing source and record videotape recorders (VTRs) under an edit controller.

Techniques

Two basic recording techniques are used: assemble editing, in which video, audio and control track are all recorded anew, and insert editing, in which only selected video or audio tracks are replaced on an existing control track. An A/B roll configuration with two source machines allowed dissolves, wipes and other transitions through a vision mixer.

Decline

From the 1990s onward, computer-based non-linear editing systems such as Avid and later Adobe Premiere and Final Cut Pro displaced linear suites for most production work, although linear tape-to-tape editing persisted in news environments and for simple dubbing well into the digital broadcasting era.
