Áras an Uachtaráin (Irish pronunciation: [ˈaːɾˠəsˠ ənˠ ˈuəxt̪ˠəɾˠaːnʲ]; "Residence of the President"), formerly the Viceregal Lodge, is the official residence and principal workplace of the President of Ireland. It is located off Chesterfield Avenue in Phoenix Park in Dublin.

The building, which has 92 rooms, was designed by amateur architect Nathaniel Clements and was built in the mid-18th century. It was originally the summer residence of the Lord Lieutenant of Ireland, the British viceroy. After the establishment of the Irish Free State in 1922, it became the residence of the Governor-General, and from 1938 the home of the President of Ireland, beginning with Douglas Hyde.

History

The original house was built by Nathaniel Clements, a park ranger and amateur architect, between 1751 and 1757. After the State bought the property in 1782, it became the summer residence of the Lord Lieutenant. Over the following decades the house was enlarged in several campaigns, notably under Francis Johnston and later Decimus Burton, who also landscaped the surrounding gardens in Phoenix Park.

Following the Anglo-Irish Treaty, the house served as the residence of the Governor-General of the Irish Free State, Timothy Healy, from 1922. It stood vacant for a period in the 1930s after the office was abolished, before being chosen as the residence of the President under the 1937 Constitution. Douglas Hyde moved in on 25 June 1938, the day of his inauguration.

The building and grounds

The house is a two-storey-over-basement villa in a restrained classical style, fronted by a portico added in the 19th century. The State Apartments include the State Reception Room, the State Dining Room, the President's Study and the Council of State Room. The gardens, covering roughly 130 acres, include formal gardens, an orchard and walled kitchen gardens, and are open to the public on guided tours on Saturdays.

Since 2007, a light has been kept in a window of the house — the "light in the window" — as a symbol of welcome to the Irish diaspora, a tradition begun by President Mary Robinson.
