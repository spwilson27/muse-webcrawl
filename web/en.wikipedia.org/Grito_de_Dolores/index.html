The Grito de Dolores ("Cry of Dolores"), also known as the Grito de la Independencia, was the call to arms issued by the Roman Catholic priest Miguel Hidalgo y Costilla in the small town of Dolores, Guanajuato, on 16 September 1810. It is regarded as the act that began the Mexican War of Independence.

Events

Hidalgo, parish priest of Dolores, belonged to a circle of conspirators in Querétaro planning an uprising against Spanish colonial rule. When the plot was discovered, Hidalgo decided to act at once: in the early morning of Sunday, 16 September 1810, he rang the church bell, gathered his parishioners, and urged them to rise against the Spanish government, freeing prisoners and arming the crowd. The exact words of the grito were not recorded; contemporary accounts have him crying "¡Viva la religión!", "¡Viva Fernando VII!" and "¡Muera el mal gobierno!" — long live religion, long live Ferdinand VII, death to bad government. Hidalgo's improvised army marched first to San Miguel and then toward Guanajuato, beginning an eleven-year war that ended with Mexican independence in 1821.

Commemoration

Each year on the night of 15 September, the President of Mexico re-enacts the grito from the balcony of the National Palace in Mexico City, ringing Hidalgo's original bell and shouting "¡Viva México!" to the crowd in the Zócalo; similar ceremonies take place in towns across the country. 16 September is Mexico's Independence Day, a national public holiday.
