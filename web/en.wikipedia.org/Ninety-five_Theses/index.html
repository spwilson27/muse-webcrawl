The Ninety-five Theses or Disputation on the Power and Efficacy of Indulgences is a list of propositions for academic disputation written in 1517 by Martin Luther, then a professor of moral theology at the University of Wittenberg. They advance Luther's critique of the practice of selling indulgences — payments said to reduce the punishment for sins — and question the pope's authority over purgatory. Their publication is traditionally regarded as the beginning of the Protestant Reformation, and 31 October is commemorated as Reformation Day.

Background and content

Indulgence preaching in Germany in 1517 was dominated by the Dominican Johann Tetzel, whose campaign — raising funds for the rebuilding of St Peter's Basilica in Rome and for Archbishop Albert of Mainz's debts — was associated with the slogan, "As soon as the coin in the coffer rings, the soul into heaven springs." Luther's theses argue that repentance is an inward, lifelong act; that the pope can remit only penalties imposed by himself; and that Christians should be taught that giving to the poor is better than buying indulgences. The famous first thesis declares: "When our Lord and Master Jesus Christ said 'Repent,' he willed the entire life of believers to be one of repentance."

Publication and spread

Tradition holds that Luther posted the theses on the door of Wittenberg's Castle Church on 31 October 1517; he certainly sent them, with a letter of protest, to Archbishop Albert. Printed copies spread through Germany within weeks and across Europe within months — one of the first great successes of the printing press in religious controversy. Luther initially intended an academic debate, but the ensuing controversy, his refusal to recant, and his excommunication (1521) made the theses the spark of a lasting schism in Western Christianity.
