Globalisation

Globalisation (Commonwealth English; American spelling globalization) is the process of interaction and integration among people, companies and governments worldwide, driven by international trade and investment and aided by information technology. It encompasses economic integration through global supply chains and financial markets, the spread of culture and media, migration, and the growth of international institutions.

[Image: Container shipping — a symbol of economic globalisation]

History

Although long-distance trade networks such as the Silk Road connected regions for millennia, globalisation accelerated with 19th-century steam shipping, the telegraph and the first era of free trade, and again after 1945 with institutions such as the GATT/WTO, containerised shipping, and — from the 1980s — liberalisation in China, India and the former Soviet bloc. The internet made financial and cultural flows nearly instantaneous.

Dimensions and debates

Economic globalisation is measured in trade-to-GDP ratios, foreign direct investment and integrated production networks. Cultural globalisation spreads languages, brands, food and entertainment worldwide, praised for cosmopolitan exchange and criticised for homogenisation. Political globalisation is reflected in bodies such as the United Nations, the IMF and the WTO. Critics, including the anti-globalisation movement that erupted at the 1999 Seattle WTO protests, argue the process deepens inequality, erodes labour and environmental standards, and concentrates corporate power; supporters credit it with lifting hundreds of millions out of poverty and lowering consumer prices. Since the 2008 financial crisis, and through events such as Brexit, trade conflicts and the COVID-19 pandemic, commentators have debated "slowbalisation" and partial deglobalisation.
