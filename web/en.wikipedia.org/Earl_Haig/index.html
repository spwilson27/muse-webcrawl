Field Marshal Douglas Haig, 1st Earl Haig, KT, GCB, OM, GCVO, KCIE (19 June 1861 – 29 January 1928) was a senior officer of the British Army. During the First World War he commanded the British Expeditionary Force (BEF) on the Western Front from late 1915 until the end of the war, including the Battle of the Somme (1916), the Third Battle of Ypres (Passchendaele, 1917), and the victorious Hundred Days Offensive of 1918.

Field Marshal Sir Douglas Haig commanded the BEF on the Western Front from 1915 to 1919.

Career

Haig, born into the Haig whisky family, was educated at Clifton College and Oxford and commissioned into the 7th Hussars. He served in the Sudan and the Second Boer War, and by 1914 commanded I Corps, then the First Army, before succeeding Sir John French as commander-in-chief of the BEF in December 1915. Under his command the BEF grew into a mass army and, with its allies, defeated Germany in 1918. He was created Earl Haig in 1919.

Legacy

Haig remains one of the most controversial figures of the war. Critics, popularised by works such as Oh! What a Lovely War and Blackadder Goes Forth, blame him for the enormous casualties of the Somme and Passchendaele and portray him as indifferent to losses; other historians credit him with professionalising the BEF and leading it to victory in 1918. After the war he devoted himself to veterans' welfare, helping found the Royal British Legion, and the Haig Fund's poppy appeal is popularly associated with his name.
