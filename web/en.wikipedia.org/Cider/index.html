Cider

Cider
Type: Fermented apple juice
Alcohol: typically 1.2–8.5% ABV
Major regions: United Kingdom, France (Brittany, Normandy), Spain (Asturias, Basque Country), United States

Cider is an alcoholic beverage made from the fermented juice of apples. ("Cider" in North American usage may also mean unfiltered, unfermented apple juice, with the fermented drink distinguished as "hard cider".) Cider making has a history of at least two thousand years; the Roman-era Celts of Britain and northern France made drinks from crab apples, and by the Middle Ages cider was a staple wherever cider apples grew.

Production

Cider is made by milling and pressing apples — traditionally specialised bittersweet and bittersharp varieties rich in tannin, such as Kingston Black and Dabinett in England, though culinary and dessert apples are widely used. The juice ferments with natural or cultured yeasts, sometimes slowly through winter ("keeving" in Normandy produces a naturally sweet, low-alcohol cider). Styles range from bone-dry farmhouse "scrumpy" in England's West Country, through bottle-fermented French cidre bouché and the flat, sharply acidic sidra of Asturias — poured from a height to aerate it — to mass-market sweet carbonated ciders and modern American craft ciders. Ice cider, concentrated by freezing, originated in Quebec.

Consumption

The United Kingdom has the world's highest per-capita cider consumption and largest producers. Cider is also an ingredient in cooking and in the Norman custom of the trou normand, a pause with Calvados between courses. Perry, the pear equivalent, is made similarly.
