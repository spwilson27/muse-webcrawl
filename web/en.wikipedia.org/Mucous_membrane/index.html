Mucous membrane

A mucous membrane or mucosa is a membrane that lines various cavities in the body of an organism and covers the surface of internal organs. It consists of one or more layers of epithelial cells overlying a layer of loose connective tissue known as the lamina propria. Mucous membranes primarily protect and lubricate the structures they line, and most secrete mucus, a thick protective fluid.

Location and function

Mucous membranes line the gastrointestinal tract, the respiratory tract, the urinary tract and the reproductive tracts, as well as exposed surfaces such as the inside of the nose, mouth, lips and eyelids. They act as a barrier against pathogens and foreign particles while keeping underlying tissues moist. In the digestive tract the mucosa also performs absorption and secretion; in the respiratory tract, mucus traps inhaled particles, which cilia then sweep away.

Structure

A typical mucosa comprises an epithelial layer, the lamina propria, and in the digestive tract a thin layer of smooth muscle called the muscularis mucosae. Mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT) within many mucous membranes forms an important part of the immune system, producing antibodies such as IgA that are secreted onto mucosal surfaces.
