Bernard Bosanquet

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Bernard Bosanquet (14 June 1848 – 8 February 1923) was an English philosopher who helped shape the British idealist movement, and who wrote on subjects including metaphysics, political philosophy, aesthetics and the philosophy of religion. He was one of the key figures in the revival of Hegelian thought in Britain.

Life

Bosanquet was born at Rock Hall near Alnwick, Northumberland. He was educated at Harrow School and Balliol College, Oxford, where he studied under T. H. Green and was influenced by the idealism of Plato, Kant and Hegel. He taught at Oxford for about a decade before resigning in 1881 to devote himself to writing and to social work in London, where he was active in the Charity Organisation Society and adult education. From 1903 to 1908 he held the chair of moral philosophy at the University of St Andrews.

Philosophy

Bosanquet's central metaphysical doctrine was absolute idealism: reality is a single, coherent, self-determining whole — the Absolute — and individual things are real only as partial expressions of it. His Gifford Lectures, published as The Principle of Individuality and Value (1912) and The Value and Destiny of the Individual (1913), are his most important works. In political theory, The Philosophical Theory of the State (1899) developed, following Rousseau and Hegel, the idea of the "real will" and the state as the embodiment of the general will. His History of Aesthetic (1892) and Three Lectures on Aesthetic (1915) were influential in the philosophy of art.
