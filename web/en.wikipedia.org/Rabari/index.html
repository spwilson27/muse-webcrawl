Rabari

Rabari men in traditional attire in the Kutch region of Gujarat.

The Rabari people (also spelled Rebari, Raika, or Dewasi) are an indigenous pastoral community found primarily in the northwestern Indian states of Gujarat, Rajasthan and Haryana, with smaller populations in Punjab, Madhya Pradesh and in the Sindh region of Pakistan. Traditionally camel, cattle, sheep and goat herders, many Rabari historically practised seasonal migration over long distances in search of pasture.

The community is famed for its rich textile traditions: Rabari women produce distinctive embroidery with chain stitch and mirrors, appliqué work and tie-dyed cloth, while Rabari men are recognisable by their white clothing, large turbans and gold earrings. Livestock, migration cycles and goddess worship — particularly of Momai Mata and other forms of Devi — are central to Rabari religious life.

Subgroups

The Rabari are divided into numerous subgroups distinguished by region, dress and dialect, including the Kachhi, Dhebaria and Vagadiya Rabari of Kutch and Saurashtra in Gujarat, and the Raika of Rajasthan, traditionally associated with camel breeding. The Raika are credited with developing the camel-herding traditions of the Thar desert; the Marwari and Bikaneri camel breeds are associated with their husbandry.

Way of life

Historically, Rabari herders moved with the monsoon, grazing their flocks on common lands, field stubble and forest margins, and selling milk, wool, animals and manure to farming communities. Since the mid-20th century, shrinking grazing commons, enclosures, protected-area restrictions and drought have pushed many Rabari toward settled agriculture, dairy co-operatives, wage labour and migration to cities. Camel numbers in particular have declined sharply, prompting community and government efforts to sustain camel pastoralism through camel-milk marketing.
