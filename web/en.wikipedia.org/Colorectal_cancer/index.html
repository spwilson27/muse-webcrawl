Colorectal cancer

Colorectal cancer (CRC), also known as bowel cancer, colon cancer or rectal cancer, is the development of cancer from the colon or rectum. It is one of the most common cancers worldwide — roughly the third most commonly diagnosed cancer and the second leading cause of cancer death. Most cases develop slowly over years from benign adenomatous polyps through an accumulation of genetic mutations (the adenoma–carcinoma sequence, classically involving APC, KRAS and TP53).

Signs and screening

Symptoms depend on tumor location and include rectal bleeding, iron-deficiency anaemia, change in bowel habit, abdominal pain and unexplained weight loss; early disease is often asymptomatic. Because removing precancerous polyps prevents cancer, screening is highly effective: strategies include colonoscopy (typically every 10 years from age 45–50 in average-risk adults), flexible sigmoidoscopy, CT colonography and stool-based tests such as the faecal immunochemical test (FIT) and guaiac-based tests.

Causes and risk factors

Most cases are sporadic, with risk increasing with age. Lifestyle factors include diets high in red and processed meat and low in fibre, obesity, physical inactivity, smoking and heavy alcohol use. Inflammatory bowel diseases (ulcerative colitis, Crohn's disease) increase risk. Around 5% of cases arise in hereditary syndromes, principally Lynch syndrome (hereditary nonpolyposis colorectal cancer) and familial adenomatous polyposis.

Treatment

Treatment is guided by TNM staging. Localised disease is treated by surgical resection; chemotherapy (e.g. fluoropyrimidine-based regimens such as FOLFOX) is used for stage III and metastatic disease, radiotherapy mainly for rectal cancer, and targeted agents (anti-VEGF, anti-EGFR) and immunotherapy — notably for mismatch-repair-deficient tumors — in selected patients. Survival ranges from over 90% five-year survival for stage I disease to around 15% for widely metastatic cancer.
