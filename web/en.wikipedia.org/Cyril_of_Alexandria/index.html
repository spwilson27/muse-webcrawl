Cyril of Alexandria

Saint Cyril, Patriarch of Alexandria (412–444), Doctor of the Church.

Cyril of Alexandria (c. 376 – 27 June 444) was the Patriarch of Alexandria from 412 until his death, and one of the most influential and controversial theologians of the early Church. He is chiefly remembered for his defence of the title Theotokos ("God-bearer") for the Virgin Mary and his victory over Nestorius at the Council of Ephesus in 431. He is venerated as a saint in the Roman Catholic, Eastern Orthodox and Oriental Orthodox churches and was declared a Doctor of the Church by Pope Leo XIII in 1882.

Patriarchate

Cyril succeeded his uncle Theophilus as patriarch in 412 after a disputed succession contested by supporters of the archdeacon Timothy. His episcopate was marked by assertions of Alexandrine authority: the expulsion of the Novatian schismatics, conflict with the imperial prefect Orestes, and violent clashes involving the city's Jewish community, which was expelled after riots in 414. The lynching of the philosopher Hypatia by a Christian mob in 415 occurred during his patriarchate; historians debate his personal responsibility, but the episode has coloured his reputation ever since.

The Nestorian controversy

When Nestorius, Patriarch of Constantinople, preached against calling Mary Theotokos, Cyril responded with a campaign of letters, including his famous Twelve Anathemas appended to his Third Letter to Nestorius. The dispute turned on Christology: Cyril insisted on the unity of Christ's person — "one incarnate nature of God the Word" — while Nestorius was accused of dividing Christ into two persons. Emperor Theodosius II summoned the Council of Ephesus (431), which Cyril's party opened before the Antiochene bishops arrived; it deposed Nestorius and affirmed Theotokos. A rival council deposed Cyril in turn, and both men were briefly imprisoned, but Cyril prevailed politically, and in 433 he accepted the Formula of Reunion with John of Antioch, balancing his position with more conciliatory language.

Writings and legacy

Cyril was a voluminous author: commentaries on John, Luke and the Pentateuch, the anti-Arian Thesaurus, treatises on the Trinity, and the apologetic Against Julian, refuting the emperor Julian's attack on Christianity. His Christology became the touchstone of later orthodoxy — the Council of Chalcedon (451) acclaimed his letters — while the Oriental Orthodox churches regard him as their foremost theologian. Modern scholarship has partly rehabilitated his theology even as his use of ecclesiastical power remains debated.
