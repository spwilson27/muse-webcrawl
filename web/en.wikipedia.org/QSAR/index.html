Quantitative structure–activity relationship

A QSAR model's predicted vs. observed activity

Quantitative structure–activity relationship (QSAR) models are regression or classification models used in the chemical and biological sciences and engineering. They relate a set of "predictor" variables — numerical descriptors of a chemical's structure, such as molecular weight, lipophilicity (log P), and electronic properties — to the potency of its biological activity or another response variable.

History and method

The field is generally dated to the work of Corwin Hansch and colleagues in the 1960s, who related the biological activity of series of compounds to substituent constants and partition coefficients, and to the Free–Wilson approach of 1964, which assigned contributions to individual structural fragments. Building a QSAR model involves selecting molecular descriptors, fitting a model (historically linear regression, now often partial least squares, random forests, or neural networks), and validating it on external data to avoid overfitting — a well-known pitfall, since chance correlations can produce impressive-looking but meaningless models.

Applications

QSAR is used in drug discovery to prioritise compounds for synthesis and testing, and in toxicology and regulatory science to predict properties such as mutagenicity, aquatic toxicity and skin sensitisation without animal testing; the OECD has published principles for the validation of QSAR models for regulatory use. Fragment-based approaches are called quantitative structure–property relationships (QSPR) when the endpoint is a physicochemical property rather than a biological activity.
