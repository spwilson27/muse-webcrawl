Progressivism

Progressives advocated reform through government action and expertise.

Progressivism is a political philosophy and reform movement that holds that progress in science, technology, economic development, and social organization is vital to improving the human condition, and that such progress should be advanced through government action, expert administration, and social reform. In the United States, the Progressive Era (roughly 1890s–1920s) produced reforms aimed at curbing corporate monopolies, corruption, and the social harms of industrialization.

Progressive Era reforms

Progressive achievements in the U.S. included antitrust enforcement, food and drug regulation (the Pure Food and Drug Act of 1906), the direct election of senators (Seventeenth Amendment), women's suffrage (Nineteenth Amendment), Prohibition (Eighteenth Amendment), initiative, referendum, and recall procedures, and conservation of public lands. Presidents Theodore Roosevelt, William Howard Taft, and Woodrow Wilson each pursued progressive agendas of varying scope.

Modern usage

In contemporary politics, "progressive" generally denotes support for social justice, labour rights, environmental protection, public provision of services such as healthcare and education, and regulation of markets, situating it on the left of most political spectra. The term is used differently across countries and periods and is sometimes contrasted with both conservatism and classical liberalism.
