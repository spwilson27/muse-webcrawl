Oxygen toxicity

Oxygen toxicity is a condition resulting from the harmful effects of breathing molecular oxygen (O2) at increased partial pressures. It mainly affects the central nervous system and the lungs, and is a hazard in deep scuba diving, hyperbaric oxygen therapy, and spaceflight.

Forms

Central nervous system toxicity — the "Paul Bert effect" — occurs at high partial pressures (typically above about 1.4–1.6 bar) and can cause visual disturbances, tinnitus, nausea, twitching, irritability, and dizziness, culminating in convulsions that are especially dangerous underwater. Pulmonary toxicity — the "Lorrain Smith effect" — results from prolonged exposure to moderately elevated oxygen, causing tracheobronchial irritation, cough, chest pain, and reduced lung capacity. Premature infants exposed to excess oxygen may develop retinopathy of prematurity.

Mechanism and prevention

Toxicity is driven by reactive oxygen species that overwhelm antioxidant defenses and damage tissues. Prevention relies on limiting oxygen partial pressure and exposure time: divers observe maximum operating depths for oxygen-rich nitrox and pure oxygen, and exposure tables (such as the NOAA limits) manage cumulative dose. Historical work by Paul Bert (1878) and J. Lorrain Smith (1899) established the two main forms.
