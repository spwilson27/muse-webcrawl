Gauge boson

In particle physics, a gauge boson is a bosonic elementary particle that acts as a force carrier — a particle that mediates one of the fundamental interactions of nature. Elementary particles interact by exchanging gauge bosons. In the Standard Model, the gauge bosons are the photon, the W and Z bosons, and the gluons.

Gauge bosons of the Standard Model

Photon

The photon (γ) is the massless carrier of the electromagnetic interaction, coupling to electric charge and described by quantum electrodynamics (QED).

W and Z bosons

The W+, W− and Z bosons mediate the weak interaction responsible for beta decay and neutrino interactions. Unlike the photon, they are massive (about 80 and 91 GeV/c² respectively), a consequence of the Higgs mechanism, which gives the weak force its very short range. They were discovered at CERN in 1983.

Gluons

The eight gluons (g) carry the strong interaction described by quantum chromodynamics (QCD). Gluons themselves carry colour charge, so they interact with each other as well as with quarks, leading to colour confinement.

Gauge theory

Gauge bosons arise in gauge theories, in which requiring a symmetry to hold locally (independently at every point in spacetime) introduces the fields whose quanta are the gauge bosons. The Standard Model is built on the gauge group SU(3) × SU(2) × U(1).

Hypothetical gauge bosons

Gravity, if quantised, would be mediated by the graviton, a hypothetical massless spin-2 boson that is not part of the Standard Model. Grand unified theories predict additional heavy gauge bosons, such as X and Y bosons, and many extensions predict a Z′ boson; none has yet been observed.
