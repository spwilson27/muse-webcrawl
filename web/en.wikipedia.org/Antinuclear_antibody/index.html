Antinuclear antibody

Antinuclear antibodies (ANAs, also known as antinuclear factor or ANF) are autoantibodies that bind to contents of the cell nucleus. In normal individuals, the immune system produces antibodies to foreign proteins (antigens) but not to human proteins (autoantigens). In some individuals, antibodies to human antigens are produced, and ANAs are a prominent marker of autoimmunity.

Testing

The ANA test is most commonly performed by indirect immunofluorescence on HEp-2 cells, which reveals both a titre (reported as a ratio such as 1:160) and a staining pattern — homogeneous, speckled, nucleolar, or centromere — that can hint at the specific autoantibodies present. Screening for ANAs is used in evaluating suspected systemic autoimmune diseases such as systemic lupus erythematosus, Sjögren's syndrome, scleroderma, mixed connective tissue disease, polymyositis, and dermatomyositis. Low titres are also found in a substantial fraction of healthy people, so a positive ANA alone does not establish disease.

Specific antibodies

Subtypes of antinuclear antibodies include anti-dsDNA, anti-Smith, anti-histone, anti-Ro/SSA and anti-La/SSB, anti-U1 RNP, anti-centromere, and anti-Scl-70 (topoisomerase I) antibodies, each associated with particular clinical syndromes.

History

The field grew from the discovery of the LE (lupus erythematosus) cell phenomenon in 1948 by Malcolm Hargraves and colleagues, and the demonstration in 1957 that the responsible serum factor reacted with cell nuclei.
