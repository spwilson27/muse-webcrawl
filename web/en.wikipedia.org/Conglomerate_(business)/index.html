Conglomerate (business)

A conglomerate is a multi-industry company — a corporation consisting of several different, sometimes seemingly unrelated businesses. In a conglomerate, the parent company owns stakes in smaller companies that each conduct business separately; the subsidiaries run independently but report to the parent's management.

History

Conglomerates became fashionable in the United States in the 1960s, when companies such as ITT, Gulf+Western, and Ling-Temco-Vought expanded through leveraged acquisitions, and investors valued them for earnings growth produced by combining firms with low price-earnings ratios. From the 1980s onward, many large conglomerates were broken up amid evidence of a "conglomerate discount" — diversified firms trading below the sum of their parts. Warren Buffett's Berkshire Hathaway is a prominent surviving example, along with large diversified groups common in Asia, such as Japan's keiretsu, South Korea's chaebol, and India's Tata Group.

Advantages and disadvantages

Supporters argue conglomerates reduce risk through diversification, allocate capital internally, and can cross-subsidize divisions. Critics argue they are inefficient, hard for investors to value, and prone to misallocating capital to underperforming units; modern markets generally favor focused companies.
