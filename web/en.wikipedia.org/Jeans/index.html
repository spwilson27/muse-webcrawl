Jeans

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Jeans are a type of trousers made from denim or dungaree cloth. Often the term "jeans" refers to a particular style of trousers, called "blue jeans", with copper-riveted pockets, which were invented by Jacob W. Davis in partnership with Levi Strauss & Co. in 1871 and patented by Jacob W. Davis and Levi Strauss on May 20, 1873. Prior to the patent, the term "blue jeans" had long been used for various garments including trousers, overalls, and coats, constructed from blue-colored denim.

History

The word "jeans" derives from the French jean fustian, referring to a twilled cotton cloth from Genoa (in French, Gênes), Italy; "denim" derives from serge de Nîmes, a fabric from Nîmes, France. Denim trousers were worn by sailors and workers for centuries, but the modern garment dates to the California Gold Rush era. In 1853 Levi Strauss founded a dry-goods business in San Francisco. A customer, tailor Jacob Davis of Reno, Nevada, devised copper rivets to reinforce stress points such as pocket corners on work trousers; the two men patented the idea in 1873, and Levi Strauss & Co. began manufacturing riveted "waist overalls" — the archetypal blue jeans, later sold as the 501 style.

Popularisation

Jeans spread from workwear to mainstream fashion in the 20th century. They were popularised in the United States by Western films and by actors such as James Dean in Rebel Without a Cause (1955), becoming a symbol of youthful rebellion in the 1950s. During the counterculture of the 1960s and 1970s jeans became near-universal casual wear, and designer jeans emerged in the late 1970s. Today jeans are produced worldwide in many fits, washes, and fabrics, including stretch denim blends.

Manufacturing and environmental impact

Traditional jeans are made of indigo-dyed warp and white weft cotton twill, sewn with contrasting thread and fitted with rivets and a button or zip fly. Finishing techniques include stonewashing, sandblasting, and laser distressing. Denim production is water- and chemical-intensive, and concerns about water use, dye pollution, and sandblasting hazards have driven interest in more sustainable methods and recycling.
