RNA world

The RNA world hypothesis proposes that early life used RNA both to store information and to catalyse reactions.

The RNA world is a hypothetical stage in the evolutionary history of life on Earth in which self-replicating RNA molecules proliferated before the evolution of DNA and proteins. The term also refers to the hypothesis that life on Earth today descends from that RNA-based world.

RNA can store genetic information, like DNA, and can catalyse chemical reactions, like an enzyme. It may therefore have supported pre-cellular life and been a major step in the evolution of cellular life. The hypothesis was given its name by Walter Gilbert in 1986, and built on earlier suggestions by Carl Woese, Francis Crick and Leslie Orgel in the 1960s.

Evidence

The strongest evidence for an RNA world comes from the ribosome: the machine that synthesises proteins in all living cells is fundamentally a ribozyme, an RNA enzyme, suggesting protein synthesis evolved in an RNA-dominated setting. Many metabolic cofactors, such as ATP, NAD and coenzyme A, are built around ribonucleotide units, which may be molecular fossils of an earlier RNA-based metabolism. Naturally occurring ribozymes, such as self-splicing introns and the RNA component of RNase P, demonstrate that RNA catalysis is not merely theoretical.

Prebiotic synthesis

A central challenge is explaining how RNA could arise spontaneously. Laboratory work, notably by John Sutherland's group in 2009, showed that activated pyrimidine ribonucleotides can form under plausible prebiotic conditions through a route that bypasses the difficult direct combination of sugars and nucleobases. Other researchers have explored alternative genetic polymers, such as peptide nucleic acid (PNA) and threose nucleic acid (TNA), as possible predecessors of RNA.

Criticism and alternatives

Critics note that RNA is chemically fragile and that no self-replicating RNA system has yet been created entirely from prebiotic starting materials. Metabolism-first hypotheses propose that self-sustaining chemical networks preceded genetic polymers. The RNA world remains, however, the most widely supported framework for the earliest evolution of life.
