Macropodidae

Kingdom: Animalia   Phylum: Chordata

Class: Mammalia   Infraclass: Marsupialia

Order: Diprotodontia

Family: Macropodidae (Gray, 1821)

Genera: about 12 living genera, ~65 species

Macropodidae is a family of marsupials commonly known as macropods, comprising the kangaroos, wallabies, tree-kangaroos, pademelons, the quokka and several other genera. The family name derives from the Greek makros ("large") and pous ("foot"), referring to the greatly enlarged hind feet used in their characteristic hopping locomotion. Around 65 living species are recognised, native to Australia and New Guinea.

Characteristics

Macropods are herbivorous marsupials ranging in size from the tiny musky rat-kangaroo (sometimes placed in its own family) to the red kangaroo, whose males can stand over 1.8 metres tall and weigh 90 kg. All share powerful hind limbs, a long muscular tail used as a prop and counterbalance, small forelimbs, and an elongated head with large ears. Many species move bipedally by hopping at speed, an extremely energy-efficient gait at higher velocities, and use a slow pentapedal walk — both forelimbs and tail planted — when grazing. Females give birth to a single tiny young after a short gestation; the joey completes development in the pouch, nourished by milk whose composition changes as it grows.

Ecology and evolution

Macropods occupy habitats from rainforest (tree-kangaroos and pademelons) to arid central deserts (red kangaroo) and rocky ranges (rock-wallabies). Like ruminant mammals, they ferment plant material in a divided stomach, though they do not chew the cud. Fossil macropods were far more diverse and included giant forms such as Procoptodon, a short-faced kangaroo that may have weighed over 200 kg and became extinct in the late Pleistocene.

Relationship with humans

Kangaroos are national symbols of Australia, appearing on its coat of arms and currency, and are harvested commercially for meat and leather under regulated quotas. Several smaller macropod species, including some rock-wallabies and the Gilbert's potoroo relatives in the broader macropodoid group, are threatened by introduced predators such as foxes and feral cats.
