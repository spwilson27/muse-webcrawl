Pope Francis

Pope Francis (Latin: Franciscus; born Jorge Mario Bergoglio, 17 December 1936 – 21 April 2025) was the 266th pope of the Catholic Church, serving from his election on 13 March 2013 until his death in 2025. He was the first Jesuit pope, the first pope from the Americas and the Southern Hemisphere, and the first non-European pope since Gregory III in the 8th century.

Early life and ministry

Bergoglio was born in Buenos Aires, Argentina, to Italian immigrant parents. Trained as a chemical technician, he entered the Society of Jesus in 1958 and was ordained a priest in 1969. He served as provincial superior of the Jesuits in Argentina during the military dictatorship and later as rector of the Colegio Máximo. He became auxiliary bishop of Buenos Aires in 1992, archbishop in 1998, and a cardinal in 2001, appointed by John Paul II. As archbishop he was noted for his humility, simple lifestyle, and advocacy for the poor, earning the nickname "the bishop of the villas" for his work in the slums (villas miserias).

Papacy

Elected in the conclave following Benedict XVI's resignation, Bergoglio chose the name Francis in honor of Saint Francis of Assisi. His pontificate emphasized mercy, care for the poor and the environment, and reform of the Roman Curia. His 2015 encyclical Laudato si' addressed climate change and ecology; other major documents include Evangelii gaudium (2013), Amoris laetitia (2016), and Fratelli tutti (2020). He convoked the Synod on Synodality and led the Church through the COVID-19 pandemic. His relatively pastoral approach to social questions and his decentralizing reforms drew both strong support and vocal conservative criticism.

Death

After a period of declining health, including a hospitalization for pneumonia in early 2025, Francis died on 21 April 2025 (Easter Monday) at the age of 88. His funeral in St. Peter's Square drew world leaders and vast crowds, and he was buried at the Basilica of Santa Maria Maggiore in Rome, as he had requested.
