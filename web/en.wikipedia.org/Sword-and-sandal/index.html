Sword-and-sandal

Sword-and-sandal, also known as peplum (from the Latin word for a Greek tunic), is a subgenre of largely Italian-made historical, mythological or Biblical epic films mostly made between 1958 and 1965. They are characterised by ancient Greek, Roman or Egyptian settings, muscular heroes such as Hercules, Maciste and Goliath, and plots drawn from classical mythology, the Bible and ancient history, usually produced relatively cheaply and dubbed for international markets.

Origins

Italian cinema had a long tradition of ancient-world epics stretching back to silent films such as Cabiria (1914). The genre's modern boom began with the international success of Hercules (1958), starring American bodybuilder Steve Reeves, which demonstrated that inexpensive Italian productions could earn enormous profits, particularly in the United States.

The boom years

Between 1958 and 1965, Italian studios produced hundreds of peplum films. Bodybuilders including Reg Park, Gordon Scott and Mark Forest played heroes borrowed from mythology or invented for the screen. Plots typically featured a lone strongman toppling a tyrant, rescuing a princess and performing feats of strength. The films were shot at Cinecittà in Rome, made extensive use of stock footage and were exported worldwide.

Decline and legacy

By the mid-1960s the market was saturated, and Italian genre production shifted to the Spaghetti Western. The sword-and-sandal formula nevertheless influenced later fantasy cinema, from Conan the Barbarian (1982) to Gladiator (2000), and the genre has been affectionately parodied and re-evaluated by critics who once dismissed it as kitsch.
