Western Schism

The Western Schism, also known as the Papal Schism or the Great Schism of the West, was a split within the Roman Catholic Church lasting from 1378 to 1417, during which rival claimants to the papacy resided in Rome and in Avignon, and — after 1409 — a third line of claimants arose at Pisa. Europe's rulers divided their allegiance between the rival popes along political lines. The schism was healed at the Council of Constance (1414–1418), which secured the resignations or depositions of the claimants and elected Martin V in 1417.

Origins

From 1309 to 1377 the papacy had resided at Avignon under French influence (the "Babylonian Captivity"). Gregory XI returned the court to Rome in 1377 and died the following March. Pressured by a Roman mob, the cardinals elected the Italian Urban VI; Urban's harsh, reforming temper soon alienated them, and a majority of cardinals withdrew, declared his election invalid on grounds of coercion, and elected Robert of Geneva as Clement VII, who settled at Avignon. Each pope excommunicated the other and built a parallel administration, court and college of cardinals.

Three popes and conciliarism

Attempts at negotiation, force and mutual abdication all failed. In 1409 cardinals from both sides convened the Council of Pisa, deposed both popes and elected Alexander V; when neither incumbent yielded, Latin Christendom had three competing popes. The debacle strengthened conciliarism — the theory that a general council outranks a pope. Finally the emperor Sigismund helped convene the Council of Constance, which deposed the Pisan pope John XXIII, accepted the resignation of the Roman pope Gregory XII, deposed the unbending Avignon pope Benedict XIII, and in November 1417 elected Martin V. A remnant of the Avignon obedience persisted in Spain for some years.

Consequences

The schism damaged the prestige of the papacy, encouraged conciliar theories that troubled the Church for a century, and fostered national churches as rulers managed the allegiances of their clergy. The Roman Catholic Church today recognises the Roman line as the legitimate popes of the period; the Avignon and Pisan claimants are classed as antipopes.
