Cash register

InventorJames Ritty (1879)
PurposeRecording sales transactions; storing cash
Key companyNational Cash Register (NCR)
Modern successorPoint-of-sale (POS) system

A cash register, sometimes called a till, is a mechanical or electronic device for registering and calculating transactions at a point of sale. It is usually attached to a drawer for storing cash and valuables, and it typically prints a receipt for the customer.

History

The first cash register was invented in 1879 by James Ritty, a saloonkeeper in Dayton, Ohio, who wanted a machine to stop employees from pilfering receipts; he called his model "Ritty's Incorruptible Cashier." The patents were sold and eventually became the basis of the National Cash Register Company (NCR), led from 1884 by John Henry Patterson, who added features such as the cash drawer, printed receipts, and the bell that rang when the drawer opened — the origin of the expression "ringing up" a sale. Elaborate brass NCR machines became fixtures of shops around the world.

Features

A traditional register includes keys or buttons for entering prices and departments, a display, a totaliser that accumulates the day's sales, a locked cash drawer that opens only during a transaction, and a printer for receipts and journal tapes. Later electronic registers added barcode scanners, tax calculation, and inventory functions.

Modern registers

From the 1970s onward, electronic and computerised point-of-sale systems progressively replaced mechanical registers. Tablet- and cloud-based POS systems are now common in retail and hospitality, though simple electronic cash registers remain in use in small businesses.
