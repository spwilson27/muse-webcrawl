Fluorescence

Fluorescence is the emission of light by a substance that has absorbed light or other electromagnetic radiation. It is a form of luminescence. In most cases, the emitted light has a longer wavelength, and therefore lower energy, than the absorbed radiation. The most striking examples occur when the absorbed radiation is in the ultraviolet region and the emitted light is in the visible range.

Mechanism

Fluorescence occurs when an electron in a molecule is excited to a higher energy state by absorbing a photon, then returns to its ground state, losing some energy as heat and emitting the remainder as a photon. Because of the energy lost, the emitted photon has a longer wavelength — a phenomenon known as the Stokes shift. The process is fast, with typical lifetimes of nanoseconds, distinguishing fluorescence from the slower afterglow of phosphorescence.

History

The phenomenon was described by Sir George Gabriel Stokes in 1852, who coined the term after the mineral fluorite, many specimens of which fluoresce strongly. Stokes observed that fluorspar glowed under ultraviolet illumination.

Applications

Fluorescence is exploited in fluorescent lamps and LEDs, highlighters and safety clothing, gemmology and mineral identification, forensic science, and banknote security features. In the life sciences, fluorescent dyes and proteins — above all the green fluorescent protein (GFP) — are indispensable tools in fluorescence microscopy and molecular biology.

Natural examples

Many minerals, scorpions, and marine organisms fluoresce. Biofluorescence is widespread among fish, amphibians and some mammals, and certain jellyfish produce the fluorescent proteins used in research.
