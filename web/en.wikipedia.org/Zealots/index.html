Zealots

The Zealots were a political movement in 1st-century Second Temple Judaism which sought to incite the people of Judea Province to rebel against the Roman Empire and expel it from the Holy Land by force of arms, most notably during the First Jewish–Roman War (66–70 CE). The term "zealot", the translation of the Hebrew kana'im (קנאים, "zealous ones"), derives from the Greek zēlōtēs and was used by the historian Josephus for several distinct groups active during the revolt.

Josephus identified Judaism of the period with four sects: the Pharisees, Sadducees, Essenes and a "fourth philosophy" founded by Judas the Galilean, who led resistance to the census of Quirinius in 6 CE on the grounds that God alone was Israel's ruler and that paying tribute to Rome was therefore apostasy. The Sicarii, named for the curved daggers (sicae) they used to assassinate collaborators, were a radical offshoot active in Jerusalem in the 50s and 60s CE.

The revolt

When revolt broke out in 66 CE, factions known as Zealots seized control of the Temple in Jerusalem and fought both the Romans and rival Jewish factions, including that of John of Gischala and Simon bar Giora. The internal strife weakened the defence of the city, which fell to Titus in 70 CE with the destruction of the Second Temple. The last stronghold, the desert fortress of Masada, fell in 73 or 74 CE; according to Josephus, its defenders died by mass suicide rather than surrender. Simon the Zealot, one of the Twelve Apostles named in the New Testament, may have belonged to or been associated with the movement.
