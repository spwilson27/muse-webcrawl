Intensive care medicine, also called critical care medicine, is a medical specialty that deals with seriously or critically ill patients who have, are at risk of, or are recovering from conditions that may be life-threatening. It includes providing life support, invasive monitoring techniques, resuscitation, and end-of-life care. Doctors in this specialty are often called intensive care physicians, critical care physicians, or intensivists.

History

The specialty is often traced to the 1952 Copenhagen polio epidemic, when anaesthetist Bjørn Ibsen organised manual positive-pressure ventilation for patients with respiratory failure, dramatically reducing mortality and leading to the first intensive care units. Florence Nightingale's practice of cohorting the sickest patients near the nursing station during the Crimean War is an earlier antecedent. ICUs spread through hospitals in the 1960s and 1970s as mechanical ventilators, monitoring and dialysis improved.

Scope

Intensive care is provided in specialised units: medical, surgical, cardiac, neurological, paediatric and neonatal ICUs. Patients commonly require support for failure of one or more organ systems — respiratory (mechanical ventilation), cardiovascular (vasopressors and inotropes), renal (renal replacement therapy) — as well as treatment of sepsis, trauma, poisoning and post-operative complications. Care is delivered by multidisciplinary teams including intensivists, nurses, respiratory therapists, pharmacists and physiotherapists, with continuous monitoring of vital signs and laboratory values.

Severity scoring

Scoring systems such as APACHE, SAPS and the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score estimate severity of illness and prognosis, and are used for benchmarking and research.

Ethics

Because ICU resources are scarce and many patients cannot consent, intensive care involves distinctive ethical questions: triage, withholding and withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment, advance directives and surrogate decision-making.
