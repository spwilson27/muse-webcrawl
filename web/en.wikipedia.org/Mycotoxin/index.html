Mycotoxin

Mould growing on stored grain; many moulds produce mycotoxins

A mycotoxin (from the Greek mykes, "fungus" and toxikon, "poison") is a toxic secondary metabolite produced by fungi, especially moulds, that is capable of causing disease and death in humans and other animals. The term is usually used to refer to the toxic chemical products produced by fungi that readily colonize crops.

Examples of mycotoxins causing human and animal illness include aflatoxin, citrinin, fumonisins, ochratoxin A, patulin, trichothecenes, zearalenone, and ergot alkaloids such as ergotamine.

Occurrence

One mould species may produce many different mycotoxins, and several species may produce the same mycotoxin. Most fungi are aerobic and are found almost everywhere in extremely small quantities due to the minute size of their spores. They consume organic matter wherever humidity and temperature are sufficient. Where conditions are right, fungi proliferate into colonies and mycotoxin levels become high.

Mycotoxins can appear in the food chain as a result of fungal infection of crops, either by being eaten directly by humans or by being used as livestock feed. Mycotoxins resist decomposition during digestion, so they remain in the food chain in meat and dairy products. Even temperature treatments, such as cooking and freezing, do not destroy some mycotoxins.

Major groups

Aflatoxins

Aflatoxins are a type of mycotoxin produced by Aspergillus species of fungi, such as A. flavus and A. parasiticus. Aflatoxin B1 is considered the most toxic and is a potent carcinogen. Aflatoxins have been linked to increased risk of liver cancer.

Ochratoxin

Ochratoxin A is produced by several species of Aspergillus and Penicillium and is one of the most abundant food-contaminating mycotoxins. It is also a frequent contaminant of water-damaged houses and of heating ducts.

Ergot alkaloids

Ergot alkaloids are produced by Claviceps purpurea, a fungus that grows on rye and related plants. Consumption of ergot-contaminated grain can cause ergotism, historically known as "St Anthony's Fire", with symptoms including convulsions and gangrene.

Trichothecenes

Trichothecenes are produced by species of Fusarium and other genera. They include T-2 toxin and deoxynivalenol (vomitoxin), and can cause alimentary toxic aleukia in humans.

Fumonisins

Fumonisins are produced by Fusarium species growing on maize and other cereals. Fumonisin B1 has been associated with oesophageal cancer in humans and liver and kidney toxicity in animals.

Health effects

The effects of mycotoxins on animal and human health range from acute poisoning to long-term effects such as immune deficiency and cancer. The scientific review "Mycotoxins revisited" notes that the impact depends on the type of toxin, the amount and duration of exposure, and the health status of the exposed individual. Aflatoxin B1 is classified by the International Agency for Research on Cancer as a Group 1 carcinogen.

Regulation

Many countries regulate mycotoxin levels in food and feed. The European Union sets maximum levels for aflatoxins, ochratoxin A, patulin, deoxynivalenol, zearalenone and fumonisins in various foodstuffs. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration sets action levels for aflatoxins in human food and animal feed.
