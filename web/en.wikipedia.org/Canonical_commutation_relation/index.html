Canonical commutation relation

In quantum mechanics, the canonical commutation relation is the fundamental relation between the position operator x̂ and the momentum operator p̂ of a particle. Postulated by Max Born, Werner Heisenberg, and Pascual Jordan in 1925 during the creation of matrix mechanics, it states that the commutator of position and momentum is a multiple of the identity:

[x̂, p̂] = x̂p̂ − p̂x̂ = iℏ

where ℏ is the reduced Planck constant. The relation replaces the classical Poisson bracket {x, p} = 1 of Hamiltonian mechanics and is one of the defining postulates of quantum theory.

Consequences

Because position and momentum fail to commute, they cannot be simultaneously measured with arbitrary precision; the general Robertson uncertainty relation applied to this commutator yields the Heisenberg uncertainty principle, Δx Δp ≥ ℏ/2. For several degrees of freedom the relations generalise to [x̂i, p̂j] = iℏ δij, with position components commuting among themselves and momentum components commuting among themselves. In the Schrödinger representation, where x̂ multiplies by x and p̂ = −iℏ ∂/∂x, the relation follows directly from the properties of differentiation.

Deeper structure

The relation implies that the operators cannot be finite-dimensional matrices (taking the trace of both sides gives a contradiction) and that they must be unbounded — subtleties that historically caused paradoxes until the Stone–von Neumann theorem established the essential uniqueness of representations of the exponentiated (Weyl) form of the relations. Analogous commutation relations for creation and annihilation operators govern bosonic fields in quantum field theory and the quantum harmonic oscillator, while fermionic fields obey canonical anticommutation relations instead.
