Falsifiability


A falsifiable claim can in principle be refuted by observation

Falsifiability (or refutability) is a deductive standard of evaluating scientific theories and hypotheses, introduced by the philosopher Karl Popper in his book The Logic of Scientific Discovery (1934). A theory or hypothesis is falsifiable if it can be logically contradicted by an empirical test that can potentially be executed with existing technologies. Popper proposed falsifiability as the demarcation criterion distinguishing science from non-science: a claim that cannot, even in principle, be shown false is not scientific.

Popper's criterion

Popper argued against the inductivist view that scientific theories are built up from verified observations. Universal statements, such as "all swans are white," can never be conclusively verified by any finite number of observations, but they can be falsified by a single counter-instance — one black swan. Scientific theories, on Popper's account, should be bold conjectures that forbid certain observable events; the more they forbid, the more informative and testable they are. A theory that survives severe attempts at falsification is said to be "corroborated," though never proven true.

Examples

Popper contrasted Einstein's general theory of relativity — which made risky predictions such as the bending of light near the Sun, tested in the 1919 solar eclipse — with Freudian psychoanalysis and Marxist historiography, which he argued could accommodate any possible observation and so explained everything while predicting nothing. In astrology, vague predictions that fit any outcome are a classic example of unfalsifiable claims.

Criticism and developments

The Duhem–Quine thesis notes that hypotheses are never tested in isolation: a failed prediction may result from a faulty auxiliary assumption rather than the core theory. Imre Lakatos proposed that research programmes, rather than single theories, be appraised as progressive or degenerating. Statisticians and philosophers such as Thomas Kuhn also questioned whether actual scientific practice follows falsificationist rules. Nevertheless, falsifiability remains a widely used standard in philosophy of science and in legal contexts, notably in decisions about teaching creationism in public schools.
