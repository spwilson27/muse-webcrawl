Reasonable suspicion

Reasonable suspicion is a legal standard of proof in United States law that is less than probable cause, the legal standard for arrests and warrants, but more than an "inchoate and unparticularized suspicion or 'hunch'"; it must be based on "specific and articulable facts", "taken together with rational inferences from those facts", as decided in Terry v. Ohio (1968). Police may briefly detain a person if they have reasonable suspicion that the person has committed, is committing, or is about to commit a crime, and may frisk the suspect for weapons if they have reasonable suspicion the person is armed and dangerous.

Terry stops

In Terry v. Ohio, the Supreme Court upheld an officer's stop-and-frisk of men whose behaviour he had observed and reasonably associated with a planned robbery. The resulting brief investigatory detention is known as a Terry stop. The Court balanced the individual's Fourth Amendment rights against the government's interest in crime prevention and officer safety.

Application

Reasonable suspicion is judged under the totality of the circumstances. Factors that may contribute include a suspect's presence in a high-crime area, nervous or evasive behaviour, flight from officers (Illinois v. Wardlow, 2000), and information from reliable informants. Courts have held that a mere refusal to cooperate, or presence in a suspicious location, is by itself insufficient. The standard also applies in other contexts, including school searches (New Jersey v. T.L.O.) and traffic stops supported by observed violations or reasonable suspicion of criminal activity.
