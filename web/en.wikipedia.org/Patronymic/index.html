Patronymic

A patronymic, or patronym, is a component of a personal name based on the given name of one's father, grandfather (avonymic), or an earlier male ancestor. It is the male equivalent of a matronymic, which is based on the given name of one's mother or female ancestor. Patronymics remain in use in many naming systems, either as true patronymics that change with every generation or as frozen family surnames derived from them.

A lineage showing how patronymics change each generation.

Examples

In Russia and other East Slavic countries, every person has a patronymic as a middle name, formed from the father's name with suffixes such as -ovich or -evich for men and -ovna or -evna for women: thus Ivan Petrovich is Ivan, son of Peter. Iceland retains a fully patronymic (and matronymic) system, in which Jón Einarsson's son might be named Ólafur Jónsson — "Ólafur, son of Jón" — rather than inheriting a family name. Historical patronymics survive as surnames across Europe: English Johnson and Richardson, Scandinavian Johansson and Pedersen, Welsh ap names compressed into Price and Bowen, Arabic names with ibn or bin ("son of"), Hebrew names with ben, and Scottish and Irish names with Mac and Ó.

History

Patronymic naming was the norm in most cultures before hereditary family surnames spread between the 10th and 19th centuries, a process usually driven by taxation, census-taking, and state administration. Some countries, such as Norway and the Netherlands, froze patronymics into surnames by law in the 19th century, while others, including Iceland, have preserved the living system into the present day.
