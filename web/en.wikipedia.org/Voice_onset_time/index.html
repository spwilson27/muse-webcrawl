In phonetics, voice onset time (VOT) is a feature of the production of stop consonants. It is defined as the length of time that passes between the release of a stop consonant and the onset of voicing, the vibration of the vocal folds, in the following sound. VOT varies systematically between voiced, voiceless unaspirated, and voiceless aspirated stops, and languages differ in where they place the boundaries between these categories.

Categories

If voicing begins before the release of the stop, the consonant has "negative VOT" (pre-voicing), as in French or Spanish /b d g/. If voicing begins shortly after release (roughly 0–30 ms), the stop is voiceless unaspirated, as in Spanish /p t k/ or English word-final stops. If voicing lags by 50 ms or more, the stop is aspirated, like English initial /p t k/ in "pin", "tin", "kin". The classic cross-language study of VOT was published by Leigh Lisker and Arthur S. Abramson in 1964.

Perception

Listeners use VOT as a primary cue for stop voicing, and perception is categorical: stimuli varying in small VOT steps are heard as switching abruptly from one phoneme category to another. Infants show sensitivity to VOT boundaries, and second-language learners often struggle to acquire the VOT settings of a new language.
