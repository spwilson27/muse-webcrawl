House of Alpin

The House of Alpin, also known as the Alpinid dynasty, Clann Chináeda, and Clann Chinaeda meic Ailpín, was the kin-group which ruled in Pictland and its successor realm of Alba from the advent of Kenneth MacAlpin (Cináed mac Ailpín) in the 840s until the reign of Malcolm II in the early eleventh century. The dynasty takes its name from Kenneth's father, Alpín mac Echdach. Its kings laid the foundations of the medieval Kingdom of Scotland.

The Alpinid kings of Alba descended from Kenneth MacAlpin (d. 858).

Origins

Tradition held that Kenneth MacAlpin, king of Dál Riata, conquered the Picts and united the two peoples into a single kingdom — the "union of 843" of later Scottish legend. Modern historians treat the process as more gradual: Gaelic influence had long been growing in Pictland, and Kenneth's dynasty, possibly itself of Pictish maternal descent, established a Gaelic-speaking kingship over both peoples. The new realm, Alba, was centred on Forteviot and Scone in modern Perthshire.

Succession and tanistry

Alpinid kingship did not follow primogeniture. Kingship alternated between two branches of the family — the descendants of Kenneth's sons Constantine I and Áed — under the system of tanistry, in which the successor was chosen from among eligible male kin, often a cousin rather than a son. The alternation produced a pattern of violent succession: many Alpinid kings died in battle or were killed by their successors, including Constantine I (877), Donald II, and Constantine II's rivals in the tenth century.

End of the dynasty

Malcolm II, who died in 1034, left no sons. Determined to secure the succession for his grandson Duncan, he is reported to have eliminated rival claimants from the other Alpinid branch. With Duncan I the kingship passed to the House of Dunkeld, descended through Malcolm's daughter Bethóc, and the direct male line of Alpin came to an end.
