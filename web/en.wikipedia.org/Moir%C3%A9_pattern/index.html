Moiré pattern

A moiré pattern is a large-scale interference pattern produced when an opaque ruled pattern with transparent gaps is overlaid on another similar pattern. The name derives from the French moiré, a type of watered silk fabric whose appearance it resembles. Moiré patterns arise when two grids are superimposed at slightly different angles or with slightly different pitches.

Occurrence

Moiré fringes appear in many settings: overlapping fences or screens, the folds of sheer fabrics, halftone printing (where printers carefully angle colour screens to control the effect), and digital imaging, where the pixel grid of a sensor interferes with fine patterns in a scene, causing colour artefacts. Television images of striped clothing are a familiar example.

Applications

Because small displacements between the grids produce large, easily measured shifts in the fringes, moiré techniques are used in strain measurement, optical alignment, and interferometry. Superposed patterns are also used as security features in banknotes and documents, and deliberately in art and design.
