Rattan, also spelled ratan (from Malay rotan), is the name for roughly 600 species of Old World climbing palms belonging to the subfamily Calamoideae. The greatest diversity is found in the tropical forests of Southeast Asia. Rattans are distinguished from other palms by their slender, solid stems and their climbing habit, supported by spines and whip-like extensions called flagella and cirri.

Description

Rattan stems grow to extraordinary lengths—some exceed 180 metres—while remaining only a few centimetres in diameter. Unlike bamboo, which is hollow, rattan stems are solid and flexible throughout their length, making them strong in tension and easy to bend and weave.

Uses

Rattan cane is a major material for furniture, baskets, mats and handicrafts; the glossy outer skin ("peel") is used for binding and weaving chair seats, while the inner core is used for wickerwork. Whole canes are used for walking sticks, martial arts weapons (as in Filipino arnis), and the canes used in corporal punishment historically in Singapore and Malaysia. Most raw rattan is produced in Indonesia, with significant amounts from the Philippines, Malaysia and other Asian countries; because rattans grow only within standing forest, their harvest is linked to forest conservation, and most commercial species are wild-harvested rather than cultivated.
