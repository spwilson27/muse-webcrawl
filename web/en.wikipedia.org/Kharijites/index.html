Kharijites

Kharijite movements held sway in parts of Persia, Arabia and North Africa in the early Islamic centuries.

The Kharijites (Arabic: الخوارج, al-Khawārij, "those who go out") were the earliest doctrinally distinct movement in Islam, emerging during the First Fitna (656–661). Their name derives from their departure from the army of the fourth caliph, Ali ibn Abi Talib, after he accepted arbitration with Mu'awiya at the Battle of Siffin (657): the seceders held that "judgement belongs to God alone" and that Ali had betrayed the Qur'an by submitting God's decision to men.

Beliefs

Kharijite doctrine held that the caliphate belonged to the most pious Muslim, regardless of lineage — even a non-Arab or a slave could qualify — and that a ruler who sinned gravely forfeited office and must be fought. Many Kharijite groups declared Muslims who committed grave sins to be unbelievers (takfīr), an extreme position carried furthest by the Azariqa of Persia. More moderate branches, notably the Ibadis and Sufriyya, rejected indiscriminate violence and survived longer.

History

The first Kharijites were annihilated by Ali at the Battle of Nahrawan (658), but survivors turned assassin: in 661 a Kharijite murdered Ali in Kufa. Kharijite revolts plagued the Umayyad and early Abbasid caliphates for two centuries. Berber Kharijites, allied with the revolt of 739–743, founded durable states in North Africa, including the Rustamid imamate at Tahert (777–909). The movement as an organised force died out by about the 10th century, except for the Ibadi branch, which survives today chiefly in Oman, the M'zab valley of Algeria, Djerba in Tunisia and the Nafusa mountains of Libya — although Ibadis themselves generally reject the Kharijite label.

Legacy

In contemporary discourse "Kharijite" is often used polemically by mainstream Muslims against extremist groups; historians caution that the medieval movement was diverse, and that analogies to modern movements are disputed.
