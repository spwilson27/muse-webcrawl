Persian gardens

The tradition of Persian gardens (Persian: bāgh) extends back more than 4,000 years and is one of the most influential garden traditions in the world. The classic design, known as the charbagh ("four gardens"), divides a rectangular enclosed space into four parts by water channels, symbolising the four elements and the four rivers of paradise.

History

The origins are often traced to the Achaemenid Empire: the palace garden of Cyrus the Great at Pasargadae (c. 550 BC) used stone water channels laid out in a geometric pattern. The English word "paradise" derives, via Greek paradeisos, from the Old Persian pairidaeza, meaning a walled enclosure.

Design elements

Persian gardens are built to provide coolness and shade in an arid climate, in deliberate contrast with the surrounding desert. Signature elements include:

  • Irrigation channels and pools fed by qanats (underground aqueducts)
  • Shaded pavilions and iwans
  • Fruit trees, roses, plane trees and cypresses
  • Intricate tilework and symmetrical geometry

Legacy

The style spread with Islam across the Middle East, North Africa, Spain and India, shaping the Mughal gardens such as those of the Taj Mahal and inspiring gardens from the Alhambra in Granada to the carpets depicting gardens ("garden carpets"). In 2011, nine Persian gardens in Iran were inscribed collectively as a UNESCO World Heritage Site, including Fin Garden in Kashan and the Eram Garden in Shiraz.
