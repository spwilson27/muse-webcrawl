Battle of Balaclava

Date25 October 1854
LocationBalaclava, Crimea
ResultIndecisive; Russians failed to cut the British supply route
BelligerentsBritish Empire, France, Ottoman Empire vs. Russian Empire

The Battle of Balaclava, fought on 25 October 1854 during the Crimean War, was part of the Allied siege of Sevastopol. Russian forces under General Pavel Liprandi attempted to break the siege by attacking the British supply port of Balaclava and the lines of redoubts along the Causeway Heights. The battle is remembered for three celebrated episodes: the stand of the "Thin Red Line", the Charge of the Heavy Brigade, and the disastrous Charge of the Light Brigade.

The fighting

Russian forces seized several Ottoman-held redoubts on the Causeway Heights in the morning. When Russian cavalry advanced toward Balaclava itself, they were repelled by the 93rd (Highland) Regiment drawn up in a line two ranks deep — immortalized by war correspondent William Howard Russell as the "thin red streak tipped with a line of steel". General Sir Colin Campbell's stand was followed by Brigadier James Scarlett's Heavy Brigade charging uphill and routing a much larger body of Russian cavalry.

Charge of the Light Brigade

Later, through a sequence of ambiguous orders from the commander-in-chief Lord Raglan, transmitted by Captain Louis Nolan to Lord Lucan and the Earl of Cardigan, the Light Brigade of British cavalry charged the wrong objective: not the retreating guns on the Causeway Heights but a Russian artillery battery at the end of the North Valley, flanked by guns on both sides. Of about 670 men who charged, more than 100 were killed and over 160 wounded in some twenty minutes. Alfred, Lord Tennyson's poem "The Charge of the Light Brigade", published weeks later, fixed the episode in popular memory as an emblem of gallantry and military blunder.

Aftermath

The battle was tactically indecisive: the Russians retained some captured redoubts and guns but failed to sever the Allied supply line, while the destruction of the Light Brigade crippled British cavalry for the rest of the campaign. The severe Allied defeat at Inkerman followed eleven days later.
