Airframe

The airframe of an aircraft is its mechanical structure, excluding the propulsion system and usually the avionics and interior fittings. It includes the fuselage, wings, tail assembly (empennage), landing gear, and control surfaces. Airframes must withstand aerodynamic and ground loads, pressurization cycles, and fatigue over tens of thousands of flights while remaining as light as possible.

Construction

Early airframes used wood and fabric over a truss framework. From the 1930s, stressed-skin aluminium construction — in which the outer skin carries much of the load — became standard, typically built as a semi-monocoque of frames, stringers, and riveted or bonded skins. Modern airliners increasingly use carbon-fibre composites, as in the Boeing 787 and Airbus A350, reducing weight and corrosion.

Loads and fatigue

Airframes are designed against limit and ultimate loads with safety factors, and tested for fatigue and damage tolerance. Pressurized fuselages flex with each flight cycle; famous fatigue failures, such as the de Havilland Comet disasters of 1954 and Aloha Airlines Flight 243 in 1988, reshaped certification and inspection practices.

Maintenance

Airlines follow scheduled inspection programmes, from line checks to heavy "D checks" in which the airframe is largely dismantled and inspected. An airframe's life is recorded in flight hours and cycles, and individual aircraft are tracked by manufacturer serial numbers and registrations.
