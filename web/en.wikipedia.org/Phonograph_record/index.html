Phonograph record

A phonograph record (also known as a gramophone record, especially in British English, a vinyl record, or simply a record or vinyl) is an analog sound storage medium in the form of a flat disc with an inscribed, modulated spiral groove. The groove usually starts near the periphery and ends near the center of the disc. The phonograph, invented by Thomas Edison in 1877, originally recorded on cylinders; Emile Berliner's flat disc system, marketed in the 1890s, gradually displaced the cylinder.

History

Early discs were made of shellac and typically played at about 78 revolutions per minute, holding some three to five minutes per side. In 1948 Columbia Records introduced the 12-inch long-playing (LP) record spinning at 33⅓ rpm with microgrooves, and RCA Victor responded in 1949 with the 7-inch 45 rpm single. Vinyl (polyvinyl chloride) replaced shellac, improving durability and sound quality.

Formats

  • LP (33⅓ rpm) — 12-inch albums holding roughly 20–25 minutes per side.
  • Single (45 rpm) — 7-inch discs with one song per side.
  • EP (extended play) — formats intermediate between singles and albums.
  • 78 rpm — the early standard for shellac discs.

Production and playback

Records are pressed from a metal stamper derived from a lacquer master cut on a lathe. In playback, a stylus (needle) rides the groove and its vibrations are converted — by a cartridge — into an electrical signal, amplified and reproduced by loudspeakers. The RIAA equalization curve, adopted in the 1950s, standardized recording and playback frequency response.

Decline and revival

Compact discs surpassed records in sales in the late 1980s, and vinyl became a niche product. Since the mid-2000s, however, vinyl sales have grown steadily — the so-called "vinyl revival" — and in the 2020s vinyl revenues surpassed those of CDs in several markets, driven by collectors, audiophiles, and younger listeners.
