Constellation

Orion, one of the most recognisable constellations.

A constellation is an area on the celestial sphere in which a group of visible stars forms a perceived outline or pattern, typically representing an animal, mythological subject, or inanimate object. The origins of the earliest constellations likely date to prehistory. In 1922, the International Astronomical Union (IAU) formally accepted the modern list of 88 constellations, and in 1928 adopted official boundaries, based largely on the work of Eugène Delporte, so that every point in the sky belongs to exactly one constellation.

History

Forty-eight of the modern constellations were described by the 2nd-century astronomer Ptolemy in his Almagest, drawing on Babylonian and Greek traditions. The remainder, mostly in the southern sky invisible to Mediterranean cultures, were added between the 16th and 18th centuries by explorers and astronomers such as Petrus Plancius, Johannes Hevelius and Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille.

Asterisms

An asterism is a pattern of stars that is not itself an official constellation, such as the Big Dipper within Ursa Major or the Summer Triangle. The stars of a constellation generally have no physical connection; they lie at very different distances and only appear grouped from Earth's viewpoint.
