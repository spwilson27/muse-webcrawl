Also calledAcute stress response; hyperarousal

The fight-or-flight response (also called the acute stress response or hyperarousal) is a physiological reaction that occurs in response to a perceived threat to survival. First described by Walter Bradford Cannon in 1915, it involves activation of the sympathetic nervous system and the release of catecholamines — chiefly adrenaline and noradrenaline — from the adrenal medulla, preparing the body to confront the threat ("fight") or escape it ("flight").

Physiology

Perception of danger, processed by the amygdala and hypothalamus, triggers two pathways: the rapid sympatho-adrenal response, releasing catecholamines within seconds, and the slower hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, releasing cortisol. Effects include increased heart rate and blood pressure, dilation of the pupils and bronchi, redistribution of blood from the gut and skin to skeletal muscles, mobilised glucose, sharpened senses, tremor and sweating — while digestion, salivation and other "rest-and-digest" functions are suppressed. The response subsides as the parasympathetic nervous system restores baseline.

Behavioural components

Beyond fight and flight, researchers recognise related reactions including freezing (attentive immobility or tonic immobility) and, in social animals, appeasement responses — sometimes summarised as "fight, flight, freeze" or "fight, flight, fright, faint". Shelley Taylor and colleagues proposed a "tend-and-befriend" pattern, emphasising affiliation and protection of offspring under stress, particularly in females.

Evolution and clinical relevance

The response is an adaptive survival mechanism conserved across vertebrates. In modern humans it can be triggered by psychological stressors rather than physical danger, and chronic or excessive activation is implicated in anxiety disorders, post-traumatic stress disorder, cardiovascular disease and other stress-related illness. Hans Selye's general adaptation syndrome modelled the longer-term stages of alarm, resistance and exhaustion.
