Devotio

Devotio
CultureAncient Rome
NatureRitual self-sacrifice of a commander in battle

A Roman commander dedicating himself to the gods of the underworld before battle.

In ancient Roman religion, devotio was an extreme form of votive offering in which a Roman commander vowed his own life—and the lives of the enemy—to the chthonic gods in exchange for victory, then deliberately charged into the enemy ranks to be killed. The ritual combined elements of a vow (votum) and a curse (consecratio), transferring the opposing army to the gods of the underworld along with the sacrificer.

The ritual

According to the historian Livy, the commander, wearing his toga in the Gabine cincture and standing upon a spear, repeated a formula dictated by the pontifex, invoking Janus, Jupiter, Mars, Quirinus, and the Di Manes, and devoting the enemy legions together with himself to the infernal gods. Having made the vow, he rode into the thick of the enemy, where his death was believed to seal the bargain and ensure that the enemy army was doomed.

The Decii

The most famous instances involve three generations of the Decii Mures. Publius Decius Mus devoted himself at the Battle of Vesuvius in 340 BC during the Latin War, and his son of the same name repeated the act at the Battle of Sentinum in 295 BC during the Samnite Wars; a grandson was said to have done likewise at Asculum in 279 BC, though this third case is doubted by historians. The Decii became exemplars of patriotic self-sacrifice in Roman moral tradition.

Devotio in other senses

The term also covered vows devoting another person or an enemy to the gods, and in a broader sense the loyalty and self-surrender expected of soldiers and clients toward their leaders—a usage that later fed into the ideology of devotion to the emperor.
