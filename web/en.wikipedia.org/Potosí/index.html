Potosí is a city in southern Bolivia, capital of the Potosí Department, famed as one of the highest cities in the world and for the fabulously rich silver mines of the Cerro Rico ("Rich Mountain") that overlook it. Founded in 1545 shortly after silver was discovered in the mountain, it became one of the largest and wealthiest cities of the Spanish Empire and the Americas.

History

Silver was discovered at Cerro Rico in 1545, traditionally attributed to the indigenous herder Diego Gualpa. The city boomed: by the early 17th century its population approached 160,000–200,000, rivalling London and Seville. Potosí's silver — extracted largely through the mita system of forced indigenous labour, under brutal conditions that cost vast numbers of lives — financed Spain's wars in Europe and flowed through global trade as far as China. The colonial mint, the Casa de la Moneda (built 1757–1773), stamped coins known worldwide. Spanish still preserves the expression vale un Potosí — "worth a Potosí" — for immense wealth.

As silver grades declined in the 18th and 19th centuries, so did the city. Tin mining later partly replaced silver. Modern cooperative mining of silver, zinc, lead, and tin continues in Cerro Rico, often in dangerous informal conditions; the mountain's shape has been altered by centuries of workings and faces collapse risks. Potosí's colonial centre was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1987 and placed on the danger list in 2014.

Geography

Potosí sits at about 4,090 metres in the Bolivian Altiplano fringes of the Cordillera Oriental, with a cold high-altitude climate. Its population is roughly 200,000–260,000 in the municipality.
