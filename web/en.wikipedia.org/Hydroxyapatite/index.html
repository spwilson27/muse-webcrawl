Hydroxyapatite (also called hydroxylapatite) is a naturally occurring mineral form of calcium apatite with the formula Ca10(PO4)6(OH)2. It is the hydroxyl endmember of the complex apatite group. Hydroxyapatite crystals are hexagonal, and the pure mineral is white, though naturally occurring forms are often brown, yellow, or green due to substitutions and impurities.

Occurrence in the body

Up to about 50% by volume and 70% by weight of human bone is a modified form of hydroxyapatite known as bone mineral. Carbonated calcium-deficient hydroxyapatite is the main mineral of which tooth enamel and dentin are composed. Hydroxyapatite crystals are also deposited in tendons, ligaments, and, pathologically, in soft tissues — as in calcific tendinitis and some forms of vascular calcification.

Medical and dental uses

Synthetic hydroxyapatite is widely used in orthopaedic and dental implant coatings because it is biocompatible and osteoconductive: bone cells grow readily on it, bonding implants to living bone. It is also used as a filler in bone grafts, in chromatography media for protein purification, and as a component of some toothpastes marketed for enamel remineralisation.

Geological occurrence

Hydroxyapatite deposits are found in sedimentary phosphorite formations and in some igneous and metamorphic rocks. Together with other calcium phosphates, it forms the basis of the phosphate rock mined for fertiliser production.
