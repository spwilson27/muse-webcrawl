Aldus Manutius

Born: c. 1449–1452, Bassiano, Papal States
Died: 6 February 1515, Venice
Occupation: Humanist scholar, printer, publisher
Known for: Aldine Press; italic type; pocket editions

Aldus Pius Manutius (c. 1449/1452 – 6 February 1515) was an Italian printer and humanist who founded the Aldine Press in Venice in 1494. He is credited with popularizing the small, portable octavo book format, commissioning the first italic typeface, and publishing authoritative editions of Greek and Latin classics that spread Renaissance humanism across Europe.

Early life

Manutius was born near Rome and studied Latin and Greek, tutoring in the courts of Ferrara and Carpi. His patron, Giovanni Pico della Mirandola, helped support his move to Venice, then Europe's printing capital, where with backing from the Barbarigo family he established his press.

The Aldine Press

Beginning with a five-volume Aristotle (1495–1498), Manutius published the first printed editions of many Greek authors — Aristophanes, Thucydides, Herodotus, Xenophon, and others — prepared with scholar-editors such as Marcus Musurus. In 1501 he launched his famous series of portable Latin classics, beginning with Virgil, printed in the new italic type cut by Francesco Griffo and bound in cheap vellum, making classical texts affordable to students. His printer's mark — a dolphin curled around an anchor, symbolizing "make haste slowly" — became one of the most imitated devices in publishing.

Legacy

Aldine editions set standards for textual accuracy and typographic elegance. Manutius also advanced punctuation, including an early form of the semicolon. After his death the press continued under his son Paulus and grandson Aldus the Younger until 1597. His name survives in the description "Aldine" for books from his press and in publishing lore generally.
