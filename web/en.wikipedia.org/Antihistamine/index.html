Antihistamine

Antihistamines are widely used to treat allergies such as hay fever.

An antihistamine is a drug that inhibits the action of histamine at histamine receptors. Drugs acting at the H1 receptor are used mainly to treat allergic reactions—such as hay fever, urticaria, and allergic conjunctivitis—and are also used for motion sickness, nausea, and as sleep aids. H2 antagonists (e.g. famotidine) reduce gastric acid secretion and are used for acid reflux and peptic ulcers.

H1 antihistamines

First generation

First-generation agents—such as diphenhydramine, chlorphenamine, promethazine, and hydroxyzine—were developed from the 1930s–40s (Daniel Bovet's work led to the first clinically used antihistamine and a share of the 1957 Nobel Prize). They cross the blood–brain barrier, causing sedation and anticholinergic effects such as dry mouth.

Second generation

Second-generation agents—such as cetirizine, loratadine, and fexofenadine—are more selective for peripheral H1 receptors and penetrate the brain poorly, so they cause much less drowsiness and are preferred for long-term allergy treatment.

Mechanism

Most antihistamines act as inverse agonists at histamine receptors, stabilising the inactive conformation of the receptor and thereby counteracting histamine released from mast cells and basophils during allergic responses. Antihistamines do not prevent histamine release and are not a substitute for epinephrine in anaphylaxis, though they are used as an adjunct.
