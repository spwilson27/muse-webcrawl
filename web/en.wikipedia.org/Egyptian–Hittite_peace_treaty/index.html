Egyptian–Hittite peace treaty

The Egyptian–Hittite peace treaty, concluded around 1259 BC between Ramesses II and Hattusili III, is the earliest surviving peace treaty in history.

The Egyptian–Hittite peace treaty, also known as the Eternal Treaty or the Silver Treaty, was concluded between the Egyptian pharaoh Ramesses II and the Hittite king Hattusili III around 1259 BC, some sixteen years after the Battle of Kadesh. It is generally considered the earliest surviving peace treaty in world history, and the only one from the ancient Near East preserved in the versions of both parties.

Background

Egypt and the Hittite Empire had contested control of Syria for decades, culminating in the famous Battle of Kadesh (c. 1274 BC), which both sides claimed as a victory. Continued hostilities, the growing threat of Assyria, and internal Hittite succession problems pushed both kings toward a negotiated settlement.

Terms

The treaty renounced territorial claims, established a non-aggression pact and a mutual defence alliance, provided for the extradition of fugitives — with a remarkable clause that returned fugitives should not be punished — and invoked the gods of both lands as witnesses. It was inscribed on a silver tablet, copies of which were exchanged between the capitals.

Surviving copies

An Akkadian-language clay tablet version was excavated at the Hittite capital Hattusa (modern Boğazkale, Turkey), while a hieroglyphic Egyptian version was carved at the temples of Karnak and the Ramesseum. A replica of the treaty hangs at the headquarters of the United Nations in New York as an emblem of diplomacy.

Sealing the alliance

Relations were further cemented in 1246 BC when Ramesses II married a daughter of Hattusili III, who received the Egyptian name Maathorneferure.
