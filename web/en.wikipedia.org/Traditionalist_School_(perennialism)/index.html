Traditionalist School (perennialism)

The Traditionalist School holds that all authentic religious traditions share a single transcendent origin.

The Traditionalist School is a group of 20th- and 21st-century thinkers who share a common outlook rooted in the philosophia perennis (perennial philosophy). Its principal founders were the French metaphysician René Guénon (1886–1951), the Ceylonese-British art historian Ananda Coomaraswamy (1877–1947), and the Swiss-German philosopher Frithjof Schuon (1907–1998).

Traditionalists hold that beneath the outward diversity of the world's religions lies a single primordial Tradition—a body of metaphysical truth transmitted from a transcendent source. They contrast "Tradition," understood as sacred and immutable principles, with modernity, which they regard as a deviation marked by materialism, individualism, and the loss of a sense of the sacred.

History

The school took shape in the 1920s and 1930s through the writings of Guénon, whose works such as The Crisis of the Modern World (1927) and The Reign of Quantity and the Signs of the Times (1945) articulated a sweeping critique of the modern world from the standpoint of Hindu metaphysics and other traditional doctrines. Coomaraswamy approached the same conclusions through the study of traditional art and symbolism. Schuon systematised the school's doctrine, emphasising the "transcendent unity of religions."

Later figures associated with the school include Titus Burckhardt, Martin Lings, Marco Pallis, and Seyyed Hossein Nasr, who brought Traditionalist ideas into the academic study of religion and the philosophy of science.

Key ideas

Perennial philosophy

Traditionalists teach that all orthodox religious traditions derive from a single metaphysical truth. Differences between traditions are held to be formal and exoteric, while their esoteric cores converge on the same Absolute.

Critique of modernity

The school views the modern world as the terminal phase of a cosmic cycle—the Kali Yuga of Hindu doctrine—characterised by the "reign of quantity": the reduction of quality to quantity, the desacralisation of knowledge, and the inversion of traditional hierarchies.

Initiation and esoterism

Traditionalist doctrine distinguishes exoteric religion, addressed to all believers, from esoterism, the inner path of realisation, which generally requires initiation into an authentic traditional organisation such as a Sufi order or a Hindu sampradaya.

Influence

Traditionalist ideas have influenced the academic field of religious studies, environmental philosophy (through Nasr's work on the sacred and nature), and various political movements—a legacy that remains contested, particularly in the case of Julius Evola, whose political writings diverged from the school's primarily metaphysical orientation.
