Lwów School of Mathematics

The Scottish Café, meeting place of the school

Active: 1920s–1939

Location: Lwów, Poland (now Lviv, Ukraine)

Key figures: Stefan Banach, Hugo Steinhaus, Stanisław Ulam

The Lwów School of Mathematics (Polish: Lwowska szkoła matematyczna) was a group of Polish mathematicians who worked together in Lwów (now Lviv, Ukraine) during the interwar period, publishing in the journal Studia Mathematica (founded 1929) and gathering at the Scottish Café to work on problems, which they recorded in the famous Scottish Book.

Members and work

The school's central figures were Stefan Banach and Hugo Steinhaus, and its members and associates included Stanisław Mazur, Stanisław Ulam, Karol Borsuk, Mark Kac, Władysław Orlicz, Juliusz Schauder, and Stefan Kaczmarz. Working mainly in functional analysis and related fields, they produced foundational results including Banach spaces, the Hahn–Banach theorem, the Banach–Steinhaus theorem, the Banach–Tarski paradox, the Schauder fixed-point theorem, and the Banach–Mazur game. Their collaborative, café-based style — posing problems with prizes ranging from coffee to a live goose — became legendary in mathematical culture.

Dissolution

The school was destroyed by the Second World War: after the German occupation of Lwów in 1941, several members were murdered, including in the massacre of Lwów professors, and others, such as Ulam (who went on to work at Los Alamos), emigrated. The Scottish Book survived the war and was later translated and published, with many of its problems solved only decades later.
