The Shenandoah Valley — the "Breadbasket of the Confederacy" — was fought over throughout 1864.

The Valley Campaigns of 1864 were a series of operations in the Shenandoah Valley of Virginia during the American Civil War. As part of Ulysses S. Grant's coordinated spring offensive of 1864, Union forces moved to clear the valley — the Confederacy's granary and a traditional invasion corridor — while Confederate forces under Jubal Early countered with a dramatic raid that reached the outskirts of Washington, D.C. The campaigns concluded with Philip Sheridan's systematic victories and his devastation of the Valley's agricultural resources, known as "The Burning".

Background

The Shenandoah Valley ran southwest-to-northeast between the Blue Ridge and Allegheny mountains. Its farms fed Robert E. Lee's Army of Northern Virginia, and its orientation let Confederate armies move "down" the valley (northeast) to threaten Maryland, Pennsylvania and Washington while screening their own movements. Stonewall Jackson's Valley Campaign of 1862 had made the region legendary.

Lynchburg Campaign (May–June 1864)

Grant ordered Franz Sigel to advance up the Valley. Sigel was defeated at the Battle of New Market (15 May 1864) by John C. Breckinridge, in a fight famous for the charge of the Virginia Military Institute cadets. Replaced by David Hunter, Union forces won at Piedmont (5 June), occupied Staunton and Lexington (burning VMI), but were repulsed at Lynchburg (17–18 June) and retreated into West Virginia, leaving the Valley open.

Early's Washington Raid (June–July 1864)

Lee detached Jubal Early's Second Corps to clear the Valley and then threaten Washington to relieve pressure on Richmond and Petersburg. Early drove down the Valley, crossed the Potomac, won the Battle of Monocacy (9 July 1864) — a tactical defeat that delayed him a crucial day — and on 11–12 July reached the defences of Washington at Fort Stevens, within sight of President Lincoln, who observed the fighting. With Union reinforcements arriving by sea, Early withdrew, burning Chambersburg, Pennsylvania, on 30 July in retaliation for Hunter's depredations.

Sheridan's campaign (August–October 1864)

Grant consolidated the Valley forces into the Army of the Shenandoah under the aggressive Philip Sheridan, with orders to follow Early "to the death" and to strip the Valley of supplies. After a month of manoeuvre, Sheridan struck: at the Third Battle of Winchester (19 September) and Fisher's Hill (22 September) he drove Early south in rout. Sheridan then fell back, burning barns, mills, crops and livestock across the Valley — over 2,000 barns and 70 mills by his own report — to make it untenable for Confederate operations.

Early, reinforced, surprised Sheridan at dawn on 19 October at Cedar Creek. Sheridan, returning from Winchester, famously rode to the field ("Sheridan's Ride") and rallied his army to a crushing counterattack victory that ended organized Confederate resistance in the Valley. He then joined Grant at Petersburg, his cavalry playing a leading role in the final Appomattox campaign.

Significance

The campaigns removed the Valley as a source of food and as a threat to Washington, boosted Northern morale in time for Lincoln's re-election (together with the fall of Atlanta), and demonstrated the Union's new strategy of targeting the Confederacy's economic base of war-making.
