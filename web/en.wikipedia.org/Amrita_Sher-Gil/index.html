Amrita Sher-Gil

Amrita Sher-Gil (30 January 1913 – 5 December 1941) was a Hungarian-born Indian painter widely regarded as one of the pioneers of modern Indian art. Born in Budapest to a Punjabi Sikh father and a Hungarian mother, she spent her childhood between Hungary and India before training in Paris, where her talent was recognized early — her painting Young Girls (1932) won a gold medal and she became the youngest member of the Paris Salon.

Life and work

Sher-Gil returned to India in 1934, driven by a desire to rediscover the traditions of Indian art. Traveling through the country, she developed a mature style that fused European techniques — particularly the influence of post-impressionists such as Paul Gauguin — with Indian themes, colors, and subjects. Works from this period include Three Girls (1935), Brahmacharis (1937), Bride's Toilet (1937), and The Village Scene (1938), which depict the quiet melancholy of rural Indian life.

Legacy

Sher-Gil died suddenly at Lahore in 1941, aged just 28, leaving around 170 canvases. The Government of India has declared her works National Art Treasures, and most are housed at the National Gallery of Modern Art in New Delhi. She is frequently described as India's Frida Kahlo and is credited with laying the foundations for generations of Indian modernists. A road in New Delhi, Amrita Shergil Marg, is named in her honor, and her work has fetched record prices for Indian art at auction.
