Epidaurus was a small city (polis) in ancient Greece, on the Argolid Peninsula at the Saronic Gulf. It is best known for its sanctuary of Asclepius, the healing god, one of the most celebrated healing centres of the classical world, and for its theatre, considered one of the most perfect examples of ancient Greek theatre architecture.

Sanctuary of Asclepius

The cult of Asclepius flourished at Epidaurus from the 6th century BC, reaching its peak in the 4th and 3rd centuries BC. Pilgrims sought cures through ritual purification, sacrifices, and incubation — sleeping in the sacred dormitory (abaton) in hope of a healing dream. The sanctuary included temples, baths, a gymnasium, a stadium, and a circular building called the Tholos, and inscribed tablets (iamata) recorded miraculous cures.

The theatre

The theatre of Epidaurus, built around 340 BC and attributed by Pausanias to Polykleitos the Younger, seats roughly 13,000–14,000 spectators and is renowned for its exceptional acoustics — a whisper on stage can be heard in the top rows — and its harmonious proportions. Buried under sediment for centuries, it was excavated in the late 19th century and is used today for performances of ancient drama during the Athens and Epidaurus Festival.

History

Epidaurus was an ally of Sparta in the Peloponnesian War. The sanctuary declined with the rise of Christianity and was damaged by earthquakes and invasions; the site was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1988.
