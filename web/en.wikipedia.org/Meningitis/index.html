Meningitis is an acute or chronic inflammation of the meninges, the protective membranes covering the brain and spinal cord. The inflammation may be caused by infection with bacteria, viruses or other microorganisms, or less commonly by certain drugs, cancers or autoimmune conditions. Because the inflammation is in close proximity to the brain and spinal cord, meningitis can be life-threatening and is treated as a medical emergency.

Signs and symptoms

The classic triad is fever, headache and neck stiffness, though not all patients have all three; confusion, photophobia, vomiting and seizures also occur. Bacterial (especially meningococcal) meningitis may produce a characteristic non-blanching petechial rash. In newborns, symptoms are non-specific: irritability, poor feeding and a bulging fontanelle.

Causes

Bacterial meningitis is most often caused by Streptococcus pneumoniae, Neisseria meningitidis (meningococcus) and, in newborns, group B streptococcus and Listeria. It carries a high risk of death and of sequelae such as hearing loss and brain damage. Viral meningitis (enteroviruses, herpes simplex) is more common and usually milder, though herpes encephalitis is an exception. Fungal and tuberculous meningitis occur particularly in people with weakened immunity.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on lumbar puncture: bacterial meningitis typically produces turbid cerebrospinal fluid with high pressure, many neutrophils, high protein and low glucose. Suspected bacterial meningitis is treated immediately with intravenous antibiotics (and often dexamethasone) without waiting for results; antiviral aciclovir is added if herpes is suspected.

Epidemiology and prevention

Large meningococcal epidemics occur in the "meningitis belt" of sub-Saharan Africa. Vaccines against Hib, pneumococcus and several meningococcal serogroups have greatly reduced incidence where they are used, and close contacts of meningococcal cases receive antibiotic prophylaxis.
