Baldwin of Flanders, first Latin Emperor of Constantinople.

Baldwin I (July 1172 – c. 1205), also known as Baldwin IX of Flanders and Baldwin VI of Hainaut, was a leader of the Fourth Crusade and the first emperor of the Latin Empire of Constantinople, which the crusaders established after their capture of the Byzantine capital in 1204.

Early life

Baldwin was the son of Baldwin V, Count of Hainaut, and Margaret I, Countess of Flanders. He married Marie of Champagne, niece of Richard I of England and Philip II of France. On his parents' deaths he inherited Flanders in 1194 and Hainaut in 1195. In Flanders he issued notable legal codes and in 1200 was among the first great nobles of northern France to take the cross at the tournament of Écry, joining the crusade preached by Fulk of Neuilly.

The Fourth Crusade and emperorship

Baldwin commanded one of the principal divisions of the crusading army that, diverted by Venice and by the pretender Alexios IV Angelos, stormed Constantinople in April 1204 and subjected it to a notorious sack. Under the Partitio Romaniae, the empire's lands were divided among the conquerors. Baldwin was elected emperor over the Venetian-favoured rival Boniface of Montferrat and was crowned on 16 May 1204 in Hagia Sophia in a ceremony modelled on Byzantine ritual.

Capture and death

Baldwin's short reign was consumed by war with the Byzantine successor states and with the Bulgarian tsar Kaloyan, whose overtures Baldwin had spurned. On 14 April 1205 the crusader army was crushed by Kaloyan's Bulgarians and Cumans at the Battle of Adrianople, and Baldwin was captured. He died in captivity at Tarnovo, in circumstances never certainly known — later legend claimed Kaloyan had him executed, and even that a skull cup was made of him. His brother Henry succeeded him in Constantinople. In 1225 a mysterious hermit claiming to be Baldwin — the "False Baldwin" — raised Flanders in revolt before being exposed and executed.
