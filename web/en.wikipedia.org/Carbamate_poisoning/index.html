Carbamate poisoning

Carbamate poisoning is poisoning caused by exposure to carbamate compounds, a class widely used as insecticides (including carbaryl, aldicarb, propoxur, and carbofuran) and in some medicines. Carbamates reversibly inhibit the enzyme acetylcholinesterase, causing acetylcholine to accumulate at nerve synapses and neuromuscular junctions.

Symptoms

Signs mirror the cholinergic toxidrome also seen in organophosphate poisoning: salivation, lacrimation, urination, defecation, miosis, bronchorrhea, bradycardia, muscle fasciculations and weakness. Because carbamates bind reversibly and do not "age" on the enzyme, poisoning is usually shorter-lived (often under 24–48 hours) than organophosphate poisoning, and central nervous system penetration is generally less.

Treatment

Treatment is supportive: removal from exposure, airway and ventilatory support, and atropine to counter muscarinic effects. The role of oximes such as pralidoxime is debated for carbamates; they are generally avoided for carbaryl but may be used when the agent is unknown or mixed.
