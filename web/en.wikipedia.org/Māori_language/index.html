The Māori language (te reo Māori, "the Māori language") is an Eastern Polynesian language spoken by the Māori, the indigenous population of mainland New Zealand. One of the country's three official languages — alongside English (de facto) and New Zealand Sign Language — it has been the focus of a major revitalization movement since the 1980s.

History

Māori arrived in New Zealand with Polynesian voyagers from about the 13th century, evolving in isolation from related languages such as Tahitian and Cook Islands Māori. Missionaries developed a Latin-based orthography in the early 19th century, and Māori literacy was widespread. After the Second World War, urbanization and English-medium schooling drove steep decline: by the 1970s fewer than 20% of Māori children spoke the language.

Revitalization

The Māori language revival began with the kōhanga reo ("language nests") preschool movement in 1982, followed by Māori-medium primary and secondary schools (kura kaupapa Māori) and universities (whare wānanga). The Māori Language Act 1987 made te reo an official language and established the Māori Language Commission (Te Taura Whiri). Māori Television (now Whakaata Māori) launched in 2004. The government strategy Maihi Karauna aims for one million speakers of basic Māori by 2040.

Linguistic features

Māori has ten consonants (p, t, k, m, n, ng, wh, r, h, w) and five vowels, each short or long, with long vowels now usually written with macrons (ā, ē, ī, ō, ū). Syllables are open, word order is typically verb–subject–object, and the language distinguishes inclusive and exclusive "we" and dual number in pronouns.
