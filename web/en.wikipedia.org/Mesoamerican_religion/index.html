Mesoamerican religion

Mesoamerican religion is a grouping of the indigenous spiritual traditions of the pre-Columbian cultures of Mesoamerica — among them the Olmec, Maya, Zapotec, Mixtec, Mexica (Aztec), Toltec and Purépecha. Despite local variation, these traditions shared a remarkably stable core of beliefs and practices maintained over some 3,000 years.

Common features

Shared features include a pantheon of nature-linked deities (rain, sun, maize, wind, death), a dualistic and cyclical cosmos, a ritual calendar combining a 260-day sacred count (tzolk'in/tonalpohualli) with a 365-day solar year, sacred ballcourts and pyramid-temples, autosacrifice and human sacrifice, and a belief that the gods had sacrificed themselves to set the sun in motion, creating a debt repaid by human offerings. The Feathered Serpent (Quetzalcoatl/Kukulkan) is found across cultures.

Cosmology

The world was conceived as layered: heavens above, the earth as a flat disk or crocodile floating in primordial waters, and an underworld (Mictlan, Xibalba) below, all connected by the World Tree. Successive creations — the Aztec Five Suns — had ended in catastrophe, and the present world would end too, motivating rituals to sustain the sun.

After the conquest

Spanish colonization imposed Catholicism, often violently, but indigenous beliefs syncretized with Christianity and survive in folk practice, festivals such as the Day of the Dead, and living Maya and Nahua religious life.
