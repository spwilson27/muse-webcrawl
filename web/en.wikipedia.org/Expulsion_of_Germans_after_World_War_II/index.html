Expulsion of Germans after World War II

German expellees from the eastern territories arriving in the western occupation zones, 1945–46.

Following Nazi Germany's defeat in World War II, ethnic Germans were expelled from Central and Eastern Europe on a massive scale. Between 1944 and 1950, an estimated 12–14 million Germans fled or were expelled from the former eastern territories of Germany annexed by Poland and the Soviet Union (East Prussia, Silesia, Pomerania, eastern Brandenburg), from Czechoslovakia (the Sudeten Germans), and from Hungary, Romania and Yugoslavia.

Phases and human cost

The first phase was flight before the advancing Red Army in 1944–45; a second phase of unorganised, often violent "wild" expulsions followed in mid-1945; and a third phase of organised transfers was authorised by the Potsdam Agreement, which called for the transfers to proceed "in an orderly and humane manner". Demographic estimates of deaths attributable to the expulsions range from roughly 500,000 (modern scholarly reconstructions) to about 2 million in older official German figures.

Integration and legacy

The expellees (Vertriebene) formed about a fifth of the population of the early Federal Republic of Germany, where they organised politically and culturally; a Charter of the German Expellees in 1950 renounced revenge while calling for a right to the homeland. The expulsions, and the related Beneš decrees in Czechoslovakia, remained sensitive issues in German–Polish and German–Czech relations for decades.
