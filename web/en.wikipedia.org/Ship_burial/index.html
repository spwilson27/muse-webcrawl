Ship burial

A ship burial or boat grave is a burial in which a ship or boat is used either as the tomb for the dead and their grave goods, or as a part of the grave goods themselves. The practice was widespread among the Germanic peoples of Northern Europe, most famously the Vikings of the Viking Age, but examples are also known from Anglo-Saxon England, the Balts, and ancient Egypt.

Famous examples

The Sutton Hoo burial (c. early 7th century) in Suffolk, England, contained a 27-metre ship buried under a mound, with a rich assemblage of grave goods including a famous helmet, shield, and treasures from the Anglo-Saxon kingdom of East Anglia. In Norway, the Oseberg ship (buried 834) contained the remains of two women and a beautifully carved vessel, while the Gokstad ship (buried c. 890) held a man's burial. The Tune ship and the boat graves at Valsgärde and Vendel in Sweden are further notable finds.

Interpretation

Ship burials are generally understood as high-status funerals, the ship symbolizing the journey of the dead to the afterlife or serving as a prestigious possession to accompany them. The Arab traveller Ahmad ibn Fadlan described a 10th-century Rus' ship funeral on the Volga in which a vessel was burned with its dead chieftain — a related but distinct ship cremation rite. Grave goods in ship burials have included weapons, tools, animals, sledges, beds, and household equipment, offering rich evidence about early medieval society and craftsmanship.
