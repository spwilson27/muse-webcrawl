Obon

Obon (お盆)
Observed byJapanese people; Japanese diaspora
TypeBuddhist festival
SignificanceHonouring the spirits of one's ancestors
DateMid-August (most of Japan); mid-July in some regions; 15th day of the 7th lunar month elsewhere
ObservancesBon Odori dancing, grave visits, floating lanterns (tōrō nagashi)

Obon (お盆) or just Bon (盆) is a Japanese Buddhist custom to honour the spirits of one's ancestors. It has been celebrated in Japan for more than 500 years and traditionally includes a dance, known as Bon Odori. The festival lasts for three days, though its starting date varies by region.

Origins

Obon derives from the Ghost Festival celebrated in China, itself rooted in the Buddhist Ullambana Sutra, which tells of the disciple Mokuren (Maudgalyayana) who rescued his mother from the realm of hungry ghosts by making offerings to monks. The Japanese festival merges this Buddhist teaching with older ancestral rites.

Observances

During Obon, families return to ancestral homes, clean and visit graves, and place offerings of food before household altars (butsudan). Lanterns are hung to guide the spirits of the dead, and at the festival's close floating lanterns (tōrō nagashi) are set on rivers and bays to guide the spirits back to the world of the dead. Communities gather for Bon Odori, folk dances performed around a raised scaffold (yagura) to music, whose steps and songs vary by region. In Kyoto, the Gozan no Okuribi — great bonfires lit on five mountains — marks the close of the festival.

Timing

Obon is conventionally reckoned as the 15th day of the seventh month of the lunar calendar. In most of Japan it is observed around mid-August (Hachigatsu Bon), in parts of the Kantō region in mid-July (Shichigatsu Bon), and on the old lunar date in some areas including Okinawa. The August period is one of Japan's busiest travel seasons, as many workers receive holidays to return to their hometowns.
