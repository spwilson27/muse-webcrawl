In geology, a dike or dyke is a sheet of rock that is formed in a fracture of a pre-existing rock body. Dikes are discordant intrusions: they cut across the bedding planes or foliation of the host rock, unlike sills, which run parallel to them. Dikes form when magma rises through a crack and solidifies underground, or when clastic sediment fills a fissure (a clastic dike).

Formation

Magmatic dikes range in thickness from a few millimetres to hundreds of metres, and can extend laterally for many kilometres. They commonly occur in dike swarms of hundreds of sub-parallel dikes radiating from a volcanic centre or mantle plume, such as the Mackenzie dike swarm in northern Canada, one of the largest on Earth. Because magma cools relatively quickly within a narrow dike, dike rocks are usually fine- to medium-grained; basaltic dikes often weather into dolerite.

Economic and scientific significance

Dikes can host or lead to mineral deposits, and their orientation records the state of stress in the crust at the time of intrusion. Radiometric dating of dikes that cut across other formations provides bracketing ages in the principle of cross-cutting relationships. On other planets, giant dike systems are invoked to explain graben and rift zones on Mars and Venus.

Notable examples

  • The Great Dyke of Zimbabwe, a layered intrusion more than 550 km long
  • Shiprock, New Mexico, a volcanic neck with radiating dikes
  • Dikes of the Isle of Arran, Scotland, a classic teaching locality
