Asymptote

In analytic geometry, an asymptote of a curve is a line such that the distance between the curve and the line approaches zero as one or both of the x and y coordinates tend to infinity. In projective geometry, an asymptote is a line tangent to the curve at a point at infinity. The term comes from the Greek ἀσύμπτωτος (asúmptōtos), "not falling together".

Types

Asymptotes may be horizontal (parallel to the x-axis), vertical (parallel to the y-axis), or oblique (slant). Horizontal asymptotes describe the end behaviour of a function; for example, the graph of f(x) = 1/x approaches the line y = 0 as x → ±∞. Vertical asymptotes typically occur where a function is undefined and blows up, such as at x = 0 for 1/x.

Rational functions

For rational functions, vertical asymptotes occur at zeros of the denominator not cancelled by the numerator. If the degree of the numerator exceeds the denominator's by exactly one, polynomial division yields an oblique asymptote; greater differences give curvilinear asymptotic behaviour instead.

Curvilinear asymptotes

More generally, a curve can approach another curve asymptotically — for example, a function may approach a parabola. The concept also extends to surfaces and to algebraic curves, where asymptotes correspond to tangents at points at infinity.

History

Apollonius of Perga used the term for the hyperbola in his Conics. The word entered common mathematical usage through works on conic sections in the early modern period.
