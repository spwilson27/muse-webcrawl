Four Freedoms

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Four Freedoms were goals articulated by U.S. President Franklin D. Roosevelt on 6 January 1941 in his State of the Union address, known as the Four Freedoms speech. Roosevelt proposed four fundamental freedoms that people "everywhere in the world" ought to enjoy:

  1. Freedom of speech and expression
  2. Freedom of worship
  3. Freedom from want
  4. Freedom from fear

Context and influence

Delivered eleven months before the United States entered the Second World War, the speech helped frame American war aims and the post-war order. The freedoms were incorporated into the Atlantic Charter (1941) and the Declaration by United Nations (1942), and echoed in the preamble of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948), championed by Eleanor Roosevelt, who called them the foundation of the declaration.

In art

The Four Freedoms inspired Norman Rockwell's famous series of four paintings, published in The Saturday Evening Post in 1943 and used in a war-bond tour that raised over $133 million. They are commemorated by the Franklin D. Roosevelt Four Freedoms Park on Roosevelt Island in New York City, designed by Louis Kahn and opened in 2012, and by the Four Freedoms Monument in Madison, Florida, Roosevelt's adopted home state's tribute.
