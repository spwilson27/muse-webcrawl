Palynology

Palynology is the study of microscopic organic-walled particles known as palynomorphs, principally pollen grains and spores, but also dinoflagellate cysts, acritarchs, chitinozoans and other microscopic structures resistant to acid digestion. The term was coined in 1944 by H. A. Hyde and D. A. Williams, from the Greek paluno ("to strew or scatter") and -logy.

Methods

Samples from sediments, peat, or rock are treated with strong acids, including hydrofluoric acid, to dissolve the mineral matrix and leave the decay-resistant organic microfossils. These are mounted on slides, identified under the microscope, and counted to produce pollen diagrams showing how assemblages change through a sequence.

Applications

Because pollen and spores are produced in vast quantities, widely dispersed, and well preserved, palynology is a central tool in several fields:

Palaeoecology and climate reconstruction — pollen records from lake sediments and peat bogs reveal past vegetation and climate change since the last ice age. Biostratigraphy — the petroleum industry uses fossil palynomorphs to date and correlate sedimentary rocks. Archaeology — pollen helps reconstruct ancient landscapes and farming practices. Forensic palynology — pollen assemblages on objects or clothing can link suspects to places. Melissopalynology — the study of pollen in honey — and allergy forecasting also draw on palynological methods.
