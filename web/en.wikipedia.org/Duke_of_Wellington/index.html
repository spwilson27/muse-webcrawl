Field Marshal Arthur Wellesley, 1st Duke of Wellington (1 May 1769 – 14 September 1852) was a British statesman, soldier, and Tory politician who was twice Prime Minister of the United Kingdom. One of the leading military and political figures of 19th-century Britain, he is best remembered for his defeat of Napoleon at the Battle of Waterloo in 1815, and is often referred to as "the Duke of Wellington" or simply "the Iron Duke".

Wellesley was born in Dublin into the Anglo-Irish Protestant Ascendancy. Commissioned as an ensign in the British Army, he served in the Low Countries and India, where he rose to prominence in the Fourth Anglo-Mysore War and the Second Anglo-Maratha War, winning decisive victories at Seringapatam (1799) and Assaye (1803).

Early life

Wellesley was the third son of Garret Wesley, 1st Earl of Mornington, and Anne Hill-Trevor. Educated at Eton and the Royal Academy of Equitation at Angers, France, he purchased a series of commissions and served as aide-de-camp in Ireland while sitting in the Irish House of Commons for Trim. His first active service came in Flanders in 1794–95 during the French Revolutionary Wars.

India

Sent to India in 1796, Wellesley distinguished himself in the Fourth Anglo-Mysore War, culminating in the storming of Seringapatam and the death of Tipu Sultan in 1799. As governor of Seringapatam and Mysore he proved an able administrator. In the Second Anglo-Maratha War of 1803 he won the Battle of Assaye—later considered by him his finest victory—along with Argaon and the fortress of Gawilghur, defeating the Maratha Confederacy forces of Scindia and the Raja of Berar.

Peninsular War

From 1808 Wellesley commanded British forces in the Peninsular War against Napoleonic France in Portugal and Spain. After victories at Roliça and Vimeiro, he returned in 1809 to win at Talavera and, in 1810, to hold the Lines of Torres Vedras against Marshal Masséna. Victories at Fuentes de Oñoro (1811), Salamanca (1812), and Vitoria (1813) drove the French from Spain; in 1814 he invaded southern France and won at Toulouse. He was created Duke of Wellington in May 1814.

Waterloo

During the Hundred Days, Wellington commanded the Allied army in Belgium while Gebhard Leberecht von Blücher led the Prussians. After the battles of Quatre Bras and Ligny on 16 June 1815, Wellington stood on the ridge of Mont-Saint-Jean near Waterloo on 18 June and repelled repeated French attacks until the Prussian arrival in the afternoon turned the battle. Napoleon's defeat ended his rule and the Napoleonic Wars. Wellington's victory, together with his earlier campaigns, made him a national hero; he is one of only a handful of commanders credited with never losing a major battle, though historians debate the point.

Political career

Wellington entered politics as a Tory, serving as Prime Minister from 1828 to 1830, a tenure marked by Catholic emancipation (1829), which he pushed through despite his instincts to avoid splitting his party, and opposition to parliamentary reform. He briefly served as caretaker prime minister in 1834 and later as Foreign Secretary and Leader of the House of Lords under Robert Peel. As Commander-in-Chief of the Forces for much of his later life, he resisted army reform. In 1848 he organised forces to protect London during the Chartist demonstrations. He died at Walmer Castle in 1852 and was given a state funeral and burial in St Paul's Cathedral, beside Horatio Nelson.

Legacy

Wellington's name is commemorated in Wellington, New Zealand, numerous towns, pubs, and schools, the Wellington boot, and Beef Wellington (an association of uncertain origin). His London home, Apsley House ("Number One, London"), and his equestrian statues remain prominent landmarks. He is remembered as a careful, defensive-minded general whose mastery of logistics, terrain, and coalition warfare made him Napoleon's most persistent British opponent.
