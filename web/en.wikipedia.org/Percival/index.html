Percival

Percival (also Perceval, Parsifal, or Parzival) is one of the knights of King Arthur in medieval romance and one of the principal heroes of the legend of the Holy Grail. Innocent and raised in ignorance of knighthood, he comes to Arthur's court, and his quest for the Grail — and his failure, on his first visit to the Grail castle, to ask the question that would heal the Fisher King — becomes one of the central narratives of Arthurian literature.

Literary history

Percival first appears prominently in Chrétien de Troyes's unfinished romance Perceval, the Story of the Grail (c. 1180s), which introduced the Grail to literature. Wolfram von Eschenbach's Middle High German epic Parzival (early 13th century) expanded the story into a sweeping tale of spiritual development. In the later French prose cycles and in Malory's Le Morte d'Arthur, Percival remains one of the three knights (with Galahad and Bors) who achieve the Grail, though Galahad takes the leading role. Richard Wagner's opera Parsifal (1882) is the most famous modern treatment.

Origins

The name may derive from Welsh Peredur, hero of the Welsh tale Peredur son of Efrawg in the Mabinogion tradition, though the relationship between the Welsh and French figures is debated. A folk etymology reads Perceval as French perce-val, "pierce the valley".
