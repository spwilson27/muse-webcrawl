Herbert Henry Dow (26 February 1866 – 15 October 1930) was a Canadian-born American chemist and industrialist who founded the Dow Chemical Company in Midland, Michigan, in 1897. A prolific inventor with more than 100 patents, he built the company on the extraction of chemicals from the rich underground brine deposits of Michigan.

Early life and education

Dow was born in Belleville, Ontario, to American parents and grew up in Cleveland, Ohio. Even as a schoolboy he experimented with inventions. He studied chemistry at the Case School of Applied Science (now part of Case Western Reserve University), graduating in 1888. His research focused on the composition of brines in Ohio and Michigan, which contained bromine and other valuable chemicals then largely imported from Germany.

Founding Dow Chemical

Dow developed an electrolytic process to extract bromine from brine far more cheaply than existing methods. After early ventures struggled or were taken over by backers, he founded the Dow Chemical Company in Midland in 1897, where the brine was especially rich. He famously broke the German bromine cartel's monopoly: when the cartel slashed prices to drive him out of the market, Dow bought up the cheap German bromine, repackaged it, and sold it back into Europe at a profit. The company diversified into chlorine, caustic soda, Epsom salts (magnesium sulfate), magnesium metal and agricultural chemicals, and during the First World War became a major supplier to the Allied war effort.

Later life and legacy

Dow was also a keen orchardist and landscape enthusiast; his home and gardens in Midland became the Dow Gardens, and his son Alden B. Dow became a noted architect. Herbert Dow died in 1930 while undergoing medical treatment in Rochester, Minnesota. He was posthumously inducted into the National Inventors Hall of Fame, and the company he founded grew into one of the largest chemical manufacturers in the world.
