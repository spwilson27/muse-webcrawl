The Group of Seven, once known as the Algonquin School, was a group of Canadian landscape painters active from 1920 to 1933. Its original members were Franklin Carmichael, Lawren Harris, A. Y. Jackson, Frank Johnston, Arthur Lismer, J. E. H. MacDonald and Frederick Varley. A. J. Casson joined in 1926, and Edwin Holgate and LeMoine FitzGerald later became members as the group expanded into the Canadian Group of Painters in 1933.

History and style

The artists, most of whom worked at Toronto design firms, were united by the belief that a distinct Canadian art could be developed through direct contact with the country's rugged northern landscape. Beginning with their first exhibition at the Art Gallery of Toronto in May 1920, they painted the lakes, pines and rock of the Canadian Shield — Algonquin Park, Georgian Bay, Algoma and the North Shore of Lake Superior — in bold colours and simplified, decorative forms influenced by Post-Impressionism and Scandinavian landscape painting. The painter Tom Thomson, who died in 1917, was a formative influence and close associate, though he was never a member.

Initially controversial, the Group was championed by the National Gallery of Canada under Eric Brown and by the mid-1920s had become the dominant force in Canadian art. Their vision of an unpopulated wilderness as the embodiment of Canadian identity has since been both celebrated and criticised, including for overlooking Indigenous presence in the landscapes they painted.

Legacy

The Group of Seven remains central to Canada's visual identity; their works are prominently held by the Art Gallery of Ontario, the National Gallery of Canada and the McMichael Canadian Art Collection, and major retrospectives continue to draw large audiences.
