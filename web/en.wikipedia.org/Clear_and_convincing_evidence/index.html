Clear and convincing evidence

Clear and convincing evidence is a standard of proof used in civil litigation in the United States and some other common-law jurisdictions. It requires that the evidence presented by a party during a trial be highly and substantially more probable to be true than not, and that the fact-finder have a firm belief or conviction in its factuality.

Position among the standards

The standard sits between the lower preponderance of the evidence — "more likely than not" — used in most civil cases, and beyond a reasonable doubt, the standard required for criminal convictions. Courts often describe clear and convincing evidence as evidence that produces in the mind of the trier of fact a firm belief or conviction as to the truth of the allegations.

Applications

The standard is typically reserved for civil matters with especially significant interests at stake, such as civil commitment proceedings, termination of parental rights, deportation, denaturalisation, and claims of fraud. In Addington v. Texas (1979), the U.S. Supreme Court held that clear and convincing evidence is the constitutional minimum for involuntary civil commitment; in Santosky v. Kramer (1982) it applied the same standard to proceedings terminating parental rights.
