Serpin

Structure of a typical serpin

Serpins are a superfamily of proteins with similar structures that were first identified for their protease inhibition activity and are found in all kingdoms of life. The acronym serpin was originally coined because the first serpins to be identified acted on chymotrypsin-like serine proteases (serine protease inhibitors). They are notable for their unusual mechanism of action, in which they irreversibly inhibit their target protease by undergoing a large conformational change to disrupt its active site.

Mechanism

Inhibitory serpins present an exposed reactive centre loop as a bait for the target protease. When the protease cleaves the loop, the serpin rapidly inserts the cleaved loop into one of its beta sheets, flinging the protease to the opposite end of the molecule and distorting its active site. The resulting covalent complex is essentially irreversible, and both proteins are destroyed — serpins are therefore described as "suicide inhibitors".

Examples and functions

The human genome encodes more than thirty serpins. Important examples include antithrombin (SERPINC1), which regulates blood coagulation; alpha-1 antitrypsin (SERPINA1), which protects lung tissue from neutrophil elastase; plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (SERPINE1), which regulates fibrinolysis; and C1 inhibitor (SERPING1). Not all serpins inhibit proteases: some function as hormone carriers (such as thyroxine-binding globulin and corticosteroid-binding globulin), chaperones, or tumour suppressors.

Disease

Mutations that destabilize serpins can cause them to polymerize within cells, producing both loss-of-function and toxic gain-of-function disease. Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency causes emphysema and liver disease; antithrombin deficiency causes thrombophilia; and mutations in neuroserpin produce a familial dementia with characteristic neuronal inclusion bodies.
