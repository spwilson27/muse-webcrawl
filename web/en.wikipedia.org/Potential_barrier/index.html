Potential barrier

Field: Classical and quantum mechanics

Quantum effect: Tunnelling

Examples: Coulomb barrier, semiconductor junctions

A potential barrier is a region of space where the potential energy of a particle is higher than in the surrounding regions, so that classically the particle can pass through only if its kinetic energy exceeds the height of the barrier. Potential barriers are a standard idealised problem in mechanics, often drawn as a "bump" in a graph of potential energy against position.

In classical mechanics, a particle with energy below the barrier height is reflected; a particle with more energy passes over. The concept appears throughout physics: the Coulomb barrier that repels approaching atomic nuclei, the energy hills between chemical states, and the built-in potentials at semiconductor junctions.

Quantum tunnelling

Quantum mechanics changes the picture fundamentally. The wave function of a particle does not vanish at the barrier but decays exponentially inside it, so there is a finite probability that a particle with energy below the barrier height will appear on the far side — the phenomenon of quantum tunnelling. The probability falls rapidly with the barrier's width and height. Tunnelling explains alpha decay, enables nuclear fusion in stars at lower temperatures than classical theory allows, and underlies devices such as the tunnel diode and the scanning tunnelling microscope.

Rectangular barrier problem

The simplest model — a one-dimensional barrier of constant height and finite width — is exactly solvable with the Schrödinger equation and is a staple of introductory quantum mechanics courses, illustrating reflection, transmission coefficients and resonant tunnelling in double barriers.
