Design–build

Design–build (or design/build, abbreviated D–B) is a project delivery system used in the construction industry. It is a method to deliver a project in which the design and construction services are contracted by a single entity known as the design-builder or design-build contractor. It can be contrasted with the traditional delivery method, design–bid–build, in which the owner contracts separately with a designer and a builder.

Overview

Under design–build, the owner signs one contract with a single point of responsibility for both design and construction. The design-build entity may be a single firm providing both services in-house, a joint venture, or a contractor-led team engaging a separate design firm as a subcontractor. Because design and construction overlap, work can be "fast-tracked": construction of early elements such as foundations may begin before the complete design is finished.

Advantages and disadvantages

Advocates cite a single source of accountability, earlier knowledge of cost, reduced administrative burden for the owner, fewer disputes between designer and contractor, and typically faster delivery. Critics note that the owner loses the independent advocate that a separately contracted architect provides, that quality control of design can be harder to enforce, and that comparing competing proposals on both price and design quality can be complex.

History and usage

The integrated master builder tradition predates the modern separation of architecture and construction. Design–build revived strongly in the United States from the 1980s onward, promoted by the Design-Build Institute of America (founded 1993), and by the early 21st century accounted for a large share of non-residential construction spending. Variants include design-build-operate and the turnkey contract.
