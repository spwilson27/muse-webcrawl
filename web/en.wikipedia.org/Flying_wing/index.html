A flying wing is a tailless fixed-wing aircraft that has no definite fuselage, with its crew, payload, fuel, and equipment housed inside the main wing structure. The concept offers a theoretically efficient aerodynamic shape, since the entire aircraft generates lift and there is no fuselage or tailplane to create drag. However, a pure flying wing is inherently unstable in pitch and yaw, and only with modern fly-by-wire flight control systems could these problems be fully mastered.

History

Tailless and flying-wing designs were investigated from the earliest days of aviation: J. W. Dunne produced inherently stable swept tailless designs before the First World War. In the 1920s and 1930s the Horten brothers in Germany developed flying-wing gliders and powered prototypes, culminating in the jet-powered Horten Ho 229 fighter-bomber of 1944. In the United States, Jack Northrop pursued the concept through the N-1M, YB-35 and jet-powered YB-49 bombers of the 1940s; stability and control problems ended those programs. Interest revived with the advent of computerised flight controls and stealth requirements, producing the Northrop B-2 Spirit stealth bomber (first flown 1989), whose successor, the B-21 Raider, was unveiled in 2022. Unmanned aircraft such as the RQ-170 Sentinel and various UCAV demonstrators have also adopted the planform.

Design considerations

Without a tail, pitch control is provided by elevons on the wing trailing edge, and sweep and twist (washout) or reflexed aerofoil sections provide longitudinal stability. Yaw control is achieved with drag rudders, split ailerons or spoilers near the wingtips. Distributing payload through the wing depth constrains the internal volume and the centre of gravity range, and passengers in proposed flying-wing airliners would sit far from the centreline, experiencing pronounced motion in turns.

Related concepts

The flying wing belongs to the broader family of tailless aircraft, which also includes deltas such as Concorde (tailless but with a distinct fuselage). Blended wing body designs, studied by NASA and manufacturers for future airliners, merge a lifting fuselage smoothly into the wings and are close relatives of the pure flying wing.
