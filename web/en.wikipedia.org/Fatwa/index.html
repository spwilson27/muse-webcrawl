Fatwa


A mufti delivering a fatwa

A fatwa (Arabic: فتوى, romanized: fatwā; plural fatāwā) is a nonbinding legal opinion on a point of Islamic law (sharia) given by a qualified Islamic jurist (mufti) in response to a question posed by a private individual, judge, or government. The person who asks for a fatwa is called a mustafti, and the act of issuing one is iftāʾ. Unlike a judge's ruling (qaḍāʾ), a fatwa is traditionally advisory rather than enforceable, though in practice influential muftis' opinions carry great weight.

History

The institution developed alongside Islamic jurisprudence in the early centuries of Islam and became central to the functioning of sharia, since it allowed the law to adapt to new circumstances not addressed in foundational texts. Collections of fatwas by prominent jurists — such as the Ottoman Shaykh al-Islam's rulings or the Indian Fatāwā ʿĀlamgīriyya — became major legal references. In many modern states, official bodies such as Egypt's Dar al-Ifta issue fatwas, while independent and online fatwa services have proliferated in the internet era.

Nature and process

A mufti derives an opinion using the classical methods of jurisprudence (uṣūl al-fiqh): the Quran, the Sunna, consensus (ijmāʿ), and analogical reasoning (qiyās), filtered through the doctrines of a legal school (madhhab). Because fatwas are nonbinding opinions rather than legislation, conflicting fatwas may coexist. Fatwas cover subjects ranging from ritual practice and family law to finance, medicine, and technology.

In popular usage

In Western media, "fatwa" became widely known after 1989, when Iran's Ayatollah Khomeini issued a fatwa calling for the death of author Salman Rushdie over the novel The Satanic Verses. Such death-sentence usages are atypical: the overwhelming majority of fatwas concern ordinary questions of religious practice.
