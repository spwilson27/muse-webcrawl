Chemical bond

A chemical bond is the association of atoms and ions that, when combined, forms molecules, crystals, and other structures. The bond may result from the electrostatic force between oppositely charged ions, as in ionic bonds, or through the sharing of electrons, as in covalent bonds, or some combination of these effects. Chemical bonds are described as having different strengths: there are "strong bonds" or "primary bonds" such as covalent, ionic, and metallic bonds, and "weak bonds" or "secondary bonds" such as dipole–dipole interactions, the London dispersion force, and hydrogen bonding.

Since opposite electric charges attract, the negatively charged electrons surrounding the nucleus and the positively charged protons within a nucleus attract each other. An electron positioned between two nuclei will be attracted to both of them, and the nuclei will be attracted toward electrons in this position. This attraction constitutes the chemical bond. The total energy of a bonded system is lower than that of the separated atoms, which is why bonds form.

History

Early theories of chemical bonding, from the mid-19th century, were used to explain the structures of substances. In 1916 the chemist Gilbert N. Lewis proposed that a chemical bond forms when two atoms share a pair of electrons, introducing the Lewis structure and the octet rule. The same year Walther Kossel described ionic bonding in terms of electron transfer. Walter Heitler and Fritz London's 1927 quantum-mechanical treatment of the hydrogen molecule founded valence bond theory, and Linus Pauling's work in the 1930s, summarized in The Nature of the Chemical Bond (1939), established the modern theory of the covalent bond.

Main types

Covalent bonds

In a covalent bond, atoms share one or more pairs of electrons. Single, double, and triple bonds involve one, two, and three shared pairs. Where the sharing is unequal because of differing electronegativity, the bond is polar covalent, carrying partial charges.

Ionic bonds

Ionic bonding arises from the electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions formed by electron transfer, as in sodium chloride. Ionic compounds typically form extended crystal lattices rather than discrete molecules.

Metallic bonds

In metallic bonding, a lattice of positive ions is held together by a delocalized "sea" of conduction electrons, accounting for metals' electrical conductivity, malleability, and lustre.

Weaker interactions

Hydrogen bonds, van der Waals forces, and dipole interactions are weaker than primary bonds but are crucial to the structures of proteins, DNA, and molecular crystals. Bond properties such as length, strength (bond dissociation energy), and angle are measured experimentally and predicted by molecular orbital and valence bond theories.
