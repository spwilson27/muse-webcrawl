Jamón

Jamón (Spanish for "ham") is a kind of dry-cured ham produced in Spain, one of the country's most celebrated foods. A whole jamón, made from a pig's hind leg, is salted and hung to cure for anywhere from six months to several years.

Types

The two principal types are jamón serrano ("mountain ham"), made from white Landrace-type pigs and cured at least seven months, and the prized jamón ibérico, made from black Iberian pigs. The finest grade, jamón ibérico de bellota, comes from free-ranging pigs that fatten on acorns (bellotas) in the oak dehesa pastures of western Spain and is cured for up to four years, developing a deep, nutty flavour and marbled fat that melts at room temperature.

Production and serving

After slaughter, legs are trimmed, salted, rested, then dried and matured in cellars (bodegas) through seasonal cycles. Jamón is traditionally sliced by hand in paper-thin pieces by a trained cortador, using a long flexible knife and a ham stand (jamonero). Spain consumes the great majority of its jamón domestically; the front-leg equivalent, cut from the shoulder, is paleta. Protected designations include Jamón de Teruel, Guijuelo, Dehesa de Extremadura, Jabugo (Huelva) and Los Pedroches.
