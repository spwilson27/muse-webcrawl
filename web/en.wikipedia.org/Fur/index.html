Fur

Fur is the thick growth of hair that covers the skin of mammals, especially when it is dense and soft. It consists typically of two layers: a dense underfur (down hair) that traps air and insulates, and longer, coarser guard hairs that protect the underfur and shed water.

Function

Fur insulates against cold, provides camouflage or display coloration, protects the skin, and in some species — such as otters — is so dense it keeps the skin dry while swimming. Most mammals are covered in fur; humans, cetaceans, and a few others such as the naked mole-rat are comparatively hairless exceptions.

Human use

Fur has been used for clothing since prehistory, and the fur trade in beaver, mink, sable, fox, chinchilla, and other pelts was a major driver of exploration and colonisation in North America and Siberia. Pelts are obtained by hunting, trapping, or fur farming. Animals reared for fur are typically skinned and the pelts dressed (tanned) before being made into garments such as coats, hats, and trims.

Controversy and alternatives

Animal-welfare campaigns since the late 20th century have sharply criticised fur farming and trapping, and a number of countries and fashion houses have banned or abandoned real fur. Faux fur, made from synthetic fibres, is the principal substitute, though it raises its own environmental questions.
