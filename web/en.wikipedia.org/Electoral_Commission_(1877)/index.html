Electoral Commission (1877)

The Electoral Commission was an ad hoc body created by the United States Congress in January 1877 to resolve the disputed 1876 presidential election between Republican Rutherford B. Hayes and Democrat Samuel J. Tilden.

The Commission met in the Capitol in February 1877 and awarded all disputed electoral votes to Hayes.

Background

In the 1876 election, Tilden won the popular vote, but the electoral votes of Florida, Louisiana, and South Carolina were disputed, with rival slates submitted by Republican and Democratic authorities. One elector from Oregon was also disputed. Twenty electoral votes were in question; Hayes needed all twenty to reach the 185 votes required for victory.

Composition

The Commission consisted of fifteen members: five senators, five representatives, and five Supreme Court justices. The congressional members were chosen to be evenly divided between the parties, and the justices included two Republicans, two Democrats, and one independent, Justice Joseph P. Bradley, who ultimately cast the deciding votes.

Decision and aftermath

The Commission voted 8–7 along party lines on each disputed slate, awarding all twenty votes to Hayes. Congress accepted the decision, and Hayes was inaugurated on March 5, 1877. The resolution was accompanied by the informal Compromise of 1877, under which federal troops were withdrawn from the South, effectively ending Reconstruction.
