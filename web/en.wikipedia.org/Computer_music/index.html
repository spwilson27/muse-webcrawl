Computer music

Computer music is the application of computing technology in music composition, to help human composers create new music or to have computers independently create music, such as with algorithmic composition programs. It includes the theory and application of new and existing computer software technologies and basic aspects of music, such as sound synthesis, digital signal processing, sound design, sonic diffusion, acoustics, electrical engineering, and psychoacoustics.

History

The origins of computer music are closely connected with the origins of electronic music, going back to the first examples of electronic musical instruments at the end of the 19th century. The first computer-generated music is often credited to the CSIR Mk1 computer in Australia in 1951, which played simple melodies such as "Colonel Bogey March". In 1957, Max Mathews at Bell Labs developed the MUSIC series of programs, beginning with MUSIC I, which laid the foundation for direct digital synthesis.

In the 1960s and 1970s, institutions such as Stanford University, MIT, and IRCAM in Paris became centres of computer music research. John Chowning's discovery of frequency modulation synthesis at Stanford led to the commercial success of the Yamaha DX7 synthesizer in 1983.

Techniques

Computer music techniques include algorithmic composition, in which formal processes generate musical material; sound synthesis methods such as additive, subtractive, granular, and physical modelling synthesis; and real-time interactive systems used in live performance. Modern production relies heavily on digital audio workstations (DAWs), MIDI sequencing, and software synthesizers.
