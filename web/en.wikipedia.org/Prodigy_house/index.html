Prodigy house

Prodigy houses are large and showy English Elizabethan and Jacobean houses built by courtiers and other wealthy families, either "noble palaces of an awesome scale" or prodigious "homes of the rich that were built with a 'prodigious' show of wealth". The term originates from the late 16th century and refers to houses such as Hardwick Hall, Longleat House, Burghley House and Hatfield House.

Periodc. 1570–1620
RegionEngland
StyleElizabethan / Jacobean
ExamplesHardwick Hall, Wollaton Hall, Longleat, Burghley House

Characteristics

Prodigy houses were characterised by their great size, symmetrical planning, extensive use of glass ("Hardwick Hall, more glass than wall"), classical ornament derived from pattern books, and prominent staircases and long galleries. They were frequently built near the royal court or on progress routes, partly to attract and accommodate a visit from the monarch, whose favour could make a courtier's career.

History

The style developed after the Dissolution of the Monasteries released large amounts of land and wealth to the landed gentry and nobility. Builders such as Robert Smythson designed houses that combined medieval great-hall traditions with Renaissance symmetry. The building boom peaked during the reigns of Elizabeth I and James I, when ostentatious building was encouraged as a display of loyalty and status.

Notable examples

  • Hardwick Hall, Derbyshire — built for Bess of Hardwick in the 1590s.
  • Wollaton Hall, Nottingham — built for Sir Francis Willoughby.
  • Longleat House, Wiltshire — rebuilt by Sir John Thynne.
  • Burghley House, Lincolnshire — built by William Cecil, Lord Burghley.
  • Audley End House, Essex — once among the largest houses in England.
