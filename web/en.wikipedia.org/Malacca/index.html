Malacca (Malay: Melaka), officially the State of Malacca, is a state in Malaysia located in the southern region of the Malay Peninsula, facing the Strait of Malacca. Its capital is Malacca City, the historic city of the Malacca Sultanate whose port was once the greatest trading emporium of Southeast Asia. In 2008, Malacca City was inscribed, together with George Town, Penang, as a UNESCO World Heritage Site, the "Historic Cities of the Straits of Malacca".

History

According to the Malay Annals, Malacca was founded around 1400 by Parameswara, a prince from Palembang. Its harbour, sheltered and ideally placed between the monsoon trading systems of China and India, grew within decades into a great entrepôt where Gujarati, Arab, Chinese, Javanese, and Malay merchants exchanged spices, silk, porcelain, and gold. Its sultans adopted Islam, and Malacca became the chief centre from which Islam spread through the archipelago.

In 1511 Afonso de Albuquerque conquered the city for Portugal, building the fortress A Famosa. The Dutch captured Malacca in 1641 in alliance with Johor and left landmarks such as the Stadthuys (c. 1650) and Christ Church (1753). Britain took over in 1795 and permanently by the Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824, administering Malacca as one of the Straits Settlements. After Japanese occupation in the Second World War, Malacca joined the Federation of Malaya, becoming part of independent Malaysia in 1957 (the declaration of independence was announced by Tunku Abdul Rahman at the Padang Pahlawan in Malacca).

Culture

Malacca's centuries of trade produced unique blended cultures: the Peranakan (Baba-Nyonya) Chinese, the Kristang-speaking Portuguese Eurasians, and the Chitty community of Indian traders, each with distinctive cuisine, dress, and architecture reflected along Jonker Street and the old town.
