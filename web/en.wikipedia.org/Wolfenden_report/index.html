Wolfenden report

The Wolfenden report, formally the Report of the Departmental Committee on Homosexual Offences and Prostitution, was published in the United Kingdom on September 4, 1957. Chaired by Sir John Wolfenden, the committee recommended that consensual homosexual behaviour between adults in private should no longer be a criminal offence — a landmark in British social and legal history.

Background

The committee was appointed after a series of high-profile prosecutions of homosexual men — including the mathematician Alan Turing in 1952 and the Montagu trial of 1954 — raised public concern about the law. Its remit also covered prostitution, on which it recommended tougher street-soliciting laws, enacted in the Street Offences Act 1959.

Recommendations and reasoning

The report argued that the law's function is to preserve public order and protect the vulnerable, not to impose private morality: "there must remain a realm of private morality and immorality which is, in brief and crude terms, not the law's business." It recommended decriminalization for men aged 21 and over, while rejecting the view that homosexuality was a disease.

Impact

The government initially declined to act, but the report galvanized campaigners, including the Homosexual Law Reform Society (founded 1958). A decade later, the Sexual Offences Act 1967 implemented Wolfenden's central recommendation for England and Wales. The report's distinction between crime and sin influenced liberal reform across the Commonwealth.
