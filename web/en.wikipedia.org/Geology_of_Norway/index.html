Geology of Norway

The geology of Norway encompasses some of the oldest rocks in Europe and records nearly three billion years of Earth history. Much of mainland Norway forms part of the Baltic Shield, an ancient Precambrian craton, overlain and thrust over by the Caledonian mountain belt formed when the continents Baltica and Laurentia collided about 425 million years ago.

A fjord carved through the ancient gneiss basement of western Norway.

Precambrian basement

The oldest rocks, Archean gneisses in Finnmark and Lofoten, approach 2.9 billion years in age. Proterozoic events including the Gothian and Sveconorwegian orogenies added further crust in the south and west. These basement rocks are exposed across much of the country where younger cover has been stripped away by erosion and glaciation.

The Caledonides

The Scandinavian Caledonides form a chain of stacked thrust sheets (nappes) transported eastward onto the Baltic Shield during the Silurian collision that closed the Iapetus Ocean. Rocks within the nappes include sediments and ophiolites from the ocean floor, and in places, such as Jotunheimen, slices of deep continental crust. Norway's highest mountains, including Galdhøpiggen, are built from these thrust sheets.

Glaciation and fjords

Repeated Pleistocene glaciations scoured the landscape, deepening river valleys into the famous fjords and depositing till across the lowlands. Since the last ice sheet melted, the land has been rebounding isostatically, rising by up to several hundred metres in the inner fjord districts. Offshore, the Norwegian continental shelf holds the sedimentary basins whose petroleum resources transformed Norway's economy from the 1970s.
