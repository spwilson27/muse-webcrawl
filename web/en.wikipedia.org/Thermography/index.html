Thermography

Infrared thermography (IRT), or thermal imaging, is a process in which a thermal camera detects radiation in the long-infrared range of the electromagnetic spectrum (roughly 9,000–14,000 nanometres) emitted by objects and produces images of that radiation, called thermograms.

Principle

All objects above absolute zero emit infrared radiation, and the amount increases with temperature (black-body radiation). A thermal camera detects this radiation similarly to how an ordinary camera detects visible light, but it works without any visible illumination, allowing it to "see" in total darkness and through smoke. Modern cameras usually use uncooled microbolometer focal plane arrays.

Applications

  • Building inspection: locating heat loss, insulation defects, moisture, and air leaks.
  • Electrical and mechanical maintenance: finding overheating components and bearing failures before breakdown.
  • Medical imaging: adjunctive assessment of circulation and inflammation; not a substitute for mammography.
  • Firefighting and law enforcement: seeing through smoke and darkness, search and rescue.
  • Night vision and surveillance in military and security contexts.
  • Process monitoring and non-destructive testing in industry.

Limitations

Accurate temperature measurement requires correcting for emissivity, reflected radiation, distance, and atmospheric conditions. Thermal images are two-dimensional maps of surface radiation, so interpretation demands training; shiny surfaces with low emissivity can give misleading readings.
