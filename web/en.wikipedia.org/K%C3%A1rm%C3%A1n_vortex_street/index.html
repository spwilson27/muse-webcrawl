Kármán vortex street

In fluid dynamics, a Kármán vortex street (or a von Kármán vortex street) is a repeating pattern of swirling vortices, caused by a process known as vortex shedding, which is responsible for the unsteady separation of flow of a fluid around blunt bodies. It is named after the engineer and fluid dynamicist Theodore von Kármán, who analysed the stability of such vortex arrangements in 1911–1912.

Formation

When fluid flows past a bluff body such as a circular cylinder at certain Reynolds numbers (roughly between about 47 and 105), the boundary layers on either side separate alternately, rolling up into vortices that are shed alternately from one side and then the other. The result downstream is two staggered rows of vortices of opposite rotation — the vortex street. The shedding frequency is characterised by the Strouhal number, approximately 0.2 for a wide range of Reynolds numbers.

Effects and examples

Alternating vortex shedding produces oscillating side forces on the body, which can cause vibration — the humming of overhead wires in wind ("aeolian tones"), the vibration of periscope masts, chimneys and bridge elements. Engineers use helical strakes on chimneys and cables to disrupt coherent shedding. In nature, striking examples appear in cloud patterns downwind of islands, visible from satellites, and in the flow behind obstacles in rivers.
