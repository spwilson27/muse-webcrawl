Anglo-Saxon runes

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Anglo-Saxon runes (Old English: rūna) are the characters of the runic alphabet used by the Anglo-Saxons to write Old English and Old Frisian from about the 5th to the 11th centuries. The Anglo-Saxon row, known as the futhorc (from its first six letters ᚠ ᚢ ᚦ ᚩ ᚱ ᚳ), expanded the older 24-character Elder Futhark to accommodate the sounds of Old English.

The futhorc

As Old English developed new vowels and consonants, scribes added runes: the row grew to 28 characters in England and up to 33 in Northumbria. New letters included ᚳ cen, ᚷ gyfu, ᛇ eoh, ᛈ peorð, ᛉ eolh, ᛝ ing and others for sounds such as /tʃ/ and /g/ that the older alphabet lacked. Each rune bore a name that was itself a meaningful word, recorded in the Old English Rune Poem, which gives a short verse for each letter.

Use and survival

Some 100 inscriptions survive from Anglo-Saxon England, carved on objects such as the Ruthwell Cross, the Franks Casket (in whalebone), coins, weapons and memorial stones; the corpus is far smaller than Scandinavia's thousands of runestones. Runes also had a literary life: the Exeter Book preserves a riddle-like rune poem and riddles signed in runes, and the poet Cynewulf embedded runic signatures in his verse. Although the Latin alphabet predominated after Christianisation, runes lingered in manuscripts and folk use well into the late medieval period, with inscriptions as late as the 16th century in some regions.
