Khalid ibn al-Walid

The Khalid ibn al-Walid Mosque in Homs, Syria, site of his tomb.

Khālid ibn al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīra al-Makhzūmī
Born: c. 585–592, Mecca, Arabia
Died: 642, Homs (or Medina), Rashidun Caliphate
Nickname: Sayf Allāh — "the Sword of God"
Known for: Unbeaten commander of the early Muslim conquests

Khalid ibn al-Walid (c. 585–592 – 642) was an Arab Muslim commander in the service of the Islamic prophet Muhammad and the caliphs Abu Bakr and Umar, who played a leading role in the Ridda wars against rebellious tribes in Arabia (632–633) and in the conquest of Sasanian Iraq (633–634) and Byzantine Syria (634–638). He is renowned in Islamic tradition as a master tactician who won over a hundred battles and is famously considered undefeated in battle.

Early life and conversion

Khalid belonged to the Banu Makhzum clan of the Quraysh tribe of Mecca, a clan responsible for the city's war affairs, and was a skilled horseman and wrestler. Initially an opponent of Islam, he commanded the Qurayshite cavalry whose manoeuvre decided the Battle of Uhud (625) against the Muslims. He converted to Islam in 629, after which Muhammad gave him the honorific Sayf Allāh, "the Sword of God", following his role in extricating the Muslim army at the Battle of Mu'tah.

Conquests

After Muhammad's death, Caliph Abu Bakr sent Khalid against the rebel tribes of Arabia, culminating in the hard-fought victory at Yamama. In 633 he invaded Iraq, winning a rapid sequence of battles against the Sasanians, including Ullais and the capture of al-Hira. Transferred to the Syrian front in 634 — a redeployment involving a celebrated desert crossing — he took Damascus and, at the decisive Battle of Yarmouk (636), destroyed the main Byzantine field army, opening Syria to Muslim rule. Caliph Umar dismissed him from supreme command in 638, for reasons debated by historians; Khalid continued to serve under Abu Ubayda ibn al-Jarrah and died in Homs in 642.

Legacy

Khalid's reputation rests on his mastery of the mobile double-envelopment, his use of reserves, and his personal bravery. His campaigns are praised in Muslim historiography and studied in modern military histories, though sources for his life derive from later Islamic tradition and are treated critically by modern scholars.
