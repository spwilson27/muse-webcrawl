Lyon (French: Lyon; historically spelled Lyons in English) is a city in east-central France, in the Auvergne-Rhône-Alpes region, situated at the confluence of the rivers Rhône and Saône, about 470 km (292 mi) south-east of Paris and 320 km (199 mi) north of Marseille. It is the second-largest metropolitan area in France after Paris and serves as the prefecture of the Metropolis of Lyon and the capital of its region.

Founded by the Romans in 43 BC as Lugdunum, Lyon became the capital of Roman Gaul and one of the most important cities of the western Roman Empire. In the Renaissance it grew rich on banking and the silk trade, later becoming synonymous with silk weaving, the cinema (invented there by the Lumière brothers in 1895), and French gastronomy: the city is widely regarded as the culinary capital of France.

History

Roman Lugdunum

Lucius Munatius Plancus founded the colony of Lugdunum in 43 BC on the hill of Fourvière. It became the administrative capital of the Three Gauls, the seat of an imperial mint, and the birthplace of the emperors Claudius and Caracalla. The emperor Augustus visited repeatedly, and the Sanctuary of the Three Gauls drew delegates from across Gaul each August. Christianity arrived early; the martyrs of Lyon, including Saint Blandina, were killed in 177 AD.

Middle Ages and Renaissance

After the fall of Rome, Lyon passed among Burgundian, Frankish and imperial rulers, eventually becoming part of the Kingdom of France in the early 14th century. Papal councils met there in 1245 and 1274, and Pope John XXII was elected in Lyon in 1316. From the late 15th century, Italian banking houses, four annual trade fairs and the introduction of silk weaving (1536) made Lyon one of Europe's great commercial cities.

Industrial era and Resistance

The silk industry, worked by the canuts of the Croix-Rousse district, drove the city's growth; the canut revolts of 1831 and 1834 were among the first industrial uprisings in Europe. During the Second World War, Lyon was a centre of the French Resistance — and of the Gestapo apparatus under Klaus Barbie, the "Butcher of Lyon" — and was liberated in September 1944.

Geography

Lyon occupies the peninsula (Presqu'île) between the Rhône and the Saône and the hills rising on either side: Fourvière to the west, topped by the Basilica of Notre-Dame de Fourvière, and Croix-Rousse to the north. The modern business district of Part-Dieu lies east of the Rhône, and the new Confluence district extends south of the Presqu'île to the rivers' meeting point.

Culture

The historic centre, including the medieval and Renaissance district of Vieux Lyon with its covered passageways (traboules), is a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The city is famous for the Fête des Lumières (Festival of Lights) held each December, originating in the tradition of placing candles in windows on 8 December in honour of the Virgin Mary. Lyon's traditional small restaurants, the bouchons, serve a cuisine built on offal, quenelles, and local wines; chefs such as Paul Bocuse made the city and its region central to modern French cooking. Interpol has its headquarters in Lyon, and the football club Olympique Lyonnais plays at the Parc Olympique Lyonnais, opened in 2016.

Economy and transport

Lyon is a major centre for banking, chemicals, pharmaceuticals (Sanofi Pasteur, bioMérieux) and biotechnology. Lyon–Saint-Exupéry Airport and the Saint-Exupéry TGV station link the city to Paris (about two hours by TGV) and the rest of Europe; the city operates a metro, tramway and the pioneering Vélo'v bike-sharing scheme.
