Brain death

DefinitionIrreversible cessation of all brain functions, including the brainstem
Key criteriaComa, absent brainstem reflexes, apnoea
First formal criteriaHarvard Committee, 1968

Brain death is the complete and irreversible loss of all brain function, including the brainstem. In most jurisdictions it is legally equivalent to death, even though ventilation and circulation may be maintained artificially for a time. It is distinct from coma and from a persistent vegetative state, in which brainstem functions persist.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis requires establishing a known, irreversible cause of catastrophic brain injury, excluding confounders such as hypothermia and sedative drugs, and demonstrating coma, the absence of brainstem reflexes (pupillary, corneal, gag, and others), and apnoea during a formal apnoea test. Ancillary tests such as EEG or cerebral blood-flow studies are used when the clinical examination cannot be completed.

History and significance

The modern concept emerged with intensive-care ventilation in the 1950s–60s; a Harvard Medical School committee published influential criteria in 1968. Brain-death determination also makes possible organ donation after neurological determination of death, since organs can remain perfused until retrieval. Legal and religious acceptance varies internationally, and a small number of high-profile cases have kept the concept publicly debated.
