A fish pond is a controlled pond, small artificial lake, or reservoir that is stocked with fish and is used in aquaculture for fish farming, for ornamental purposes, or is a part of a fish farm. Fish ponds were a common feature of estates, monasteries, and villages in medieval Europe and have been used in China for thousands of years as part of integrated farming systems.

History

Fish farming in ponds began in China over 4,000 years ago, famously recorded by Fan Li in the 5th century BCE, and spread throughout Asia with polyculture of several carp species occupying different feeding niches in the same pond. In medieval Europe, monasteries and manors maintained stew ponds for carp, pike, and bream, which provided fresh fish on the many fast days of the Catholic calendar. In Hawaii, ancient fishponds (loko iʻa) enclosed coastal shallows with lava-rock walls and were a sophisticated form of aquaculture reserved for chiefs.

Design and management

Productive ponds are usually earthen, sized from a few hundred square metres to several hectares, and about 1–2 metres deep, with separate inlets and outlets so they can be drained for harvesting. Management concerns water quality — dissolved oxygen (often maintained by aerators or by phytoplankton photosynthesis), pH, ammonia from fish waste and uneaten feed — fertilisation to encourage plankton growth, supplementary feeding (sometimes with fishmeal-based feeds), and control of predators and weeds. Polyculture and integrated systems, such as rice–fish farming or fertilising ponds with livestock manure, maximise production per unit area.

Ornamental ponds

Garden ponds stocked with koi and goldfish are maintained for decoration rather than food. They typically include filtration, pumps for circulation, and marginal and floating plants, and must be deep enough to protect fish from freezing and predators such as herons.
