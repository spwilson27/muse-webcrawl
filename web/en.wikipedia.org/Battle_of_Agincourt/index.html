The Battle of Agincourt (25 October 1415) was an English victory in the Hundred Years' War. The army of King Henry V — outnumbered, exhausted, and weakened by dysentery after the siege of Harfleur and a long march toward Calais — defeated a much larger French army near the village of Azincourt in northern France.

Battle

The English took up a defensive position on a narrow, freshly ploughed, muddy field between two woods, which funnelled the French advance. Henry's army consisted largely of longbowmen (about five of every six men), protected by sharpened wooden stakes planted in the ground. After an initial arrow storm, waves of French dismounted men-at-arms advanced through deep mud in heavy armour, becoming exhausted and crowded together; many were crushed or suffocated, and the English archers joined the melee with swords and axes. French losses included thousands of men-at-arms and much of the kingdom's military leadership; English losses were comparatively light, probably only a few hundred.

A controversial episode was Henry's order to kill many French prisoners when a renewed French attack (and a raid on the English baggage) threatened his position — an action judged harshly even by some contemporaries.

Numbers

Traditional accounts gave extreme odds (as much as 5:1 or 6:1). Modern historians debate the figures, with estimates ranging from about 6,000–9,000 English against 12,000–25,000 French, but agree the French significantly outnumbered the English.

Legacy

Agincourt was the high point of Henry V's career; within five years the Treaty of Troyes made him heir to the French throne. The battle is immortalized in Shakespeare's Henry V, including the St Crispin's Day speech ("We few, we happy few, we band of brothers"). It is often grouped with Crécy and Poitiers as the great longbow victories of the war.
