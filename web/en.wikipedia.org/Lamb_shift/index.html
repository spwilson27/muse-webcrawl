Lamb shift

The Lamb shift is an effect in quantum mechanics that was first described by Willis Lamb in 1947. It is a small difference in energy between two energy levels, 2S1/2 and 2P1/2, of the hydrogen atom which should otherwise be degenerate in Dirac's relativistic theory.

Lamb and Robert Retherford measured the splitting using microwave spectroscopy on a beam of hydrogen atoms, finding a separation of about 1,057 MHz. The result, presented at the Shelter Island Conference in 1947, spurred the development of modern quantum electrodynamics (QED).

Explanation

According to QED, the electron continually interacts with vacuum fluctuations of the electromagnetic field and with virtual electron–positron pairs. These effects — chiefly electron self-energy and vacuum polarisation — shift the S-state slightly upward relative to the P-state, because the S-state electron spends more time very close to the nucleus and samples the modified potential there. Hans Bethe produced the first successful non-relativistic calculation of the shift shortly after Lamb's announcement.

Significance

The Lamb shift remains one of the most precisely tested predictions of quantum electrodynamics and a classic example of how "empty" vacuum has measurable physical effects. Lamb received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1955 for the discovery.
