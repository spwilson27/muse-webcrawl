The Château d'Angers is a castle in the city of Angers in the Loire Valley, in the département of Maine-et-Loire in France. Founded in the 9th century by the Counts of Anjou, it was expanded to its current size in the 13th century. The castle occupies a rocky promontory overlooking the River Maine and is famous for its massive black-and-white banded curtain walls and seventeen round towers, and for housing the Apocalypse Tapestry.

History

The site was used by the counts of Anjou from the 9th century. Around 1230, during the minority of Louis IX, the regent Blanche of Castile rebuilt the castle as a fortress against Brittany, adding the great wall circuit with its towers built of dark schist and pale tuffeau stone. In the 14th and 15th centuries the counts of Anjou, including Louis I, Duke of Anjou, maintained a glittering court here. In the 16th century the fortress was adapted to artillery: under orders of Henri III during the Wars of Religion, the towers were truncated to the level of the walls so that cannon could fire from the ramparts, giving them their present squat profile. Later used as a prison and military barracks, the castle held prisoners of the Revolutionary government, and during the Second World War was occupied by German forces and damaged by bombing.

Apocalypse Tapestry

Since 1954 the castle has displayed the Apocalypse Tapestry (Tenture de l'Apocalypse), a vast cycle of tapestries commissioned by Louis I of Anjou around 1375 and woven in Paris, probably by Nicolas Bataille's workshop from cartoons by Jean Bondol. Depicting scenes from the Book of Revelation, it originally measured about 140 metres in length; roughly 100 metres survive and are shown in a purpose-built gallery, making it one of the largest and oldest surviving tapestry cycles in the world.

The castle today

The interior includes the great hall, the chapel of Sainte-Geneviève, terraced gardens within the walls, and the royal apartments. The château is administered by the Centre des monuments nationaux and is a listed historic monument, open to the public as a museum.
