Economic liberalisation in India

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The economic liberalisation in India refers to the series of policy changes aimed at opening up the country's economy to greater participation by private and foreign investment. Initiated in 1991 under the government of Prime Minister P. V. Narasimha Rao and Finance Minister Manmohan Singh, the reforms dismantled much of the "Licence Raj" system of industrial licensing and permits that had governed the Indian economy since independence, and shifted India from a largely state-led model toward a market-oriented economy.

Background

By 1991 India faced a severe balance of payments crisis: foreign exchange reserves had fallen to barely enough to cover a few weeks of imports, inflation was high, and the fiscal deficit had grown sharply. The collapse of the Soviet Union, a major trading partner, and the spike in oil prices following the Gulf War worsened the situation. India sought an emergency loan from the International Monetary Fund, which was conditioned on structural reforms, and pledged gold reserves as collateral.

Reforms

The New Economic Policy announced in July 1991 included:

  • Abolition of industrial licensing for all but a handful of strategic industries.
  • Opening of many sectors previously reserved for the public sector to private investment.
  • Automatic approval of foreign direct investment up to 51% in many industries.
  • Reduction of import tariffs and removal of most quantitative restrictions on trade.
  • Devaluation of the rupee and movement toward current-account convertibility.
  • Financial sector reforms, deregulation of interest rates, and greater autonomy for the Reserve Bank of India.
  • Disinvestment of government stakes in public sector undertakings.

Impact

The reforms were followed by a marked acceleration in growth: India's GDP growth rose from an average of around 3.5% per year in the decades before 1991 (sometimes called the "Hindu rate of growth") to 6–8% in many years after the reforms. Foreign investment and trade expanded, the information technology and services sectors grew rapidly, and poverty rates declined, though economists continue to debate how much of the improvement is attributable to the reforms and how evenly the gains have been shared.
