Comics Code Authority

The Comics Code Authority (CCA) was formed in 1954 by the Comics Magazine Association of America as an alternative to government regulation of the American comic book industry. Comics approved by the authority bore its seal on their covers. The Code imposed strict content standards that profoundly shaped mainstream comics for decades.

Background

Public concern about crime and horror comics grew in the late 1940s and early 1950s, fed by psychiatrist Fredric Wertham's book Seduction of the Innocent (1954), which argued that comics contributed to juvenile delinquency. United States Senate subcommittee hearings on juvenile delinquency in 1954 put publishers under intense pressure, and the industry created the CCA to censor itself pre-emptively.

The Code

The original code prohibited, among many other things, graphic depictions of crime and violence, horror themes including the words "horror" and "terror" in titles, vampires, werewolves, ghouls, and the walking dead; it required that good always triumph over evil, that authority figures never be shown disrespectfully, and that romance avoid any suggestion of sexual content. EC Comics, publisher of acclaimed crime and horror titles, was effectively driven from the field, while superhero comics adapted to the sanitised regime.

Decline

The Code was revised in 1971, allowing some horror elements, and again in 1989. From the 1970s onward, publishers increasingly issued comics without the seal, distributing them through direct-market comic shops rather than newsstands. Marvel Comics abandoned the Code in 2001; DC Comics and Archie Comics followed in 2011, ending the CCA's role in the industry.
