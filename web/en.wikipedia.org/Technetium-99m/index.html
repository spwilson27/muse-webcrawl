Technetium-99m (⁹⁹ᵐTc) is a metastable nuclear isomer of technetium-99, symbolized as 99mTc. It is the most commonly used radioisotope in medical diagnostic imaging, used in tens of millions of nuclear medicine procedures every year — roughly four-fifths of all diagnostic nuclear medicine procedures worldwide.

Properties

Technetium-99m decays by isomeric transition with a half-life of about 6 hours, emitting a gamma ray of 140.5 keV. This energy is well suited to detection by gamma cameras, while the short half-life and the absence of primary particle emissions keep the radiation dose to the patient low. The decay product, technetium-99, is a very weak beta emitter with a half-life of about 211,000 years.

Production and use

Hospitals obtain 99mTc from a "generator" containing the parent isotope molybdenum-99 (half-life 66 hours), produced mainly in a small number of nuclear research reactors by fission of uranium-235. Eluting the generator with saline yields sodium pertechnetate, which can be used directly or combined with kits of ligands to make radiopharmaceuticals targeted at the thyroid, brain, myocardium, skeleton, liver, kidneys, and other organs. Imaging is typically performed with single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT).

Supply concerns

Dependence on a handful of ageing reactors has caused repeated global shortages, notably in 2009–2010, prompting work on alternative production routes using cyclotrons and accelerators.
