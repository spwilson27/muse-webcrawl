Levodopa

Levodopa, also known as L-DOPA and sold under many brand names, is an amino acid that is made and used as part of the normal biology of some plants and animals, including humans. Humans, as well as a portion of the other animals that utilise L-DOPA in their biology, make it via biosynthesis from the amino acid L-tyrosine. L-DOPA is the precursor to the neurotransmitters dopamine, norepinephrine (noradrenaline), and epinephrine (adrenaline), which are collectively known as catecholamines. As a drug, it is used in the clinical treatment of Parkinson's disease and dopamine-responsive dystonia, most commonly in combination with carbidopa (as levodopa/carbidopa, e.g. Sinemet) or benserazide, which block its breakdown outside the brain.

Medical use

Levodopa remains the most effective symptomatic treatment for Parkinson's disease. Unlike dopamine itself, it crosses the blood–brain barrier, and is converted to dopamine by aromatic L-amino acid decarboxylase in the brain, replenishing the neurotransmitter lost as dopaminergic neurons of the substantia nigra degenerate. Co-formulated peripheral decarboxylase inhibitors (carbidopa or benserazide) reduce nausea and cardiovascular side effects and increase the fraction reaching the brain. Its dramatic effects were popularised by Oliver Sacks' Awakenings, describing patients with encephalitis lethargica treated in 1969.

Side effects and long-term complications

Common side effects include nausea, orthostatic hypotension, somnolence and hallucinations. With years of treatment, many patients develop motor complications — fluctuations between "on" and "off" states and involuntary movements called levodopa-induced dyskinesia — managed by adjusting dosing, adding COMT or MAO-B inhibitors, amantadine, deep brain stimulation, or continuous infusion therapies.

History

L-DOPA was first isolated from the seeds of the broad bean, Vicia faba, in 1910–13 by Markus Guggenheim. Its biological significance emerged in the 1950s when Arvid Carlsson showed it reversed reserpine-induced immobility in animals, work contributing to his Nobel Prize. Clinical use in Parkinson's disease was established by George Cotzias in 1967, who demonstrated that large oral doses produced sustained benefit.
