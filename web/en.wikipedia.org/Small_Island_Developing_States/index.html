Small Island Developing States

Small Island Developing States (SIDS) are a distinct group of developing countries that face specific social, economic and environmental vulnerabilities. The United Nations recognizes SIDS in three geographical regions: the Caribbean, the Pacific, and the Atlantic, Indian Ocean and South China Sea (AIS). The group comprises some 39 UN member states and 18 associate members of UN regional commissions, including countries such as Barbados, Fiji, Jamaica, the Maldives, Mauritius, Samoa, Singapore and Trinidad and Tobago.

Shared vulnerabilities

SIDS share small land areas and populations, remoteness from markets, narrow resource bases and heavy dependence on a few sectors such as tourism and fisheries. They are exceptionally exposed to natural hazards — tropical cyclones, floods and droughts — and to sea-level rise driven by climate change, which threatens freshwater supplies, coastal infrastructure and, for low-lying atoll nations such as Tuvalu, Kiribati and the Marshall Islands, habitability itself. Their small economies face high transport and energy costs and limited capacity to diversify, and many carry heavy public-debt burdens aggravated by disaster recovery.

International recognition

SIDS were first formally recognized at the 1992 UN Conference on Environment and Development in Rio de Janeiro. Subsequent global conferences — Barbados (1994), Mauritius (2005), Samoa (2014, which produced the SAMOA Pathway) and Antigua and Barbuda (2024) — have adopted programmes of action addressing sustainable development, climate adaptation, oceans and financing. Through the Alliance of Small Island States (AOSIS), SIDS negotiate largely as a bloc in climate diplomacy and have been prominent advocates of limiting warming to 1.5 °C and of the Loss and Damage fund agreed at recent climate summits.

Development priorities

Agendas for SIDS emphasize resilient infrastructure, renewable energy to replace costly imported diesel, sustainable tourism and fisheries, disaster-risk reduction, access to concessional finance not strictly tied to income per capita, and protection of ocean resources within their large exclusive economic zones — leading some to describe themselves as "large ocean states."
