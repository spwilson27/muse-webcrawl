Spacecraft attitude control


A reaction wheel assembly

Spacecraft attitude control is the process of controlling the orientation of a spacecraft (vehicle or satellite) with respect to an inertial frame of reference or another entity such as the celestial sphere, certain fields, and nearby objects. The attitude of a spacecraft is its rotational orientation in space, and the systems that sense and control it are often called the attitude determination and control system (ADCS).

Purposes

Attitude control is required so that antennas can be pointed at ground stations, solar arrays at the Sun, scientific instruments at their targets, and engines in the correct direction for manoeuvres. A spacecraft must also manage disturbance torques from gravity gradients, solar radiation pressure, residual magnetism, and atmospheric drag in low orbits.

Sensors

Attitude is determined using sensors such as sun sensors, Earth (horizon) sensors, star trackers, magnetometers, and gyroscopes. Star trackers provide the most accurate absolute attitude by matching observed star fields against a catalogue, while gyroscopes measure rotation rates and are integrated over time.

Actuators

Torque is applied by several devices. Reaction wheels and momentum wheels exchange angular momentum with the spacecraft by spinning flywheels; control moment gyroscopes tilt spinning rotors for larger torques, as used on the International Space Station; magnetorquers push against the Earth's magnetic field, mainly to desaturate the wheels; and small thrusters provide impulsive control, especially during detumbling and station-keeping. Passive methods include spin stabilization and gravity-gradient booms.
