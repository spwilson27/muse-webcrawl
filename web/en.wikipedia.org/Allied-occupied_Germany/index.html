Allied-occupied Germany refers to the period from the defeat of Nazi Germany in the Second World War in 1945 until 1949, when Germany was divided into four occupation zones administered by the United States, the United Kingdom, the Soviet Union and France, and governed jointly through the Allied Control Council in Berlin.

Division into zones

The division of Germany was agreed in principle at the Yalta Conference of February 1945 and elaborated at the Potsdam Conference of July–August 1945. Germany east of the Oder–Neisse line was placed under Polish and Soviet administration, and its German population was largely expelled westward. Berlin, though deep inside the Soviet zone, was itself split into four sectors. Austria was similarly divided into four zones until 1955.

Occupation policy

The Allies pursued the "four Ds": denazification, demilitarisation, democratisation and decentralisation. The Nuremberg trials prosecuted major war criminals in the American zone. Early plans for pastoralisation, such as the Morgenthau Plan, were abandoned as the Western powers shifted toward rebuilding the German economy. Denazification proceeded most rigorously, though unevenly, and millions of Germans passed through screening procedures.

From zones to two states

As Cold War tensions grew, the American and British zones merged economically into the Bizone in 1947, joined by the French zone in 1948 (the Trizone). The currency reform of June 1948 and the Soviet blockade of West Berlin (1948–49), answered by the Berlin Airlift, sealed the division. In May 1949 the Federal Republic of Germany (West Germany) was founded in the Western zones; in October 1949 the German Democratic Republic (East Germany) followed in the Soviet zone. The two German states remained divided until reunification in 1990.
