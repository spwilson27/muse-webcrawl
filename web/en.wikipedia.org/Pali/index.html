Pali is a Middle Indo-Aryan liturgical language of India. It is the language of the Pāli Canon (Tipiṭaka), the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism, and remains the sacred and scholarly language of Theravāda communities in Sri Lanka, Myanmar, Thailand, Laos, and Cambodia. Although no longer a vernacular, Pali continues to be studied and chanted daily by Buddhist monastics and laypeople.

Origins

The word pāli originally meant "text" or "line" (of scripture), as opposed to the commentary (aṭṭhakathā), and only later came to name the language itself. Pali is a composite literary language based on western Indian dialects of the Buddha's era, related to the Prakrits and simpler than Sanskrit; it is probably not the exact dialect the Buddha spoke, but a lingua franca through which his teachings were transmitted. The canon was committed to writing in Sri Lanka in the 1st century BCE.

Features

Pali has ten vowels and a consonant system with retroflex sounds, like other Indo-Aryan languages. It simplified many Sanskrit clusters and merged its sibilants. Its grammar retains eight cases, three genders, and a rich system of compounds, making it close in structure to Sanskrit but somewhat more regularised.

Literature

Beyond the canon itself — the Vinaya (monastic discipline), Sutta (discourses), and Abhidhamma (systematic philosophy) — Pali literature includes the commentaries of Buddhaghosa (5th century CE), chronicles such as the Sri Lankan Mahāvaṃsa, and poetry and grammar composed across the Theravāda world over two millennia. The Pali Text Society, founded in 1881, made the texts widely available in Roman script and English translation.
