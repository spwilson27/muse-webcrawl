Derry

Derry
Irish: Doire
Country: Northern Ireland, United Kingdom
County: County Londonderry
Population: approx. 85,000 (city proper)

Derry, officially Londonderry, is the second-largest city in Northern Ireland and the fifth-largest city on the island of Ireland. It lies in the north-west of Northern Ireland, close to the border with County Donegal in the Republic of Ireland, on the River Foyle, which flows into the sea lough of the same name a few miles to the north.

The city is famous for its intact 17th-century defensive walls, among the finest examples of walled cities in Europe, and for its central role in the history of Northern Ireland, including the Siege of Derry of 1689 and the events of the Troubles in the late 20th century.

Names

The name Derry derives from the Irish Doire, meaning "oak grove". The older English name Londonderry arose after the city was granted to the livery companies of London in the early 17th century. The choice of name remains a matter of political sensitivity: unionists generally favour "Londonderry" while nationalists generally favour "Derry". The city council was renamed Derry City Council in 1984, while the legal name of the city remains Londonderry.

History

Derry is one of the oldest continuously inhabited places in Ireland. A monastery was founded on the site in the 6th century, traditionally by Saint Columba. The settlement was attacked repeatedly by Viking raiders in the early medieval period.

During the Plantation of Ulster, the city was rebuilt and fortified between 1613 and 1619 by the Honourable The Irish Society on behalf of London companies. The resulting walls, with their gates and bastions, have survived intact. In 1689, during the Williamite War, the city endured a 105-day siege by Jacobite forces; the shutting of the gates by thirteen apprentice boys became a celebrated episode in Protestant tradition.

The Troubles

Derry was a major flashpoint at the start of the Troubles. The Battle of the Bogside in August 1969 saw three days of rioting in the city's nationalist Bogside district. On 30 January 1972, British paratroopers shot 26 unarmed civil rights protesters in the Bogside, killing 14 — an event known as Bloody Sunday. A second public inquiry, the Saville Inquiry, reported in 2010 and concluded the killings were unjustified.

Geography and culture

The historic city stands on the west bank of the Foyle, with the modern suburb of Waterside on the east bank, linked by bridges including the Peace Bridge, opened in 2011. Derry was the inaugural UK City of Culture in 2013. Landmarks include St Columb's Cathedral, the Guildhall, and the murals of the Bogside.
