A fish is an aquatic, anamniotic, gill-bearing vertebrate animal with swimming fins and a hard skull, but lacking limbs with digits. Fish can be grouped into the more basal jawless fish and the more common jawed fish, the latter including all living cartilaginous and bony fish, as well as the extinct placoderms and acanthodians.

Most fish are cold-blooded, their body temperature varying with the surrounding water, though some large active swimmers like white shark and tuna can hold a higher core temperature. Fish can communicate acoustically with each other. Most fish species have paired fins and are covered in scales. Fish are abundant in most bodies of water and are an important resource for humans around the world, especially as food.

Evolution

The earliest organisms that can be classified as fish were soft-bodied chordates that appeared during the Cambrian Period. Although they lacked a true spine, they possessed notochords which allowed them to be more agile than their invertebrate counterparts. Fish would continue to evolve through the Paleozoic era, diversifying into a wide variety of forms. The first fish with jaws, the placoderms, appeared in the Silurian and greatly diversified in the Devonian, often called the "Age of Fishes". Bony fish, distinguished by the presence of swim bladders, eventually became the dominant group; lobe-finned fish gave rise to tetrapods, and hence to all land vertebrates including humans.

Diversity

The term "fish" most precisely describes any non-tetrapod craniate with gills throughout life and, if present, limbs in the shape of fins. There are over 33,000 described species, more than all other vertebrate groups combined, ranging from the tiny Paedocypris, under 8 mm long, to the whale shark, which can exceed 12 metres. Most species are ray-finned fish (Actinopterygii); sharks, rays and skates form the cartilaginous fish (Chondrichthyes); hagfish and lampreys are jawless fish (Agnatha).

Anatomy and physiology

Fish typically breathe by extracting oxygen from water through gills — feathery organs rich in capillaries — although lungfish and some others can also breathe air. A two-chambered heart pumps blood through a single-loop circulation. Many fish propel themselves using lateral undulation of the body and tail fin, steering and stabilising with the paired pectoral and pelvic fins. The lateral line system detects water movements and vibrations and is used to sense prey, predators and obstacles.

Ecology

Fish occupy nearly every aquatic environment, from high mountain streams to the abyssal and hadal zones of the deepest oceans, and from polar seas to thermal springs exceeding 40 °C. They perform essential roles as predators, prey, grazers and nutrient recyclers in aquatic food webs.

Importance to humans

Fishing and fish farming supply a major share of the world's animal protein. Fish are also kept as pets, displayed in public aquariums, and hold deep cultural and religious significance in many societies. Overfishing, habitat destruction, pollution and climate change have driven declines in many wild stocks.
