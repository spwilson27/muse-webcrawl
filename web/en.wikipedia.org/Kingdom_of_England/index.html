Kingdom of England

Existed: c. 927 – 1707
Capital: Winchester, later Westminster/London
First king: Æthelstan
Succeeded by: Kingdom of Great Britain (Acts of Union 1707)

The Kingdom of England was a sovereign state on the island of Great Britain from the early 10th century — when it was established from various Anglo-Saxon kingdoms — until 1 May 1707, when it united with the Kingdom of Scotland to form the Kingdom of Great Britain under the Acts of Union.

History

By about 927 Æthelstan, king of the Anglo-Saxons, had conquered the last remaining Viking kingdom of York and become the first ruler of a unified England. The kingdom survived the Danish conquest under Cnut and was transformed by the Norman Conquest of 1066, which replaced the English aristocracy and reshaped its language, law, and feudal order. Under the Plantagenets, England developed Parliament, the common law, and fought the Hundred Years' War against France.

The Tudor period brought the English Reformation, overseas expansion, and the personal union with Scotland after 1603 under James I. The 17th century saw civil war, the execution of Charles I, the republican Commonwealth under Oliver Cromwell, the Restoration, and the Glorious Revolution of 1688, which established the supremacy of Parliament. In 1707 the parliaments of England and Scotland ratified the Treaty of Union, dissolving the English state into the new Kingdom of Great Britain.

Government

England was a monarchy whose institutions — shire courts, the royal council (witan), later Parliament, and the exchequer — grew steadily more centralised. From the 13th century the king increasingly governed in cooperation with Parliament, a balance decisively shifted by the constitutional settlements of the 17th century.
