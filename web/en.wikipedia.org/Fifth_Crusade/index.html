The Fifth Crusade (1217–1221) was a campaign by Western European Christians to recover Jerusalem from the Ayyubid dynasty by first conquering Egypt, the centre of Muslim power in the region. Proclaimed by Pope Innocent III and organised under his successor Honorius III, the crusade succeeded in capturing the Nile Delta port of Damietta in 1219, but ended in disaster when its army was trapped by the flooding Nile near Mansurah in 1221 and forced to surrender both the city and its ambitions.

Background and early campaign

The crusade was launched at the Fourth Lateran Council in 1215. Contingents from Hungary, Austria and the Kingdom of Jerusalem campaigned inconclusively in the Levant in 1217–18, including a failed attack on Mount Tabor. In 1218 the crusaders adopted the strategy of striking Egypt, landing near Damietta; after a hard siege in which the Frisians' floating siege engine broke the harbour chain, the starving city fell in November 1219.

Damietta and defeat

Sultan al-Kamil repeatedly offered to exchange Damietta for Jerusalem and most of the former Kingdom of Jerusalem, but the papal legate Cardinal Pelagius, who had assumed effective leadership, refused, expecting reinforcements from Emperor Frederick II that never came. During the crusade, Francis of Assisi famously crossed the lines in 1219 to preach to al-Kamil, who received him courteously. In July 1221 Pelagius advanced on Cairo; al-Kamil opened the Nile sluices, flooding the plain and cutting off the crusader army, which surrendered in August. Under the peace, the crusaders evacuated Damietta and left Egypt in return for their freedom, with an eight-year truce.

Legacy

The failure humiliated the papacy and discredited Pelagius; the strategy of attacking Jerusalem through Egypt would be tried again, with similar results, by Louis IX in the Seventh Crusade. Frederick II's diplomacy in the Sixth Crusade would recover Jerusalem without battle a decade later.
