The Himyarite Kingdom (Arabic: مملكة حِمْيَر, Mamlakat Ḥimyar) was a polity in the southern highlands of Yemen, in the southwestern corner of the Arabian Peninsula, which existed from about 110 BC until the 520s AD. The Himyarites, first mentioned in Roman sources such as Pliny the Elder's Natural History, rose from their capital at Zafar (near modern Yarim) to conquer the neighbouring kingdoms of Saba (Sheba), Qataban, and Hadhramaut, eventually uniting all of South Arabia. Around AD 400 they also conquered the central Arabian tribal kingdom of Kinda and briefly exercised influence across much of the peninsula.

History

Rise and unification

The Himyarites emerged as a tribe of the southern highlands in the late 2nd century BC. Under kings such as Shammar Yuhar'ish (c. 275–300 AD), titled "King of Saba and Dhu Raydan and Hadhramaut and Yamnat", Himyar absorbed the ancient Sabaean kingdom and completed the unification of Yemen. The kings of the united kingdom adopted grand titles extending their claims to the tribes of the desert and the highlands.

Monotheistic turn

In the late 4th century, the Himyarite court abandoned the old South Arabian polytheism in favour of a monotheistic cult, invoking Rahmanan, "the Merciful", Lord of heaven and earth. By the early 5th century the royal inscriptions show clear Jewish influence, and several Himyarite kings appear to have adopted Judaism; the conversion is also described in later Christian and Islamic traditions, including the story of the king (often identified with Abu Karib As'ad) converted by rabbis from Yathrib.

Dhu Nuwas and the Aksumite conquest

The last Himyarite king, Yusuf As'ar Yath'ar—known in Arabic tradition as Dhu Nuwas—took power around 517–522 AD. A fervent convert to Judaism (or defender of it), he massacred the Christian population of Najran, an event remembered in Christian martyrologies as the martyrdom of Arethas and his companions and alluded to in the Qur'an's story of the "People of the Ditch" (Ashab al-Ukhdud). The massacre provoked intervention by the Christian kingdom of Aksum in Ethiopia, encouraged by Byzantium: the Aksumite king Kaleb invaded in 525, defeated and killed Dhu Nuwas, and installed a client king, ending independent Himyarite rule. A later Aksumite viceroy, Abraha, made himself effectively independent and ruled Yemen until about 570, when the Persians of the Sasanian Empire intervened, after which Yemen became a Persian satrapy until the coming of Islam.

Economy and culture

Himyarite Yemen stood at the hinge of the incense trade: frankincense and myrrh from Dhofar and the Hadhramaut moved north through its ports and caravan cities, and its harbours, such as Qana and Aden, linked the Mediterranean world with India and East Africa. The kingdom inherited the monumental South Arabian tradition of temple building, irrigation works (the famous Marib dam was maintained under Himyarite rule), and inscriptions in the Musnad script. Its language, Himyaritic, is attested in inscriptions and in medieval Arabic sources, though the bulk of the epigraphic record is in Sabaic. Himyarite coinage, initially imitating Athenian and later Roman types, was minted for centuries.

Legacy

"Himyar" remained a byword for ancient Yemeni civilisation in Arabic literature, and genealogical traditions traced many Yemeni tribes to Himyar ibn Saba. The kingdom's dramatic end—Dhu Nuwas, the Najran martyrs, Abraha's expedition against Mecca in the "Year of the Elephant"—looms large in Ethiopian, Syriac, and Islamic historiography.
