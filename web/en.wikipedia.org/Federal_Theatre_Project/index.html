Federal Theatre Project

The Federal Theatre Project (FTP) was a New Deal program created in 1935 under the Works Progress Administration (WPA) to fund theatre and other live artistic performances during the Great Depression. Directed by playwright and educator Hallie Flanagan, it employed thousands of out-of-work theatre artists and presented productions to millions of Americans, many of them first-time theatregoers, often free of charge.

Scope and productions

At its peak the project employed some 12,700 people and operated units in 31 states. It produced more than a thousand productions, including classical plays, children's theatre, marionette theatre, dance, and touring companies. Its units included the famed Negro Theatre Unit — whose Harlem production of Macbeth set in Haiti, directed by the young Orson Welles, became a sensation — as well as Yiddish, German, Spanish, and Italian-language companies.

The Living Newspaper

The project's most innovative form was the Living Newspaper, dramatizations of current events and social issues researched by teams of writers and researchers. Productions addressed farm poverty (Triple-A Plowed Under), the housing crisis (One-Third of a Nation), public power (Power), and venereal disease (Spirochete). The first planned Living Newspaper, Ethiopia, about the Italian invasion of Ethiopia, was canceled under government pressure — an early sign of the political controversy that would engulf the project.

Censorship and demise

Conservatives in Congress attacked the project for productions they considered leftist or sympathetic to communism. Elmer Rice resigned as New York director in protest over the Ethiopia cancellation, and the Federal Theatre's staging of Marc Blitzstein's pro-union musical The Cradle Will Rock was shut down in 1937, prompting Welles and John Houseman to stage it in defiance with the cast performing from the audience. In June 1939 Congress eliminated the project's funding, making it the only Federal One arts project to be terminated.

Legacy

The Federal Theatre Project launched or sustained the careers of figures such as Orson Welles, John Houseman, Arthur Miller, and Canada Lee, and demonstrated that large-scale public funding of the arts could reach mass audiences. Its records, held by the Library of Congress, remain a major resource for theatre history.
