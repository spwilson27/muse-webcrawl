Auditing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


The audit risk model

Auditing is the systematic process of obtaining and evaluating evidence regarding assertions about economic actions and events to ascertain the degree of correspondence between those assertions and established criteria, and communicating the results to interested users. It is the discipline underlying the conduct of an audit.

Audit process

A typical external audit proceeds through client acceptance, planning, and risk assessment; evaluation of internal controls; substantive procedures, including tests of details and analytical procedures; and completion, culminating in the auditor's report. Key concepts include audit risk (the product of inherent risk, control risk, and detection risk), materiality, and professional scepticism.

Types of auditors

External auditors are independent firms engaged to opine on financial statements. Internal auditors are employees who provide assurance on internal processes. Government auditors, such as those of the US Government Accountability Office, audit public funds. Forensic auditors investigate suspected fraud.

Regulation

In the United States, audits of public companies are overseen by the Public Company Accounting Oversight Board (PCAOB), created by the Sarbanes–Oxley Act. Internationally, the IAASB issues the International Standards on Auditing. The "expectation gap" — the difference between what the public expects an audit to achieve and what it actually does — remains a persistent theme in the literature.
