New York v. United States

New York v. United States, 505 U.S. 144 (1992), is a United States Supreme Court case that established the modern "anti-commandeering doctrine": the federal government may not compel state legislatures to enact federal regulatory programs or force state officials to administer them.

Background

The Low-Level Radioactive Waste Policy Amendments Act of 1985 gave states incentives to provide for disposal of low-level radioactive waste generated within their borders. One provision, the "take-title" clause, required states that failed to arrange disposal by a deadline to take ownership of the waste and bear liability for it.

Decision

Writing for a 6-3 majority, Justice O'Connor held that the take-title provision crossed the line between encouragement and coercion: Congress cannot "commandeer" state legislative processes by directly compelling them to enact and enforce a federal scheme. The other incentives, monetary grants and access restrictions, were upheld as permissible uses of the spending and commerce powers. The doctrine was extended to state executive officials in Printz v. United States (1997) and reaffirmed in Murphy v. NCAA (2018).
