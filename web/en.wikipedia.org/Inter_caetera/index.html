Inter caetera

Inter caetera ("Among other [works]") is a papal bull issued by Pope Alexander VI on 4 May 1493. Together with the related bulls of 1493, it granted to the Catholic Monarchs of Spain — Ferdinand II of Aragon and Isabella I of Castile — all lands "discovered and to be discovered" west and south of a meridian drawn 100 leagues west of the Azores and Cape Verde islands, provided they were not already in the possession of another Christian prince.

Background

Following Christopher Columbus's return from his first voyage in March 1493, Spain sought papal confirmation of its claims to the newly encountered lands to forestall Portuguese rivalry. Alexander VI, a Spaniard by birth (Rodrigo Borgia), issued a series of bulls in spring 1493: Inter caetera of 3 May, Eximiae devotionis, and the better-known second Inter caetera of 4 May, which fixed the dividing line in the Atlantic. A further bull, Dudum siquidem (September 1493), extended Spanish claims toward the Indies by any route.

Terms and aftermath

The bulls exhorted the Spanish monarchs to evangelize the inhabitants of the new lands and purported to donate sovereignty over them. Portugal, dissatisfied with the line, negotiated directly with Spain, and the 1494 Treaty of Tordesillas moved the meridian to 370 leagues west of Cape Verde — the line by which Brazil later fell to Portugal. Protestant and rival Catholic powers such as France and England never accepted the papal donation, and the bulls are frequently discussed as early documents of the so-called "Doctrine of Discovery" in international and indigenous law.

Legacy

Inter caetera remained a reference point in Spanish colonial law and in later legal disputes. In March 2023 the Vatican formally repudiated the Doctrine of Discovery, stating that the papal bulls of the era "did not adequately reflect the equal dignity and rights of indigenous peoples" and had been manipulated by colonial powers.
