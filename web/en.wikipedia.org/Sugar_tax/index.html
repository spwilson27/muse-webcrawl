Sugar tax

A sugar tax, also known as a soda tax or sweetened beverage tax (SBT), is a tax or surcharge (food-related fiscal policy) designed to reduce the consumption of sweetened beverages by increasing the price of drinks containing added sugar or sweeteners. Drinks affected include carbonated soft drinks, sports drinks, energy drinks, and sweetened fruit juices. The tax often applies only to drinks with a sugar content above a certain threshold, encouraging manufacturers to reformulate products.

Adoption

Mexico introduced a tax of one peso per litre in 2014, followed by studies showing reduced purchases of taxed drinks. The United Kingdom's Soft Drinks Industry Levy, announced in 2016 and effective from 2018, is tiered by sugar content and drove widespread reformulation. In the United States, cities including Berkeley, Philadelphia, San Francisco, Oakland, Albany, Boulder, and Seattle have enacted beverage taxes, beginning with Berkeley in 2015. South Africa, Portugal, France, and several other countries have national measures.

Debate

Supporters argue the taxes reduce obesity and diabetes and correct health externalities; critics argue they are regressive, harm small businesses, and shift purchases to untaxed substitutes or neighbouring jurisdictions. Health economists generally find measurable reductions in purchases of taxed beverages, with ongoing study of longer-term health effects.
