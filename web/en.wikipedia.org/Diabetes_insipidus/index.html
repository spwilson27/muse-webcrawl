Diabetes insipidus

Water balance in diabetes insipidus is disrupted by vasopressin deficiency or resistance.

Diabetes insipidus (DI) is a condition characterised by the excretion of large volumes of dilute urine and intense thirst. It results from a deficiency of the hormone vasopressin (antidiuretic hormone, ADH) or from the kidneys' failure to respond to it. Despite the shared name, DI is unrelated to diabetes mellitus; the word "diabetes" refers to the copious urine flow in both conditions, while "insipidus" reflects that the urine, unlike in diabetes mellitus, is not sweet.

Types

Central diabetes insipidus arises from damage to the hypothalamus or posterior pituitary, reducing vasopressin production or release. Causes include head injury, tumours, surgery, and genetic mutations in the vasopressin gene. Nephrogenic diabetes insipidus occurs when the kidneys fail to respond to vasopressin, commonly due to mutations in the vasopressin V2 receptor or aquaporin-2 genes, or to drugs such as lithium. Gestational DI occurs in pregnancy when placental enzymes break down vasopressin, and dipsogenic DI results from excessive fluid intake driven by a defect in the thirst mechanism.

Signs and symptoms

Patients may produce 3 to 20 litres of urine per day, with consequent dehydration, hypernatraemia, nocturia, and an overwhelming craving for cold water. In infants, symptoms include irritability, poor feeding, and failure to thrive.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis typically uses the water deprivation test, in which urine concentration is measured after a period without fluids, followed by administration of desmopressin: central DI responds with concentrated urine, whereas nephrogenic DI does not. Measurement of copeptin, a stable surrogate for vasopressin, is increasingly used.

Treatment

Central DI is treated with desmopressin, a synthetic vasopressin analogue. Nephrogenic DI is managed with a low-salt diet, adequate hydration, and thiazide diuretics, which paradoxically reduce urine volume. Treating any underlying cause is essential.
