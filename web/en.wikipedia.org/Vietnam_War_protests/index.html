Vietnam War protests

Protests against the Vietnam War took place in the 1960s and 1970s, mainly in the United States, but also in other countries. The movement against U.S. involvement in the Vietnam War began with demonstrations in 1964 and grew into a broad social movement that influenced public opinion and, ultimately, U.S. policy toward the war.

Beginnings

Early protests were small, organized by pacifist and left-wing groups. As American troop levels escalated after 1965, the movement broadened to include students (notably Students for a Democratic Society), civil rights activists, clergy, veterans, and mainstream organizations. "Teach-ins" began at the University of Michigan in March 1965 and spread to campuses nationwide.

Major events

Key moments included the October 1967 March on the Pentagon, protests at the 1968 Democratic National Convention in Chicago, the Moratorium to End the War in Vietnam (15 October 1969), which drew millions nationwide, and the nationwide student strike following the Kent State shootings of May 1970. Draft resistance, including public draft-card burnings, desertion, and the work of organizations such as Vietnam Veterans Against the War, was a major strand of the movement.

Effects

Historians debate the movement's impact, but it is generally credited with constraining escalation, shifting public opinion — by 1971 a majority of Americans considered the war a mistake — and contributing to the end of the draft and the Paris Peace Accords of 1973. The protests are a defining feature of the cultural memory of the 1960s.
