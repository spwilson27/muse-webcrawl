Random dot stereogram

TypeStereoscopic visual stimulus / optical illusion
Invented byBéla Julesz (1960)
PurposeStudy of binocular depth perception (stereopsis)

A random dot stereogram (RDS) is a pair of images made of random dots which, when viewed with a stereoscope or with the eyes converged or diverged appropriately, causes a sensation of depth: a pattern seems to stand out above or below the background, although no such pattern is visible in either image alone.

History and significance

The Hungarian-American psychologist Béla Julesz created random dot stereograms in 1960 while working at Bell Labs. His invention was scientifically momentous: because the individual images contain no monocular cues — no contours, no familiar objects — the perception of depth must arise purely from the brain's matching of the two eyes' views. Julesz called this cyclopean perception, after the mythical Cyclops' single central eye, and it demonstrated that stereoscopic depth could be computed by the visual system before object recognition, overturning influential theories of perception.

How it works

In a typical RDS, a region of dots in one image is shifted horizontally relative to the other image. When the two views are fused, the visual system interprets the shift (binocular disparity) as depth, and the shifted region appears to float in front of or behind the dot field.

Descendants

The autostereogram — popularised by the Magic Eye books of the 1990s — is a single-image descendant of the random dot stereogram, encoding the second view within repeating columns of the same picture so that depth can be seen without a stereoscope.
