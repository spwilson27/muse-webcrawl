Cerebral shunt

Procedure
IndicationHydrocephalus
TypesVentriculoperitoneal (VP), ventriculoatrial (VA), lumboperitoneal (LP)
SpecialtyNeurosurgery

A cerebral shunt is a medical device implanted by neurosurgeons to relieve pressure on the brain caused by fluid accumulation in hydrocephalus. The shunt diverts excess cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) from the cerebral ventricles to another part of the body, most commonly the peritoneal cavity of the abdomen, where it can be reabsorbed. The most widely used type is the ventriculoperitoneal (VP) shunt.

Composition

A typical shunt consists of a ventricular catheter inserted into a lateral ventricle, a one-way valve mechanism (often programmable to adjust the opening pressure) that regulates flow and prevents backflow, and a distal catheter tunnelled beneath the skin to the drainage site. Shunts are usually made of silicone and plastic and may be implanted in patients of any age, from newborns — in whom hydrocephalus is a common congenital condition — to the elderly with normal pressure hydrocephalus.

Complications

Shunt malfunction is common; many patients require one or more revisions during their lifetimes. Complications include mechanical obstruction, infection (often requiring removal and external drainage), overdrainage causing subdural hematoma, and underdrainage with persistent symptoms of raised intracranial pressure. Despite these risks, shunting remains the standard treatment for most forms of hydrocephalus.
