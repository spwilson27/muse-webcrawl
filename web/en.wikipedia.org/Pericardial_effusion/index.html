A pericardial effusion is an abnormal accumulation of fluid in the pericardial cavity, the double-walled sac surrounding the heart. The pericardium normally contains 15 to 50 ml of fluid; an effusion can range from a small, incidental collection to more than a litre of fluid that impairs cardiac filling.

Causes and symptoms

Common causes include viral or bacterial pericarditis, tuberculosis, cancer (especially lung and breast cancer and lymphoma), kidney failure with uraemia, autoimmune diseases such as lupus, hypothyroidism, chest trauma and complications of cardiac surgery or myocardial infarction. Small effusions are often asymptomatic. Larger or rapidly accumulating effusions cause dyspnoea, chest discomfort, light-headedness and, when intrapericardial pressure rises enough to compress the heart, the life-threatening emergency of cardiac tamponade, classically with Beck's triad of hypotension, muffled heart sounds and distended neck veins.

Diagnosis and treatment

Echocardiography is the diagnostic test of choice; CT and cardiac MRI can also define the effusion, and ECG may show low voltages or electrical alternans. Management depends on cause and haemodynamic effect: anti-inflammatory drugs for pericarditis, and urgent pericardiocentesis — needle drainage, sometimes followed by a surgical pericardial window — for tamponade or large symptomatic effusions.
