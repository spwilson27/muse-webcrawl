Olympia (Greek: Ὀλυμπία) is an ancient sanctuary site in the western Peloponnese, Greece, in the valley of the Alpheios river. It was the great Panhellenic sanctuary of Zeus and the home of the ancient Olympic Games, held every four years from 776 BC in honour of the god.

The sanctuary

The sacred precinct, the Altis, contained the great Temple of Zeus (completed c. 456 BC), which housed Phidias's colossal gold-and-ivory statue of Zeus — one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World — and the older Temple of Hera, in front of which the modern Olympic flame is lit. Around them stood treasuries of the Greek city-states, the workshop of Phidias, the palaestra and gymnasium, and the stadium, entered through a vaulted tunnel and seating some 45,000 spectators.

History

Worship at Olympia reaches back to the Bronze Age. The games grew from local contests into the foremost festival of the Greek world, drawing athletes and pilgrims from every city. They were suppressed in the late fourth century AD as pagan rites, and the site was later ruined by earthquakes and buried under metres of river silt.

Excavation and legacy

German archaeologists excavated Olympia systematically from 1875, uncovering the temples, stadium and treasures including the Hermes of Praxiteles. The site, a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1989, inspired Pierre de Coubertin's revival of the Olympic Games in 1896; the flame for each modern Games is still kindled at Olympia and relayed to the host city.
