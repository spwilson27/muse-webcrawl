Anchor escapement

The anchor escapement is an escapement used in pendulum clocks. Invented around 1670 — it is credited to Robert Hooke, and was introduced into practice by the clockmaker William Clement — it superseded the earlier verge escapement and, together with the pendulum, made possible the longcase ("grandfather") clock and a dramatic improvement in timekeeping accuracy.

How it works

The escapement consists of an escape wheel with pointed teeth and an anchor-shaped piece (the anchor, or pallets) attached to the pendulum's suspension. As the pendulum swings, the anchor rocks, alternately catching and releasing the escape wheel, one tooth at a time. Each swing lets the wheel advance by one tooth and gives the pendulum a small impulse to replace energy lost to friction. The anchor's shape allows the pendulum to swing in a much smaller arc — about 3–6 degrees — than the verge required, permitting a long, heavy seconds pendulum with a better rate.

Recoil and the deadbeat

The standard anchor is a recoil escapement: after a pallet catches a tooth, the continuing swing of the pendulum pushes the wheel slightly backwards, visible as the seconds hand recoiling at each tick. Recoil causes extra wear and rate variation. Around 1715 George Graham's deadbeat escapement, with specially shaped pallet faces, eliminated recoil and became the standard for precision regulators, though the cheaper anchor remained dominant in domestic clocks for two centuries.
