English Baroque is a term used to describe the architecture built in England during the short-lived vogue for the Baroque style between the Great Fire of London in 1666 and roughly the Treaty of Utrecht in 1713. Unlike the Baroque of continental Europe, the English variant was comparatively restrained and classical, blending French classicism and Dutch influences with native Palladian traditions.

Development

The style emerged during the rebuilding of London after the Great Fire, under the direction of Sir Christopher Wren, whose masterpiece, St Paul's Cathedral (1675–1710), dominates the period; Wren also designed the Royal Hospital Chelsea and numerous City churches with inventive steeples. The brief flowering of a grander, more exuberant Baroque came with the soldier-dramatist Sir John Vanbrugh and his collaborator Nicholas Hawksmoor, whose Castle Howard (from 1699) and Blenheim Palace (1705–1722) brought a massive, dramatic grandeur to the English country house.

Decline

After about 1714, taste shifted decisively toward the stricter Palladianism promoted by Lord Burlington and Colen Campbell, and the Baroque was denounced as foreign and extravagant; Vanbrugh's and Hawksmoor's work fell out of favour until a revival of interest in the twentieth century, championed by critics including Kerry Downes.
