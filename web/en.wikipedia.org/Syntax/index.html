Syntax

In linguistics, syntax is the study of how words and morphemes combine to form larger units such as phrases and sentences. It concerns word order, grammatical relations, hierarchical structure and the rules — explicit or tacit — that determine which strings of words are grammatical in a language.

Basic concepts

Speakers group words into constituents (phrases) that behave as units; tests such as substitution and movement reveal this hidden structure, drawn as tree diagrams. Languages differ typologically in basic word order — SVO (English, Mandarin), SOV (Japanese, Turkish), VSO (Irish, Classical Arabic) and others — yet all are constrained by shared structural principles.

History

The Indian grammarian Pāṇini's Aṣṭādhyāyī (c. 4th century BCE) gave a formal grammar of Sanskrit of astonishing rigour. Modern syntactic theory began with Noam Chomsky's Syntactic Structures (1957), which introduced generative grammar and transformational rules; later frameworks include Government and Binding theory, the Minimalist Program, Head-driven Phrase Structure Grammar and dependency grammar following Tesnière.

In computing

In computer science, syntax denotes the formal rules governing well-formed programs in a programming language, specified by grammars such as BNF and checked by parsers — a usage modelled on the linguistic one; a "syntax error" is a violation of those rules. See Swift for a language noted for its syntax design.
