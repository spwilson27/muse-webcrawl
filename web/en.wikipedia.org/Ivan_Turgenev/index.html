Ivan Sergeyevich Turgenev (9 November 1818 – 3 September 1883) was a Russian novelist, short-story writer, poet, and playwright. His novel Fathers and Sons (1862) is regarded as one of the major works of 19th-century fiction.

Life and work

Turgenev was born into a wealthy landowning family at Oryol and educated in Moscow, St. Petersburg, and Berlin. His first major publication, A Sportsman's Sketches (1852), a collection of stories depicting the life of serfs, was influential in shaping public opinion against serfdom. He went on to write a series of novels — Rudin, Home of the Gentry, On the Eve, Fathers and Sons, Smoke, and Virgin Soil — that chronicled Russian society and its generational and ideological conflicts.

Fathers and Sons

Fathers and Sons introduced the character of Bazarov, a young nihilist who became one of the most debated figures in Russian literature; the novel popularised the term "nihilism" itself. Turgenev spent much of his later life in Western Europe, chiefly in France, where he was a close friend of Gustave Flaubert and an important conduit between Russian and European literature.
