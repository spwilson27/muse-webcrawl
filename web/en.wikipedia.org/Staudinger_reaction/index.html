Staudinger reaction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Reaction scheme of the Staudinger reduction of an azide to an amine.

The Staudinger reaction is a chemical reaction of an organic azide with a phosphine or phosphite to produce an iminophosphorane intermediate. Hydrolysis of the intermediate yields an amine and a phosphine oxide; the overall transformation, reduction of an azide to a primary amine, is called the Staudinger reduction. The reaction is named after its discoverer, the German chemist Hermann Staudinger, who reported it in 1919.

Mechanism

The phosphorus atom of the phosphine attacks the terminal nitrogen of the azide, forming a phosphazide intermediate. Loss of dinitrogen gas gives an iminophosphorane (aza-ylide), which upon aqueous workup hydrolyses to the amine and triphenylphosphine oxide (when triphenylphosphine is used). Evolution of nitrogen gas provides much of the driving force.

Applications

The Staudinger reduction is valued for its mildness and selectivity: azides can be carried through syntheses as masked amines and unmasked under conditions that leave many other functional groups untouched. A variant, the Staudinger ligation, developed by Carolyn Bertozzi and Eliana Saxon, uses an electrophilic trap on the phosphine to form a stable amide bond and is a foundational bioorthogonal reaction used to label biomolecules in living systems.
