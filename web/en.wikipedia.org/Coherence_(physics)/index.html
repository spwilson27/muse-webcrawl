Coherence (physics)

Coherence expresses the potential for two waves to interfere. In physics, two wave sources are perfectly coherent if their frequency and waveform are identical and they have a constant phase difference. Coherence is an ideal property of waves that enables stationary (temporally and spatially constant) interference and is fundamental to optics, holography, and quantum mechanics.

Types of coherence

Temporal coherence describes the correlation between a wave and a delayed copy of itself, and is quantified by the coherence time and coherence length; it is inversely related to the source's spectral bandwidth, so monochromatic lasers are highly temporally coherent while white light has a coherence length of only a few micrometres. Spatial coherence describes the correlation between points of a wave at the same instant, governing how uniformly a wavefront can interfere with itself, as in Young's double-slit experiment.

Measurement

Interferometers such as the Michelson and Mach–Zehnder designs measure coherence by the visibility of interference fringes as path difference is varied. In quantum optics, coherence theory is formulated with correlation functions of the field, developed by Roy Glauber, distinguishing first-order (fringe) coherence from higher-order photon correlations.

Applications

Coherence underlies lasers, holography, optical coherence tomography in medicine, radio interferometry in astronomy, and matter-wave interferometry. In quantum mechanics, coherent superpositions contrast with decohered mixtures; loss of quantum coherence — decoherence — is a central obstacle in quantum computing.
