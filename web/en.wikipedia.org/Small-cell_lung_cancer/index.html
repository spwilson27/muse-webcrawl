Small-cell lung cancer

Small-cell lung cancer (SCLC) is a type of highly malignant cancer that most commonly arises within the lung, although it can occasionally arise in other body sites, such as the cervix, prostate and gastrointestinal tract. Compared with non-small-cell lung cancer, it has a faster doubling time, higher growth fraction and earlier development of widespread metastases. It accounts for roughly 15% of lung cancers and is strongly associated with cigarette smoking.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms may include cough, breathlessness, chest pain, coughing up blood, fatigue and weight loss, but the disease is often advanced at diagnosis. Because SCLC cells can secrete hormones, patients commonly develop paraneoplastic syndromes: the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH), causing low blood sodium, and ectopic Cushing's syndrome; the neurological syndrome Lambert–Eaton myasthenic syndrome is also associated. Compression of the superior vena cava by bulky central tumors can cause facial swelling and distended neck veins.

Pathology and staging

Under the microscope, SCLC consists of small, round cells with scant cytoplasm and finely granular chromatin — historically called "oat cells." Nearly all cases show inactivation of the tumor suppressors TP53 and RB1. SCLC is classically staged as limited stage (disease confined to one hemithorax and regional nodes that can be encompassed in a tolerable radiation field) or extensive stage (disease beyond those bounds), alongside the general TNM system.

Treatment and prognosis

SCLC is usually very sensitive to chemotherapy and radiation initially, but relapse with resistant disease is common. Limited-stage disease is treated with combination chemotherapy (typically a platinum drug plus etoposide) given concurrently with thoracic radiation, and immune checkpoint inhibitors have been added to first-line treatment of extensive-stage disease. Prophylactic cranial irradiation may be offered because the brain is a common site of spread. Overall prognosis remains poor, with five-year survival of around 20–25% for limited-stage and only a few percent for extensive-stage disease, though immunotherapy has produced modest improvements.
