In geology, saltation (from Latin saltus, "leap") is a mode of sediment transport in which particles are carried by a fluid — wind or water — in a series of short hops and bounces along the bed. It lies between traction (rolling and sliding along the bottom) and suspension (particles carried aloft in the flow).

Mechanism

A saltating grain is lifted by aerodynamic or hydrodynamic forces and turbulence, follows a ballistic arc, and strikes the bed, often ejecting further grains in a splash effect that sustains the process. In air, typical hop heights are a few centimetres; sand grains of roughly 70–500 micrometres usually move this way, while finer dust enters suspension and coarser grains creep along the surface. Ralph Bagnold's classic studies of desert sand (1941) established the physics of aeolian saltation.

Effects

Saltation builds and migrates sand dunes, sorts desert sediments, and abrades rock surfaces (ventifacts). In rivers, saltating gravel shapes channel beds and moves the bed load downstream. The same process operates on other worlds: images from Mars rovers show dunes shaped by saltation in the thin Martian atmosphere, and it has been inferred on Saturn's moon Titan.
