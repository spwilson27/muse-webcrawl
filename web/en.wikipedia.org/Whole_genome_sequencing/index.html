Whole genome sequencing

FieldGenomics, bioinformatics
PurposeDetermining the complete DNA sequence of an organism's genome
First human genomeHuman Genome Project, completed 2003

Whole genome sequencing (WGS), also known as full genome sequencing, complete genome sequencing, or entire genome sequencing, is the process of determining the entirety, or near entirety, of the DNA sequence of an organism's genome at a single time. This entails sequencing all of an organism's chromosomal DNA as well as DNA contained in the mitochondria.

History

The first genomes sequenced were those of viruses in the 1970s, followed by the bacterium Haemophilus influenzae in 1995. The Human Genome Project, using Sanger sequencing, produced a reference human genome by 2003 at a cost of roughly three billion dollars. "Next-generation" massively parallel sequencing technologies from the mid-2000s reduced costs dramatically — sequencing a human genome fell below ,000 and eventually to a few hundred dollars — while long-read platforms improved assembly of repetitive regions. A complete, gapless human genome was published by the Telomere-to-Telomere consortium in 2022.

Method

Modern WGS typically fragments DNA, sequences the fragments on flow cells by synthesis or by nanopore sensing, and assembles or maps the reads against a reference using bioinformatics pipelines. Coverage depth of 30× or more is standard for detecting variants in human clinical sequencing.

Applications

WGS is used in clinical diagnosis of rare genetic disease, cancer genomics, pathogen surveillance and outbreak tracing, agricultural breeding, evolutionary biology, and ancestry and forensic analysis. It is distinguished from whole exome sequencing, which targets only protein-coding regions.
