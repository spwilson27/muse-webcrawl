Faun

A faun (Latin: faunus) is a half-human, half-goat creature in Roman mythology, typically depicted with the body of a man and the legs, ears and horns of a goat. Fauns were woodland spirits associated with nature, fertility and rustic music, and were attendants of the god Faunus. In Greek mythology their counterparts are the satyrs, though satyrs originally had horse-like rather than goat-like features, and the two types became conflated in later art and literature.

Mythology

Faunus, from whom the fauns take their name, was an ancient Italian god of nature, prophecy and the countryside, sometimes identified with the Greek Pan. Fauns were said to roam the forests and plains, playing rustic music on pipes and pursuing nymphs. Unlike the sometimes frightening satyrs of Greek tradition, Roman fauns were generally portrayed as gentle, if mischievous, creatures. The poet Horace invoked Faunus as a protector of flocks, and Ovid described the festival of the Lupercalia, at which priests called Luperci — associated with Faunus — ran through the streets of Rome.

In art and literature

The faun has been a recurring figure in Western art. The Barberini Faun, a Hellenistic marble sculpture now in Munich, depicts a sleeping faun in relaxed abandon. In modern literature, Mr Tumnus in C. S. Lewis's The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe (1950) is a faun, and Stéphane Mallarmé's poem L'après-midi d'un faune (1876) inspired Debussy's orchestral prelude Prélude à l'après-midi d'un faune and Nijinsky's ballet Afternoon of a Faun.
