Keyline design

Keyline design is a system of landscape design and management developed by the Australian engineer and farmer P. A. Yeomans (Percival Alfred Yeomans, 1904–1984) from the 1940s onward. It organises the layout of a farm — its water storage, roads, tree belts, fences and cultivation patterns — around the natural topography of valleys and ridges, with the aim of distributing rainfall evenly across the landscape, building topsoil fertility, and making farms drought-resilient and profitable without heavy irrigation.

Principles

Yeomans' "Scale of Permanence" orders design decisions from the least changeable to the most: climate, land shape, water supply, roads, trees, buildings, subdivision, and finally soil. In any valley, the keypoint is the point where the valley floor steepens into the valley sides — typically the highest point at which water can most economically be stored. A contour line through the keypoint, the keyline, becomes the reference for the property's layout: farm dams are sited along it, and cultivation lines run parallel to it above and below, drifting rainwater slowly from wet valley floors toward dry ridges instead of letting it concentrate and erode.

Keyline ploughing

The keyline plough is a subsoiler with shanks set to rip along keyline cultivation patterns without inverting the soil. Yeomans claimed repeated passes, combined with flood-flow irrigation from keyline dams and rest periods, could rapidly deepen topsoil — converting subsoil to living topsoil within a few years — and greatly increase soil water retention. The method spread from Yeomans' properties "Yobarnie" and "Nevallan" in New South Wales.

Legacy

Keyline design strongly influenced Bill Mollison and David Holmgren, founders of permaculture, who acknowledged Yeomans as a precursor. It continues to inform regenerative agriculture and watershed restoration practice, though some of Yeomans' stronger claims about soil formation rates remain contested among soil scientists.
