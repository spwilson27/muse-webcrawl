Widgery Tribunal

The Widgery Tribunal was a British government inquiry established in February 1972 to investigate the events of Bloody Sunday, 30 January 1972, when British soldiers shot 26 unarmed civilians during a civil rights march in Derry, Northern Ireland, killing 14. Chaired by the Lord Chief Justice, Lord Widgery, the tribunal reported within ten weeks, on 19 April 1972.

Findings

The Widgery Report largely exonerated the soldiers, concluding that while some firing "bordered on the reckless," the soldiers had been fired upon first and that some of the victims had been handling weapons or explosives — claims based largely on contested forensic tests. The report was widely rejected by the families of the victims and by Irish nationalists, who regarded it as a whitewash.

Aftermath

Campaigning by the families led the British government to establish a second, far more extensive inquiry, the Saville Inquiry, in 1998. Reporting in 2010, Saville concluded that the killings were "unjustified and unjustifiable," that none of the victims posed a threat, and that soldiers had fired first. Prime Minister David Cameron formally apologised on behalf of the government. The contrast between the two inquiries made Widgery a lasting symbol of official injustice in Northern Ireland.
