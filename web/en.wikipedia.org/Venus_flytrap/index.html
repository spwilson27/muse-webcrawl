Venus flytrap

The Venus flytrap (Dionaea muscipula) is a carnivorous plant native to subtropical wetlands on the East Coast of the United States in North Carolina and South Carolina, within a small region around Wilmington. It is the only species in the monotypic genus Dionaea, in the sundew family Droseraceae.

Trapping mechanism

The plant's trap is formed by the terminal portion of each leaf, divided by a midrib into two lobes hinged together. On the inner surfaces are tiny trigger hairs: if an insect touches a hair twice within about 20 seconds (or two hairs in succession), the lobes snap shut in roughly a tenth of a second, forming a cage. Continued struggling stimulates the plant to seal the trap and secrete digestive enzymes, absorbing the prey over about a week — a supplement to photosynthesis in nutrient-poor, acidic soils.

Description and habitat

The plant grows as a small rosette from a bulb-like rhizome and produces tall flower stalks bearing white flowers, held well above the traps so that pollinators are not caught. It inhabits moist, sandy, peaty soils in longleaf pine savannas. Because of its restricted range and habitat loss, the Venus flytrap is considered vulnerable and is widely cultivated as a novelty houseplant.

History

Charles Darwin described the plant in his 1875 book Insectivorous Plants, calling it "one of the most wonderful plants in the world".
