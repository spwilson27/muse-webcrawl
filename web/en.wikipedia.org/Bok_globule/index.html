In astronomy, Bok globules are isolated and relatively small dark nebulae, containing dense cosmic dust and gas from which star formation may take place. They appear as dark, opaque patches silhouetted against bright emission nebulae or fields of stars. Bok globules are found within H II regions and typically have a mass of about 2 to 50 solar masses within a region about a light year or so across.

History

Bok globules were first observed by astronomer Bart Bok in the 1940s. In a paper published in 1947, Bok and E. F. Reilly hypothesized that these clouds were the "cocoons" in which stars are born — gravitationally collapsing clouds of gas and dust analogous to insect cocoons undergoing metamorphosis. The hypothesis was difficult to confirm because the globules are opaque to visible light; only with infrared and radio observations, beginning in the 1990s, were embedded protostars and warm inner cores detected, confirming that Bok globules are sites of active star formation.

Characteristics

Bok globules contain molecular hydrogen, carbon oxides and helium, and around 1% (by mass) of silicate dust. Their internal temperatures are very low, around 10 kelvin. Many contain dense cores that split into two or three gravitationally bound fragments, and observations suggest they commonly form binary and multiple star systems. Well-known examples include the globules in the Eagle Nebula region and Barnard 68, a particularly dark and nearby globule about 500 light-years away in Ophiuchus.
