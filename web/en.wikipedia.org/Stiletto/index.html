Stiletto

A stiletto (Italian: [stiˈletto]) is a specialised knife or dagger with a long slender blade and needle-like point, used primarily for thrusting and stabbing. The word also denotes the stiletto heel, a long, thin, high heel on women's shoes, named for its resemblance to the blade.

The dagger

The stiletto began to gain fame during the late Middle Ages, when it was the secondary weapon of knights: its slim profile could slip through the gaps in mail and plate, and through the eye slits of a visor. In Renaissance Italy it became associated with assassins — Venetian and French authorities repeatedly banned its carry. The blade's cross-section is often triangular or square, stiff and thrust-oriented rather than edged.

Switchblade usage

In 20th-century American usage, "stiletto" commonly refers to a spring-loaded Italian-style switchblade with a slender bayonet blade, an association cemented by 1950s films and the subsequent Switchblade Knife Act of 1958.

Stiletto heel

The stiletto heel, popularised in the 1950s by designers such as Roger Vivier (working for Dior) and Salvatore Ferragamo, uses a steel core to support a heel as narrow as a few millimetres and typically 5–10 cm (2–4 in) or more in height. The heel became an enduring fashion icon, though health professionals criticise its effects on posture and feet.

Other uses

"Stiletto" also names a pointed surgical probe, the mandrel inside a trocar, and several military items, including the Italian-era dagger issued to Arditi assault troops in the First World War.
