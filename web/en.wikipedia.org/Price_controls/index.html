Price controls

Price controls are restrictions set in law by governments on the prices that can be charged for goods and services in a market. The intent behind implementing such controls can stem from the desire to maintain affordability of goods even during shortages, and to slow inflation, or, alternatively, to ensure a minimum income for providers of certain goods or to try to achieve a living wage.

Types

The two primary forms of price control are price ceilings, which set a maximum price that sellers may charge, and price floors, which set a minimum price. A ceiling set below the market equilibrium price is said to be binding; standard economic analysis predicts it will produce a shortage, as quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied. A floor set above equilibrium, such as a minimum wage, is predicted to produce a surplus of the good or service in question.

Historical examples

Price controls are among the oldest economic regulations: the Roman emperor Diocletian's Edict on Maximum Prices of 301 AD attempted to cap prices across the empire. During the Second World War, the United States Office of Price Administration imposed comprehensive ceilings and rationing. In 1971, President Richard Nixon imposed a 90-day freeze on wages and prices as part of his New Economic Policy to combat inflation. Rent control in cities such as New York is a long-running example of a price ceiling on housing.

Debate

Economists generally argue that price controls distort market signals, reduce quality, discourage production, and generate black markets, though supporters contend they can protect consumers from price gouging and runaway inflation in emergencies and monopolised markets.
