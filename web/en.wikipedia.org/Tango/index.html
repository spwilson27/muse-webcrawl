Tango

Tango is a partner dance and social dance that originated in the 1880s along the Río de la Plata, the natural border between Argentina and Uruguay, in the working-class port neighbourhoods of Buenos Aires and Montevideo. It developed from a fusion of European, African, and local influences — including candombe, habanera, and milonga — and is danced to tango music, typically led by the bandoneón. In 2009 UNESCO inscribed tango on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

History

Tango was born in the arrabales (outskirts) of Buenos Aires among immigrants and Afro-Rioplatense communities. Initially scandalous for its close embrace, it crossed the Atlantic around 1910 and became a craze in Paris, London, and New York, which laundered its reputation and sent it back to Argentine high society. The Golden Age of the 1930s–1950s produced great orchestras — D'Arienzo, Di Sarli, Troilo, Pugliese — and the voice of Carlos Gardel defined the tango song. After decades of decline under rock music and dictatorship, Astor Piazzolla's nuevo tango revolutionized the music, and a global social-dance revival began in the 1980s.

Styles

Argentine tango is improvised within an embrace, built on walking, turns (giros), pauses, and embellishments, with close (milonguero) and open salon styles. Ballroom tango, codified in England, is a separate, standardized form. Related dances include the faster milonga and the waltz-like vals. Social dancing takes place at milongas, governed by codes of invitation by eye contact (cabeceo) and dance sets (tandas) separated by cortinas.

Music

A classic tango orchestra (orquesta típica) combines bandoneones, violins, piano, and double bass. Signature works include "La Cumparsita" (1916), the most famous tango of all, composed by Uruguayan Gerardo Matos Rodríguez.
