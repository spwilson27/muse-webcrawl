The Stuart Restoration was the return of the Stuart monarchy to the kingdoms of England, Scotland and Ireland in May 1660, when Charles II was restored to the throne after the Interregnum that had followed the execution of his father, Charles I, in 1649 and the period of republican rule under Oliver and Richard Cromwell.

Background

After the death of Oliver Cromwell in 1658, his son Richard proved unable to control the army or Parliament and resigned in May 1659. Political chaos followed until General George Monck marched south from Scotland, secured London, and arranged for the readmission of MPs excluded since 1648. The resulting Convention Parliament invited Charles to return.

The Restoration settlement

In the Declaration of Breda (April 1660) Charles promised a general pardon, liberty of conscience, and settlement of land disputes by Parliament. Charles landed at Dover on 25 May 1660 and entered London on 29 May, his 30th birthday. The Indemnity and Oblivion Act granted a free pardon except to the regicides, of whom ten were executed; the corpses of Cromwell, Ireton and Bradshaw were posthumously hanged at Tyburn.

Consequences

The Restoration re-established the Church of England, reopened the theatres, and began a period of cultural flourishing and scientific advance — the Royal Society received its charter in 1662. Yet disputes over religion, finance and royal power persisted through the reigns of Charles II and James II, culminating in the Glorious Revolution of 1688.
