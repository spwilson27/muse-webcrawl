Technicolor

A three-strip Technicolor camera, the hallmark of classic Hollywood colour cinematography.

Technicolor is a series of colour motion picture processes, the first version dating to 1916 and followed by improved versions over several decades. It was the most widely used colour process in Hollywood during the Golden Age of Hollywood, and became famous for its saturated, vivid hues — as seen in films such as The Wizard of Oz and Gone with the Wind (both 1939) and Disney's animated features.

Three-strip process

The best-known version, Process 4 (introduced 1932), used a special camera with a beam-splitting prism that recorded the scene on three strips of black-and-white film — one each for the red, green, and blue components. Release prints were made by dye-transfer imbibition printing, in which cyan, magenta, and yellow dyes were transferred onto a single film strip, producing prints of exceptional colour stability. Walt Disney held an exclusive contract for the three-colour process in animation for several years, beginning with the Silly Symphony Flowers and Trees (1932).

Decline and later history

The introduction of Eastmancolor single-strip negative stock in the 1950s, which was far cheaper and could be used in ordinary cameras, displaced three-strip photography, and the last three-strip feature was made in 1955. Dye-transfer printing continued into the 1970s. The Technicolor company evolved into a film processing and digital services business; its name and rainbow logo remain associated in the public mind with classic Hollywood colour.
