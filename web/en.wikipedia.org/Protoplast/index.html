Protoplast

Protoplasts are cells stripped of their walls, kept in an osmotically balanced medium.

A protoplast is a biological cell whose cell wall has been completely removed, usually by mechanical means or by enzymatic digestion — cellulases and pectinases for plant cells, lysozyme for bacteria, and lytic enzymes for fungi. The term was introduced by Hanstein in 1880 for the living unit enclosed by the plasma membrane. Cells that retain part of their wall are called spheroplasts.

Preparation and handling

Because the wall normally resists turgor pressure, isolated protoplasts must be suspended in an isotonic or hypertonic medium to prevent them bursting. Plant protoplasts are commonly prepared from leaf mesophyll tissue and can be induced to regenerate a new wall, divide and form callus from which whole plants can be regenerated — a basis of plant tissue culture.

Uses

Protoplasts are a major tool in plant biotechnology. Protoplast fusion allows somatic hybridisation between sexually incompatible species; isolated protoplasts readily take up DNA, viruses and organelles, making them useful for genetic transformation studies and for patch-clamp investigation of membrane channels. In microbiology, protoplasts and spheroplasts are used to study membrane structure and antibiotic action.
