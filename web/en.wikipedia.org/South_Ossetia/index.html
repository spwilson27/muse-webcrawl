South Ossetia, officially the Republic of South Ossetia – the State of Alania, is a partially recognised state in the South Caucasus. It is internationally recognised by most states as part of Georgia, within the Georgian regions of Shida Kartli and neighbouring areas. Russia, Venezuela, Nicaragua, Nauru and Syria recognise it as an independent state. Its capital and largest city is Tskhinvali.

History

The Ossetians descend from the Alans, an Iranian-speaking people who settled in the Caucasus in the Middle Ages. In the Soviet Union, the South Ossetian Autonomous Oblast was created within the Georgian Soviet Socialist Republic in 1922. As the USSR collapsed, tensions between Ossetians and the Georgian government escalated into the 1991–1992 South Ossetia War, which ended with a ceasefire and a Russian-Georgian-Ossetian peacekeeping arrangement, leaving the region de facto independent but unrecognised.

In August 2008, fighting erupted again in the Russo-Georgian War. Russian forces intervened, and after the war Russia recognised South Ossetia (and Abkhazia) as independent states. Georgia regards the territory as occupied, and most ethnic Georgians were displaced from the region.

Geography

South Ossetia lies on the southern slopes of the Greater Caucasus, a mountainous region of valleys drained by the Liakhvi and other rivers. The Roki Tunnel through the main Caucasus ridge is its principal link to North Ossetia–Alania in Russia. Winters are cold and snowy; the economy depends heavily on agriculture and on Russian subsidies and military presence.

Politics and status

The republic has its own president, parliament and armed forces, closely integrated with Russia. In 2015 South Ossetia signed a treaty of alliance and integration with Moscow, and Russian border guards patrol its boundary with Georgia proper. The Geneva International Discussions, co-chaired by the EU, OSCE and UN, remain the main forum addressing the conflict. Most of the international community continues to support Georgia's territorial integrity.

Demographics

Before the 2008 war the population was estimated at around 70,000, roughly two-thirds ethnic Ossetian and one-third ethnic Georgian. Displacement reduced the population; estimates since range between about 50,000 and 55,000, overwhelmingly Ossetian, with Georgian villages largely emptied. Ossetian and Russian are the principal languages of administration and daily life.
