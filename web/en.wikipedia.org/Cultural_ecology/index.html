Cultural ecology

Cultural ecology is the study of human adaptations to social and physical environments. Human adaptation refers to both biological and cultural processes that enable a population to survive and reproduce within a given or changing environment. This may be carried out diachronically, examining entities that existed in different periods, or synchronically, examining a present-day system and its components.

The central argument of cultural ecology is that the natural environment, in small-scale or subsistence societies dependent in large part upon it, is a major contributor to social organization and other human institutions. In industrial societies, where the environment plays a much less conspicuous role in human affairs, cultural ecology is of more limited scope.

History

The term was coined by the American anthropologist Julian Steward in the 1930s and 1940s. Steward developed a methodology he called the "method of cultural ecology," which involved identifying the techno-environmental and techno-economic features of a society, the behavioral patterns by which those features exploited the environment, and the extent to which those behavioral patterns affected other aspects of culture. His influential 1955 work Theory of Culture Change set out the framework of multilinear evolution.

Later scholars, including Marshall Sahlins, expanded the field, distinguishing between "specific evolution," in which cultures adapt to particular environments, and "general evolution," concerning the overall complexity of human society. In the 1960s and 1970s, cultural ecology merged in part with ecological anthropology and political ecology, which place greater emphasis on power relations, colonialism, and global economic forces.

Key concepts

  • Culture core — the constellation of features most closely related to subsistence activities and economic arrangements.
  • Multilinear evolution — the idea that cultural change follows multiple pathways shaped by local environments rather than a single universal sequence of stages.
  • Cultural materialism — a related research strategy, associated with Marvin Harris, emphasizing infrastructure (technology, economy, demography) as the primary driver of sociocultural systems.
