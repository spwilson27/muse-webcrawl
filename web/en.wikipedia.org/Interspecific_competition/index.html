Interspecific competition

Interspecific competition, in ecology, is a form of competition in which individuals of different species compete for the same resources in an ecosystem, such as food, water, light or space. It contrasts with intraspecific competition, which occurs between members of the same species, and it is a major force shaping the structure of biological communities.

Mechanisms

Competition may be by exploitation, in which one species uses a resource faster or more efficiently and thereby reduces its availability to others, or by interference, in which individuals directly prevent others from accessing resources, through aggression, territoriality or chemical means such as allelopathy in plants. Apparent competition occurs indirectly, when two prey species harm each other by supporting a shared predator.

Outcomes

The competitive exclusion principle, associated with G. F. Gause's experiments on Paramecium, holds that two species competing for exactly the same limiting resource cannot coexist indefinitely: the stronger competitor will drive the weaker to local extinction. In nature, coexistence is often maintained by niche differentiation — differences in diet, habitat use or timing of activity — known as resource partitioning.

Mathematical models such as the Lotka–Volterra competition equations describe how the outcome depends on the relative strength of interspecific versus intraspecific competition. Interspecific competition also drives evolutionary change, including character displacement, where competing species diverge in traits in areas where they co-occur.

Examples

Classic cases include competition between introduced grey squirrels and native red squirrels in Britain, between barnacles on rocky shores (studied by Joseph Connell), and among Darwin's finches on the Galápagos Islands during drought years.
