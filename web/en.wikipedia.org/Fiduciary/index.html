Fiduciary

Legal systemCommon law (equity)
Core dutiesLoyalty, care (prudence)
ExamplesTrustee, guardian, company director, attorney
Origin of termLatin fiducia — trust, confidence

A fiduciary is a person who holds a legal or ethical relationship of trust with another person (the principal or beneficiary) and is obliged to act in that person's best interests. Typically, a fiduciary prudently takes care of money or assets for another person. One party, for example a corporate trustee, has the duty to act in good faith and with loyalty toward the other, whose assets are at stake.

Fiduciary duties

The two classic duties are the duty of loyalty — the fiduciary must act solely in the beneficiary's interest, avoiding conflicts of interest and self-dealing — and the duty of care, often expressed as the "prudent person" standard: to manage the assets with the care, skill and caution of a prudent person. The famous formulation is that of Justice Benjamin Cardozo in Meinhard v. Salmon (1928), requiring "the punctilio of an honor the most sensitive".

Relationships recognised as fiduciary

Trustees of a trust, executors of estates, guardians and conservators, agents acting under a power of attorney, company directors in relation to their company, and attorneys in relation to clients are standard examples. Whether financial advisers owe fiduciary duties has been contested in regulation; in the United States the Department of Labor's attempts to extend a fiduciary rule to retirement advice were litigated and revised repeatedly, while registered investment advisers are fiduciaries under the Investment Advisers Act of 1940.

Breach and remedies

Breach of fiduciary duty may give rise to remedies in equity, including account of profits, rescission, constructive trusts and damages. See also: Trust law, Fiduciary duty, Conflict of interest, Agency (law).
