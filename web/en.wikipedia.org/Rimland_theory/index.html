Rimland theory

The Rimland theory is a concept in geopolitics developed by the Dutch-American strategist Nicholas J. Spykman (1893–1943), a professor at Yale University. Presented in his posthumous work The Geography of the Peace (1944), it holds that the coastal fringes — the "rimland" — of Eurasia, rather than its interior "heartland," are the key to controlling the Eurasian continent and hence world politics.

Contrast with the Heartland theory

Halford Mackinder's Heartland theory (1904) argued that whoever controlled the interior of Eurasia would command the "World-Island." Spykman inverted the dictum: "Who controls the Rimland rules Eurasia; who rules Eurasia controls the destinies of the world." The Rimland — comprising Western Europe, the Middle East, South Asia and East Asia's coasts — combines large populations, rich resources and access to both land and sea, making it the decisive zone of contention between land power and sea power.

Influence

Spykman's analysis influenced early Cold War American strategy, particularly the policy of containment articulated by George F. Kennan: the alliance systems built along Eurasia's periphery (NATO, CENTO, SEATO and bilateral Pacific alliances) were frequently interpreted as Rimland strategies designed to prevent any single power from dominating the coastal regions.
