American poetry

American poetry refers to the poetry written in the United States. It emerged in the colonies as British settlers strove to add their voices to English poetry in the New World, and it developed a distinctive national character in the nineteenth century with the work of Walt Whitman and Emily Dickinson.

History

Early colonial verse included Anne Bradstreet, the first published poet of North America, and the bestselling elegist Michael Wigglesworth. Phillis Wheatley, enslaved in Boston, published her Poems on Various Subjects in 1773. The nineteenth-century "Fireside Poets"—Longfellow, Whittier, Holmes, and Lowell—were immensely popular, but it was Whitman's free-verse Leaves of Grass (1855) and Dickinson's compressed, idiosyncratic lyrics, published after her death, that transformed American poetry.

Modernism and after

Early twentieth-century modernists, including Ezra Pound, T. S. Eliot, Wallace Stevens, William Carlos Williams, H.D., and Marianne Moore, remade poetic form. The Harlem Renaissance produced Langston Hughes and Claude McKay. Mid-century movements included the confessional poets (Robert Lowell, Sylvia Plath, Anne Sexton), the Beats (Allen Ginsberg), the New York School, and Black Mountain poets. Contemporary American poetry is strikingly diverse, encompassing figures such as Robert Frost, Elizabeth Bishop, Gwendolyn Brooks, Robert Hayden, Mary Oliver, Billy Collins, and the poet laureates appointed by the Library of Congress.
