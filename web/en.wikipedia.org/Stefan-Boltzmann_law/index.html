Stefan–Boltzmann law

Field: Thermal physics
Discovered by: Josef Stefan (1879); derived by Ludwig Boltzmann (1884)
Constant: σ = 5.670374419×10−8 W·m−2·K−4

The Stefan–Boltzmann law, also known as Stefan's law, states that the total radiant heat power emitted by a surface is proportional to the fourth power of its absolute temperature. For an ideal black body, the radiant exitance is:

j = σT4

where σ is the Stefan–Boltzmann constant and T is the temperature in kelvins.

History

The law was deduced experimentally by Josef Stefan in 1879 from measurements by John Tyndall, and derived theoretically by Stefan's student Ludwig Boltzmann in 1884 using thermodynamics and Maxwell's theory of radiation pressure. It can also be obtained by integrating Planck's law over all wavelengths.

Applications

The law is central to astrophysics: applied to the Sun, it yields an effective surface temperature of about 5,778 K from the solar luminosity and radius. It is likewise used to estimate the temperatures of stars and planets, to calculate the Earth's equilibrium temperature, and in engineering heat-transfer calculations, where real surfaces are described by an emissivity between 0 and 1 multiplying the black-body result.
