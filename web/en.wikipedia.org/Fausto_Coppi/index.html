Angelo Fausto Coppi (1919–1960) was an Italian cyclist, widely regarded as one of the greatest road racing cyclists of all time. Known as Il Campionissimo ("the Champion of Champions"), he won the Giro d'Italia five times (1940, 1947, 1949, 1952, 1953) and the Tour de France twice (1949, 1952), becoming the first rider to win the Giro–Tour double in the same year, in 1949, a feat he repeated in 1952.

Career

Coppi turned professional in 1939 and won his first Giro in 1940 at age 20. His career was interrupted by the Second World War, during which he served in North Africa and was taken prisoner by the British. After the war he resumed racing with the Bianchi team and developed a famous rivalry with the older Gino Bartali, which divided Italian fans. Coppi was a supreme climber and time trialist who pioneered modern training, diet and team tactics; his long solo breakaways, such as the 192-kilometre solo ride in the 1949 Giro, became legendary.

Later life and death

Coppi won the World Road Race Championship in 1953. His later career was shadowed by injuries, a doping controversy over amphetamine use common in the era, and public scandal over his relationship with Giulia Occhini, the "White Lady". In late 1959 he contracted malaria on a hunting trip in Burkina Faso; the disease was misdiagnosed on his return to Italy and he died on 2 January 1960, aged 40.
