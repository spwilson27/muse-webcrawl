McGirt v. Oklahoma, 591 U.S. 894 (2020), is a landmark United States Supreme Court decision holding that the reservation lands of the Muscogee (Creek) Nation in eastern Oklahoma, established by treaty in the 19th century, were never disestablished by Congress, and therefore remain "Indian country" for purposes of federal criminal law. As a result, major crimes committed by Native Americans on those lands must be prosecuted by the federal government (or the tribes), not by the state of Oklahoma.

Background

Jimcy McGirt, an enrolled member of the Seminole Nation, had been convicted in Oklahoma state court of serious sex crimes committed within the historical boundaries of the Creek Reservation and sentenced to over 1,000 years in prison. On appeal he argued the state lacked jurisdiction because the crimes occurred in Indian country and the Major Crimes Act assigns prosecution to the federal government. The question turned on whether the Creek reservation — promised after the forced removal of the Creek people to Oklahoma in the 1830s — had been disestablished during the allotment era and Oklahoma statehood.

Holding

Writing for a five-justice majority, Justice Neil Gorsuch held that only Congress can disestablish a reservation and must do so clearly; neither allotment, demographic change, nor a century of state practice had erased the Creek reservation. The opinion famously begins: "On the far end of the Trail of Tears was a promise." McGirt's state conviction was vacated; he was subsequently convicted in federal court. Chief Justice John Roberts dissented, warning of disruptive consequences for state authority over a region that includes much of Tulsa and nearly two million people.

Aftermath

Federal and tribal courts extended the reasoning to the reservations of the Cherokee, Chickasaw, Choctaw, and Seminole Nations, effectively recognising that much of eastern Oklahoma remains reservation land. The decision transformed criminal jurisdiction, prompted thousands of case reviews, and led to new federal, state, and tribal cooperation agreements; in Oklahoma v. Castro-Huerta (2022) the Court partially qualified it by allowing states concurrent jurisdiction over crimes by non-Indians against Native victims in Indian country.
