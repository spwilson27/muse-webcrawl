Endotoxins are lipopolysaccharides (LPS) found in the cell walls of Gram-negative bacteria; they are released mainly when the bacteria die and their cell walls break apart. The term "endotoxin" is sometimes loosely used for any bacterial toxin associated with the cell, but in bacteriology it is reserved for the LPS complex, in contrast to exotoxins, which are actively secreted proteins.

Structure

LPS consists of three parts: the O antigen (a repeating polysaccharide chain that varies between strains), a core oligosaccharide, and lipid A, a phosphorylated glucosamine disaccharide decorated with fatty acids. Lipid A anchors the molecule in the bacterial outer membrane and is responsible for most of its toxic activity in animals.

Biological effects

In humans, LPS is detected by the innate immune receptor TLR4 (with MD-2 and CD14), triggering strong inflammatory responses. Small amounts cause fever — LPS is a classic pyrogen — while large amounts entering the bloodstream during severe infection can provoke septic shock, with fever, falling blood pressure, intravascular coagulation, and multi-organ failure.

Testing and control

Because endotoxins are heat-stable and ubiquitous, pharmaceuticals and medical devices must be tested for contamination. The standard test uses Limulus amebocyte lysate, a reagent from horseshoe crab blood that clots in the presence of LPS; recombinant alternatives are increasingly used. Depyrogenation is typically achieved by dry heat at high temperature.
