Simultaneous interpretation

Simultaneous interpretation (SI) is a mode of interpreting in which the speaker makes a speech and the interpreter reformulates the speech into a language his audience understands at the same time, with only a few seconds of lag. It contrasts with consecutive interpreting, in which the interpreter waits for the speaker to pause before rendering a segment of speech.

History

Simultaneous interpretation came to prominence at the Nuremberg trials of 1945-1946, where proceedings were conducted in English, French, Russian, and German. The system, developed with equipment supplied by IBM, allowed the trials to proceed far more quickly than consecutive interpretation would have permitted. The technique was subsequently adopted by the United Nations and is now standard at international conferences.

Equipment and working conditions

Interpreters typically work in soundproof booths in pairs, listening through headphones and speaking into microphones. The audience selects the desired language channel on individual receivers. Because of the intense concentration required, interpreters usually alternate every 20 to 30 minutes.

Variants

Whispered interpretation, or chuchotage, is a form of simultaneous interpretation in which the interpreter sits beside a small number of listeners and whispers the translation. Signed language interpretation at conferences is also generally performed simultaneously.
