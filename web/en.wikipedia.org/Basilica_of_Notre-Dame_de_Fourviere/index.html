Basilica of Notre-Dame de Fourvière


The basilica overlooking Lyon from Fourvière hill.

The Basilica of Notre-Dame de Fourvière is a minor basilica in Lyon, France. It was built with private funds between 1872 and 1896 in a dominant position overlooking the city from the Fourvière hill. Its design, by architect Pierre Bossan, combines Romanesque and Byzantine revival elements, and its four octagonal towers have earned it the local nickname of "the upside-down elephant".

History

The hill of Fourvière — named from the Roman Forum Vetus of Lugdunum — has been a Marian shrine since the Middle Ages. A chapel dedicated to Our Lady stood on the site, and in 1643 Lyon's aldermen vowed to make an annual procession there after the city was spared from plague. A gilded statue of the Virgin, placed atop the chapel tower in 1852, remains a city landmark; its inauguration on 8 December became the origin of Lyon's Festival of Lights (Fête des Lumières). After Lyon was spared invasion during the Franco-Prussian War, citizens subscribed to build the present basilica in thanksgiving; it was consecrated in 1896 and given basilica status in 1897.

Architecture

The interior is richly decorated with mosaics, stained glass, and marble, depicting the Virgin's role in the history of France and the Church. Beneath the basilica is the crypt of Saint Joseph. The site includes gardens and an esplanade with panoramic views over Lyon, and forms part of the UNESCO World Heritage listing of the historic site of Lyon.
