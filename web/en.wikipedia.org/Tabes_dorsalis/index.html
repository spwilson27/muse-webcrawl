Tabes dorsalis

SpecialtyNeurology, infectious disease
CauseUntreated Treponema pallidum infection (syphilis)
OnsetTypically 5–20 years after initial infection
TreatmentPenicillin

Tabes dorsalis is a late manifestation of neurosyphilis, characterized by the slow degeneration of the neural tracts in the dorsal columns of the spinal cord. It is caused by untreated infection with the bacterium Treponema pallidum, the causative agent of syphilis. The term "tabes" comes from the Latin word for "wasting away".

Signs and symptoms

Classic features include lightning pains (brief, sharp, shooting pains), loss of proprioception and vibration sense leading to sensory ataxia, a characteristic high-stepping "tabetic gait", and loss of deep tendon reflexes. Pupillary abnormalities such as Argyll Robertson pupils, which constrict with accommodation but not in response to light, are also classic. Bladder dysfunction, optic atrophy, and Charcot joints (destructive joint changes from loss of pain sensation) may occur. A positive Romberg's sign—swaying or falling when standing with the eyes closed—reflects the loss of proprioceptive input.

Pathophysiology

The disease results from demyelination and degeneration of the posterior (dorsal) columns of the spinal cord, which carry information about proprioception, vibration, and fine touch. Damage to the dorsal roots also contributes to the loss of reflexes and the characteristic pain crises.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is based on the clinical picture together with serological tests for syphilis and examination of the cerebrospinal fluid. Treatment with intravenous penicillin can halt progression of the disease, though established neurological damage is often irreversible. Tabes dorsalis has become rare in the antibiotic era but still occurs in patients with untreated or inadequately treated syphilis, including those co-infected with HIV.
