Richard Baxter

Richard Baxter (12 November 1615 – 8 December 1691) was an English Nonconformist church leader and theologian from Rowton, Shropshire, who has been described as the "chief of English Protestant Schoolmen." He made his reputation in his ministry at Kidderminster in Worcestershire, which began in 1641 and in which he turned an ignorant, unruly parish into a model of godly order through systematic pastoral visitation and catechizing. Baxter was a prolific writer — some 140 books — and a leading figure among the English Presbyterians, though he resisted party labels and spent much of his life seeking comprehension and unity among Protestants.

Early life

Baxter was born at Rowton in Shropshire and educated at the free school at Wroxeter and largely by private reading after unsound schooling; he was confirmed under the influence of Puritan writings and, narrowly, dissuaded from going to court. Ordained in 1638, he served briefly as a schoolmaster and curate before being invited to Kidderminster in 1641, where he remained (with wartime interruption) for some nineteen years.

Kidderminster ministry

At Kidderminster, a rough weaving town, Baxter instituted a celebrated regime of personal catechizing: with an assistant he visited households systematically, examining and instructing each person in the faith. His The Reformed Pastor (1656) distilled this experience into a manual that remains a classic of pastoral theology. His practical devotional writings, above all The Saints' Everlasting Rest (1650) — written during illness and army service — and A Call to the Unconverted (1658), reached enormous readerships and were translated into many languages, including (by others) into Native American languages and Welsh.

Civil War and Restoration

During the Civil War Baxter served as a chaplain in the Parliamentary army, preaching against sectarian excess and later deploring the execution of Charles I and the rise of Cromwell, with whom he disputed. At the Restoration he became a royal chaplain and took part in the Savoy Conference (1661), attempting to revise the Prayer Book to accommodate Puritan scruples; the failure of comprehension led to the Act of Uniformity 1662, under which Baxter was ejected from his living with some two thousand other ministers.

Later life and persecution

Deprived of a pulpit, Baxter wrote voluminously from Acton and London, corresponded with churchmen of every party (his "mere Christianity" anticipated later ecumenical language), and suffered under the penal laws against Nonconformists. In 1685 he was tried before Judge Jeffreys for alleged sedition in his Paraphrase on the New Testament and imprisoned. His autobiography, the Reliquiae Baxterianae, was published posthumously in 1696. He died in London in 1691; his wife Margaret (née Charlton), whose memoir he wrote, had died in 1681.

Works and theology

Baxter's theology combined Reformed doctrine of grace with strong emphasis on free will in responding to the gospel offer, a mediating position (sometimes called "Baxterianism") that attracted both admiration and controversy; he opposed strict antinomianism and high Calvinist double predestination while also resisting Arminianism. His Aphorisms of Justification (1649) sparked a long debate. His hymn "Ye holy angels bright" remains in use. C. S. Lewis popularized Baxter's phrase "mere Christianity" as the title and theme of his own apologetic work.
