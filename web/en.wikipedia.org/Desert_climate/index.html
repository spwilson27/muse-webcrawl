Desert climate

The desert climate (in the Köppen climate classification, BW) is a climate in which evaporation far exceeds precipitation. Desert climates are subdivided into hot desert climates (BWh) and cold desert climates (BWk).

Characteristics

A region is classified as desert when its annual precipitation falls below a threshold that depends on temperature — often less than about 250 mm (10 in) per year. Deserts cover roughly one-third of Earth's land surface. Typical features include sparse vegetation, large daily temperature swings in hot deserts, and low humidity.

Hot desert climate (BWh)

Hot deserts, such as the Sahara, the Arabian Desert, and the deserts of Australia, lie mainly in the subtropics under persistent high-pressure systems. Summer daytime temperatures regularly exceed 40 °C, and some locations have recorded the highest air temperatures on Earth.

Cold desert climate (BWk)

Cold deserts, such as the Gobi and high-elevation deserts of Central Asia and the Great Basin, have warm summers but bitterly cold winters, often a result of continentality or rain shadow effects.

Causes

Desert climates arise from subtropical subsidence of air, rain shadows behind mountain ranges, distance from moisture sources, and cold ocean currents along coasts — as in the Atacama and Namib deserts, among the driest places on Earth.
