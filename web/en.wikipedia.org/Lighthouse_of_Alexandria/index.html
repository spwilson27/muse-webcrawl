The Lighthouse of Alexandria, sometimes called the Pharos of Alexandria, was a lighthouse built by the Ptolemaic Kingdom of Egypt between about 280 and 247 BC on the island of Pharos at the harbour of Alexandria. One of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World, it was among the tallest human-made structures on Earth for many centuries, and its name — Pharos — became the word for "lighthouse" in many languages, including French (phare) and Italian (faro).

Structure

Ancient descriptions and coins indicate a tower in three tapering tiers: a square lower section, an octagonal middle section and a cylindrical top, crowned by a statue, with a mirror or open flame at the summit to guide ships. Estimates of its height range from about 100 to 130 metres. A broad spiral ramp is said to have allowed fuel to be carried up by pack animals.

Destruction

The lighthouse was badly damaged by earthquakes in 956 and 1303 and collapsed completely by about 1323; the traveller Ibn Battuta reported in 1349 that the ruin was no longer enterable. In 1480 the Mamluk sultan Qaitbay built a citadel on the site, reusing some of the fallen stone. In 1994, archaeologists surveying the harbour floor found hundreds of massive blocks and statues attributed to the lighthouse, and the site is now an underwater archaeological park.

Legacy

The Pharos defined the architectural template for lighthouses for over a millennium and remains a symbol of Alexandria, appearing on the flag and seal of the Alexandria Governorate.
