Electrotyping


An electrotype plate used for letterpress printing

Electrotyping (also galvanotypy) is a chemical method for forming exact reproductions of a model or mould by electrodeposition of copper (occasionally other metals) onto a mould, and separating the resulting thin metal shell, which is then backed with molten type metal to make a durable printing plate or reproduction object. The process is closely related to electroforming.

History

Electrotyping was developed around 1838–1840, with key contributions from Moritz von Jacobi in Russia, Thomas Spencer and C. J. Jordan in England, and Joseph A. Adams in the United States, who applied it to wood engravings for printing. By the 1850s it was widely used in the printing trade: an "electro" allowed publishers to duplicate type pages and illustrations without resetting type, to print simultaneously on multiple presses, and to preserve plates for reprints.

Process

A mould is taken of the original — set type, a wood engraving, or another object — in wax, lead, or later plastic. The mould surface is made electrically conductive, typically by dusting with graphite, and suspended in a copper electrolyte bath as the cathode. Copper deposits on the mould over hours; the thin shell is removed, strengthened by pouring molten lead alloy onto its back, trimmed, and mounted type-high for use on a letterpress.

Applications

Beyond book and newspaper printing, electrotyping reproduced art objects, medals and sculptures — firms such as Elkington & Co. produced electrotype copies of historic metalwork for museums — and later served in making phonograph record stampers and in some printing of high-quality illustrations. The technique declined as offset lithography and photopolymer plates displaced letterpress in the twentieth century.
