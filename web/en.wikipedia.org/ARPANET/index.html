The Advanced Research Projects Agency Network (ARPANET) was the first wide-area packet-switched network with distributed control and one of the first computer networks to implement the TCP/IP protocol suite. Both technologies became the technical foundation of the Internet. The ARPANET was established by the Advanced Research Projects Agency (ARPA) of the United States Department of Defense.

History

The ARPANET was conceived under J. C. R. Licklider, who promoted the idea of an "Intergalactic Computer Network". Building on the packet-switching concepts of Donald Davies and Paul Baran, the network's first four nodes were installed in late 1969 at UCLA, the Stanford Research Institute, UC Santa Barbara, and the University of Utah. The first host-to-host message was sent on 29 October 1969 — an attempted "LOGIN" that crashed the system after two letters, "LO". Bolt, Beranek and Newman (BBN) built the Interface Message Processors (IMPs) that formed the network's switching fabric.

Electronic mail, introduced by Ray Tomlinson in 1971, quickly became the network's most popular application. In 1973 the Network Control Protocol (NCP) was supplemented by the work of Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn on what became TCP/IP; on 1 January 1983 ARPANET officially switched to TCP/IP — the "flag day" that marks a key milestone toward the modern Internet.

Growth and decline

By the 1980s ARPANET's military traffic was split off into MILNET (1983), and the NSFNET, created by the National Science Foundation in 1985, increasingly carried academic traffic. ARPANET was formally decommissioned on 28 February 1990.

Legacy

ARPANET pioneered packet switching at scale, demonstrated the viability of resource sharing between remote computers, and incubated the protocol culture — including the Request for Comments (RFC) series — that still governs Internet standardisation.
