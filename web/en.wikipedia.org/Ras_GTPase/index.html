Ras proteins switch between active GTP-bound and inactive GDP-bound states.

Ras is a family of related proteins belonging to the small GTPase superfamily, which act as binary molecular switches in intracellular signal transduction. The three classic human Ras genes — HRAS, KRAS and NRAS — encode near-identical 21 kDa proteins anchored to the inner face of the plasma membrane, where they relay signals from cell-surface receptors to pathways controlling proliferation, differentiation and survival, notably the RAF–MEK–ERK cascade and PI3K signalling.

Mechanism

Ras is active when bound to GTP and inactive when bound to GDP. Guanine nucleotide exchange factors (GEFs, such as SOS, recruited by activated growth-factor receptors) promote GDP release and GTP loading; GTPase-activating proteins (GAPs, such as neurofibromin and p120GAP) accelerate Ras's weak intrinsic GTP hydrolysis, switching it off. Prenylation of a C-terminal CAAX motif targets Ras to membranes.

Cancer

Ras genes were the first human oncogenes identified (from rat sarcoma retroviruses). Point mutations — most often at codons 12, 13 or 61 — cripple GTP hydrolysis, locking Ras "on"; such mutations occur in roughly a fifth to a third of human cancers, including most pancreatic adenocarcinomas (KRAS). Long considered "undruggable", Ras yielded to covalent inhibitors of the KRAS G12C mutant, sotorasib (approved 2021) and adagrasib, which exploit a pocket in the GDP-bound state.
