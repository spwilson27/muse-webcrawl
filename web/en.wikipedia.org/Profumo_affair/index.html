Profumo affair

The Profumo affair was a major scandal in British politics during the early 1960s. John Profumo, the Secretary of State for War in Harold Macmillan's Conservative government, had an extramarital affair with 19-year-old model Christine Keeler, who was also involved with Yevgeny Ivanov, a Soviet naval attaché and spy. When Profumo denied the affair in the House of Commons and was later shown to have lied, he resigned in June 1963; the scandal severely damaged the government's credibility.

Background

Profumo met Keeler in July 1961 at a party at Cliveden, the Astor estate, where she was swimming nude in the pool. Their brief affair overlapped with her involvement with Ivanov, raising fears of a security breach at the height of the Cold War. Society osteopath Stephen Ward, who had introduced them, was at the centre of the overlapping circles.

Exposure and resignation

Rumours circulated for months. On 22 March 1963 Profumo made a personal statement to the Commons denying "any impropriety whatever" in his acquaintance with Keeler. As evidence mounted — including a letter Profumo had written to Keeler — he admitted on 4 June 1963 that he had misled the House, and resigned from the government and Parliament. Ward was prosecuted for living off immoral earnings in a widely criticised trial and died by suicide before sentencing was completed.

Aftermath

Lord Denning's official report (September 1963) concluded there had been no security leak but criticised ministers' handling of the affair. Macmillan resigned in October 1963 (officially on health grounds), and the Conservatives lost the 1964 general election. Profumo spent the rest of his life doing charitable work at Toynbee Hall in London, eventually regaining a measure of public respect. The affair remains a byword for political scandal and establishment hypocrisy; it was dramatised in the film Scandal (1989) and the BBC series The Trial of Christine Keeler (2019).
