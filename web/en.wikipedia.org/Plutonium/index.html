Plutonium is a chemical element with the symbol Pu and atomic number 94. It is an actinide metal of silvery-gray appearance that tarnishes when exposed to air, and forms a dull coating when oxidised. Plutonium is radioactive and its most important isotope, plutonium-239, is fissile, making the element the primary material in most nuclear weapons and a fuel in some nuclear reactors.

Discovery

Plutonium was first produced and isolated on December 14, 1940, by Glenn T. Seaborg, Edwin McMillan, Joseph W. Kennedy and Arthur Wahl at the University of California, Berkeley, by bombarding uranium-238 with deuterons in a cyclotron. It was named after the dwarf planet Pluto, following the naming of uranium after Uranus and neptunium after Neptune. Because of wartime secrecy, the discovery was not published until 1948.

Characteristics

Plutonium displays more distinct solid phases (allotropes) than any other element — six at ambient pressure — with densities varying by up to 25 percent, an unusual and troublesome property for metallurgy. It is pyrophoric in finely divided form, and it slowly accumulates helium from its own alpha decay, which damages its crystal structure over time. Almost all plutonium is synthetic, produced in nuclear reactors when uranium-238 absorbs neutrons; only trace amounts occur naturally in uranium ores, and the long-lived isotope plutonium-244 exists in vanishingly small primordial quantities.

Applications

Plutonium-239 was the fissile core of the Trinity test device and the Fat Man bomb dropped on Nagasaki in 1945. Modern nuclear arsenals rely primarily on plutonium pits, and separated civilian plutonium is recycled as mixed oxide (MOX) fuel. Plutonium-238, which generates substantial decay heat, powers radioisotope thermoelectric generators on deep-space missions including the Voyager probes, the Cassini orbiter, the New Horizons probe and the Curiosity and Perseverance Mars rovers.

Toxicity and safety

Plutonium is a radiological hazard primarily when inhaled, since alpha particles cannot penetrate skin but damage tissue when emitted inside the body; it accumulates in the liver and bone surfaces. Its chemical toxicity is comparable to that of other heavy metals. Criticality accidents during processing have caused several deaths in the history of the nuclear industry, so plutonium is handled in glove boxes under strict mass and geometry controls.
