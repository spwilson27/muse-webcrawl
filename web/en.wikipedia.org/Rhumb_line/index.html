Rhumb line

In navigation, a rhumb line, rhumb or loxodrome is an arc crossing all meridians of longitude at the same angle — that is, a path with constant bearing as measured relative to true north. A vessel following a rhumb line needs no change of course, which made such lines fundamental to navigation from the age of sail onward.

Properties

On the surface of a sphere, a rhumb line (other than the equator or a meridian) spirals toward the pole, circling it infinitely often while approaching it asymptotically, yet its total length from the equator to the pole is finite. Except along meridians and the equator, a rhumb line is not the shortest path between two points — that is a great circle — but for most voyages the difference is small and the simplicity of a single constant bearing is advantageous.

History and cartography

Rhumb lines appeared on medieval portolan charts as networks of straight lines radiating from compass roses, reflecting courses between ports. The Portuguese mathematician Pedro Nunes first analysed the curve in 1537, showing that a constant-bearing course is a spiral, not a great circle. Gerardus Mercator's projection (1569) was designed so that rhumb lines plot as straight lines, making it the standard chart for navigation: a navigator could draw a straight line between two points and read off the course to steer.

Modern use

Although great-circle routes are standard for long oceanic and polar air routes, rhumb-line sailing remains common for shorter passages and in electronic charting, where distances and bearings are trivially computed from the loxodromic formulae using meridional parts.
