Golf course architecture

Golf course architecture is the art and practice of designing golf courses. A golf course architect plans the routing of the holes across a site, and the form of tees, fairways, greens, bunkers, water hazards, and rough, balancing playability for different skill levels with aesthetics, drainage, maintenance, and environmental impact.

Design philosophies

Three broad philosophies are commonly described. The penal school punishes missed shots directly, with hazards placed where an errant ball is expected to land. The strategic school, associated with architects such as Alister MacKenzie, places hazards to offer choices: a riskier line gives an easier next shot. The heroic school combines the two, inviting players to attempt a carry over a hazard with commensurate reward.

Historical development

Early courses, such as the links of Scotland, evolved from the natural terrain with minimal shaping. In the early 20th century, the so-called Golden Age of golf architecture — featuring MacKenzie (Augusta National, Cypress Point), Donald Ross (Pinehurst No. 2), and Harry Colt — established principles of strategic design. After World War II, Robert Trent Jones Sr. popularised longer, harder "championship" layouts, while Pete Dye revived visual intimidation and small greens. A modern minimalist movement, led by designers such as Tom Doak and the team of Coore and Crenshaw, has returned to naturalistic, site-sensitive design and classic course restoration.

Elements

Key elements include the routing (the sequence and flow of holes), green complexes (size, contour, and surrounding hazards), bunkering style, teeing grounds offering varied yardages, and the use of native vegetation and natural landforms. Contemporary practice increasingly addresses water conservation, habitat creation, and reduced chemical inputs.
