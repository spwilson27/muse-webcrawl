In Islamic law, riba (Arabic: ربا, ribā) refers to interest charged on loans, regarded as an unjust and exploitative gain, and is prohibited in the Quran. The prohibition is the cornerstone of Islamic banking and finance, which structures transactions to avoid interest.

Scriptural basis

The Quran repeatedly condemns riba: verses 2:275–280 (Al-Baqarah) declare that "God has permitted trade and forbidden riba", threaten those who persist in it, and enjoin creditors to grant debtors more time or remit the debt as charity; verses 3:130, 4:161, and 30:39 also address it. Hadith literature extends the prohibition to unequal exchanges of certain commodities — gold, silver, wheat, barley, dates, and salt (the "six commodities" tradition), which must be traded like-for-like and hand-to-hand.

Interpretation

Classical jurisprudence distinguished riba al-nasi'ah (increase charged for deferment of a debt, i.e., interest on loans) from riba al-fadl (excess in spot exchanges of the specified commodities). The majority of scholars, past and present, hold that the prohibition covers all modern bank interest. A minority of modernist interpreters have argued that the Quran condemns only usurious, exploitative lending as practised in pre-Islamic Arabia, not moderate regulated interest — a view rejected by most juristic bodies, including the fiqh academies of the Muslim World League and the OIC.

Islamic finance

To comply with the prohibition, Islamic finance uses asset-backed and risk-sharing contracts: murabaha (cost-plus sale), ijara (leasing), sukuk (asset-based certificates), and profit-and-loss sharing partnerships such as mudarabah and musharakah. The industry, which grew from experiments in Egypt and Malaysia in the 1960s–70s, is valued in the trillions of dollars and is overseen by Sharia boards and standard-setting bodies such as AAOIFI. Critics debate whether many instruments differ from interest-bearing loans in substance or only in legal form.
