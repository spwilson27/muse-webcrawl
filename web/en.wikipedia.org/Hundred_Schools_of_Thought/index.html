Hundred Schools of Thought

Summary

Period: c. 6th century – 221 BCE

Era: Spring and Autumn and Warring States periods, China

Major schools: Confucianism, Taoism, Mohism, Legalism

The Hundred Schools of Thought were philosophies and schools of thought that flourished in ancient China during the Spring and Autumn period and the Warring States period (roughly from the 6th century BCE until 221 BCE). This era of political fragmentation and social change produced an extraordinary flowering of intellectual debate, as thinkers travelled between rival states offering advice to rulers.

Major schools

Confucianism, founded on the teachings of Confucius and developed by Mencius and Xunzi, emphasised ritual, filial piety, and moral self-cultivation. Taoism, associated with Laozi and Zhuangzi, advocated harmony with the natural order and non-interference. Mohism, founded by Mozi, promoted universal love and meritocracy. Legalism, developed by thinkers such as Han Fei, stressed strict laws and state power, and became the governing philosophy of the Qin dynasty. Other schools included the Yin-Yang school, the Logicians (School of Names), the Agriculturalists, and the Diplomatists of the School of Vertical and Horizontal Alliances.

Decline and legacy

The period ended with the Qin unification of China in 221 BCE and the Han dynasty's subsequent adoption of Confucianism as state orthodoxy under Emperor Wu, which marginalised the other schools. Nevertheless, the texts and ideas of the Hundred Schools era remain foundational to Chinese philosophy and culture.
