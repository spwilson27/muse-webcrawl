Activity coefficient

An activity coefficient is a factor used in thermodynamics to account for deviation of a mixture of chemical substances from ideal behavior. In an ideal mixture, the microscopic interactions between each pair of chemical species are the same; real mixtures differ, and the activity coefficient γ corrects the concentration to give the effective "activity" a = γx for a component of mole fraction x, so that thermodynamic equations written for ideal systems remain valid.

Definition

The chemical potential of a species in a mixture is written μ = μ° + RT ln a, where the activity a replaces the concentration. For electrolytes, individual ion activity coefficients cannot be measured directly, so a mean ionic activity coefficient γ± is defined for the dissociated salt as a whole.

Theories

For dilute ionic solutions the Debye–Hückel theory (1923) predicts activity coefficients from ionic strength, charge and ion size, successfully explaining the limiting law observed at low concentrations. At higher concentrations, empirical and semi-empirical extensions — the Davies equation, the Pitzer equations for brines, and for non-electrolyte mixtures the local-composition models Wilson, NRTL and UNIQUAC, plus the group-contribution method UNIFAC — are standard tools in chemical engineering.

Applications

Activity coefficients enter equilibrium constant calculations, solubility and vapor–liquid equilibrium predictions, geochemical modeling of natural waters, and the design of distillation and extraction processes, where even modest non-ideality can create azeotropes or liquid–liquid splits.
