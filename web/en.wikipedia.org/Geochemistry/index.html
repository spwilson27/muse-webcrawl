Geochemistry

Geochemists study the chemical composition and processes of the Earth and other planets.

Geochemistry is the science that uses the tools and concepts of chemistry to explain the mechanisms behind major geological systems such as the Earth's crust and oceans. It overlaps with geophysics and draws on analytical chemistry, employing techniques such as mass spectrometry, chromatography and spectroscopy to measure the abundance and distribution of chemical elements and their isotopes.

History

The discipline is often dated to the work of Victor Goldschmidt in the early twentieth century, who classified elements according to their affinity for iron (siderophile), rock (lithophile), sulfur (chalcophile) or gases (atmophile). Frank Wigglesworth Clarke's compilations of the average chemical composition of rocks laid an earlier quantitative foundation. Modern geochemistry expanded rapidly after the Second World War with the development of isotope geochemistry.

Branches

  • Isotope geochemistry — uses stable and radiogenic isotope ratios for dating rocks and tracing processes, including radiometric dating.
  • Organic geochemistry — studies organic compounds in sediments, central to petroleum exploration.
  • Aqueous geochemistry — examines chemical reactions in natural waters.
  • Biogeochemistry — focuses on cycles of elements through living systems.
  • Cosmochemistry — the chemistry of extraterrestrial materials such as meteorites.

Methods

Geochemists determine elemental abundances in parts per million or billion using instruments such as inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometers. Field sampling of rocks, soils, waters and gases is followed by laboratory analysis; results feed models of processes such as magma differentiation, weathering and ocean circulation.
