Digital Command Control

Digital Command Control (DCC) is a standard for a system to operate model railways digitally. When first introduced in the early 1990s, DCC was a relatively new technology and offered significant improvements in reliability and operational flexibility over the traditional direct-current (DC) block system and the earlier alternating-current (AC) analogue systems.

DCC permits multiple locomotives on the same electrical section of track to be independently controlled. A command station encodes digital packets of information onto the track power itself; each locomotive carries a small electronic decoder that reads the packets, responds only to those addressed to it, and controls its motor, lights and sound functions accordingly.

History

Digital control systems for model trains were developed in the 1980s by several manufacturers, including Hornby's Zero 1 system. The modern DCC standard grew out of work by the German firm Lenz Elektronik, whose system was adopted and standardised by the United States National Model Railroad Association (NMRA) in 1993. The NMRA standards and recommended practices define the electrical characteristics of the track signal and the content of the command packets, allowing decoders and command stations from different manufacturers to interoperate.

How it works

The command station takes input from one or more throttles (hand-held controllers) and generates a series of digital packets. These packets are amplified by a booster and applied to the rails as a bipolar signal that simultaneously supplies power and data. The signal alternates polarity at a rapid rate; the timing of the pulses encodes binary ones and zeroes.

Each packet contains an address byte identifying the locomotive (or accessory) it is intended for, an instruction byte specifying speed, direction or function states, and an error-detection byte. Locomotive decoders rectify the track power for their own electronics and motor drive, and use pulse-width modulation to control motor speed.

Decoders and functions

Mobile decoders typically offer control of motor speed and direction, headlights and a growing number of auxiliary function outputs for ditch lights, beacons, couplers and sound. Sound decoders add a small amplifier and speaker capable of reproducing prime-mover, horn, bell and brake sounds synchronised with the locomotive's motion. Stationary (accessory) decoders operate turnouts, signals and other layout devices using the same packet stream.

Advantages and limitations

Because control is addressed to the locomotive rather than to a block of track, operators can run trains independently anywhere on the layout, perform realistic meets and switching, and consist (multiple-unit) locomotives with individual speed trimming. Wiring is simplified compared with multi-cab DC block control. Limitations include the cost of equipping each locomotive with a decoder and the modest bandwidth of the track signal.
