Loess

Loess (from the German Löss) is a clastic, predominantly silt-sized sediment formed by the accumulation of wind-blown dust. It covers about ten percent of the Earth's land surface and large areas of Europe, Asia and North America. Loess deposits may be hundreds of metres thick and are typically buff-coloured, homogeneous, porous and friable, breaking into vertical cliffs when dry.

Properties

Loess particles are typically 0.01 to 0.05 mm in diameter, composed mainly of quartz, feldspar and mica with carbonate cement. Because it stands in vertical faces yet is easily excavated, loess has historically been cut into dwellings — notably the cave houses of the Loess Plateau in China. When wet, however, loess loses strength and is highly erodible.

Formation

Most loess derives from glacial grinding: meltwater rivers deposited silt on floodplains, and strong winds carried it to blanket the surrounding terrain during the cold, dry conditions of ice ages. Desert sources also contribute, as with the loess of the Chinese Loess Plateau, much of which is carried from the Gobi Desert.

Soils and agriculture

Weathered loess forms some of the world's most fertile agricultural soils, underlying the grain belts of Ukraine, the American Midwest and northern China. The same erodibility that enriches floodplains also fuels severe soil erosion; the Yellow River takes its name and colour from loess sediment.
