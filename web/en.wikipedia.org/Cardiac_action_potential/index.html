Cardiac action potential

The cardiac action potential is the brief change in membrane potential across the membrane of a heart muscle cell (cardiomyocyte) that triggers contraction. It differs from the action potential in neurons and skeletal muscle in having a prolonged plateau phase, lasting some 300 milliseconds, which synchronises contraction and prevents tetany of the heart.

[Image: The five phases of the ventricular action potential, showing the plateau phase]

Phases

In ventricular and atrial myocytes the action potential has five phases. Phase 4 is the resting membrane potential, about −90 mV. Phase 0 is rapid depolarisation caused by a massive influx of sodium ions through voltage-gated sodium channels. Phase 1 is brief early repolarisation from a transient outward potassium current. Phase 2 is the plateau, in which calcium influx through L-type calcium channels — the same calcium that triggers contraction — balances potassium efflux. Phase 3 is repolarisation, as calcium channels close and potassium efflux predominates.

Pacemaker cells

Cells of the sinoatrial and atrioventricular nodes lack a stable resting potential; their membranes drift upward during phase 4 (the "pacemaker potential", driven partly by the funny current If through HCN channels) until threshold is reached, setting the heart's rhythm. Antiarrhythmic drugs are classified (Vaughan Williams classification) largely by which currents of the action potential they block.
