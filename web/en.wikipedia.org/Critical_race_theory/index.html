Critical race theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


CRT originated in American law schools in the 1970s–80s

Critical race theory (CRT) is an academic field and intellectual movement that emerged in American legal scholarship in the 1970s and 1980s. It examines how race and racism intersect with legal, social and political systems, holding that racism is not merely individual prejudice but can be embedded in laws, policies and institutions.

Origins and key ideas

Founding figures include Derrick Bell, Kimberlé Crenshaw (who coined the term and also developed the concept of intersectionality), Richard Delgado, Cheryl Harris and Alan Freeman. Core themes commonly identified by its scholars include: the ordinariness of racism in society; "interest convergence" (Bell's thesis that advances in civil rights occur when they align with dominant interests); the social construction of race; differential racialisation; the critique of liberalism and of claims of legal neutrality and colourblindness; and the valuing of storytelling and counter-narrative.

CRT developed out of critical legal studies and radical feminism, and has influenced fields such as education, political science and ethnic studies, giving rise to related frameworks including Latino critical theory (LatCrit) and Asian American legal scholarship.

Public debate

From 2020 onward, critical race theory became the subject of intense political controversy in the United States, with several states enacting laws or policies restricting how race and related concepts may be taught in public schools — measures that supporters describe as preventing divisive teaching and critics describe as mischaracterising the academic framework and chilling legitimate instruction about American history.
