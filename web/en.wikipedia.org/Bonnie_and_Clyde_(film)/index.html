Bonnie and Clyde (film)

Bonnie and Clyde is a 1967 American biographical neo-noir crime film directed by Arthur Penn and starring Warren Beatty and Faye Dunaway as the title characters Clyde Barrow and Bonnie Parker. The film also features Michael J. Pollard, Gene Hackman, and Estelle Parsons. The screenplay is by David Newman and Robert Benton. Bonnie and Clyde is considered one of the first films of the New Hollywood era and a watershed in American cinema: it broke many cinematic taboos and, for some members of the youth generation, represented a counterculture embrace of violent outlaws.

Plot

In Depression-era Texas, ex-con Clyde Barrow meets bored waitress Bonnie Parker when he tries to steal her mother's car. The two embark on a spree of robberies across the central United States, joined by C. W. Moss, Clyde's brother Buck, and Buck's wife Blanche. As their crimes escalate and killings mount, the gang enjoys folk-hero fame while a relentless manhunt closes in. After Buck is killed and Blanche captured, the wounded Bonnie and Clyde are ambushed and killed in a hail of bullets by a posse led by Texas Ranger Frank Hamer—a climax rendered in landmark slow-motion editing.

Production and style

Newman and Benton's script was first offered to François Truffaut and Jean-Luc Godard before Arthur Penn accepted; the film bears the influence of the French New Wave in its tonal shifts between comedy and brutality, its location shooting in Texas, and its frank sexuality and violence. The final ambush scene, shot with multiple cameras at different speeds and edited by Dede Allen, became one of the most imitated sequences in cinema.

Reception and legacy

Initial reviews were divided, and Warner Bros. released it hesitantly, but critic Pauline Kael's influential praise and strong word of mouth turned it into a major hit; it won Academy Awards for Best Supporting Actress (Estelle Parsons) and Best Cinematography (Burnett Guffey) from ten nominations. The film's success helped inaugurate the director-driven New Hollywood of the 1970s. It was selected for the US National Film Registry in 1992 and ranks on the American Film Institute's lists of the greatest American films; Dunaway's beret-and-skirt look sparked a fashion revival.
