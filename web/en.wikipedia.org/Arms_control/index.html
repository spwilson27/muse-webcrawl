Arms control

Arms control is a term for international restrictions upon the development, production, stockpiling, proliferation and usage of small arms, conventional weapons and weapons of mass destruction. Arms control is typically exercised through diplomacy, which seeks to impose such limitations upon consenting participants through international treaties and agreements, although it may also comprise efforts by a nation or group of nations to enforce limitations upon a non-consenting country.

History

Early arms control efforts included the Hague Conventions of 1899 and 1907 and the naval limitation treaties of the interwar period, such as the Washington Naval Treaty of 1922. During the Cold War, arms control became a central element of superpower relations, producing agreements such as the Partial Nuclear Test Ban Treaty (1963), the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty (1968), the SALT agreements of the 1970s, the Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty (1987) and the START treaties limiting strategic nuclear arsenals.

Enforcement and verification

Effective arms control depends on verification — measures such as on-site inspections, satellite monitoring and data exchanges that allow parties to confirm compliance. Agreements covering chemical and biological weapons include the Biological Weapons Convention (1972) and the Chemical Weapons Convention (1993), the latter administered by the Organisation for the Prohibition of Chemical Weapons. Critics argue that arms control can freeze existing advantages or be exploited by states that cheat, while supporters contend it reduces the risk of war and lowers the costs of arms racing.
