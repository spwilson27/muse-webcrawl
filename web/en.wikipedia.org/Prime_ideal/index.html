Prime ideal

In algebra, a prime ideal is an ideal in a ring with the property that if the product of two elements lies in the ideal, then at least one of the elements lies in the ideal. Prime ideals generalise the notion of prime numbers: in the ring of integers, the prime ideals are exactly the ideals generated by a prime number, together with the zero ideal.

Definition

An ideal P of a commutative ring R is prime if it has the following two properties: P is a proper ideal (P ≠ R), and for any two elements a and b of R, if the product ab is in P, then a is in P or b is in P. Equivalently, P is prime if and only if the quotient ring R/P is an integral domain.

Examples

In the integers, the ideal (p) generated by a prime number p is prime, since Z/(p) is a field. In a polynomial ring k[x] over a field k, the ideal generated by an irreducible polynomial is prime. The zero ideal is prime exactly when the ring itself is an integral domain.

Properties

Every maximal ideal is prime, because a field is an integral domain, but the converse is false in general. The preimage of a prime ideal under a ring homomorphism is prime. The set of all prime ideals of a ring, its spectrum, forms the basis of the Zariski topology in algebraic geometry.

Motivation

Prime ideals arose in the study of algebraic number theory, where Dedekind showed that ideals in rings of algebraic integers factor uniquely into prime ideals, restoring a form of unique factorisation where ordinary elements fail to factor uniquely (as with 6 = 2·3 = (1+√−5)(1−√−5) in Z[√−5]).
