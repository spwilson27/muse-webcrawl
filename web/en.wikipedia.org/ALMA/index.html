The Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array (ALMA) is an astronomical interferometer of 66 radio telescopes in the Atacama Desert of northern Chile. Sitting on the Chajnantor plateau at 5,000 metres altitude — one of the driest and highest observatory sites on Earth — it observes light at millimetre and submillimetre wavelengths, between infrared light and radio waves.

Design

ALMA's antennas can be moved across the plateau into configurations up to 16 kilometres wide, giving the array a maximum angular resolution of about 0.005 arcseconds, sharper than the Hubble Space Telescope at visible wavelengths. Signals from all antennas are combined by a dedicated supercomputer correlator. The array is operated by a partnership of Europe (ESO), North America (NRAO) and East Asia (NAOJ), in cooperation with Chile, and cost roughly US$1.4 billion, making it the most expensive ground-based telescope of its era.

Science

ALMA studies the cold universe: the gas and dust from which stars and planets form. Its celebrated results include detailed images of protoplanetary discs such as that around HL Tauri, observations of distant galaxies in the early universe, and contributions to the Event Horizon Telescope's first image of a black hole shadow in 2019.
