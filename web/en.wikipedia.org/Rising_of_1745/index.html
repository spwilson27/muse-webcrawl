The Jacobite rising of 1745, also known as the Forty-five Rebellion or simply the '45, was an attempt by Charles Edward Stuart to regain the British throne for his father, James Francis Edward Stuart. It was the last of the Jacobite risings aimed at restoring the exiled House of Stuart.

Background

James Stuart had been exiled after the Glorious Revolution of 1688. Previous risings in 1689, 1715, and 1719 had failed. Charles, popularly known as Bonnie Prince Charlie, landed on the island of Eriskay in the Outer Hebrides in July 1745 with only a handful of companions, relying on support from Highland clans.

The campaign

Charles raised his standard at Glenfinnan on 19 August 1745. His army captured Edinburgh and defeated government forces under Sir John Cope at the Battle of Prestonpans on 21 September. The Jacobites then invaded England, reaching Derby on 4 December, but turned back two days later when expected English and French support failed to materialise.

Retreating into Scotland, the Jacobites won a further success at the Battle of Falkirk Muir in January 1746. The campaign ended at the Battle of Culloden on 16 April 1746, where the Jacobite army was decisively defeated by government troops commanded by the Duke of Cumberland.

Aftermath

Charles escaped to France after five months as a fugitive in the Highlands. The government responded with harsh measures: leaders were executed or exiled, the Disarming Act was enforced, wearing of Highland dress was prohibited until 1782, and heritable jurisdictions were abolished. The rising effectively ended Jacobitism as a serious military threat.
