Operational level of war

The operational level of war (also called operational art) is the level of command that connects the strategic objectives of a war to the tactical employment of forces in individual battles. At this level, commanders plan and conduct campaigns and major operations — sequences of battles and manoeuvres arranged in time and space to achieve strategic goals within a theatre of operations.

The operational level links strategy to tactics through campaigns.

Levels of war

Modern doctrine commonly distinguishes three levels. The strategic level concerns national objectives and the overall conduct of the war. The tactical level concerns individual engagements fought by units. Between them, the operational level — typically exercised by theatre, army group, army or corps headquarters — decides where and when battles are fought, how forces are sustained, and how tactical successes are accumulated into strategic effect.

Origins

The concept emerged from the experience of the First World War, in which tactical victories could not be converted into decisive results across vast fronts. Soviet theorists of the 1920s and 1930s — notably Alexander Svechin, who coined "operational art", and Mikhail Tukhachevsky, with his theory of deep operations — first formalised the idea, envisaging successive operations conducted deep into the enemy rear. The German practice of Blitzkrieg achieved similar effects without the same formal theory. The United States Army formally adopted the operational level in its 1982 edition of FM 100-5, influenced by concepts such as AirLand Battle.

Key considerations

Operational art involves identifying the enemy's centre of gravity, sequencing operations and phases, arranging logistics and lines of communication, and recognising the culminating point at which an offensive can no longer be sustained.
