Escalator

An escalator is a moving staircase which carries people between floors of a building or structure. It consists of a motor-driven chain of individually linked steps on a track which cycle on a pair of tracks which keep them horizontal. Escalators are often used around the world in places where lifts would be impractical, or they can be used in conjunction with them. Principal areas of usage include department stores, shopping malls, airports, transit systems, convention centers, hotels, arenas, stadiums, and public buildings.

History

The earliest patent for an escalator-like machine was issued in 1859 to Nathan Ames of Michigan for a steam-driven "revolving stairs", which was never built. In 1892 Jesse W. Reno patented an "endless conveyor or elevator" — an inclined moving walkway with cleats — installed as a novelty ride at Coney Island in 1896. Charles Seeberger redesigned the device with flat steps, joined the Otis Elevator Company, and produced the first step-type escalator made public at the Paris Exposition of 1900, where it won first prize; the word "escalator", coined by Seeberger from scala (steps) and "elevator", was an Otis trademark until the 1950s, when it was ruled generic. The first escalator on the London Underground opened at Earl's Court station in 1911.

Design and operation

Modern escalators run at up to about 0.5 metres per second and can carry thousands of people per hour, far exceeding a lift's throughput. Steps are driven by a continuous chain looped around a drive gear, guided so they flatten at the top and bottom landings, where comb plates mesh with the step treads. Safety devices include emergency stop buttons, skirt brushes, and sensors that halt the machine if objects jam the combs. Etiquette varies: in London and much of the world riders stand on the right and walk on the left; in Japan the standing side differs between Tokyo (left) and Osaka (right). Notable installations include the Central–Mid-Levels escalators in Hong Kong, the longest outdoor escalator system in the world, and the deep escalators of metro systems such as Kyiv's Arsenalna station.
