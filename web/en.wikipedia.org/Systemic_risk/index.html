Systemic risk

In finance, systemic risk is the risk of collapse of an entire financial system or market, as opposed to the risk associated with any one individual entity, group or component of a system. It can be defined as "financial system instability, potentially catastrophic, caused or exacerbated by idiosyncratic events or conditions in financial intermediaries", and is sometimes described as a negative externality generated by the interconnections of the financial system.

Causes

Systemic risk arises from the interdependence of financial institutions through lending, derivatives exposures and payment systems. When one institution fails, its creditors and counterparties may suffer losses that cause them to fail in turn, producing a cascade or "domino effect". Common triggers include asset price bubbles, excessive leverage, maturity mismatch, and herd behaviour among investors. The financial crisis of 2007–2008 is the most widely studied modern example, in which the failure of Lehman Brothers threatened the stability of the global banking system.

Measurement

Economists have proposed several measures of systemic risk, including CoVaR, which estimates the value at risk of the financial system conditional on an institution being in distress, and SRISK, which estimates the capital shortfall an institution would face in a severe market decline. Network models of interbank exposures are also used to simulate contagion.

Regulation

Following the 2008 crisis, regulators introduced macroprudential policies aimed at systemic risk, including higher capital requirements for systemically important financial institutions, regular stress tests, and resolution regimes intended to allow large firms to fail without collapsing the system. The Dodd–Frank Act in the United States and the Basel III framework internationally are central examples.
