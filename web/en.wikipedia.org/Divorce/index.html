Divorce

Divorce (also known as dissolution of marriage) is the process of terminating a marriage or marital union. Divorce usually entails the cancelling or reorganising of the legal duties and responsibilities of marriage, dissolving the bonds of matrimony between a married couple under the rule of law of the particular country or state. Divorce laws vary considerably around the world, but in most countries, divorce is a legal process that requires the sanction of a court or other authority.

Grounds and procedure

Historically, divorce generally required proof of fault — adultery, cruelty, desertion or insanity. From the late 20th century many jurisdictions adopted no-fault divorce, beginning with California in 1969, allowing dissolution on grounds of irreconcilable differences or irretrievable breakdown. Divorce proceedings typically resolve alimony (spousal support), child custody and support, division of property and debts.

History

Divorce existed in ancient Athens, Rome and Japan, though frequently on terms favouring husbands. In medieval Christian Europe marriage was treated as indissoluble, though annulment was possible. England allowed full divorce only by private Act of Parliament until the Matrimonial Causes Act 1857 created civil divorce courts. In the United States divorce rates rose through the 20th century, peaking around 1980. Malta was among the last countries to legalise divorce, in 2011; the Philippines remains notable for lacking a general divorce law.

Statistics and effects

Divorce rates differ widely by country and are associated with factors such as age at marriage, education and economic independence of women. Research examines effects on children, household income and health; outcomes vary substantially by family circumstances.

Religious views

The Roman Catholic Church does not recognise divorce, though it grants annulments. Judaism permits divorce through the get; Islamic law provides for talaq and judicial divorce (khula), with rules varying by school and country.
