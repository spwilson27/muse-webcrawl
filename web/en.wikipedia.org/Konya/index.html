Konya is a major city in central Turkey, on the southwestern edge of the Central Anatolian Plateau, and the capital of Konya Province, Turkey's largest province by area. One of the oldest continuously inhabited cities in the world, it is best known as the capital of the Seljuk Sultanate of Rum and as the home of the 13th-century Sufi mystic and poet Jalal al-Din Muhammad Rumi, whose tomb and the Mevlana Museum are major pilgrimage and tourist sites.

History

The Konya plain was settled from Neolithic times — the celebrated site of Çatalhöyük lies nearby — and the city, known in antiquity as Iconium, was visited by Saint Paul according to the Acts of the Apostles. After the Battle of Manzikert (1071) it fell to the Seljuk Turks and became the capital of the Sultanate of Rum, reaching its golden age in the 12th and 13th centuries with splendid mosques, madrasas, and caravanserais. It later passed to the Karamanids and then to the Ottoman Empire in the 15th century.

Rumi and the Mevlevi order

Rumi settled in Konya in 1228 and died there in 1273; his mausoleum, marked by a distinctive green conical tower, forms part of the Mevlana Museum. The Mevlevi Sufi order founded by his followers — famous for the whirling dervishes' sema ceremony — is headquartered in the city, and the annual Mevlana festival each December draws visitors from around the world.

Modern city

Today Konya is a conservative, prosperous industrial and agricultural centre, a centre of machinery, food processing, and Turkey's wheat belt, served by high-speed rail links to Ankara and Istanbul. Landmarks include the Alaeddin Mosque, the Karatay and İnce Minare madrasas, and the nearby Sille village.
