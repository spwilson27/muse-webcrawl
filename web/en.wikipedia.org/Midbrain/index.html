Midbrain

The midbrain or mesencephalon is the rostral-most portion of the brainstem connecting the brainstem to the forebrain. It is traversed by the cerebral aqueduct and contains structures involved in vision, hearing, motor control, sleep and wakefulness, arousal (alertness), and temperature regulation. Together with the pons and medulla oblongata, it forms the brainstem.

Structure

The midbrain is divided into the tectum (the dorsal "roof", containing the superior and inferior colliculi — the corpora quadrigemina) and the tegmentum, separated by the cerebral aqueduct. Ventrally lie the cerebral peduncles, including the substantia nigra and the crus cerebri. The red nucleus and the nuclei of cranial nerves III (oculomotor) and IV (trochlear) are also located here.

Function

The superior colliculi coordinate visual reflexes and orienting movements of the eyes and head; the inferior colliculi relay auditory information. The substantia nigra pars compacta produces dopamine essential to movement — its degeneration causes Parkinson's disease. The periaqueductal gray modulates pain, and the reticular formation within the tegmentum regulates arousal and consciousness.

Development

The midbrain develops from the mesencephalon, the middle of the three primary brain vesicles, and changes relatively little in structure compared with the forebrain and hindbrain derivatives.
