John Hampden

Born: c. June 1595, London, England
Died: 24 June 1643 (aged 48), Thame, Oxfordshire
Cause of death: Wounds received at the Battle of Chalgrove Field
Occupation: Politician, soldier
Allegiance: Parliament (Roundheads)

John Hampden (c. June 1595 – 24 June 1643) was an English landowner and politician whose refusal to pay ship money made him a national figure in the resistance to Charles I, and who became one of Parliament's leading figures at the start of the English Civil War.

Ship money case

Hampden, a wealthy Buckinghamshire landowner and cousin of Oliver Cromwell, was assessed twenty shillings in ship money in 1635. His refusal led to the celebrated case of R v Hampden (1637–38). Although the judges found narrowly for the Crown, the case publicised the constitutional argument that the king could not tax without Parliament's consent.

Long Parliament

In the Long Parliament from 1640, Hampden was one of its most influential managers, one of the "Five Members" whose attempted arrest by Charles I in January 1642 precipitated the slide to war.

Death

When war came, Hampden raised a regiment of foot and fought at Edgehill. On 18 June 1643 he was mortally wounded at the Battle of Chalgrove Field in a skirmish with Prince Rupert's cavalry, dying six days later at Thame.

Legacy

Hampden is commemorated as a patriot hero of parliamentary liberty; statues and institutions across Britain and the Commonwealth bear his name, including Hampden Park in Glasgow.
