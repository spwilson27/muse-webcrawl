Suppletion

Suppletion
FieldMorphology (linguistics)
DefinitionUse of an unrelated word form to fill a gap in a paradigm
Classic examplesgo/went; good/better; be/am/is/was

In linguistics and etymology, suppletion is traditionally understood as the use of one word as the inflected form of another when the two words are not cognate. For linguists, suppletion is a form of morphological irregularity in which a change in a word's grammatical features is not predictable by phonology or regular derivation, but is supplied by a completely different root.

Examples

  • The English verb go takes its past tense went from the unrelated verb wend.
  • The adjective good has suppletive comparatives better and best; bad similarly yields worse and worst.
  • The verb be assembles forms from several Indo-European roots: am/is/are (from *h₁es-), was/were (from *h₂wes-), and be/been (from *bʰuH-).
  • In French, aller ("to go") mixes forms from Latin ambulare, vadere, and ire.
  • Irish bean ("woman") has the unrelated plural mná.

Strong and weak suppletion

Scholars distinguish strong suppletion, where the forms share no phonological material (go/went), from weak suppletion, where the forms are recognisably related but follow no productive pattern, as in buy/bought or the German sein paradigm. Suppletion tends to survive most tenaciously in the highest-frequency words of a language, which resist analogical levelling precisely because speakers encounter them so often.
