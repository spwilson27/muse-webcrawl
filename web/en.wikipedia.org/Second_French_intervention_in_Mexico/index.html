Second French intervention in Mexico

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The intervention installed Maximilian of Habsburg as Emperor of Mexico, with French backing.

The Second French intervention in Mexico (1861–1867) was an invasion of the Republic of Mexico by the Second French Empire, initially supported by Britain and Spain, which installed Archduke Maximilian of Austria as Emperor of the Second Mexican Empire. It followed Mexico's suspension of foreign debt payments under President Benito Juárez and took place while the United States was preoccupied with its Civil War.

Background

After the Reform War, Mexico's liberal government was bankrupt. In 1861 Juárez suspended payments on foreign debt, prompting Britain, Spain, and France to sign the Convention of London and occupy Veracruz. Britain and Spain withdrew when France's ambitions became clear: Napoleon III sought to establish a French-backed monarchy in Mexico as a check on American expansion and a foothold in the Americas.

Course of the war

French forces suffered a famous defeat at the Battle of Puebla on 5 May 1862 — commemorated as Cinco de Mayo — but reinforced troops captured Puebla and Mexico City in 1863. An assembly of Mexican conservatives offered the crown to Maximilian, who arrived in 1864 with his wife Carlota. Maximilian's liberal policies alienated his conservative supporters while failing to win over republicans. Juárez's government retreated north but never surrendered.

Collapse

After the American Civil War ended, the United States pressed France diplomatically, invoking the Monroe Doctrine, and supplied Juárez's forces. Facing threats in Europe from Prussia, Napoleon III withdrew French troops in 1866–1867. Maximilian refused to abdicate and was captured at Querétaro and executed by firing squad on 19 June 1867, an event immortalized in paintings by Édouard Manet. Juárez restored the republic, and the intervention became a defining episode of Mexican national resistance.
