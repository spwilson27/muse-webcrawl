In chemistry, a radical (commonly free radical) is an atom, molecule or ion that has at least one unpaired valence electron. These unpaired electrons make radicals highly reactive toward other substances, or even toward themselves; most radicals are short-lived, though some — such as nitric oxide and molecular oxygen — are stable enough to be biologically important.

Formation and behaviour

Radicals form by homolytic bond cleavage (from heat, light or redox reactions) and typically react by hydrogen abstraction, addition to double bonds, or radical–radical combination. They drive chain reactions in combustion, atmospheric chemistry (including ozone-layer chemistry via chlorine radicals) and radical polymerisation used to make plastics such as polystyrene and PVC.

In biology

Reactive oxygen species such as superoxide and the hydroxyl radical are produced by metabolism and immune cells. They can damage DNA, proteins and lipids ("oxidative stress") and are implicated in ageing and disease; organisms defend themselves with enzymes such as superoxide dismutase and antioxidants. Nitric oxide, a radical, is a vital signalling molecule in blood-vessel control — work recognised by the 1998 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.
