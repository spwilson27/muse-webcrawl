Impeachment in the United States

Impeachment in the United States

Impeachment in the United States is the process by which the House of Representatives brings charges against a federal official (including the president) and the Senate conducts the trial. Article II of the Constitution provides that the president, vice president, and all civil officers shall be removed from office on impeachment for, and conviction of, "Treason, Bribery, or other high Crimes and Misdemeanors". The House impeaches by simple majority; conviction requires two-thirds of the Senate, with the Chief Justice presiding when the president is tried.

Presidential impeachments

Four presidential impeachment proceedings have reached the Senate. Andrew Johnson was impeached in 1868 amid Reconstruction conflicts and acquitted by a single vote. Bill Clinton was impeached in 1998 on charges of perjury and obstruction of justice arising from the Lewinsky scandal and acquitted in 1999. Donald Trump was impeached twice — in 2019 (abuse of power and obstruction of Congress, over pressure on Ukraine) and in 2021 (incitement of insurrection after the January 6 Capitol attack) — and acquitted both times. Richard Nixon resigned in 1974 before the full House could vote on articles approved by its Judiciary Committee. No U.S. president has ever been removed by impeachment.

Other officials

Federal judges account for most impeachments: the House has impeached 21 individuals in total, of whom eight — all judges — were convicted and removed. The process also exists at state level for governors and other officers; several governors, most recently Rod Blagojevich of Illinois in 2009, have been removed by it.
