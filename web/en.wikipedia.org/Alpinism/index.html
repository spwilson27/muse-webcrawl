Alpinism

Alpinism, also called mountaineering in its alpine form, is the sport of climbing mountains in the style developed in the European Alps: self-sufficient, lightweight ascents using a combination of rock climbing, ice climbing, and glacier travel, traditionally in a single push and by fair means.

Alpinism
Origin: European Alps, 19th century
Golden age: 1854–1865
Key skills: Rock, ice, mixed climbing

History

The "golden age of alpinism" (1854–1865) saw first ascents of most major Alpine peaks, culminating in Edward Whymper's ascent of the Matterhorn in 1865. The Alpine Club was founded in London in 1857. In the 20th century the style was exported to the Himalayas, Karakoram, and Patagonia, where "alpine style" came to contrast with siege-style expedition tactics using fixed ropes and porters.

Techniques and ethics

Alpinists emphasise speed, lightness, and self-reliance, carrying all gear, often without supplementary oxygen. Hazards include avalanches, crevasses, rockfall, altitude illness, and storms. Modern developments include solo ascents, winter ascents, and ski-mountaineering descents.

Notable figures

Pioneers include Michel-Gabriel Paccard and Jacques Balmat (Mont Blanc, 1786), Albert Mummery, and later Reinhold Messner, Jerzy Kukuczka, and Walter Bonatti.
