The neoclassical synthesis is the mid-20th-century reconciliation of Keynesian economics with neoclassical economics that dominated mainstream macroeconomics from the 1940s to the 1970s. The term was popularized by Paul Samuelson in his textbook Economics (1948). Its core claim was that Keynesian demand management applies in the short run, when wages and prices are sticky, while in the long run — at full employment — the economy behaves as classical theory describes, with allocation governed by relative prices and supply.

Origins and content

John Hicks's 1937 paper "Mr. Keynes and the 'Classics'" recast Keynes's General Theory as the IS–LM model, in which goods-market (IS) and money-market (LM) equilibrium jointly determine output and interest rates. Franco Modigliani (1944) argued that Keynesian unemployment equilibrium rested on wage rigidity, embedding Keynes within a general-equilibrium framework. Samuelson's textbook spread the synthesis worldwide: microeconomics remained neoclassical (supply, demand, marginal analysis), while macroeconomics used Keynesian tools — the consumption function, multiplier, and fiscal and monetary policy — to stabilize output around a full-employment trend explained by the neoclassical (Solow) growth model.

Influence

The synthesis underpinned postwar economic policy in the United States and other Western countries, informing the Employment Act era, the Kennedy–Johnson tax cut of 1964 (advised by synthesis Keynesians such as Walter Heller and James Tobin), and standard textbook teaching for a generation. Its practitioners built large-scale econometric models used for forecasting and policy simulation.

Criticisms and decline

From within, Joan Robinson derided the synthesis as "bastard Keynesianism," arguing it distorted Keynes's emphasis on uncertainty and effective demand; post-Keynesians kept this critique alive. From without, monetarists under Milton Friedman attacked its fiscal activism and its exploitation of the Phillips curve trade-off. The stagflation of the 1970s, together with the Lucas critique (1976) and the rise of new classical macroeconomics, displaced the synthesis as the research frontier — though elements survived in the later "new neoclassical synthesis" (New Keynesian DSGE models) that again combined optimizing microfoundations with sticky prices and active monetary policy.
