The Giant's Causeway (Irish: Clochán an Aifir) is an area of about 40,000 interlocking basalt columns, the result of an ancient volcanic fissure eruption, on the north coast of County Antrim in Northern Ireland, about three miles (4.8 km) northeast of the town of Bushmills. It was declared a World Heritage Site by UNESCO in 1986 and a national nature reserve by the Department of the Environment for Northern Ireland in 1987. In a 2005 poll of Radio Times readers, the Giant's Causeway was named the fourth-greatest natural wonder in the United Kingdom.

Geology

Some 50 to 60 million years ago, during the Paleocene epoch, Antrim was subject to intense volcanic activity as part of the North Atlantic Igneous Province, when highly fluid molten basalt intruded through chalk beds to form an extensive lava plateau. As the lava cooled rapidly — probably on contact with the sea — contraction caused it to fracture into regular, mostly hexagonal pillars, much as drying mud cracks into polygons. The columns range up to 12 metres (39 ft) high, and the solidified lava is in places 28 metres thick. Similar columnar basalt formations exist at Fingal's Cave on the Scottish island of Staffa, from the same lava flows.

Legend

In Irish mythology, the Causeway was built by the giant Fionn mac Cumhaill (Finn McCool) as a stepping-stone path to Scotland, so that he could fight the Scottish giant Benandonner. In the best-known version, Fionn, learning that Benandonner was far larger than he, disguised himself as a baby; when Benandonner saw the "infant's" immense size, he fled in terror back to Scotland, tearing up the Causeway behind him so that Fionn could not follow. A related geological formation in Scotland gave the two sites a shared mythology long before their common volcanic origin was understood.

Tourism

The Causeway attracted visitors throughout the 19th century, and the Giant's Causeway and Bushmills Railway carried tourists from 1883. The site is in the care of the National Trust; a visitor centre opened in 2012, built into the hillside with a grass roof after the previous centre burned down in 2000. Named rock formations along the shore include the Giant's Boot, the Wishing Chair, the Camel, and the Organ. The site draws hundreds of thousands of visitors a year and is one of Northern Ireland's most popular tourist attractions.
