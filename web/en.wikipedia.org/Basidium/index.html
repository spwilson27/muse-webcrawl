Basidium

A basidium (plural basidia) is a microscopic spore-producing structure found on the hymenophore of fruiting bodies of basidiomycete fungi. It is the defining reproductive cell of the phylum Basidiomycota, the group that includes most mushrooms, bracket fungi, puffballs, rusts, and smuts. Each basidium typically bears four basidiospores externally on slender projections called sterigmata.

Structure

The basidium develops from a dikaryotic hyphal cell in which the two nuclei fuse (karyogamy), producing a diploid nucleus that immediately undergoes meiosis. The four resulting haploid nuclei migrate through the sterigmata into the developing spores. In most species the basidia are club-shaped cells borne on the gills, pores, or other spore-bearing surfaces of the fruiting body.

Types

Basidia are classified by the arrangement of their internal walls after meiosis. Holobasidia are single-celled and undivided, whereas phragmobasidia are divided by septa, as in many jelly fungi. Older classifications used these forms to subdivide the Basidiomycota.

Role in the life cycle

Basidiospores are typically forcibly discharged in a process called ballistochory. The small size and enormous number of spores produced by a single fruiting body allow basidiomycete fungi to disperse widely by wind.
