Metallicity

In astronomy, metallicity is the abundance of elements present in an object that are heavier than hydrogen and helium. Most of the ordinary matter in the universe is hydrogen and helium, created in the Big Bang; all heavier elements — which astronomers collectively call "metals" — were synthesised by nucleosynthesis in stars and supernovae and dispersed into the interstellar medium.

Measurement

For stars, metallicity is usually expressed as [Fe/H], the logarithm of the ratio of iron to hydrogen abundance compared with the Sun. The Sun's metallicity by mass is about 0.0134–0.02. Metallicity is measured from spectral absorption lines and is a key input to models of stellar structure and evolution.

Stellar populations

Metallicity underpins the classification of stars into populations: metal-rich, young Population I stars like the Sun; older, metal-poor Population II stars in the galactic halo and globular clusters; and the hypothetical first-generation Population III stars, formed from pristine gas with essentially zero metallicity.

Significance

Metallicity affects stellar colour, lifetime and winds, the cooling of gas clouds, and the formation of planets — giant planets are found preferentially around metal-rich stars. Galaxies build up metallicity over cosmic time as successive stellar generations enrich their gas.
