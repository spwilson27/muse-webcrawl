Ramsey theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Ramsey theory is a branch of combinatorics that studies the conditions under which order must appear within large structures. Named after the British mathematician and philosopher Frank P. Ramsey, who proved the foundational theorem in 1928 (published 1930), the field is guided by the principle that complete disorder is impossible: any sufficiently large mathematical structure, however it is coloured or arranged, must contain a highly regular substructure. A well-known informal slogan of the field, due to Theodore Motzkin, is that Ramsey theory is the search for "order in chaos".

Ramsey's theorem

In its graph-theoretic form, Ramsey's theorem states that for any pair of positive integers s and t there is a smallest integer R(s, t), the Ramsey number, such that any graph on at least R(s, t) vertices, with its edges coloured red or blue, must contain either a red complete subgraph on s vertices or a blue complete subgraph on t vertices. A classic illustration is the "party problem": R(3, 3) = 6 says that among any six people there are three who mutually know each other or three who are mutual strangers — whichever way acquaintance is arranged, structure is forced.

Ramsey numbers

Exact values of Ramsey numbers are known only for small parameters: R(3, 3) = 6, R(3, 4) = 9, R(4, 4) = 18 and R(4, 5) = 25. The value of R(5, 5) remains unknown, lying somewhere in the low-to-mid 40s. Paul Erdős famously remarked that if hostile aliens demanded the value of R(5, 5) humanity should marshal all its computers to find it, but if they demanded R(6, 6) humanity should attempt to destroy the aliens. Computing Ramsey numbers is a major computational challenge, and the known bounds were improved in 2023 when the exponential upper bound for diagonal Ramsey numbers was reduced for the first time since 1935.

Other results

The field includes the infinite version of Ramsey's theorem; van der Waerden's theorem on monochromatic arithmetic progressions in coloured integers; the Hales–Jewett theorem; Schur's theorem; and Szemerédi's theorem. Ramsey-theoretic arguments are used in number theory, geometry, logic, theoretical computer science and the theory of Banach spaces (through Ramsey's theorem's connections with infinitary combinatorics and set theory).
