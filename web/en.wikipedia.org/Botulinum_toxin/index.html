Botulinum toxin

Botulinum toxin is a neurotoxic protein produced by the bacterium Clostridium botulinum and related species. It is among the most potent toxins known, and it causes the flaccid paralysis seen in botulism by preventing the release of the neurotransmitter acetylcholine at the neuromuscular junction.

Mechanism

The toxin enters nerve terminals and cleaves SNARE proteins required for synaptic vesicles to fuse with the cell membrane. Without acetylcholine release, muscles cannot contract, producing paralysis. Effects wear off over weeks to months as the nerve terminal recovers and sprouts new endings.

Medical and cosmetic use

In highly diluted, purified form, botulinum toxin type A is widely used as a drug, best known by the brand name Botox. Medical applications include treating muscle spasticity, cervical dystonia, chronic migraine, overactive bladder, strabismus, and excessive sweating. Cosmetic injections temporarily relax facial muscles to reduce wrinkles, an effect lasting roughly three to four months. Other brand names include Dysport, Xeomin, and Jeuveau.

Safety

Although extremely toxic in uncontrolled exposures — botulism can be fatal through respiratory paralysis — therapeutic doses are carefully standardized. Side effects of injections are usually local and temporary, such as pain at the injection site or drooping of a nearby muscle.
