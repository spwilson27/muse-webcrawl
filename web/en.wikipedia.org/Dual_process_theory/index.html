Dual process theory

Diagram of System 1 and System 2 thinking (placeholder image)

Dual process theory, in psychology, proposes that thought operates in two fundamentally different ways: a fast, automatic, unconscious mode and a slow, deliberate, conscious one. In the terminology popularized by Daniel Kahneman in Thinking, Fast and Slow (2011), these are called System 1 and System 2.

System 1 and System 2

System 1 operates automatically and quickly, with little or no effort and no sense of voluntary control. It generates impressions, intuitions and feelings, detects simple relations, and handles tasks such as orienting to a sudden sound or reading words on a billboard. System 2 allocates attention to effortful mental activities, including complex computations, deliberate comparisons and self-control. System 2 is associated with the subjective experience of agency, choice and concentration, but it is also "lazy" and often endorses System 1's outputs uncritically.

History

Distinctions between automatic and controlled processing run from William James through 20th-century cognitive psychology. Jonathan Evans, Keith Stanovich, Richard West, and Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky developed modern versions; Kahneman and Tversky's heuristics-and-biases programme catalogued systematic errors arising from System 1 heuristics, such as anchoring, availability and representativeness.

Evidence and applications

Experiments such as the Cognitive Reflection Test show predictable conflicts between intuitive and reflective answers. Dual process ideas have been applied in behavioural economics, medicine, law, education and the study of moral judgment, where they frame debates about reasoning biases and how to correct them.

Criticism

Critics argue that the two "systems" are not discrete systems at all, that traits attributed to them do not always cluster together, and that the framework risks becoming a flexible story rather than a precise theory. Defenders respond that it is best understood as a useful set of labels for families of processes.
