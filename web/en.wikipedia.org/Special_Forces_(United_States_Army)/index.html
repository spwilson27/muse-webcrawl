Special Forces (United States Army)


Special Forces soldiers of the US Army

The Special Forces of the United States Army, widely known as the Green Berets due to their distinctive service headgear, are a special operations force tasked with seven primary missions: unconventional warfare, foreign internal defense, direct action, counterterrorism, counterinsurgency, special reconnaissance, and information operations. Their motto, De oppresso liber ("to free the oppressed"), reflects their heritage of working with indigenous resistance movements.

History

The Special Forces trace their lineage to the First Special Service Force and the Office of Strategic Services of the Second World War. The 10th Special Forces Group (Airborne) was activated in 1952 at Fort Bragg, North Carolina, under Colonel Aaron Bank. President John F. Kennedy championed the force and authorized the green beret as its headgear in 1961. Green Berets served extensively as advisers in Vietnam, and in later decades in El Salvador, the Persian Gulf War, Afghanistan, and Iraq.

Organization

Special Forces are organized into groups, five on active duty (1st, 3rd, 5th, 7th, and 10th) and two in the Army National Guard (19th and 20th), each oriented toward a geographic region. The basic operating element is the twelve-man Operational Detachment Alpha (ODA, or "A-Team"), in which each soldier is cross-trained in a specialty — weapons, engineering, medicine, or communications — and in a foreign language and culture of the assigned region.

Selection and training

Candidates pass through Special Forces Assessment and Selection (SFAS) and then the Special Forces Qualification Course (the "Q Course") at Fort Liberty (formerly Fort Bragg), a pipeline of one to two years including language training and the Robin Sage unconventional warfare exercise. Graduates are awarded the green beret and the Special Forces Tab.
