Seedling

A dicot seedling shortly after germination, showing its two seed leaves (cotyledons).

A seedling is a young sporophyte developing out of a plant embryo from a seed. Seedling development begins with the germination of the seed and typically involves the emergence of the radicle (the first root), the hypocotyl and the plumule. A seedling has three main parts: the radicle, the hypocotyl (embryonic stem), and the cotyledons (seed leaves). The two classes of flowering plants (angiosperms) differ in their seedlings: monocots have one cotyledon and dicots have two.

Germination and growth

Germination may be epigeal, where the cotyledons are raised above the ground and often become photosynthetic, as in beans, or hypogeal, where the cotyledons remain below ground, as in peas. Before its true leaves begin photosynthesis, the seedling depends on food stored in the endosperm or cotyledons. Photomorphogenesis — the developmental response to light — governs the switch from dark growth (skotomorphogenesis, producing a pale, elongated seedling) to normal development once the shoot reaches light.

Conditions and care

Seedlings require water, oxygen, suitable temperature and, after emergence, light. They are vulnerable to damping-off, a disease caused by fungi such as Pythium and Rhizoctonia that rots the stem at soil level. Gardeners raise seedlings in trays or pots under controlled conditions and "harden them off" — gradually acclimatising them to outdoor conditions — before transplanting. In forestry, tree seedlings are grown in nurseries for reforestation and plantation establishment.
