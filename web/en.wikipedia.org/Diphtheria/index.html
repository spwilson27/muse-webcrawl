Diphtheria

Diphtheria is an infection caused by the bacterium Corynebacterium diphtheriae. Most infections are asymptomatic or have a mild clinical course, but lethal outcomes are associated with the production of diphtheria toxin. Signs and symptoms may vary from mild to severe, and usually start two to five days after exposure.

The characteristic thick grey pseudomembrane on the tonsils.

Signs and treatment

Symptoms often include a sore throat, fever, swollen glands, and weakness. A thick grey or white patch (pseudomembrane) on the throat, tonsils, or nasal passages can obstruct breathing. The toxin can damage the heart (myocarditis) and nerves, causing paralysis. Treatment involves diphtheria antitoxin, first developed by Emil von Behring in the 1890s, together with antibiotics such as erythromycin or penicillin; airway support may be required.

Prevention and history

Diphtheria is largely preventable through vaccination with diphtheria toxoid, included in combination vaccines such as DTP. Once a leading cause of death in children, it is now rare where immunization coverage is high, though outbreaks occur amid war and collapsing health systems. The 1925 serum run to Nome, Alaska—commemorated by the Iditarod—dramatized emergency antitoxin delivery.
