Research data management

Research data management (RDM) is the planning, organisation, documentation, storage, sharing, and preservation of data created or used during a research project. It covers material ranging from laboratory measurements and survey responses to software, images, interviews, simulations, and physical samples represented by digital records.

Data lifecycle

RDM follows the research lifecycle. Before collection, researchers decide what data are needed, which formats and standards to use, how consent and intellectual-property rights will be handled, and where the data will ultimately be preserved. During a project, active storage requires clear file names, version control, access controls, quality checks, and regular backups. At completion, selected data are prepared for deposit in a repository, assigned documentation and persistent identifiers, and retained or disposed of according to policy.

Data management plans

A data management plan is a living document describing responsibilities, collection methods, metadata, storage, security, legal and ethical constraints, sharing arrangements, preservation, and costs. Many research funders require one because public funding increasingly carries expectations that results should be verifiable and reusable.

FAIR principles

The FAIR principles state that data should be Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, and Reusable. FAIR does not necessarily mean open: access may be mediated where privacy, consent, cultural authority, safety, or commercial rights require restrictions.

Metadata and repositories

Metadata explain who created data, when and how they were collected, what variables and units mean, and under what licence they may be reused. Repositories preserve files, validate deposits, expose metadata to search systems, and may issue DOIs. Disciplinary repositories often support specialised standards, while institutional and general-purpose repositories serve broader communities.

Challenges

Effective management must balance openness with confidentiality, Indigenous data sovereignty, cybersecurity, long-term format obsolescence, and the cost of curating very large datasets. Poor documentation can make technically preserved data unusable; good RDM therefore treats documentation as part of the research record, not an administrative afterthought.
