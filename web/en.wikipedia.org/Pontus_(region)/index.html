Pontus (Greek: Πόντος, "sea") is a historical region on the southern coast of the Black Sea, in present-day northeastern Turkey. The name originally referred to the sea itself (the Pontos Euxeinos, the "Hospitable Sea") and came to denote the coastal hinterland stretching from the Halys river eastward toward Colchis, bounded to the south by the Pontic Mountains.

History

Greek colonies such as Sinope, Trapezus and Amisus were founded on the coast from the 7th century BC. After Alexander the Great, the region coalesced into the Hellenistic Kingdom of Pontus under the Mithridatic dynasty, reaching its zenith under Mithridates VI, Rome's formidable adversary in the Mithridatic Wars. Defeated by Pompey, Pontus was absorbed into the Roman provincial system and later formed part of the Byzantine world and the Empire of Trebizond (1204–1461), before passing to the Ottoman Empire.

Pontic Greeks

The region was home for millennia to the Pontic Greeks, speakers of the Pontic dialect, whose communities were uprooted by the 1923 population exchange between Greece and Turkey. Pontic culture — its music (the kemenche lyra), dances and cuisine — survives in the Greek diaspora.
