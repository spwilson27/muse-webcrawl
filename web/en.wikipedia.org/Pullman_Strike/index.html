Pullman Strike
Date: 11 May – early August 1894
Location: Pullman, Chicago; spreading nationwide
Parties: American Railway Union vs Pullman Company
Outcome: Strike broken; union leader Eugene V. Debs jailed

The Pullman Strike was a nationwide railroad strike in the United States that lasted from May to August 1894. A turning point for the American labour movement, it pitted the American Railway Union (ARU), led by Eugene V. Debs, against the Pullman Company, the railroads, and the federal government.

Background

During the depression following the Panic of 1893, the Pullman Company cut wages repeatedly — by an average of about 25 per cent — but refused to lower the rents and prices it charged in its company town of Pullman, Illinois. Workers joined the ARU and, after the company rejected arbitration, walked out on 11 May 1894.

The boycott and federal intervention

In June the ARU began a boycott: union members refused to handle trains carrying Pullman cars, paralysing rail traffic across much of the country. President Grover Cleveland's administration obtained a sweeping federal injunction and sent federal troops to Chicago, over the protest of Illinois governor John Peter Altgeld. Violence erupted on 4 July; strikers burned and overturned cars, and soldiers and deputy marshals killed a number of workers.

Outcome

The strike collapsed by early August. Debs was imprisoned for contempt of court, and the Supreme Court upheld the government's action in In re Debs (1895). The ARU was destroyed, Pullman workers were forced to sign anti-union pledges, and the episode radicalised Debs, who emerged from prison as the nation's leading socialist. A federal commission later censured the company, and in 1898 the Illinois courts ordered Pullman to sell the town's residential property.
