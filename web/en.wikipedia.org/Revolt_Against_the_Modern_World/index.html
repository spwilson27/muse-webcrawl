Revolt Against the Modern World

Revolt Against the Modern World (Italian: Rivolta contro il mondo moderno) is a book by the Italian esotericist and political philosopher Julius Evola, first published in 1934. It is regarded as his best-known and most influential work, setting out the Traditionalist critique of modernity that Evola developed in dialogue with the ideas of René Guénon.

Content

The book is divided into two parts. The first, "The World of Tradition", describes what Evola presents as the principles of a traditional civilisation: a sacred, hierarchical order oriented toward transcendence, in which kingship, priesthood, asceticism, caste and ritual express metaphysical truths. The second part, "Genesis and Face of the Modern World", interprets history as a process of decline or involution through successive ages — corresponding to the Hesiodic and Hindu doctrine of cycles, including the Kali Yuga of Hindu cosmology — culminating in the modern world of materialism, egalitarianism and the rule of economics, which Evola considered a regression from earlier forms of civilisation.

Reception and influence

Although published under Fascist Italy, the book criticised aspects of the regime as well as liberalism, democracy and communism. It became a foundational text for Traditionalist and radical-right circles in post-war Europe. An English translation by Guido Stucco was published by Inner Traditions in 1995. Scholars of esotericism and the far right study the work as a key statement of Evola's thought, while critics regard its celebration of hierarchy and its doctrines of race and caste as extremist.
