Possibilism (geography)

Possibilism in geography is the theory that the physical environment sets certain constraints or limitations, but culture is otherwise determined by social conditions, and human communities can choose among a range of possibilities offered by their surroundings. It arose in the early 20th century as a reaction against environmental determinism, which held that climate and terrain largely dictate the character and development of societies.

Origins

The doctrine is associated above all with the French geographer Paul Vidal de la Blache (1845–1918), founder of the French school of regional geography. Vidal argued that a region offers a set of possibilities from which its inhabitants, guided by their genre de vie (way of life), select and develop particular ones — the same Mediterranean environment, for example, supported very different societies in ancient Greece, North Africa, and Spain. The term "possibilism" itself was coined by the historian Lucien Febvre, who summarised the view in 1922: "There are no necessities, but everywhere possibilities; and man, as master of the possibilities, is judge of their use."

Development

In the United States, possibilist thinking was carried forward by Carl Sauer and the Berkeley School of cultural geography, who emphasised the human transformation of landscapes. Later geographers such as Griffith Taylor proposed a middle position — "neo-determinism" or "stop-and-go determinism" — in which the environment directs but does not compel, like a traffic controller who alters the rate but not the ultimate direction of progress. Critics of pure possibilism objected that it understated real environmental limits, a debate renewed in contemporary discussions of climate change adaptation, where societies demonstrably have choices but within hard physical bounds.
