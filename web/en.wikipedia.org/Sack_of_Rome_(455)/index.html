Sack of Rome (455)

Date2–16 June 455
LocationRome, Western Roman Empire
BelligerentsVandal Kingdom vs. Western Roman Empire
ResultRome thoroughly plundered over two weeks

The Sack of Rome in 455 was the capture of Rome by the Vandals of King Genseric, who sailed from Carthage and plundered the city for two weeks. It was the second sack of Rome in the fifth century, after that of the Visigoths under Alaric in 410, and is the origin of the modern word "vandalism".

Background

The Western emperor Valentinian III had betrothed his daughter Eudocia to Genseric's son Huneric, but in March 455 Valentinian was murdered by Petronius Maximus, who seized the throne and married Valentinian's widow Licinia Eudoxia, intending Eudocia for his own son. According to tradition, Eudoxia appealed to Genseric for help. The Vandal fleet — the Vandals had built a powerful navy since taking Carthage in 439 — arrived at the mouth of the Tiber in late May.

The sack

Pope Leo I met Genseric outside the walls, reprising his famous embassy to Attila, and persuaded him to refrain from arson and massacre; in exchange the city opened its gates on 2 June. For fourteen days the Vandals methodically stripped Rome of movable wealth — including the treasures of the Temple in Jerusalem that Titus had brought from the sack of 70 AD, the gilded bronze tiles of the Temple of Jupiter Optimus Maximus, and vast quantities of gold, silver, art and slaves. Licinia Eudoxia and her daughters, among thousands of captives, were carried back to Carthage.

Aftermath

Unlike the Visigothic sack of 410, the Vandal sack was thorough but comparatively orderly: there was little burning or slaughter, but the systematic removal of treasure over a fortnight devastated the city, which never recovered its former wealth. The captive princess Eudocia was married to Huneric as originally planned. The event demonstrated that the Western Empire could no longer defend even its ancient capital, two decades before its final collapse in 476.
