Canterbury

Canterbury is a cathedral city in Kent, in south-east England. Historically a market town on the River Stour, it became one of the most famous pilgrimage destinations in medieval Europe and remains the seat of the Archbishop of Canterbury, leader of the Church of England and the worldwide Anglican Communion. Its population is about 55,000, including a large student population.

History

The settlement began as the Belgic tribal capital Durovernon and became the Roman town of Durovernum Cantiacorum. In 597 Saint Augustine arrived on a mission from Pope Gregory I and founded what became Canterbury Cathedral, making the city the birthplace of English Christianity. After the murder of Archbishop Thomas Becket in the cathedral in 1170, his shrine drew pilgrims from across Europe, inspiring Geoffrey Chaucer's The Canterbury Tales. The shrine was destroyed during the Reformation in 1538.

The cathedral and old town

Canterbury Cathedral, St Augustine's Abbey, and St Martin's Church together form a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Much of the medieval city wall and street plan survives, including the Westgate Towers and the half-timbered houses of the King's Mile. The city suffered heavy bombing in the Baedeker raids of 1942 and was rebuilt after the war.

Modern Canterbury

The University of Kent, Canterbury Christ Church University, and the University for the Creative Arts make Canterbury a significant student city. Tourism, centred on the cathedral, remains a major part of the economy, and the city is served by high-speed rail to London.
