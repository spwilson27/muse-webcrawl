Brumalia

TypeAncient Roman / Byzantine festival
Observed24 November – 17 December (later traditions vary)
Associated deitiesSaturn, Ceres, Bacchus

The Brumalia was an ancient Roman solstice festival honouring Saturn, Ceres, and Bacchus, generally held for a month ending around the winter solstice. The name derives from bruma, "shortest day" or winter solstice. In Byzantine times the festival evolved into a celebration beginning on 24 November and continuing for 24 days, in which each day was associated with a letter of the Greek alphabet and revellers feasted at the homes of friends whose names began with the corresponding letter.

Origins

Tradition attributed the festival's founding to Romulus. The original Roman observance of the bruma was a one-day midwinter festival; as the season's festivities expanded, the Brumalia became associated with the vintage and with Bacchus as god of wine, and was celebrated with feasting, drinking, and merriment during the darkest part of the year.

Byzantine period

In the Eastern Roman (Byzantine) Empire the Brumalia remained popular for centuries despite repeated condemnation by the Church; the Council in Trullo (692) attempted to ban it along with other pagan survivals. Emperors hosted banquets at court, and the festival's alphabet custom is described by the chronicler John Malalas and later sources.

Legacy

Some scholars have linked elements of Brumalia and neighbouring midwinter festivals, such as Saturnalia, to later Christmas-season customs, although the extent of direct influence is debated.
