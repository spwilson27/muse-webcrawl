Diogenes of Sinope (c. 412/404 – c. 323 BC), also known as Diogenes the Cynic, was a Greek philosopher and one of the founders of Cynic philosophy. Born in Sinope, he was exiled from his native city for defacing the currency and moved to Athens, where he became a pupil of Antisthenes and adopted an extreme ascetic lifestyle.

Life

Diogenes made a virtue of poverty. He lived in a large ceramic jar (a pithos) in the marketplace of Athens, owned nothing but a wooden bowl — which he threw away after seeing a child drink from cupped hands — and begged for his food. He carried a lantern in daylight, claiming to be "looking for an honest man." When Alexander the Great visited him and asked if there was anything he could do for him, Diogenes famously replied, "Yes — stand out of my sunlight."

Captured by pirates and sold into slavery in Crete, he was bought by Xeniades of Corinth, whose children he tutored. He spent the rest of his life in Corinth, where he died around 323 BC, reportedly on the same day as Alexander the Great.

Philosophy

Diogenes taught that happiness comes from living according to nature, in self-sufficiency (autarky) and shamelessness about natural acts, rejecting wealth, power, reputation, and social convention. He called himself a kosmopolitēs — a "citizen of the world" — one of the earliest uses of the idea of cosmopolitanism. His provocative public behavior — eating, sleeping, and performing bodily functions in the marketplace — was a deliberate performance of his doctrine, using parrhesia (frank speech) and anaideia (shamelessness) to expose the hypocrisy of convention.

Legacy

The principal source for Diogenes' life is the Lives and Opinions of Eminent Philosophers by Diogenes Laërtius. His figure became a lasting cultural symbol of contempt for luxury and of philosophy lived rather than merely written; his name is also attached to Diogenes syndrome, a term for extreme self-neglect.
