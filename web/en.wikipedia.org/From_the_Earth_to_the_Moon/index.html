From the Earth to the Moon

From the Earth to the Moon: A Direct Route in 97 Hours, 20 Minutes (French: De la Terre à la Lune) is an 1865 novel by Jules Verne, one of the earliest works of modern science fiction. It tells the story of the Baltimore Gun Club, a society of American Civil War artillery enthusiasts, who build an enormous cannon — the Columbiad — and fire a crewed projectile at the Moon.

Plot

After the Civil War, Impey Barbicane, president of the Gun Club, proposes firing a projectile to the Moon. The project captures worldwide enthusiasm and funding. A French adventurer, Michel Ardan, persuades the club to make the projectile hollow so that he, Barbicane, and Captain Nicholl can ride inside. The sequel, Around the Moon (1870), completes the voyage: the projectile misses the Moon, orbits it, and returns to splash down in the Pacific.

Influence

Verne attempted realistic calculations of the required muzzle velocity and chose Florida as the launch site — near the actual site later used by NASA. The novel inspired early spaceflight pioneers including Konstantin Tsiolkovsky and Robert Goddard, and was famously adapted in Georges Méliès's 1902 film A Trip to the Moon. The three travellers' names and the Florida launch were echoed by Apollo 11, whose command module was named Columbia.
