Siberian Traps


Traps basalt on the Putorana Plateau

The Siberian Traps (Russian: Сибирские траппы) are a large region of volcanic rock, known as a large igneous province, in Siberia, Russia. The massive eruptive event that formed the traps is one of the largest known volcanic events of the last 500 million years. The eruptions continued for roughly two million years, spanning the Permian–Triassic boundary about 251.9 million years ago, and are widely regarded as the principal cause of the end-Permian mass extinction, the most severe extinction event in Earth's history.

Extent and volume

The traps cover about 2 million square kilometres (770,000 sq mi) of east-central Russia — an area roughly the size of Western Europe — with an estimated original extent several times larger before erosion. Volume estimates for the basalt range from about 1 to 4 million cubic kilometres. The name "traps" derives from the Swedish trappa, meaning stairs, referring to the step-like hills of the basalt landscape.

Environmental effects

The eruptions released immense quantities of carbon dioxide, sulfur gases, and halogens, amplified by magma intruding through coal- and evaporite-rich sediments of the Tunguska Basin. The resulting global warming, ocean acidification and anoxia, and possible ozone damage are linked to the extinction of some 80–90% of marine species and about 70% of terrestrial vertebrate species. Nickel-rich emissions may also have favoured methane-producing microbes.

Resources

The province hosts the Norilsk-Talnakh deposits, among the world's largest sources of nickel, copper, and platinum-group metals, formed in magma conduits associated with the traps.
