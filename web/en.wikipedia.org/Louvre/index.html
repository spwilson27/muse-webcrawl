The Louvre (Musée du Louvre) is a national art museum in Paris and the world's most-visited museum. Housed in the Louvre Palace on the Right Bank of the Seine, it holds some 35,000 works on display from prehistory to the 21st century over about 72,735 square metres of gallery space. Its best-known treasures include the Mona Lisa, the Venus de Milo, the Winged Victory of Samothrace and the Code of Hammurabi.

History of the palace

The Louvre began as a fortress built by Philip II around 1190; remnants of its medieval keep survive in the basement. Rebuilt as a Renaissance palace under Francis I, who acquired the Mona Lisa, it was expanded by successive monarchs. Louis XIV moved the court to Versailles in 1682, leaving the palace to academies and artists. After the Revolution, the National Assembly decreed it a museum, which opened on 10 August 1793.

Collections

The museum is organized into eight curatorial departments: Egyptian Antiquities; Near Eastern Antiquities; Greek, Etruscan and Roman Antiquities; Islamic Art; Sculpture; Decorative Arts; Paintings; and Prints and Drawings. Napoleon vastly enlarged the collections through conquest — much was later restituted — and the museum continued to grow under the Third Republic and beyond.

The Pyramid and modern era

President François Mitterrand's Grand Louvre project (1981–1999) doubled exhibition space underground and gave the museum its iconic glass pyramid entrance, designed by I. M. Pei and inaugurated in 1989. A sister institution, Louvre Abu Dhabi, opened in 2017. The museum's popularity has led to daily visitor caps and timed ticketing.
