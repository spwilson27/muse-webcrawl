Judah Folkman

Moses Judah Folkman (February 24, 1933 – January 14, 2008) was an American physician and biologist who was a professor at Harvard Medical School and director of the Vascular Biology Program at Boston Children's Hospital. He founded the field of angiogenesis research — the study of the growth of new blood vessels.

Angiogenesis hypothesis

In a landmark 1971 paper in the New England Journal of Medicine, Folkman proposed that tumour growth is angiogenesis-dependent: solid tumours cannot grow beyond a few millimetres without recruiting their own blood supply, so blocking angiogenesis could starve and shrink tumours. The hypothesis was initially met with scepticism but was vindicated as Folkman's laboratory and others isolated angiogenic factors such as bFGF and VEGF and natural inhibitors including angiostatin and endostatin.

Legacy

Anti-angiogenic therapy became a recognised strategy in oncology; the VEGF antibody bevacizumab (Avastin) was approved for cancer treatment in 2004, and related drugs are used against macular degeneration. Folkman's persistence in pursuing an unpopular idea over decades made him a celebrated example of scientific tenacity. He published more than 400 papers and received many honours, including election to the National Academy of Sciences.
