Prebiotic (nutrition)

Prebiotics are compounds in food that induce the growth or activity of beneficial microorganisms such as bacteria and fungi. The most common example is in the gastrointestinal tract, where prebiotics can alter the composition of organisms in the gut microbiome.

The concept was introduced by Glenn Gibson and Marcel Roberfroid in 1995. The modern consensus definition from the International Scientific Association for Probiotics and Prebiotics (ISAPP, 2017) describes a prebiotic as "a substrate that is selectively utilised by host microorganisms conferring a health benefit."

Characteristics

For a substance to act as a prebiotic it must resist digestion and absorption in the upper gastrointestinal tract, be fermentable by the intestinal microflora, and selectively stimulate the growth or activity of beneficial bacteria, particularly bifidobacteria and lactobacilli. Fermentation yields short-chain fatty acids such as butyrate, propionate and acetate, which nourish colonocytes and modulate immunity and metabolism.

Sources

Established prebiotics include fructo-oligosaccharides (FOS), inulin, and galacto-oligosaccharides (GOS). They occur naturally in foods such as chicory root, Jerusalem artichoke, garlic, onions, leeks, asparagus, bananas and barley, and are added to infant formula, yoghurts, cereals and supplements. Candidate prebiotics under study include resistant starch, pectin, beta-glucans and polyphenols.

Health effects and evidence

Prebiotics increase stool bulk and frequency and enhance absorption of minerals such as calcium. Evidence for benefits in irritable bowel syndrome, metabolic health and immune function is growing but mixed, and most health claims are not authorised by regulators such as the EFSA. Excessive doses commonly cause gas, bloating and discomfort. A combination of prebiotics with live probiotic organisms is called a synbiotic.
