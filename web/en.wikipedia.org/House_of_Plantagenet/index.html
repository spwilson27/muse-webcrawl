House of Plantagenet

House of Plantagenet
Country: England, France
Founded: 12th century
Founder: Geoffrey Plantagenet, Count of Anjou
Final ruler: Richard III (d. 1485)

The House of Plantagenet was a royal house which originated from the lands of Anjou in France. The name Plantagenet is used by modern historians to identify four distinct royal houses: the Angevins, who were also Counts of Anjou; the main line of Plantagenets following the loss of Anjou; and the houses of Lancaster and York, the Plantagenets' two competing cadet branches.

Origins

The family name derives from Geoffrey V, Count of Anjou (1113–1151), nicknamed Plantagenet — from the Latin planta genista, the broom plant, which he reputedly wore in his hat as a badge. Geoffrey's son, Henry II, became King of England in 1154, founding the Angevin line and ruling an empire of territories stretching from Scotland to the Pyrenees, including much of France.

The Plantagenet kings

Fourteen Plantagenet kings reigned from Henry II in 1154 to Richard III in 1485. They include Richard I "the Lionheart", John (who sealed Magna Carta in 1215), Edward I, Edward III (who began the Hundred Years' War and claimed the French throne), and Henry V, victor at Agincourt. The dynasty saw the development of Parliament, the common law, and England's wars in Scotland, Wales and France.

Wars of the Roses and fall

In the 15th century the cadet branches of Lancaster and York fought the Wars of the Roses for the throne. The main line ended with the death of Richard III at the Battle of Bosworth Field in 1485, defeated by Henry Tudor, who founded the Tudor dynasty. The last legitimate Plantagenet, Edward, Earl of Warwick, was executed in 1499, and Margaret Pole, Countess of Salisbury — the last direct Plantagenet — was executed in 1541.
