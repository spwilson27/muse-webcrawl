Tubal ligation (commonly known as having one's "tubes tied") is a surgical procedure for female sterilisation in which the fallopian tubes are permanently blocked, clipped, cut or removed, preventing eggs from reaching the uterus and sperm from reaching the egg. It is considered a permanent method of contraception and is one of the most commonly used forms of birth control worldwide.

Procedure

The operation is usually performed laparoscopically under general anaesthesia, often as day surgery, or at the time of a caesarean section or shortly after vaginal delivery. Methods include applying clips or rings, cauterising and cutting a segment of the tube, or complete removal of the tubes (salpingectomy), which is increasingly favoured because it also reduces the risk of ovarian cancer, many cases of which are now thought to originate in the fallopian tube.

Effectiveness

Tubal ligation is highly effective, with a failure rate of less than one percent in the first year. When pregnancy does occur after the procedure, a higher proportion are ectopic. The procedure does not affect hormone production, menstruation or sexual function.

Reversal

Although surgical reversal is sometimes possible, it is expensive, not always successful, and pregnancy is not guaranteed; in-vitro fertilisation is an alternative. For this reason, counselling guidelines emphasise that the procedure should be regarded as permanent.
