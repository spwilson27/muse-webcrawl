The hypophyseal portal system is a system of blood vessels in the microcirculation at the base of the brain connecting the hypothalamus with the anterior pituitary (adenohypophysis). Its main function is the rapid transport of releasing and inhibiting hormones from the hypothalamus to the anterior pituitary, allowing minute quantities of hormone to act on pituitary cells before being diluted in the general circulation.

Anatomy

Branches of the superior hypophyseal arteries form a primary capillary plexus in the median eminence of the hypothalamus. From this plexus, long portal veins descend along the pituitary stalk (infundibulum) to a secondary capillary plexus in the anterior pituitary, where the blood bathes the hormone-secreting cells. Blood then drains into the cavernous sinuses and the systemic circulation. A smaller set of short portal vessels links the posterior pituitary region to the anterior lobe.

Hormones transported

The portal blood delivers thyrotropin-releasing hormone (TRH), corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH), gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH), growth-hormone-releasing hormone (GHRH), and the inhibiting hormones somatostatin and dopamine (which suppresses prolactin secretion) to their target cells in the anterior pituitary. Interruption of the stalk — by tumour, trauma, or surgery — therefore characteristically raises prolactin while reducing the other anterior pituitary hormones.

History

The portal vessels were described anatomically in the 1930s by Gregory Popa and Una Fielding, and their physiological role was established through the work of Geoffrey Harris and others, founding the discipline of neuroendocrinology.
