The Māori (spelled Maori in this article's URL form) are the indigenous Polynesian people of mainland New Zealand (Aotearoa). They originated from East Polynesian settlers who arrived by ocean-going canoes (waka) in waves around 1250–1300 CE. Over centuries they developed a distinctive tribal culture, language (te reo Māori), mythology and art forms, organised into tribes (iwi) and subtribes (hapū).

History

Contact with Europeans began in 1642 with Abel Tasman and intensified after Captain James Cook's voyages from 1769. The Treaty of Waitangi (1840) between the British Crown and Māori chiefs became the founding document of colonial New Zealand, but land confiscations and the New Zealand Wars caused severe population decline by the late 19th century. A cultural and political renaissance from the 1970s advanced Māori language revival, treaty settlements and political representation.

Culture

Traditional arts include carving (whakairo), weaving (raranga), tattooing (tā moko) and the haka, a posture dance famous through New Zealand rugby. The meeting house (wharenui) and marae remain centres of community life. Te reo Māori became an official language in 1987.

Demographics

Māori make up roughly 17–18 percent of New Zealand's population, with significant communities in Australia. The largest iwi include Ngāpuhi, Ngāti Porou, Ngāi Tahu and Waikato-Tainui.
