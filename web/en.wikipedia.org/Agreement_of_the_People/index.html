An Agreement of the People was a series of manifestos, published between 1647 and 1649, proposing a written constitutional settlement for England after the English Civil War. Drafted by the Levellers and the elected Agitators of the New Model Army, the Agreements are among the earliest proposals for a democratic constitution founded on popular sovereignty and reserved individual rights.

Title page of the Agreement of the People as presented to the Council of the Army, 1648.

Contents

The Agreements proposed that political authority derives from the consent of the people, expressed through a foundational document standing above Parliament itself. Their principal demands included:

  • Redistribution of parliamentary seats according to population;
  • Dissolution of the existing Parliament and regular, biennial elections thereafter;
  • A greatly widened franchise — in the first version, extending the vote to most adult men;
  • Freedom of religion, with no power reserved to Parliament to compel religious observance;
  • Equality before the law, freedom from military conscription, and an end to imprisonment for debt;
  • Certain "native rights" reserved to the people that no representative could lawfully abridge.

The Putney Debates

The first Agreement was debated by the Army Council at Putney in October and November 1647. In the famous Putney Debates, Colonel Thomas Rainsborough argued that "the poorest he that is in England hath a life to live, as the greatest he," while Henry Ireton defended a franchise tied to property. No settlement was reached; Oliver Cromwell and the grandees suppressed the more radical army mutinies that followed, notably at Ware in November 1647.

Later versions and legacy

A second, more moderate Agreement was presented to Parliament in December 1648, and a third — drafted by John Lilburne and others from the Tower of London — appeared on 1 May 1649. None was enacted: the Leveller movement was crushed after the mutiny at Burford in May 1649. Historians nevertheless regard the Agreements as landmark documents in democratic thought, anticipating later written constitutions, bills of rights and the idea that fundamental rights limit legislative power.
