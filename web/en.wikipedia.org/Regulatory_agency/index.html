Regulatory agency

Regulatory agencies oversee sectors from food safety to financial markets.

A regulatory agency (also regulatory authority, regulatory body or regulator) is a public authority or government agency responsible for exercising autonomous authority over some area of human activity in a regulatory or supervisory capacity. Regulatory agencies are generally created by statute and empowered to set standards, issue licences, inspect regulated entities, and enforce rules through sanctions ranging from warnings and fines to revocation of permission to operate.

Functions

Typical functions include rulemaking (often through notice-and-comment procedures), licensing and authorisation, monitoring and inspection, enforcement, and adjudication of disputes. Agencies regulate for reasons including consumer protection, public health and safety, environmental protection, financial stability and the promotion of competition where markets alone may not deliver public-interest outcomes.

Independence

Many regulators are designed to be at least partly independent of direct political control — for example the independent agencies of the United States federal government, such as the Securities and Exchange Commission and the Federal Communications Commission, whose commissioners serve fixed terms. Independence is intended to insulate technical decisions from electoral pressures, though critics raise concerns about democratic accountability and "regulatory capture" by the industries overseen.

Examples

Well-known regulators include the U.S. Food and Drug Administration, the Federal Reserve, the UK's Financial Conduct Authority and Ofcom, and the European Medicines Agency. Within countries, regulators may also exist at state or regional level.
