The Great Highland bagpipe (Scottish Gaelic: a' phìob mhòr, "the great pipe") is a type of bagpipe native to Scotland, and the Scottish analogue to the great Irish warpipes. It has acquired widespread recognition through its use in pipe bands of the British military and in civilian bands around the world. The instrument is a wind instrument: air is stored in a bag, kept under pressure by the player's arm, and fed to a double-reed melody pipe (the chanter) and three single-reed drones resting over the shoulder.

Design

The bagpipe comprises a hide or synthetic air reservoir (the bag), a blowpipe through which the piper inflates it, a conical-bored chanter with a double reed that plays nine notes (a mixolydian scale), and three drones — two tenors and one bass, an octave lower — tuned to the chanter's A. Because the chanter sounds continuously, melodic articulation relies on grace notes and other ornaments rather than tonguing or rests. The chanter's pitch has risen over the 20th century to around B♭ or above, considerably sharper than its nominal A.

Music

Highland pipe music divides into ceòl mòr ("great music") — the pibroch, a slow, elaborate art music of theme and variations often commemorating laments, salutes and gatherings — and ceòl beag ("little music"): marches, strathspeys, reels, hornpipes and jigs used for dancing and band repertoire. Classic pibrochs include "Lament for the Children" and "The Big Spree."

History

Bagpipes are ancient in Europe and Asia, but the distinctively Scottish Highland form is documented from the 16th century, with hereditary piping families such as the MacCrimmons of Skye — pipers to MacLeod of Dunvegan — credited with developing ceòl mòr. After the Jacobite defeat of 1746 piping declined in some accounts (the popular claim that it was banned as an "instrument of war" rests largely on one trial case), but regimental piping preserved and codified the tradition in the British Army.

Pipe bands

The modern pipe band — pipers with side, tenor and bass drummers, graded and competing under associations such as the Royal Scottish Pipe Band Association — emerged from military practice. The World Pipe Band Championships, held in Glasgow, draw bands worldwide; leading bands have included the Field Marshal Montgomery, Shotts and Dykehead, Simon Fraser University, and Inveraray & District pipe bands.
