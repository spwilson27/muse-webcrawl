Tribune of the plebs

Plebeian tribunes were sacrosanct protectors of the common people of Rome.

Established494 BC (traditionally)
Number2 originally; 4, then 10 from 457 BC
Key powersVeto (intercessio), auxilium, sacrosanctity
Elected byPlebeian Council (Concilium Plebis)

Tribune of the plebs, tribune of the people or plebeian tribune (Latin: tribunus plebis) was the first office of the Roman state that was open to the plebeians, and was, throughout the history of the Republic, the most important check on the power of the Roman Senate and magistrates.

These tribunes had the power to convene and preside over the Plebeian Council; to summon the Senate; to propose legislation; and to intervene on behalf of plebeians in legal matters, but the most significant power was to veto the actions of consuls and other magistrates, thus protecting the interests of the plebeians. The tribunes of the plebs were sacrosanct, meaning that any assault on their person was prohibited by law and punishable by death, an inviolability guaranteed by a collective oath of the plebs.

History

According to tradition, the office was created in 494 BC after the first secession of the plebs, when the plebeians withdrew from Rome to the Sacred Mount. The number of tribunes rose from two to ten by 457 BC. Over the following two centuries, plebeian pressure won landmark measures such as the Lex Hortensia (287 BC), which made plebiscites binding on all citizens. In the late Republic, tribunes such as Tiberius and Gaius Gracchus used the office to pursue sweeping land and grain reforms, and became central figures in the Republic's political violence. Augustus and later emperors assumed tribunician power (tribunicia potestas) for life, making it a core element of imperial authority and a way of dating imperial reigns.
