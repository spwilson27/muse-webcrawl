Ekman spiral

The Ekman spiral is a structure of currents or winds near a horizontal boundary in which the direction of flow rotates as one moves away from the boundary. It is named after the Swedish oceanographer Vagn Walfrid Ekman, who presented the theory in his 1905 doctoral thesis, building on observations made by Fridtjof Nansen during the Fram expedition: Nansen had noticed that icebergs drifted 20–40° to the right of the wind direction in the Arctic.

Theory

When wind blows over the ocean surface, friction drags the surface layer along, but the Coriolis effect deflects the moving water — to the right in the Northern Hemisphere, to the left in the Southern. Each layer is dragged by the layer above and deflected further, so the current speed decreases with depth while its direction rotates steadily, tracing a spiral. At the base of the Ekman layer (typically 10–100 m deep), the current flows opposite to the surface current and is negligible in magnitude.

Ekman transport

The net transport of the whole Ekman layer is at 90° to the wind direction — to the right of the wind in the Northern Hemisphere. This Ekman transport has major consequences: winds blowing along a coast can push surface water offshore and draw nutrient-rich deep water upward (upwelling), supporting rich fisheries off Peru, California and northwest Africa; conversely, onshore transport causes downwelling. Convergence and divergence of Ekman transport in the open ocean drive Ekman pumping, which helps power the great subtropical ocean gyres.

Atmospheric counterpart

An analogous spiral occurs in the atmosphere near the ground, where friction slows the wind and the flow turns across the isobars toward low pressure with decreasing height; this layer is central to boundary-layer meteorology.
