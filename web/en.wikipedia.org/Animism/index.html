Animism

Animism (from Latin anima, "breath, spirit, life") is the belief that objects, places, and creatures all possess a distinct spiritual essence. Animism perceives all things — animals, plants, rocks, rivers, weather systems, human handiwork, and perhaps even words — as animated and alive, and can be found in many indigenous and folk religious traditions around the world, as well as in aspects of major religions including Shinto and forms of Hinduism and Buddhism.

Origins of the concept

The term was developed by the anthropologist Sir Edward Burnett Tylor in his book Primitive Culture (1871), where he proposed animism as the earliest form of religion: a belief in souls and spirits that he argued arose from attempts to explain dreams, death, and trance experiences. Tylor's evolutionary framework — animism developing into polytheism and then monotheism — was influential but is no longer accepted as a literal history.

Later perspectives

Later scholars reinterpreted animism in less dismissive terms. Irving Hallowell's work with the Ojibwe described "other-than-human persons" with whom social relations are maintained; more recently, anthropologists such as Nurit Bird-David and Philippe Descola have treated animism as a relational ontology — a way of experiencing personhood as extending beyond humans — rather than a failed theory of nature. In this "new animism", the focus is on respectful relationships with the more-than-human world.

Animism in world religions

Animistic elements persist within many established religions: Shinto's veneration of kami inhabiting natural features, Hindu reverence for sacred rivers and trees, folk Catholic veneration of saints and relics, and the phi beliefs of Thailand. Many Indigenous peoples' traditional worldviews, from the Sámi to Aboriginal Australians and Native American nations, are commonly described as animistic.
