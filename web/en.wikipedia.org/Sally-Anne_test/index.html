Sally-Anne test

Sally-Anne test
The doll-play scenario used in the test
PurposeAssess theory of mind / false-belief understanding in children
IntroducedWimmer & Perner (1983); adapted by Baron-Cohen, Leslie & Frith (1985)
FieldsDevelopmental psychology; autism research

The Sally-Anne test is a psychological test used to measure a child's ability to attribute false beliefs to others — a key component of theory of mind. It was adapted by Simon Baron-Cohen, Alan Leslie and Uta Frith in 1985 from an earlier false-belief task by Heinz Wimmer and Josef Perner (1983), and became central to research on autism.

Procedure

The experimenter acts out a story with two dolls. Sally places a marble in her basket and leaves the room. While she is away, Anne moves the marble into her own box. Sally returns, and the child is asked: "Where will Sally look for her marble?" To answer correctly — the basket — the child must understand that Sally holds a belief that is false, i.e. that she does not know the marble was moved. Control questions check memory and reality knowledge ("Where is the marble really? Where was it in the beginning?").

Findings

Typically developing children usually pass from about four years of age. In the landmark 1985 study, most children with Down syndrome passed, while about 80% of autistic children answered with the marble's real location, suggesting a specific delay or difference in theory-of-mind development in autism. The result helped launch the "mindblindness" account of autism.

Criticism and later work

Later research has questioned how cleanly the test isolates belief reasoning: it also demands language, memory and executive control, and some autistic children pass it while some non-autistic children fail. Simpler "implicit" false-belief tasks suggest some sensitivity to others' beliefs appears in infancy, prompting debate about what the Sally-Anne test really measures.
