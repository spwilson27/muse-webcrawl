James Watt

James Watt (30 January 1736 – 25 August 1819) was a Scottish inventor, mechanical engineer and chemist whose improvements to the steam engine were fundamental to the Industrial Revolution. His 1776 steam engine, developed in partnership with Matthew Boulton, used a separate condenser that drastically reduced wasted energy compared with earlier Newcomen engines.

Born: 30 January 1736, Greenock, Scotland

Died: 25 August 1819, Handsworth, England

Known for: Watt steam engine, separate condenser, horsepower

Fields: Mechanical engineering

Life and work

Watt trained as an instrument maker and was employed by the University of Glasgow, where in 1763 he was asked to repair a model Newcomen engine. Recognising the design's inefficiency — the cylinder was repeatedly heated and cooled — he conceived the separate condenser, patented in 1769. In 1775 he entered partnership with Birmingham manufacturer Matthew Boulton; the firm of Boulton & Watt supplied engines to mines, mills and factories across Britain.

Watt's further inventions included the double-acting engine, the sun-and-planet gear, the centrifugal governor, and the concept of horsepower for rating engine output. With William Murdoch he also pioneered chemical bleaching processes. The SI unit of power, the watt, is named in his honour.

Legacy

Watt is widely regarded as one of the most influential engineers in history. His retirement home, Heathfield Hall in Handsworth, preserved his workshop, now displayed at the Science Museum in London.
