Helicobacter pylori

Helicobacter pylori, previously known as Campylobacter pylori, is a gram-negative, flagellated, spiral (helical) bacterium. Mutants can have a rod or curved rod shape, that cause the bacterium to lose its pulmonary function. Its helical body (from which the genus name, Helicobacter, derives) is thought to have evolved in order to penetrate the mucoid lining of the stomach and thereby establish infection. The bacterium was first identified in 1982 by the Australian doctors Barry Marshall and Robin Warren.

Signs and symptoms

Most people infected with H. pylori never experience symptoms or complications. Individuals infected with H. pylori have a 10–20% lifetime risk of developing peptic ulcers and a 1–2% risk of acquiring stomach cancer. Inflammation of the pyloric antrum is more likely to lead to duodenal ulcers, whereas inflammation of the corpus is more likely to lead to gastric ulcers and gastric carcinoma.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis can be made by urea breath test, stool antigen test, blood antibody test, or biopsy during gastroscopy (histology, rapid urease test, or culture).

Treatment

Treatment typically consists of a proton-pump inhibitor combined with two antibiotics (such as clarithromycin and amoxicillin or metronidazole) for one to two weeks, known as triple therapy. Bismuth-containing quadruple therapy is used where clarithromycin resistance is common.

History

Barry Marshall and Robin Warren demonstrated in the early 1980s that many peptic ulcers are caused by H. pylori rather than stress and that antibiotics can cure them. In 1984, Marshall famously drank a broth of the bacterium and developed gastritis, fulfilling Koch's postulates. In 2005, Marshall and Warren were awarded the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for the discovery.
