Levator palpebrae superioris muscle

The levator palpebrae superioris is the muscle in the orbit that elevates the superior (upper) eyelid. It arises from the sphenoid bone, just above the optic foramen, and travels forwards above the superior rectus muscle before broadening into an aponeurosis that inserts into the skin of the upper eyelid and the superior tarsal plate. It is supplied by the superior division of the oculomotor nerve (cranial nerve III). The levator works in opposition to the orbicularis oculi, which closes the eyelid.

Structure

The muscle originates at the apex of the orbit, above the optic canal, and runs forwards along the roof of the orbit. Near the orbital rim its fleshy belly gives way to a broad aponeurosis; the medial and lateral horns of this aponeurosis attach to the orbital bones, and the sheet inserts into the anterior surface of the superior tarsus and the skin of the eyelid, forming the upper lid crease. Closely associated is the superior tarsal muscle (Müller's muscle), a smooth-muscle layer supplied by sympathetic fibres that contributes a small amount of lift to the lid.

Function

By raising the upper eyelid, the levator uncovers the pupil and keeps the visual field clear. Its activity is coordinated with the superior rectus (both share innervation from the superior division of CN III), so the lid rises as the eye looks up. Normal levator function is about 15 mm or more of eyelid excursion from downgaze to upgaze.

Clinical significance

Failure of the levator produces ptosis (drooping of the eyelid). Causes include congenital underdevelopment of the muscle (the commonest cause of congenital ptosis), age-related stretching or dehiscence of the aponeurosis (involutional ptosis), oculomotor nerve palsy, and conditions such as myasthenia gravis and Horner syndrome (the latter affecting Müller's muscle). Ptosis surgery commonly repairs, resects or advances the levator aponeurosis, graded by measured levator function.
