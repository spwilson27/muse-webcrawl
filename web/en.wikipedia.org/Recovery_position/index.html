Recovery position

The recovery position keeps an unconscious casualty's airway clear while allowing fluids to drain from the mouth.

The recovery position refers to a set of positions of the human body in which an unconscious but breathing casualty can be placed as part of first aid treatment. An unconscious person in a supine (face-up) position may not be able to maintain an open airway as a conscious person would: the tongue can fall back and obstruct the airway, and vomit or blood may be aspirated into the lungs. Placing the casualty on their side, with the head supported and tilted so fluids drain from the mouth, greatly reduces these risks.

Technique

Although exact details vary between first-aid authorities, the standard sequence is: kneel beside the casualty, place the nearest arm at a right angle to the body, bring the far arm across the chest and hold the back of the hand against the near cheek, pull up the far knee, and roll the casualty toward you onto their side. The head is then tilted back slightly to keep the airway open, and the bent top leg stabilizes the body. Breathing should be monitored continuously until medical help arrives, and the casualty should not be left alone.

Types and variants

Several variants exist, including the lateral recumbent position taught by ambulance services and the HAINES (High Arm IN Endangered Spine) position, intended to reduce neck movement where spinal injury is suspected. In hospital and surgical practice, related lateral positions are used for procedures and for patients at risk of aspiration. The recovery position should generally not be used if it would aggravate a suspected spinal injury, though maintaining an airway takes priority over spinal concerns in most first-aid guidance.

History

Placing unconscious patients on their side was practised in nineteenth-century medicine, notably by Robert Bowles in treating apoplectic and stertorous breathing; the modern first-aid position was formalized and popularized through twentieth-century first-aid manuals such as those of St John Ambulance and the Red Cross, with periodic revisions by the International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation.
