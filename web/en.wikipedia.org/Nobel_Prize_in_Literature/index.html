The Nobel Prize in Literature is one of the five Nobel Prizes established by Alfred Nobel's 1895 will. It has been awarded annually since 1901 by the Swedish Academy to an author who, in the words of the will, has produced "in the field of literature the most outstanding work in an ideal direction". It is widely regarded as the world's most prestigious literary award.

Selection

Candidates are nominated by academy members, professors of literature, writers' organizations and previous laureates; the Academy's Nobel Committee narrows the field through the spring and summer, and the laureate is announced in early October at the Academy in Stockholm. The prize may honour a single work but usually recognizes a lifetime's achievement; unlike the science prizes it is never shared.

History and controversies

The first laureate was the French poet Sully Prudhomme (1901). The prize has often been criticized as Eurocentric in its early decades; Rabindranath Tagore (1913) was the first non-European laureate. Famous omissions include Tolstoy, Joyce, Proust, Kafka and Borges. Jean-Paul Sartre declined the prize in 1964; Boris Pasternak was forced by Soviet authorities to refuse it in 1958. The award to songwriter Bob Dylan (2016) provoked debate over the boundaries of "literature". In 2018 the prize was postponed amid a scandal in the Swedish Academy, and two prizes were awarded in 2019 (Olga Tokarczuk and Peter Handke), the latter also controversial.
