Seed

A seed is an embryonic plant enclosed in a protective outer covering, called the seed coat. It is the product of the ripened ovule of gymnosperm and angiosperm plants, which occurs after fertilization and some growth within the mother plant. The formation of the seed is part of the process of reproduction in seed plants, the spermatophytes, which include the gymnosperms and flowering plants (angiosperms).

Structure

A typical seed consists of three basic parts: the embryo, the young plant itself, consisting of the radicle (embryonic root), plumule (embryonic shoot), and one or two cotyledons (seed leaves); a supply of nutrients for the embryo, stored either in the cotyledons or in a specialized tissue called the endosperm; and the protective seed coat (testa), formed from the integuments of the ovule. Monocots such as grasses have a single cotyledon, while dicots have two.

Development and dormancy

Seeds develop from fertilized ovules: the zygote forms the embryo while a second fertilization event in angiosperms produces the endosperm. Many seeds enter a period of dormancy, surviving desiccation and unfavourable seasons; some can remain viable for centuries. Germination begins when the seed absorbs water (imbibition), activating metabolism so the radicle emerges.

Dispersal

Seeds are dispersed by wind, water, animals, and explosive mechanisms of the parent plant, often packaged in fruits that attract animals or aid transport. Dispersal reduces competition with the parent and colonizes new habitats.

Importance

Seeds are the basis of most human food: cereals, legumes, nuts, and many oil crops are seeds, and seed-saving underlies agriculture. The largest seed is that of the coco de mer palm, weighing up to about 25 kg, while orchid seeds are dust-like, containing almost no food reserve.
