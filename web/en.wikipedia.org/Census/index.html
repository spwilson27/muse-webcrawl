Census

A census is the procedure of systematically acquiring, recording, and calculating population information about the members of a given population. Modern censuses are typically conducted every ten years by national governments under guidance from the United Nations.

History

Population counts were taken in ancient Egypt, China, and Rome — the Roman census underpinned the office of censor, and the word derives from Latin censere, "to assess". The modern era of censuses began in the 18th and 19th centuries; the United States has held a decennial census since 1790, mandated by its constitution for apportioning seats in the House of Representatives, and Britain has held one since 1801.

Methods

Censuses collect data on residence, age, sex, marital status, occupation, education, and housing. Methods range from door-to-door enumeration to postal and online self-response, and some countries (notably in Scandinavia) compile register-based censuses from administrative records. Results inform government funding, electoral boundaries, and planning; raw schedules are usually released publicly after a long confidentiality period.

Challenges

Undercounting of minorities, homeless people, and migrants is a persistent problem, as are cost, privacy concerns, and declining response rates.
