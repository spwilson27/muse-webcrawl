Edison effect

The Edison effect is the historical name for thermionic emission: the flow of charged particles called thermions from a heated metal surface. Thomas Edison rediscovered and patented the phenomenon in 1883 while investigating why the filaments of his incandescent lamps blackened the glass unevenly.

History

Edison inserted an extra electrode into a lamp bulb and observed that current flowed across the vacuum from the hot filament to a positively charged plate, but not in reverse. He patented this "Edison effect" device (US patent 307,031) — often called the first patent for an electronic device — though he found no practical application for it. John Ambrose Fleming later developed it into the Fleming valve (1904), the first vacuum diode, and Lee de Forest's addition of a grid produced the triode, launching electronics.

The effect was explained by Owen Willans Richardson, whose law (Richardson's law) describes the current density emitted as a function of temperature and work function; Richardson received the 1928 Nobel Prize in Physics.

Applications

Thermionic emission underlies vacuum tubes, cathode-ray tubes, electron microscopes, and thermionic converters. It is also related to the Schottky effect and field emission.
