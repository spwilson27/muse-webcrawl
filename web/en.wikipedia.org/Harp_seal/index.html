Harp seal

KingdomAnimalia
OrderCarnivora
FamilyPhocidae
GenusPagophilus
SpeciesP. groenlandicus
Conservation statusLeast Concern (IUCN)

The harp seal (Pagophilus groenlandicus), also known as the saddleback seal, is a species of earless seal native to the northernmost Atlantic Ocean and parts of the Arctic Ocean. Adults are recognizable by the black harp- or wishbone-shaped marking on the back of their otherwise light grey fur, and by their black face. The species name groenlandicus means "from Greenland."

Description

Adult harp seals grow to about 1.7 to 2.0 metres (5.6 to 6.6 ft) in length and weigh 115 to 140 kilograms (254 to 309 lb). Males are slightly larger than females and have a more pronounced harp marking. Pups are born with a soft white coat, called lanugo, which led to their being known as "whitecoats"; the coat is moulted after about two weeks, passing through stages nicknamed "raggedjackets" and, in juveniles with spotted silver-grey fur, "beaters" and spotted "bedlamers."

Distribution and migration

Harp seals are divided into three populations: one in the White Sea and Barents Sea, one in the northwest Atlantic off Canada and Greenland (the largest, migrating between the Canadian Arctic and the Gulf of St. Lawrence and Newfoundland), and one near Jan Mayen in the Greenland Sea. They are highly migratory, travelling thousands of kilometres annually between feeding areas in subarctic waters and breeding grounds on pack ice.

Life history

Females haul out on sea ice in late February and March to give birth, usually to a single pup. Pups nurse on extremely fat-rich milk for about 12 days, growing from roughly 11 kg at birth to around 36 kg at weaning, after which the mother mates again and leaves. Females reach sexual maturity at about five to six years. Harp seals feed on small fish such as capelin, Arctic cod and herring, and on crustaceans including krill and amphipods. Their principal predators include polar bears, orcas, Greenland sharks and humans.

Hunting and conservation

Harp seals have long been hunted for their fur, meat and oil, most notably in the annual Canadian commercial seal hunt off Newfoundland and in the Gulf of St. Lawrence, which has attracted sustained international protest since the 1970s. The import of whitecoat pelts was banned in the European Economic Community in the 1980s, and the European Union later banned most seal products. The northwest Atlantic population has been estimated at several million animals, and the species is listed as Least Concern by the IUCN, though declining sea ice due to climate change is considered a long-term threat to breeding habitat.
