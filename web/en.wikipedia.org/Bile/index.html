Bile

Bile or gall is a dark-green-to-yellowish-brown fluid produced by the liver of most vertebrates that aids the digestion of lipids in the small intestine. In humans, bile is produced continuously by the liver and stored and concentrated in the gallbladder. After eating, this stored bile is discharged into the first section of the small intestine, the duodenum.

Composition

Bile is composed of about 95% water and 5% dissolved solutes, including bile salts (bile acids conjugated with glycine or taurine), bilirubin (the pigment derived from the breakdown of haem, which also gives faeces their brown colour), phospholipids (mainly lecithin), cholesterol, and electrolytes. Hepatic bile has a pH of about 7.6–8.6; gallbladder bile becomes more concentrated and slightly less alkaline.

Function

Bile salts emulsify dietary fat globules into smaller droplets, greatly increasing the surface area available to pancreatic lipase, and they form micelles that carry the products of fat digestion — fatty acids, monoglycerides, cholesterol, and fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) — to the intestinal wall for absorption. Bile is also the main route for excretion of bilirubin, cholesterol, and many drugs and toxins cleared by the liver.

Enterohepatic circulation

About 95% of bile salts secreted into the gut are reabsorbed in the terminal ileum and returned to the liver via the portal vein, a recycling process called the enterohepatic circulation. The small fraction lost in faeces is replaced by new synthesis from cholesterol in the liver.

Clinical significance

Disorders of bile production or flow cause cholestasis and jaundice. Cholesterol or pigment stones forming in the gallbladder (gallstones) can obstruct the cystic or common bile duct, causing biliary colic, cholecystitis, cholangitis, or obstructive jaundice. In humoral medicine, yellow bile (Greek cholē) and black bile were two of the four humours.
