Content analysis

Content analysis systematically codes texts and media to identify patterns.

Content analysis is a research method for the systematic, objective study of texts and other recorded communication, including books, newspapers, speeches, television programmes, advertisements, websites, and social media posts. By coding material into categories according to explicit rules, researchers can quantify patterns in content and draw inferences about producers, audiences, and cultural trends.

History and procedure

The method was formalised in the mid-twentieth century, notably by Bernard Berelson, who defined it in 1952 as the "objective, systematic, and quantitative description of the manifest content of communication", and it was used extensively in wartime propaganda analysis. A typical study samples material, defines a coding scheme reflecting the research question, trains coders, checks inter-coder reliability, and analyses the resulting frequencies and associations statistically. Later scholars such as Klaus Krippendorff broadened the approach to include inferences about context and meaning.

Quantitative and qualitative forms

Quantitative content analysis counts predetermined categories, for example the frequency of violence in television drama. Qualitative content analysis interprets latent themes and rhetoric more inductively. Computer-assisted methods, from word-frequency analysis to modern natural language processing and machine learning, now allow very large corpora, such as millions of news articles or posts, to be coded automatically, though questions of validity and interpretation remain.

Applications

Content analysis is widely used in media studies, political science, public health, and marketing, for example to track stereotypes in advertising, agenda-setting in news coverage, or misinformation online.
