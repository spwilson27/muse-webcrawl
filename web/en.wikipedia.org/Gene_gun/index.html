Gene gun

A gene gun, or particle delivery system, is a device used to deliver exogenous DNA (transgenes), RNA, or protein into cells by means of heavy-metal microparticles. The technique, known as biolistics (from "biological ballistics"), was originally developed for transforming plant cells, whose rigid cell walls make many other delivery methods difficult.

Design and operation

In a gene gun experiment, DNA is coated onto microscopic particles of gold or tungsten, typically about one micrometre in diameter. The particles are loaded onto a carrier and accelerated to high velocity by a burst of compressed helium gas (or, in early designs, a gunpowder charge or electric discharge), firing them into target tissue placed in a partial vacuum chamber. Particles that penetrate cells deposit their DNA cargo, which can then be expressed by the cell or integrated into its genome.

Applications

Biolistics was a breakthrough for plant genetic engineering because it works on species and tissues that resist transformation by Agrobacterium, including important cereals such as maize and wheat. It remains especially useful for transforming plastids (chloroplasts), which other methods reach poorly. The method is also used in animal research, for example to deliver DNA vaccines into skin, where the particles directly transfect epidermal cells and provoke an immune response. Its disadvantages include relatively low and variable transformation efficiency, multiple-copy insertions, and physical damage to target tissue compared with biological delivery methods.

History

The first gene gun was developed in the mid-1980s by John C. Sanford, Edward Wolf, and Nelson Allen at Cornell University, and was commercialised by DuPont and later Bio-Rad as the PDS-1000/He particle delivery system, a helium-driven hand-held and benchtop device still widely used in laboratories.
