Free energy principle

The free energy principle is a theoretical framework in neuroscience and theoretical biology, developed principally by Karl Friston, which proposes that self-organising systems — from cells to brains — maintain their integrity by minimising a quantity called variational free energy, an information-theoretic bound on surprise (negative log evidence for a system's model of its world).

Core ideas

According to the principle, a biological system resists disorder by confining itself to a limited set of states consistent with its survival, and it does so by acting on the world and updating internal states so as to make its sensory observations unsurprising under its generative model. Perception corresponds to updating beliefs to reduce free energy ("predictive coding" under the principle), while action ("active inference") changes the sensory data themselves to fit predictions. Variational free energy is an upper bound on surprise that a system can compute and minimise locally, making the framework a candidate processing theory for the whole brain.

Scope and criticism

Proponents present the principle as a unifying theory linking homeostasis, perception, learning, attention, and decision-making, with applications in computational psychiatry and artificial intelligence. Critics contend that in its most general form the principle is difficult to falsify, that key quantities cannot be measured directly in real brains, and that some of its empirical successes belong more narrowly to predictive coding models. It remains an active and contested research program.
