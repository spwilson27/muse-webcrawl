Ecological resilience

In ecology, resilience is the capacity of an ecosystem to respond to a perturbation or disturbance by resisting damage and recovering quickly. The concept was introduced into ecological literature by Canadian ecologist C. S. Holling in 1973 and has since become central to the study of ecosystem management, sustainability and social–ecological systems.

Definitions

Holling distinguished ecological resilience from the engineering notion of resilience as the speed of return to a single equilibrium. Ecological resilience instead measures the amount of disturbance an ecosystem can absorb before it shifts into a different stable state — a regime shift — governed by different processes and structures. A commonly used definition describes resilience as "the capacity of a system to absorb disturbance and reorganize while undergoing change so as to still retain essentially the same function, structure, identity, and feedbacks."

Alternative stable states

Many ecosystems can exist in more than one stable configuration. Shallow lakes, for example, may be clear and plant-dominated or turbid and algae-dominated; coral reefs may shift to algal reefs after overfishing and disease. Once a threshold is crossed, returning to the original state can be difficult or impossible, a phenomenon known as hysteresis.

Resilience and biodiversity

Biodiversity is thought to contribute to resilience through functional redundancy and response diversity: if one species declines after a disturbance, others with similar roles can compensate. Loss of diversity can therefore erode the resilience of an ecosystem and make regime shifts more likely.

Applications

Resilience thinking is applied in fisheries and forest management, disaster planning, climate adaptation and urban design. The Stockholm Resilience Centre and the Resilience Alliance promote research integrating ecological and social dimensions of resilience.
