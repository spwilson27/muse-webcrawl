A tropical cyclone is a rapidly rotating storm system characterised by a low-pressure centre, a closed low-level atmospheric circulation, strong winds, and a spiral arrangement of thunderstorms producing heavy rain and squalls. Depending on its location and strength, it is called a hurricane (in the Atlantic and Northeast Pacific), a typhoon (Northwest Pacific), or simply a tropical cyclone (South Pacific and Indian Ocean).

Structure and formation

A mature tropical cyclone has a calm central eye, typically 30–65 km across, ringed by the eyewall where the strongest winds and heaviest rain occur, with spiral rainbands extending outward. Cyclones form over ocean waters of at least about 26.5 °C, where abundant moisture, low wind shear, and the Coriolis effect (absent at the equator) allow organised convection to consolidate around a disturbance. The strongest observed, such as Typhoon Tip (1979), reached minimum pressures near 870 hPa; the most intense Atlantic hurricane by pressure was Wilma (2005), at 882 hPa.

Classification and impacts

Systems are classified by maximum sustained wind: tropical depression, tropical storm (when names are assigned), and hurricane/typhoon strength, rated Category 1–5 on the Saffir–Simpson scale in the Atlantic. Tropical cyclones are among the most destructive natural disasters: the 1970 Bhola cyclone in present-day Bangladesh killed perhaps 300,000–500,000 people, mostly by storm surge. Landfalling storms cause wind damage, catastrophic storm surge, inland flooding, and spawned tornadoes; weakening follows over land or cold water, though remnants can transition into powerful extratropical cyclones.
