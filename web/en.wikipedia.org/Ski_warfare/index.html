Ski warfare

Ski warfare is the use of ski-equipped soldiers in war, practised wherever armies must operate in deep snow and severe cold. Ski troops combine the mobility of skis with infantry combat, often wearing white camouflage and pulling supply sleds.

History

Military use of skis is recorded in Scandinavia as early as the Danish–Swedish wars of the 16th–18th centuries; legend even credits ski-borne messengers with saving the infant Norwegian prince Håkon Håkonsson in 1206 (the Birkebeiners). In the First World War, Alpine and ski units fought in the Italian Alps and the Carpathians. Ski troops became famous in the Winter War of 1939–40, in which Finnish ski infantry, using encirclement tactics (motti), inflicted heavy losses on Soviet forces, most famously at the Battle of Suomussalmi and the Raate Road.

Modern ski troops

Several countries maintain specialist winter and mountain units, including Norway, Sweden, Finland, Italy's Alpini, France's Chasseurs Alpins, and the mountain warfare schools of the United States and other NATO states. Training emphasizes cross-country skiing while hauling equipment, cold-weather survival, and operations in arctic conditions. Military ski patrols evolved into the modern biathlon, which began as a military exercise combining skiing and shooting.
