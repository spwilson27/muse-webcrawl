Organic chemistry

Organic chemistry is a subdiscipline within chemistry involving the scientific study of the structure, properties and reactions of organic compounds and organic materials, i.e. matter in its various forms that contain carbon atoms. Study of structure determines their composition and chemical formula, study of properties includes physical and chemical properties and evaluation of reactivity, and study of organic reactions includes synthesis of target compounds. Because of carbon's ability to catenate (form chains and rings with other carbon atoms) and to bond with hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen and other elements, the number of known organic compounds runs into the tens of millions.

History

Organic chemistry was originally defined as the chemistry of compounds from living organisms, under the doctrine of vitalism. In 1828 Friedrich Wöhler synthesised urea from inorganic ammonium cyanate, undermining vitalism. Structural theory followed in the 1850s–60s with Kekulé, Couper and Butlerov; van 't Hoff and Le Bel proposed tetrahedral carbon in 1874; and the 20th century added electronic theories of bonding and reaction mechanisms (Robinson, Ingold), conformational analysis, and spectroscopic identification. Woodward's total syntheses (e.g. vitamin B12, with Eschenmoser) and later catalysis — including cross-couplings and asymmetric catalysis rewarded by multiple Nobel Prizes — made organic synthesis a mature creative discipline.

Classification and concepts

Organic compounds are classified by functional groups — alcohols, aldehydes, ketones, carboxylic acids, amines, alkenes, alkynes and aromatic rings among others — which largely determine chemical behaviour. Hydrocarbons are divided into alkanes, alkenes, alkynes and arenes. Core concepts include isomerism and stereochemistry, resonance, acidity and basicity, nucleophiles and electrophiles, and named reaction mechanisms drawn with curved arrows.

Applications

Organic chemistry underpins the pharmaceutical, petrochemical, polymer, dye, agrochemical and food industries, and interfaces with biochemistry and materials science. Techniques such as NMR and IR spectroscopy, mass spectrometry and chromatography are its everyday analytical tools.
