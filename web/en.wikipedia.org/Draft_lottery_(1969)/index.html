Draft lottery (1969)

The draft lottery of 1969 was the first random selection method used by the United States Selective Service System to determine the order in which men were called for military service during the Vietnam War. Held on December 1, 1969, it assigned a random sequence number (RSN) to every day of the year; men born between 1944 and 1950 were called in order of the number assigned to their birthdate. It replaced the older "oldest first" system, which had left men in uncertain limbo for years.

The drawing

366 blue plastic capsules, each containing a slip of paper with one date of the year (including February 29), were placed in a large glass bowl, stirred, and drawn one at a time on live television and radio. The first capsule drawn, by Congressman Alexander Pirnie of New York, contained September 14, which received random sequence number 001; the last drawn was June 8 (366). In 1970, men in the pool were called for induction in number order up to 195.

Statistical controversy

The 1969 drawing was soon criticised as insufficiently random: statisticians showed that birthdays later in the year received systematically lower (earlier-called) numbers, because capsules were added month by month and inadequately mixed, so December dates tended to be drawn early. A famous analysis published in Science in 1971 confirmed the bias. For the 1970 and later lotteries officials used a double-randomisation procedure — two drums, one for dates and one for numbers, prepared with the help of the National Bureau of Standards — to ensure genuine randomness.

Subsequent lotteries and legacy

Further lotteries were held in 1970, 1971, and 1972 covering new cohorts, though draft calls dwindled as the U.S. withdrew from Vietnam; no one was inducted from the 1972 drawing, and induction authority lapsed in 1973 as the military moved to an all-volunteer force. The lottery remains a vivid cultural memory of the Vietnam era, dramatising the randomness of conscription for a generation of American men.
