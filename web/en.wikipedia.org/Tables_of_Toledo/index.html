Tables of Toledo

The Tables of Toledo (also called the Toledan Tables) were astronomical tables used to predict the movements of the Sun, Moon, and planets relative to the fixed stars. They were compiled around 1080 by a group of astronomers working in Toledo, in the Taifa of Toledo in Islamic Iberia (al-Andalus), under the patronage of the emir Yahya al-Qadir.

A medieval manuscript of planetary tables in the Toledan tradition

Origins

The tables drew heavily on the earlier work of the Persian astronomer al-Khwarizmi and the Andalusian astronomer al-Zarqali (known in Latin as Arzachel), whose observations at Toledo provided much of the empirical basis. They embodied the Ptolemaic model of the universe, using geometrical models with deferents and epicycles to compute planetary positions for the meridian of Toledo.

Influence

Translated into Latin in the 12th century—Gerard of Cremona, one of the most prolific translators of the Toledo School of Translators, rendered them accessible to European scholars—the Toledan Tables became the most widely used astronomical tables in Christian Europe for several centuries. They remained in circulation until they were gradually superseded by the Alfonsine tables, compiled in the 13th century under Alfonso X of Castile, which were themselves based partly on the Toledan material.

Contents

The tables included instructions (canons) for computing planetary longitudes, tables of mean motions, equations for correcting mean positions to true positions, eclipse tables, and trigonometric tables of sines. Surviving manuscripts preserve them in numerous versions, reflecting adaptations by later copyists and astronomers to other meridians and epochs.
