Independent suspension

Independent suspension is any automobile suspension system that allows each wheel on the same axle to move vertically (i.e., reacting to a bump on the road) independently of the others. This is contrasted with a beam axle or live axle system in which the wheels are linked. In most modern vehicles, the front wheels are independently suspended; many also have independent rear suspension (IRS).

Advantages

Independent suspension typically offers better ride quality and handling characteristics, because of lower unsprung weight and the ability of each wheel to follow the road surface without disturbing the opposite wheel. Camber and toe angles can be controlled through the suspension's travel, improving tyre contact and grip. Disadvantages include higher cost, greater complexity, and, in some designs, changes in track width or camber under load.

Types

Common types of independent suspension include the MacPherson strut (widely used at the front of modern cars for its compactness and low cost), double wishbone suspension (favoured for performance applications), multi-link suspension, trailing arm and semi-trailing arm designs, and the swing axle used in some early designs. Transverse leaf spring arrangements also served as independent front suspension on some historic vehicles.

History

The concept dates to the earliest automobiles, but it became widespread in the 1930s with cars such as the Mercedes-Benz 380 and the Citroën Traction Avant; Chevrolet introduced independent front suspension ("knee-action") in 1934. Independent rear suspension appeared on production cars such as the 1961 Jaguar E-Type and became common on passenger cars from the 1980s onward.
