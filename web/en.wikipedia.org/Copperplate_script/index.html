Copperplate script
TypeCalligraphic script / handwriting style
PeriodDeveloped 17th–18th centuries; flourished 19th century

Copperplate is a style of calligraphic writing and a class of typefaces that imitate it. The name comes from the copperplate engravings used to print the copybooks from which the hand was taught: the engraved exemplars of English "roundhand" writing masters of the 17th and 18th centuries defined the style, which is characterised by dramatic contrast between thick downstrokes — produced by pressure on a flexible pointed nib — and hairline upstrokes, elegant oval forms, and a consistent slant of about 54–55 degrees.

Characteristics and practice

Copperplate is written with a pointed pen, traditionally held in an oblique holder so the nib aligns with the slant. Letterforms are built from a small set of basic strokes (underturns, overturns, compound curves) joined on a baseline; capital letters are large, looped, and often heavily flourished. The script dominated formal and commercial handwriting in the English-speaking world in the 19th century and was taught widely in business colleges. In the United States, related hands such as Spencerian script and the later Palmer Method adapted its principles for everyday writing.

Copperplate typefaces

Typefaces named "Copperplate" — most famously Copperplate Gothic (Frederic W. Goudy, 1901, for American Type Founders) — are not scripts but small-capital serif designs with subtly flared serifs, imitating the look of engraved lettering on copper plates; they are widely used for stationery, signage, and film credits.
