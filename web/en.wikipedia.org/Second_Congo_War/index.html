Second Congo War

Date2 August 1998 – 18 July 2003
LocationDemocratic Republic of the Congo
CombatantsDRC and allies (incl. Angola, Zimbabwe, Namibia) vs. Rwandan- and Ugandan-backed rebel movements
ResultTransitional government; Sun City Agreement

The Second Congo War, also known as the Great War of Africa, began on 2 August 1998 in the Democratic Republic of the Congo and formally ended in July 2003. It was the largest war in modern African history, directly involving nine African nations and some twenty-five armed groups, and — through its aftermath of disease, displacement and malnutrition — it is considered the deadliest conflict worldwide since the Second World War, with excess deaths estimated in the millions (one widely cited survey put the figure at around 5.4 million between 1998 and 2007).

Background

The war grew out of the First Congo War (1996–97), in which Rwanda and Uganda backed Laurent-Désiré Kabila's rebels in overthrowing the long-serving dictator Mobutu Sese Seko. Within a year Kabila fell out with his foreign sponsors; in July 1998 he ordered Rwandan and Ugandan troops out of the country. Rwanda and Uganda responded by backing a new rebellion, the Rally for Congolese Democracy (RCD), based in the east. Zimbabwe, Angola, Namibia and others intervened on Kabila's side, turning a regional rebellion into a continental war.

Course of the war

Rebel and Rwandan forces advanced rapidly across the country and nearly took Kinshasa before Angolan and Zimbabwean intervention saved the government. Thereafter the war hardened into a stalemate, with the front line dividing the country: government forces and their allies held the west, while Rwanda and Uganda (which themselves fought each other at Kisangani in 1999–2000) and rival rebel movements controlled the mineral-rich east, where exploitation of coltan, gold, diamonds and timber financed the fighting. The Lusaka Ceasefire Agreement of 1999 was never fully implemented. Laurent Kabila was assassinated in January 2001 and succeeded by his son Joseph Kabila, whose accession revived diplomacy. The Inter-Congolese Dialogue produced the Sun City Agreement (2002–03), Rwanda withdrew its forces, and in July 2003 a transitional government of national unity took office, formally ending the war.

Aftermath

Although the war officially ended, armed violence has continued in the eastern provinces, notably in Ituri and the Kivus, involving militias and, in the 2020s, the resurgent M23 movement. The conflict was marked by mass atrocities, the systematic use of sexual violence, and the recruitment of child soldiers; the United Nations peacekeeping mission MONUC (later MONUSCO) became one of the largest in UN history.
