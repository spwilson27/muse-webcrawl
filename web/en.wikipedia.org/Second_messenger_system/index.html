Second messenger system

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Second messengers relay and amplify signals from cell-surface receptors to intracellular targets.

Second messengers are intracellular signaling molecules released by cells in response to exposure to extracellular signaling molecules — the first messengers. Second messenger systems amplify the strength of a signal received at a receptor on the cell surface and relay it to target molecules inside the cell, in the cytosol or nucleus, producing a physiological response such as enzyme activation, altered gene expression, or ion channel opening.

Discovery

The concept originated with Earl Wilbur Sutherland Jr., who in the 1950s discovered cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) while studying how epinephrine stimulates glycogen breakdown in the liver. Sutherland received the Nobel Prize in 1971, and cAMP became the prototype of a second messenger.

Common second messengers

  • Cyclic nucleotides — cyclic AMP (cAMP) and cyclic GMP (cGMP), produced by adenylyl and guanylyl cyclases.
  • Calcium ions (Ca2+) — released from the endoplasmic reticulum or entering through channels, regulating proteins such as calmodulin and protein kinase C.
  • Inositol phosphates — inositol trisphosphate (IP3) and diacylglycerol (DAG), generated by phospholipase C cleavage of membrane phospholipids.
  • Gases — nitric oxide (NO), which diffuses between cells and activates guanylyl cyclase.

Mechanism

Typically, a first messenger such as a hormone or neurotransmitter binds a receptor — often a G protein-coupled receptor — which activates an enzyme that synthesizes the second messenger. Because each activated enzyme produces many messenger molecules, the signal is greatly amplified in a cascade. The response is terminated by enzymes such as phosphodiesterases, which degrade cyclic nucleotides, and by pumps that restore calcium to resting levels.
