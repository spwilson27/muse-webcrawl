Renewable energy

Renewable energy, also known as clean energy, is energy derived from natural sources that are replenished at a higher rate than they are consumed, such as sunlight, wind, the movement of water, and geothermal heat. Renewable energy sources are plentiful and all around us; by contrast, fossil fuels — coal, oil and gas — are non-renewable resources that take hundreds of millions of years to form and release harmful greenhouse gas emissions when burned.

Main sources

Solar energy is captured by photovoltaic panels or concentrated solar power. Wind energy uses turbines onshore and offshore. Hydropower, the largest and oldest renewable electricity source, exploits falling or flowing water. Geothermal energy taps heat from the Earth's interior for power and heating. Bioenergy comes from organic material, and ocean energy includes tidal and wave power, still at an early stage of development.

Growth and economics

Costs of solar modules and wind turbines fell dramatically in the 2010s, making renewables the cheapest source of new electricity in most of the world. Global renewable capacity additions have set records year after year, led by China. Renewables supplied around 30% of global electricity in the early 2020s, a share expected to keep rising as electrification of transport and heating proceeds.

Integration and challenges

Wind and solar are variable ("intermittent") sources, so high penetrations require grid flexibility: energy storage (batteries, pumped hydro), interconnection, demand response and firm low-carbon capacity. Other issues include land use, supply chains for minerals such as lithium and rare earths, and permitting.

Policy

Support policies have included feed-in tariffs, auctions, tax credits (such as the U.S. Inflation Reduction Act of 2022), renewable portfolio standards and net metering. The Paris Agreement and national net-zero targets provide the overarching framework for deployment as part of the energy transition.
