Trade deficit

A trade deficit is an economic measure of international trade in which a country's imports exceed the value of its exports. A trade deficit represents an outflow of domestic currency to foreign markets and is one component of the current account; the opposite condition is a trade surplus. It is sometimes referred to as a negative balance of trade (BOT).

Measurement and causes

The balance of trade covers goods and, in broader measures, services. Deficits can arise from a strong currency making imports cheap, high domestic consumption and low savings, comparative disadvantage in manufacturing, or strong economic growth that draws in foreign goods and capital. In accounting terms, a current-account deficit is matched by a capital-account surplus: foreigners invest the difference in the country's assets, such as bonds, shares, and property.

Economic debate

Economists disagree about whether trade deficits are harmful. The mercantilist tradition, and critics of persistent deficits, point to lost manufacturing jobs and industrial capacity. Mainstream economic analysis generally holds that deficits are not inherently good or bad: they may reflect attractive investment opportunities and can raise living standards through cheaper and more varied goods, but large persistent deficits financed by borrowing can create vulnerabilities. Bilateral deficits (between two particular countries) are widely regarded by economists as poor indicators of policy distortions, since trade patterns depend on supply chains and comparative advantage.

Examples

The United States has run annual trade deficits since the 1970s, reaching several hundred billion dollars per year in goods and services, while countries such as China and Germany have run large surpluses in many recent years.
