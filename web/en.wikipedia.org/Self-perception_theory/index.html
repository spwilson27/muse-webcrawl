Self-perception theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Self-perception theory holds that people infer their attitudes by observing their own behavior.

Self-perception theory is an account of attitude formation developed by psychologist Daryl Bem in the late 1960s and early 1970s. It asserts that people develop their attitudes — where there is no previous attitude due to a lack of experience or ambiguity — by observing their own behavior and concluding what attitudes must have caused it. People, in other words, are "observers" of their own behavior much as they are of others'.

Relation to cognitive dissonance

Bem proposed self-perception as a simpler alternative explanation for many findings attributed to Leon Festinger's cognitive dissonance theory, including the classic induced-compliance experiments in which participants paid little to praise a boring task reported liking it more. Bem argued — and showed with "interpersonal simulation" studies — that outside observers given only descriptions of the actors' behavior drew the same conclusions, suggesting no aversive dissonance state was needed. Later research concluded that both processes operate: dissonance explains attitude change when prior attitudes are strong and contradicted, while self-perception explains change when attitudes are weak or ambiguous.

Applications and evidence

The theory helps explain the overjustification effect, in which providing external rewards for an intrinsically enjoyable activity reduces later interest — because people attribute their behavior to the reward — and the foot-in-the-door effect, in which agreeing to a small request increases compliance with a larger one, as people infer they are the kind of person who helps. Self-perception has been applied in marketing, therapy, and research on emotion, where facial feedback studies test whether expressive behavior shapes felt attitudes.
