Logos

The Greek word λόγος (logos)

Logos (UK: /ˈloʊɡɒs/, US: /ˈloʊɡoʊs/; Ancient Greek: λόγος, romanized: lógos, lit. "word, discourse, or reason") is a term in Western philosophy, psychology and rhetoric, and in Christian theology, with a wide range of meanings. It derives from the verb legō, "to speak, to count, to reckon," and can signify speech, account, reason, proportion, and the rational principle governing the universe.

In Greek philosophy

Heraclitus (c. 535–475 BCE) used logos for the universal rational order underlying the constant flux of the world — the law "according to which all things come to pass." The Stoics developed this into a cosmic principle: the active, divine reason that orders matter, identified with God and fate, and present in human beings as a fragment (the logos spermatikos).

Aristotle, in his Rhetoric, defined logos as one of the three modes of persuasion, alongside ethos (the speaker's character) and pathos (emotion): argument from logos appeals to reason and the internal consistency of the argument itself.

In religion

In the Hellenistic Judaism of Philo of Alexandria, the Logos is God's intermediary creative and governing power. The opening of the Gospel of John — "In the beginning was the Word (Logos), and the Word was with God, and the Word was God" — identifies the Logos with Jesus Christ, a doctrine central to Christian theology and the source of the field's name, Christology.

Modern uses

The term survives in the suffix -logy ("the study of") and in concepts such as Carl Jung's contrast of logos (rational, masculine principle) with eros, and in contemporary rhetoric as the appeal to logic and evidence.
