The Siege of Leningrad was a prolonged military blockade undertaken by the Axis powers against the Soviet city of Leningrad (now Saint Petersburg) during the Eastern Front of World War II. Lasting 872 days, from 8 September 1941 to 27 January 1944, it was one of the longest and most destructive sieges in history, and is regarded as one of the deadliest, with civilian deaths from starvation, cold, and bombardment commonly estimated at around one million or more.

The blockade

German Army Group North, commanded by Field Marshal Wilhelm Ritter von Leeb, cut the last land connection to the city on 8 September 1941, while Finnish forces held the northern approaches. Hitler ordered that the city be starved into submission rather than stormed. Rations for civilians fell, in the terrible winter of 1941–42, to as little as 125 grams of bread per day for dependants.

The Road of Life

The only supply route was across Lake Ladoga — by boat in summer and over the ice "Road of Life" in winter — which brought in food and evacuated hundreds of thousands of civilians, though never in sufficient quantity. Soviet offensives gradually loosened the blockade: Operation Iskra opened a narrow land corridor in January 1943, and the Leningrad–Novgorod Offensive fully lifted the siege on 27 January 1944.

Legacy

The siege became a central symbol of Soviet endurance. Dmitri Shostakovich's Seventh Symphony, written largely in the besieged city and performed there in August 1942 by starved musicians, embodied its cultural resistance. Leningrad was among the first cities named a Hero City of the Soviet Union.
