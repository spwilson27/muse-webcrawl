Corona

A corona (plural coronae or coronas) is the outermost layer of a star's atmosphere. The Sun's corona extends millions of kilometres into outer space and is most easily seen during a total solar eclipse, when it appears as an irregularly shaped glow surrounding the darkened solar disc. Its name is the Latin word for "crown", from the Ancient Greek korōnē, meaning a garland or wreath.

The corona during the total solar eclipse of 2017.

Physical characteristics

The corona is composed of plasma at extremely high temperatures — roughly 1 to 3 million kelvin — yet at exceedingly low density, around a trillion times thinner than Earth's atmosphere at sea level. Because it is so hot, it emits strongly in X-rays, but because it is so tenuous, its visible light is about a million times fainter than the Sun's surface and is normally overwhelmed.

The coronal heating problem

The corona is several hundred times hotter than the photosphere beneath it (about 5,800 K), an apparent paradox that has driven decades of research. Proposed mechanisms include the dissipation of Alfvén waves generated by convection, and countless tiny flares called nanoflares produced by magnetic reconnection, as advanced by Eugene Parker. NASA's Parker Solar Probe, launched in 2018, has flown through the outer corona to sample it directly, crossing the Alfvén surface in 2021.

Structure

The corona is shaped by the Sun's magnetic field into helmet streamers over active regions, polar plumes, and coronal holes — cooler, open-field regions that are the source of the fast solar wind. Coronal loops, great arches of plasma following magnetic field lines, are seen in extreme ultraviolet images. Violent coronal mass ejections can expel billions of tonnes of plasma into space and disturb Earth's magnetosphere.

Observation

Coronae can be studied during eclipses, with coronagraphs that artificially occult the solar disc (an instrument invented by Bernard Lyot in the 1930s), and from space telescopes such as SOHO and the Solar Dynamics Observatory. Spectroscopy of coronal emission lines historically revealed highly ionised iron — once attributed to a fictitious element "coronium" before Bengt Edlén showed in the 1940s that the lines came from iron stripped of half its electrons, proving the corona's million-degree temperature.
