The morin khuur (Mongolian: морин хуур, "horse fiddle"), also known as the horsehead fiddle, is a traditional Mongolian bowed stringed instrument. It is one of the most important musical instruments of the Mongol people, and is considered a symbol of the nation of Mongolia. The morin khuur is one of the Masterpieces of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity identified by UNESCO.

Description

The instrument consists of a trapezoidal wooden-framed sound box to which two strings are attached. The strings are made of hair from a horse tail, strung between a carved wooden scroll — almost always in the shape of a horse's head — and the body. It is played with a bow also strung with horsehair, held in an underhand grip. The two strings are traditionally tuned a fifth apart, and the player stops the strings with the knuckles and fingernails of the left hand rather than pressing them to a fingerboard.

Legend

A popular legend tells of a shepherd named Namjil the Cuckoo, who received a gift of a magical winged horse. A jealous woman had the horse's wings cut off, and the horse fell from the sky and died. The grieving shepherd made a fiddle from the horse's skin, bones, and hair, and played songs about his beloved steed — explaining the horse's head carved on the instrument and its neighing, wind-like tones.

Cultural role

The morin khuur accompanies dances, songs, storytelling, and ceremonies, and its music often imitates the sounds of horses and the steppe. Traditionally every Mongolian family kept one in a place of honour in the ger (yurt). In 2008 the traditional music of the morin khuur was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.
