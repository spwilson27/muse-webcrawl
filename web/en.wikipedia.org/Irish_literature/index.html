Irish literature is literature written in the Irish language, Latin, or the English of Ireland by writers born or resident in Ireland. For a nation of its size, Ireland has made a disproportionately large contribution to world literature in both languages.

Early literature

Irish-language literature is among the oldest vernacular literatures in Europe. Early medieval monks preserved mythological and heroic cycles, including the Ulster Cycle centred on Cú Chulainn and the epic Táin Bó Cúailnge ("The Cattle Raid of Cooley"). Bardic poetry flourished under Gaelic lordship until the collapse of the old order in the 17th century, lamented in the work of poets such as Dáibhí Ó Bruadair.

Anglo-Irish literature

From the 18th century, Irish writers in English — Jonathan Swift, author of Gulliver's Travels (1726), Oliver Goldsmith and Maria Edgeworth — became central to English letters. In the 19th century Bram Stoker wrote Dracula (1897), and Oscar Wilde dominated the London stage.

The Revival and modernism

The Irish Literary Revival of the late 19th and early 20th centuries, led by W. B. Yeats and Lady Gregory and embodied in the Abbey Theatre, sought a national literature rooted in Irish myth and life. James Joyce revolutionized the novel with Ulysses (1922) and Dubliners. Playwrights such as J. M. Synge and Seán O'Casey redefined drama.

Contemporary literature

Samuel Beckett's Waiting for Godot (1953) reshaped modern theatre; Seamus Heaney became Ireland's fourth Nobel laureate in Literature. Later generations include Edna O'Brien, John Banville, Colm Tóibín, Anne Enright, Sally Rooney and Paul Murray, while Irish-language writing continues with figures such as Máirtín Ó Direáin and Nuala Ní Dhomhnaill.
