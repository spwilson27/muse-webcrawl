Hashshashin

The Hashshashin (from Arabic Ḥashshāshīyīn) were the Nizari Ismaili Shia Muslims of Persia and Syria from the 11th to the 13th century who became famous in Western legend as the "Assassins". Operating from mountain fortresses such as Alamut in Persia and Masyaf in Syria, they carried out targeted killings of prominent political and religious figures.

History

The Nizari state was founded by Hasan-i Sabbah, who seized the fortress of Alamut in 1090. Facing far more powerful neighbours — the Seljuk Turks and later the Crusader states — the Nizaris adopted assassination as an asymmetric tactic, striking at leaders rather than fielding large armies. Their victims included the Seljuk vizier Nizam al-Mulk (1092) and Conrad of Montferrat, King of Jerusalem (1192).

Name and legends

The name is usually linked to the Arabic word for hashish, and Crusader-era tales claimed the killers were drugged with hashish and promised paradise — stories popularised by Marco Polo's account of the "Old Man of the Mountain". Most modern historians regard the drug stories as hostile legend rather than fact; the term may originally have been a local slur meaning outcasts or rabble.

Decline

The Persian Nizari state was destroyed by the Mongols under Hülegü, who captured Alamut in 1256. The Syrian branch survived until it was subdued by the Mamluk sultan Baibars in the 1270s. The word "assassin" in many European languages derives from their name.
