Walther Hermann Nernst (June 25, 1864 – November 18, 1941) was a German physical chemist who was one of the founders of modern physical chemistry. He is known for the Nernst equation, which relates electrode potential to concentration, and for formulating the third law of thermodynamics, for which he received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1920.

Career

Nernst studied at the universities of Zürich, Berlin, Graz, and Würzburg, where he received his doctorate in 1887. He worked with Wilhelm Ostwald at Leipzig and in 1894 became the first professor of physical chemistry at Göttingen, where he founded an institute that became a world center for the new discipline. In 1905 he moved to Berlin.

Contributions

In 1889 Nernst derived his equation for electrochemical cell potentials. In 1905–1906 he presented his "heat theorem," stating that entropy changes approach zero as temperature approaches absolute zero — the third law of thermodynamics, which enabled calculation of chemical equilibria from thermal data alone. He also invented the Nernst lamp, an early infrared light source with a ceramic incandescent body that found commercial success before tungsten filament lamps displaced it, and worked on the photochemistry of chain reactions and on the theory of reaction rates. During the First World War he worked on chemical weapons programs. His students included several future Nobel laureates.
