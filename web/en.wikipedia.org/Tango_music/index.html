Tango music

Tango — a musical genre and dance originating in the working-class port districts of Buenos Aires, Argentina, and Montevideo, Uruguay, in the late 19th century. Signature instrument: the bandoneón. In 2009, UNESCO inscribed tango on its Intangible Cultural Heritage list.

Tango is a style of music in 2/4 or 4/4 time that originated among European immigrant populations of Argentina and Uruguay (collectively, the "Rioplatenses"). It is traditionally played on a solo guitar, guitar duo, or an ensemble known as the orquesta típica, which includes at least two violins, piano, double bass, flute, and at least two bandoneóns. Tango may be purely instrumental or may include a vocalist.

Origins

Tango emerged in the 1880s–1890s in the port neighbourhoods of Buenos Aires and Montevideo, blending Afro-Rioplatense rhythms such as candombe and milonga with the Cuban habanera, European polkas and mazurkas, and Andalusian flamenco influences. Initially danced in bars and brothels of the poor districts, it scandalized and then captivated society, conquering Paris in the 1910s and returning home respectable.

Golden age

From the mid-1930s to the mid-1950s, tango's Golden Age filled dance halls with the orchestras of Juan D'Arienzo, Carlos Di Sarli, Aníbal Troilo, and Osvaldo Pugliese. Singer Carlos Gardel, killed in a 1935 plane crash in Medellín, became the genre's emblematic voice and an Argentine national icon.

Nuevo tango

Astor Piazzolla's nuevo tango of the 1950s–80s fused tango with jazz and classical techniques, transforming it into concert music — works such as Libertango and The Four Seasons of Buenos Aires are now standards. Electronic tango (Gotan Project, Bajofondo) carried the genre into the 21st century, and UNESCO listed tango as Intangible Cultural Heritage in 2009.
