Fondue

CourseMain course
Place of originSwitzerland
RegionRomandy, Savoy
Serving temperatureHot
Main ingredientsCheese, white wine, garlic, kirsch

Fondue is a Swiss melted cheese dish served in a communal pot (caquelon) over a portable stove (réchaud) heated with a candle or spirit lamp, and eaten by dipping bread into the cheese using long-stemmed forks. Since the 1950s, the term "fondue" has been generalized to other dishes in which a food is dipped into a communal pot of liquid kept hot in a fondue pot: chocolate fondue, in which pieces of fruit or pastry are dipped into a melted chocolate mixture, and fondue bourguignonne, in which pieces of meat are cooked in hot oil or broth.

Cheese fondue served in a traditional caquelon.

History

The earliest known recipe for a form of cheese fondue comes from a 1699 book published in Zurich, under the name Käss mit Wein zu kochen ("to cook cheese with wine"). It calls for grated cheese to be melted with wine and for bread to be dipped in it. The name derives from the French verb fondre, "to melt." Cheese fondue was promoted as a Swiss national dish by the Swiss Cheese Union in the 1930s as a way of increasing cheese consumption, and it became popular in North America in the 1960s.

Preparation

A cheese fondue mixture typically contains cheese, wine, and seasoning. Traditional recipes call for a mixture of cheeses such as Gruyère and Emmentaler, or Gruyère and Vacheron. The pot is rubbed with garlic, white wine is heated, and grated cheese is stirred in until melted; a small amount of kirsch and cornstarch is often added to stabilize the emulsion. The fondue is kept warm at the table, and diners dip cubes of bread speared on long forks into the cheese.

Variants

Regional variants include the moitié-moitié ("half-half") of Fribourg, made with Gruyère and Vacheron Fribourgeois; the French Savoyard fondue, made with Comté, Beaufort, and Emmental; and Neuchâtel fondue. Broth-based fondue chinoise and oil-based fondue bourguignonne use raw meat cooked at the table.
