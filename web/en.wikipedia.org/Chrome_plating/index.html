Chrome plating

Chrome plating (less commonly chromium plating) is a technique of electroplating a thin layer of chromium onto a metal object. A chrome-plated item is called chrome. The chromed layer can be decorative, provide corrosion resistance, facilitate cleaning, or increase surface hardness. Sometimes, a less expensive imitator of chrome may be used for aesthetic purposes.


Decorative chrome plating gives a bright, mirror-like finish, as on automotive and motorcycle parts.

Process

The typical process involves degreasing and cleaning the part, followed by plating baths of copper and nickel that provide a smooth, corrosion-resistant foundation. The final chromium layer is deposited from a bath of chromic acid (chromic anhydride, CrO3) with sulfate catalyst, using lead anodes and direct current. Decorative chromium layers are extremely thin — on the order of 0.25–1 micrometre — and derive most of their corrosion protection from the underlying nickel. "Hard chrome" (industrial or engineering chrome), by contrast, is deposited directly on steel in thicknesses of tens or hundreds of micrometres to provide wear resistance on hydraulic rods, piston rings and moulds.

Types

  • Decorative chrome — thin, bright finish over copper/nickel undercoats, used on car trim, plumbing fixtures and furniture.
  • Hard chrome — thick, matte-to-satin engineering coating for wear and low friction.
  • Black chrome — dark decorative finish from modified baths.
  • Trivalent chromium plating — newer baths based on Cr(III) salts, developed as a safer, less toxic alternative to hexavalent chromium.

Health and environmental concerns

Conventional chrome plating uses hexavalent chromium, a known carcinogen, and chromic-acid baths can emit hazardous mists. The industry is therefore subject to strict regulation (including workplace exposure limits and emissions rules), and mist suppressants, closed-loop systems and trivalent chromium or alternative coatings have been increasingly adopted. Chrome plating also entered environmental-law history through the Hinkley groundwater contamination case in California, which concerned the use of chromium compounds as corrosion inhibitors.
