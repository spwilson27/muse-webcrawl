The 1966 FIFA World Cup Final was the final match of the 1966 FIFA World Cup, played on 30 July 1966 at Wembley Stadium in London between England and West Germany. England won 4–2 after extra time to claim the Jules Rimet Trophy — the country's first, and to date only, World Cup title. The match is one of the most famous in football history, remembered for Geoff Hurst's hat-trick and his controversial second goal, judged by the linesman to have crossed the line after bouncing off the crossbar.

Route to the final

England, managed by Alf Ramsey, progressed from a group containing Uruguay, Mexico and France, then beat Argentina in a bad-tempered quarter-final and Portugal in the semi-final, where Bobby Charlton scored twice. West Germany topped its group, thrashed Uruguay 4–0 in the quarter-finals and defeated the Soviet Union 2–1 in the semi-finals.

Match summary

West Germany took the lead after 12 minutes through Helmut Haller, but Hurst equalised six minutes later with a header from a quickly taken free kick. Martin Peters put England ahead in the 78th minute, and England appeared to have won until Wolfgang Weber scrambled in an equaliser in the last minute of normal time to make it 2–2.

In extra time, Hurst struck a shot that hit the underside of the crossbar and bounced down; after consulting linesman Tofiq Bahramov, referee Gottfried Dienst awarded the goal — an incident debated ever since, with modern analyses suggesting the whole ball probably did not cross the line. In the final seconds, with some spectators already invading the pitch, Hurst completed his hat-trick — the first in a World Cup final — as commentator Kenneth Wolstenholme uttered the famous line: "Some people are on the pitch... they think it's all over... it is now!" Captain Bobby Moore received the trophy from Queen Elizabeth II.

Legacy

Hurst remains the only player to have scored a hat-trick in a men's World Cup final until Kylian Mbappé matched the feat in 2022. The 1966 victory is a landmark in English sporting culture; the match ball and Hurst's shirt are iconic exhibits, and the "goal that never was" debate continues in both countries.
