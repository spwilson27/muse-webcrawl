Rutherford scattering

Alpha particles deflected by a dense, positively charged nucleus.

Rutherford scattering is the elastic scattering of charged particles by the Coulomb interaction with an atomic nucleus. It was analysed by Ernest Rutherford in 1911 to explain the results of the Geiger–Marsden (gold foil) experiments of 1909, in which a small fraction of alpha particles were deflected through large angles, some nearly straight backwards.

The Rutherford model

Rutherford concluded that the atom's positive charge and most of its mass are concentrated in a tiny central nucleus, overturning J. J. Thomson's plum pudding model. The Rutherford differential cross-section describes how the probability of scattering depends on the scattering angle, the charges of the particles, and their kinetic energy, falling off as 1/sin4(θ/2).

Applications

The theory underlies Rutherford backscattering spectrometry, an analytical technique in materials science, and remains a foundational result of nuclear and particle physics, valid when the particles' energies are low enough that the impact parameter stays outside the nucleus itself.
