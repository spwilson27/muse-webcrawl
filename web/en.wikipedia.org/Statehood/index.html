Statehood

Statehood is the status of being a state. The term is used in several senses: in international law, the condition of an entity that satisfies the criteria of a sovereign state; and in federations such as the United States, the status of a constituent unit admitted to the union with full rights, as in the phrase "granted statehood".

International law

Under the Montevideo Convention of 1933, a state as a person of international law should possess a permanent population, a defined territory, a government, and the capacity to enter into relations with other states. Whether statehood also requires recognition by other states is debated between the declarative and constitutive theories of statehood. Entities with limited recognition, such as Kosovo, Taiwan, and Palestine, illustrate the political character of the question.

United States

Article IV of the United States Constitution empowers Congress to admit new states. Since 1787, 37 states have been admitted beyond the original thirteen, most recently Alaska and Hawaii in 1959. Territories seeking statehood—including Puerto Rico and the District of Columbia—have held status referendums and debated the issue in Congress for decades.

Other federations

In countries such as Australia, India, and Mexico, constituent states have likewise attained or lost statehood through constitutional processes, and movements for new states continue in several federations.
