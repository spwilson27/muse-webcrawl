Carbonara

Carbonara (Italian: [karboˈnaːra]) is an Italian pasta dish from Rome made with eggs, hard cheese, cured pork and black pepper. The dish arrived at its modern form, with its current name, in the middle of the 20th century. It is usually made with spaghetti, though other long pastas such as fettuccine, rigatoni or bucatini may be used.

Ingredients and preparation

The cheese is usually Pecorino Romano, Parmesan (Parmigiano-Reggiano), or a mixture of the two. The cured pork is typically guanciale (cured pork cheek); pancetta is a common substitute outside Italy. The sauce is made by tossing hot pasta with beaten eggs and grated cheese off the heat, so that the eggs thicken into a creamy emulsion without scrambling. Cream is not part of the traditional Roman recipe, and its addition is a frequent subject of culinary debate.

Origins

Theories about the origin of the dish's name abound: it may derive from the carbonari (charcoal burners and members of the secret Carbonari society), from the coal miners, or simply from the specks of black pepper resembling coal dust. The dish does not appear in print before the Second World War; one popular account links its creation to the liberation of Rome in 1944, when American soldiers' rations of eggs and bacon met Italian pasta cooking. Ada Boni's 1930 cookbook of Roman cuisine does not mention it, underlining its modern origins.

Variations

Outside Italy, carbonara is often made with cream, peas, mushrooms or garlic, all departures from the Roman canon. In Rome the protected core of the recipe — guanciale, Pecorino Romano, egg (whole or yolk), black pepper, no cream — is a matter of civic pride and frequent controversy.
