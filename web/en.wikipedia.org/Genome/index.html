Genome

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A genome is the complete set of genetic material of an organism. It consists of DNA (or RNA in RNA viruses) and includes both the genes and the non-coding sequences. The term, a blend of "gene" and "chromosome", was coined by Hans Winkler in 1920. Genome sizes vary enormously, from a few thousand base pairs in small viruses to over 100 billion in some plants and lungfish.

Composition

In eukaryotes the genome is organised into chromosomes in the cell nucleus, supplemented by small genomes in mitochondria (and, in plants, chloroplasts) — relics of ancient endosymbiosis. Only a minority of most eukaryotic genomes codes for protein: the human genome of about 3.2 billion base pairs contains some 20,000 protein-coding genes, while the rest is made up of introns, regulatory sequences, repetitive elements and transposons, much of it once dismissed as "junk DNA" but increasingly recognised as functional in regulation.

Sequencing and analysis

The first complete genome sequenced was that of the bacterium Haemophilus influenzae in 1995. The Human Genome Project published a draft human sequence in 2001 and a "complete" sequence in 2003; the last gaps, in the telomere-to-telomere assembly, were filled in 2022. Falling sequencing costs — now well under a thousand dollars per human genome — have enabled population-scale genomics, genome-wide association studies, and clinical diagnosis of rare diseases. The study of genomes is called genomics.

Evolution

Genomes evolve through mutation, gene duplication, horizontal gene transfer, genome duplication and loss of DNA. Comparing genomes (comparative genomics) reveals evolutionary relationships and conserved functional elements.
