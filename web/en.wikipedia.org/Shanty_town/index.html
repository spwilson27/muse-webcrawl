Shanty town

An informal settlement built from corrugated iron and other salvaged materials.

A shanty town, squatter settlement, or informal settlement is a settlement of improvised housing, often built by residents from salvaged materials such as corrugated metal, plywood, plastic sheeting, and cardboard, on land that the inhabitants do not own or rent legally. Shanty towns are found mainly in developing countries but also exist in developed nations, particularly on the margins of large cities.

They are known by many local names: favelas in Brazil, barriadas in Peru, villas miserias in Argentina, gecekondus in Turkey, jhuggis and slums in India, and "townships" in South Africa. Characteristics commonly include overcrowding, inadequate access to clean water and sanitation, insecure tenure, and high exposure to fire, flooding, and disease. The United Nations estimates that around one billion people—roughly a quarter of the world's urban population—live in slums and informal settlements.

Causes

Shanty towns arise from rapid urbanisation combined with poverty, rural-to-urban migration, housing shortages, unemployment, war and displacement, and weak or unaffordable formal housing supply. Residents often build incrementally, improving their homes over years as resources allow, and many settlements develop strong community organisations, informal economies, and eventually political representation.

Responses

Government responses have ranged from forced eviction and demolition to in-situ upgrading—providing water, sanitation, electricity, roads, and secure tenure—and, more recently, participatory slum-upgrading programmes supported by UN-Habitat. Famous large shanty towns include Kibera in Nairobi, Dharavi in Mumbai, Rocinha in Rio de Janeiro, and Khayelitsha in Cape Town.
