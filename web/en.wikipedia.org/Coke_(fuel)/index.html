Coke (fuel)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Coke
CompositionCarbonaceous solid; typically 86–92% carbon
Produced fromBituminous coal by destructive distillation
Main useBlast furnace fuel and reducing agent in iron and steel making

Coke is a grey, hard, and porous coal-based fuel with a high carbon content. It is made by heating coal or petroleum in the absence of air — a process known as destructive distillation or pyrolysis. Historically in the United Kingdom, coke was also the main domestic fuel for stoves and furnaces where it was suitable for heating.

The name also refers to the dry distillation residue of petroleum feedstock ("petroleum coke" or petcoke). This article focuses on coal-derived coke.

Production

Coking coal is heated in coke ovens to about 1,000–1,100 °C for many hours in an oxygen-deficient atmosphere, driving off volatile compounds as coke oven gas, coal tar, and ammonia liquor, and leaving a fused mass of nearly pure carbon and ash. In modern by-product plants, these volatiles are recovered as valuable chemicals. The hot coke is pushed from the oven and quenched with water or dry (inert-gas) cooling.

Uses

Coke's most important use is in blast furnaces, where it serves simultaneously as fuel, as a reducing agent converting iron oxide to metal, and as a permeable support for the furnace burden. Abraham Darby's use of coke for smelting iron at Coalbrookdale in 1709 was a landmark of the Industrial Revolution, freeing ironmaking from dependence on charcoal. Coke is also used in foundry cupolas, in the production of calcium carbide and other chemicals, and historically in domestic heating.

History

Coal was coked in China as early as the 9th century. In England, coke was used to dry malt in the 17th century before its adoption in iron smelting. The growth of integrated steelworks made coke production a major industry; the conversion of coke oven gas and tar underpinned much of the early organic chemical industry.
