Mughal gardens

Mughal gardens are a type of garden built by the Mughals in the Persian tradition, characterised by the charbagh layout: a quadrilateral garden divided by walkways and flowing water channels into four smaller parts, representing the four rivers of the Islamic paradise and the four elements. They were built across the Mughal Empire from the sixteenth to the eighteenth century.

History

The founder of the dynasty, Babur, laid out the first Mughal gardens at Kabul and Agra (Ram Bagh), lamenting India's lack of running water and importing Central Asian garden traditions. Under Akbar, Jahangir and Shah Jahan the type reached its zenith: the gardens of Humayun's Tomb in Delhi, the Shalimar Gardens of Srinagar and Lahore, and the garden of the Taj Mahal at Agra, where the fourfold plan organises the mausoleum precinct around a raised marble water tank.

Design elements

Key elements include high enclosing walls, symmetrically arranged water channels and pools with fountains, terraces on sloping sites, pavilions (baradari), and carefully ordered planting of flowering trees, fruit trees and cypresses symbolising life and death. Mughal gardens served as places of leisure, courtly gathering, and frequently of imperial burial.
