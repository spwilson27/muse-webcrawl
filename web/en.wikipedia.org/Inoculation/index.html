Inoculation

Inoculation is the act of introducing a microorganism, serum, or other substance into a living organism for purposes of immunity, diagnosis, or experiment. In its historical medical sense, inoculation refers specifically to variolation — the deliberate introduction of material from smallpox pustules into a healthy person to induce a mild infection and subsequent immunity to smallpox. In modern usage, the word is often used loosely as a synonym for vaccination and immunisation, though the terms have distinct technical meanings.

History of smallpox inoculation

Variolation was practised in China and India for centuries before being reported in Britain and colonial America in the early eighteenth century. Lady Mary Wortley Montagu, who observed the practice in Ottoman Constantinople, promoted it in Britain after 1721; in Boston, Cotton Mather and Zabdiel Boylston applied it during the 1721 smallpox epidemic despite fierce controversy. Variolation carried a real risk — roughly one or two deaths per hundred recipients, and the inoculated could spread full smallpox — but it was far safer than natural infection. In 1796 Edward Jenner demonstrated that inoculation with cowpox (vaccination) gave protection with far less risk, and vaccination progressively replaced variolation, which Britain banned in 1840.

Other uses

In microbiology, inoculation is the introduction of microorganisms into a culture medium, such as streaking bacteria onto an agar plate. In agriculture, seeds and soil may be inoculated with beneficial microbes (for example, nitrogen-fixing Rhizobium for legumes). Inoculation theory in social psychology, developed by William McGuire, uses the term metaphorically for resistance to persuasion built up by exposure to weakened counterarguments.
