Lithophyte

Lithophytes are plants that grow on or in rocks. They obtain moisture and nutrients from the atmosphere, rain, leaf litter accumulating in cracks, and their own decaying tissues. Lithophytes are among the pioneer organisms that colonise bare rock surfaces and contribute to soil formation.

Types

  • Epilithic species grow on the surface of rocks.
  • Endolithic species grow within cracks and pores of rocks.
  • Chasmophytes grow in fissures where soil or debris collects.

Adaptations

Lithophytes must tolerate scarce water, few nutrients and large temperature swings. Many have fleshy water-storing tissues, deep or adhesive roots, and slow growth. Examples include many orchids, bromeliads, ferns, mosses, and species of fig (Ficus) that start life on rock or cliff faces. Some lithophytes in nutrient-poor settings are carnivorous, such as certain bladderworts.

Ecological role

By prising apart cracks with their roots and contributing organic matter when they die, lithophytes help weather rock into soil, preparing the substrate for later plant communities in primary succession.
