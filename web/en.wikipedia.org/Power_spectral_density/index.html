Power spectral density

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
A power spectral density plot shows how a signal's power is distributed over frequency.

In signal processing, the power spectral density (PSD) of a signal describes how its power is distributed over frequency. Formally, for a wide-sense stationary random process, the PSD is the Fourier transform of the process's autocorrelation function — a result known as the Wiener–Khinchin theorem. The total power of the signal equals the integral of the PSD over all frequencies, in accordance with Parseval's theorem, so the PSD provides the frequency-domain counterpart of time-domain power.

Power spectral density

Units: Power per hertz (e.g. W/Hz; often dB/Hz)

Definition: Fourier transform of the autocorrelation

Key result: Wiener–Khinchin theorem

Estimation

In practice the PSD of a measured signal must be estimated from a finite record. The simplest estimator, the periodogram, computes the squared magnitude of the discrete Fourier transform; it is noisy and does not converge as more data are added. Welch's method improves on this by splitting the record into overlapping windowed segments, computing a periodogram for each, and averaging, trading frequency resolution for reduced variance. Multitaper methods use several orthogonal windows (Slepian sequences) for a similar trade-off. These estimators are standard tools in fields from audio engineering and vibration analysis to neuroscience, where PSDs of EEG signals reveal characteristic rhythms, and radio astronomy.

Applications

PSD analysis characterises noise: "white" noise has a flat PSD, while pink (1/f) noise falls with frequency. Engineers use PSDs to specify vibration environments for equipment, design filters, analyse communications channels, and detect signals buried in noise. Related quantities include the amplitude spectral density (the square root of the PSD) and, for energy signals, the energy spectral density.
