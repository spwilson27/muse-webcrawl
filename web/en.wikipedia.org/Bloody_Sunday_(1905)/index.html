Bloody Sunday (1905)

Bloody Sunday, also known as the Red Sunday, was a massacre on 22 January [O.S. 9 January] 1905 in St. Petersburg, Russia, when unarmed demonstrators, led by the priest Georgy Gapon, were fired upon by soldiers of the Imperial Guard as they marched towards the Winter Palace to present a petition to Tsar Nicholas II. Estimates of the dead range from about 200 to over 1,000.

Background

Industrial strikes spread through St. Petersburg in January 1905 after four workers were dismissed from the Putilov Iron Works. Gapon's Assembly of Russian Factory and Mill Workers organized a peaceful procession carrying a petition asking for improved working conditions, universal suffrage, an end to the Russo-Japanese War, and a constituent assembly. The marchers carried icons, portraits of the Tsar, and church banners, and many brought their families.

The massacre

At several points in the city, troops fired on the columns of marchers, and Cossacks and hussars charged the crowds. Nicholas II was not at the Winter Palace that day. The shootings destroyed the popular image of the Tsar as the kindly "father" of his people that many marchers had held.

Aftermath

News of the massacre provoked a wave of strikes and unrest across the empire, starting the Russian Revolution of 1905. The upheaval forced Nicholas II to issue the October Manifesto, promising civil liberties and an elected Duma. Bloody Sunday is widely regarded as a turning point that fatally undermined the autocracy's legitimacy.
