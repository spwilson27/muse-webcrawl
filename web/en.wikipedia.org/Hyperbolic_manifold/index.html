Hyperbolic manifold

In mathematics, a hyperbolic manifold is a space where every point looks locally like hyperbolic space of a certain dimension. A complete Riemannian manifold of dimension n > 1 is hyperbolic if it has constant sectional curvature −1; equivalently, it is a quotient of n-dimensional hyperbolic space by a discrete group of isometries acting freely and properly discontinuously.

Dimension 2

Hyperbolic surfaces are obtained from quotients of the hyperbolic plane. By the Gauss–Bonnet theorem, a closed orientable surface admits a hyperbolic structure exactly when its genus is at least 2. Such surfaces have finite area and are rigid in a weaker sense than higher-dimensional manifolds: their deformations are described by Teichmüller space.

Dimensions 3 and higher

Hyperbolic 3-manifolds arise naturally in knot theory, since the complement of many knots in the 3-sphere carries a complete hyperbolic metric of finite volume — a fact central to Thurston's geometrization programme. The figure-eight knot complement is the classic example. By Mostow's rigidity theorem, in dimensions n ≥ 3 the hyperbolic structure of a finite-volume manifold is unique: geometric invariants such as volume are topological invariants.
