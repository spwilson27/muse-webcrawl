Oliver Twist

Oliver Twist; or, The Parish Boy's Progress
Author: Charles Dickens
Published: 1838 (serial 1837–39)
Genre: Social novel
Publisher: Richard Bentley

Oliver Twist; or, The Parish Boy's Progress is a novel by Charles Dickens, published as a serial from 1837 to 1839 and in book form in 1838. It was Dickens's second novel and is notable for its unromanticised depiction of criminals, workhouses and the lives of London's poor.

Plot

Oliver Twist is born in a workhouse, his mother dying shortly after his birth. Raised under the harsh regime of the parish beadle Mr Bumble, he is famously punished after asking for more gruel. Apprenticed to an undertaker, he runs away to London, where he falls in with a gang of child pickpockets led by the elderly fence Fagin and the brutal burglar Bill Sikes, aided by the Artful Dodger and Nancy. Saved by the kindly Mr Brownlow, Oliver is recaptured and forced to join a burglary, during which he is wounded. Taken in by the Maylie family, he eventually uncovers the secret of his parentage — he is the son of Edwin Leeford and Agnes Fleming — and the villain Monks, his half-brother, is exposed. Fagin is hanged, Sikes dies fleeing, Nancy is murdered, and Oliver is adopted by Brownlow.

Themes

Dickens wrote the novel partly to attack the Poor Law Amendment Act 1834 and the misery of the workhouse system. It contrasts the corruption of institutions and the criminal underworld with the innate goodness embodied by Oliver, who remains innocent despite his environment.

Adaptations

The novel has been adapted many times, including the stage musical Oliver! (1960) by Lionel Bart and its 1968 film version, which won the Academy Award for Best Picture, as well as David Lean's 1948 film starring Alec Guinness as Fagin.
