Leigh syndrome

Leigh syndrome (also called Leigh disease or subacute necrotizing encephalomyelopathy) is an inherited neurometabolic disorder that affects the central nervous system. It is named after British neuropsychiatrist Archibald Denis Leigh, who first described the condition in 1951. It is a mitochondrial disease characterised by progressive loss of mental and motor abilities, typically beginning in infancy or early childhood, and usually resulting in death within a few years, often from respiratory failure.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms typically appear after an initial period of normal development and may be triggered by infection or other stress. They include loss of motor milestones, hypotonia progressing to spasticity, ataxia, seizures, ophthalmoplegia and nystagmus, abnormal breathing patterns, lactic acidosis, and failure to thrive. MRI characteristically shows symmetrical necrotic lesions in the basal ganglia, brainstem and other subcortical structures.

Causes

Leigh syndrome can be caused by mutations in more than 75 genes, in both mitochondrial DNA (maternally inherited) and nuclear DNA (autosomal recessive or X-linked). The genes involved encode components of the mitochondrial respiratory chain — including complexes I, II, IV and V (ATP synthase) — or factors needed for their assembly, such as SURF1. The resulting failure of oxidative phosphorylation hits the energy-hungry brain hardest.

Treatment

There is no cure; management is supportive and symptomatic, sometimes including coenzyme Q10, vitamin supplements and treatment of acidosis, though evidence is limited. Genetic counselling is recommended for affected families.
