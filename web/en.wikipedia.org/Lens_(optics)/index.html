Lens (optics)

A lens is a transmissive optical device that focuses or disperses a light beam by means of refraction. A simple lens consists of a single piece of transparent material, usually glass or plastic, with at least one curved surface; a compound lens is built from several simple lenses (elements) aligned on a common axis to correct aberrations. Lenses are used in spectacles, cameras, telescopes, microscopes, binoculars and projectors, and a natural lens forms part of the human eye.

Types

A convex (converging) lens is thicker at the centre than at the edges and brings parallel light to a real focus; it produces magnified images and corrects long-sightedness (hyperopia). A concave (diverging) lens is thinner at the centre and spreads light rays as if coming from a virtual focus; it corrects short-sightedness (myopia). Meniscus, plano-convex and other shapes combine these profiles. The distance from the lens to its focal point is the focal length, and optical power is measured in dioptres, the reciprocal of focal length in metres.

Aberrations and compound lenses

Simple lenses suffer from spherical aberration, chromatic aberration (colour fringing, because refraction varies with wavelength), coma and astigmatism. Compound designs such as the achromatic doublet — pairing crown and flint glass — and modern multi-element photographic lenses minimise these defects; aperture stops and coatings further improve performance.

History

Reading stones and burning glasses were known in antiquity; spectacles appeared in Italy in the late 13th century, and the telescope and compound microscope around 1600 transformed science. The word "lens" derives from the Latin lens, "lentil", owing to the shape of a biconvex lens.
