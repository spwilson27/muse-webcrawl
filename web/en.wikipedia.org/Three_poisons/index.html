Three poisons

The three poisons (Sanskrit: triviṣa; Tibetan: dug gsum) in Buddhism, also known as the three unwholesome roots (Sanskrit: akuśala-mūla; Pāli: akusala-mūla), are a group of three mental states at the root of suffering. They are:

  • Moha – delusion or confusion (also avidyā, ignorance)
  • Rāga – greed, sensual attachment
  • Dveṣa – aversion, hatred

These three poisons are considered to be the primary causes that keep beings trapped in saṃsāra, the cycle of rebirth. They are depicted at the center of the Wheel of Life (bhavacakra) as a pig or boar (delusion), a bird or rooster (attachment), and a snake (aversion), chasing one another in a circle.

Descriptions

Moha (delusion)

Moha is a state of mental bewilderment and confusion, a misunderstanding of the nature of reality. It is often used synonymously with avidyā (ignorance), the first of the twelve nidānas. Delusion is considered the fundamental root from which greed and hatred arise.

Rāga (attachment)

Rāga is any form of greed, desire, lust, or attachment. It involves exaggeratedly projecting desirable qualities onto an object and clinging to it. Rāga is symbolized by the rooster in the Wheel of Life.

Dveṣa (aversion)

Dveṣa is aversion, ill will, anger, or hatred directed toward oneself, others, or unpleasant experiences. It includes pushing away what is perceived as undesirable and is symbolized by the snake.

Opposite wholesome qualities

The three poisons are countered by the three wholesome roots (kuśala-mūla): non-delusion or wisdom (amoha / prajñā), non-attachment or generosity (alobha), and non-aversion or loving-kindness (adveṣa / maitrī). Buddhist practice, particularly the Noble Eightfold Path, aims at uprooting the poisons and cultivating these wholesome qualities.

Alternative classifications

Some traditions give expanded lists. The Mahāyāna tradition sometimes adds pride (māna) and doubt (vicikitsā) to form a group of five poisons. In Vajrayāna, the five poisons correspond to the five wisdoms and the five buddha families, and practices such as the generation stage aim at transmuting them rather than merely suppressing them.
