Occipital condyles

The occipital condyles are two rounded prominences on the undersurface of the occipital bone of the skull, situated on either side of the foramen magnum. They articulate with the superior articular surfaces of the atlas (the first cervical vertebra), forming the atlanto-occipital joints, which permit nodding movements of the head.

The occipital condyles flank the foramen magnum on the base of the skull.

Structure

Each condyle is oval or kidney-shaped, convex downward, and covered with articular cartilage. The long axes of the two condyles converge anteriorly. Immediately lateral to each condyle is the hypoglossal canal, through which the hypoglossal nerve (cranial nerve XII) exits the skull; occasionally the canal is divided by a bony spicule. A depression behind each condyle, the condylar fossa, receives the posterior margin of the atlas during extension of the head.

Function

The atlanto-occipital joints are synovial joints of the condyloid type. Their principal movements are flexion and extension — the "yes" nodding motion — together with a small amount of lateral flexion. Rotation of the head occurs mainly at the atlanto-axial joint below.

Comparative anatomy

Mammals and birds possess a single occipital condyle, whereas amphibians and reptiles typically have either a single condyle or, in some reptiles, a tripartite arrangement. The paired condyles of mammals reflect the contribution of the exoccipital bones to the articulation.

Clinical significance

Fractures of the occipital condyles can result from high-energy head trauma and may injure the hypoglossal nerve or the vertebral arteries. Congenital fusion of the condyles with the atlas (occipitalization) can restrict head movement.
