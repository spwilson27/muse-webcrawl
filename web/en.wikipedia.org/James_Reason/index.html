James Tootle Reason CBE (1 May 1938 – 4 June 2025) was a British psychologist, professor of psychology at the University of Manchester and a leading authority on human error and organisational accidents. His "Swiss cheese model" of accident causation — in which hazards penetrate successive defensive layers only when their weaknesses ("holes") align — became one of the most influential ideas in safety science, applied in aviation, medicine, nuclear power and other high-risk industries.

Career

Reason studied psychology at the University of Manchester, taking his PhD there, and worked at the RAF Institute of Aviation Medicine and the US Naval Aerospace Medical Research Laboratory in Pensacola before returning to Manchester, where he became professor of psychology in 1977 and remained for the rest of his academic career. His early research dealt with motion sickness and spatial disorientation in pilots.

Human error research

Reason's major contribution was a systematic theory of human error. In Human Error (1990) he distinguished skill-based slips and lapses, rule-based mistakes and knowledge-based mistakes, and separated "active failures" at the sharp end of work from "latent conditions" — organisational weaknesses lying dormant in a system, like pathogens, until they combine with local triggers to cause disaster. His analysis of catastrophes such as Bhopal, Chernobyl, King's Cross and the Herald of Free Enterprise sinking argued that major accidents almost always have long organisational incubations and cannot be blamed simply on the last person to touch the system.

Influence

Reason's later books, including Managing the Risks of Organizational Accidents (1997) and The Human Contribution (2008), helped shift safety management toward "just culture," error reporting systems and patient safety in healthcare, where his ideas influenced national programmes in Britain and elsewhere. He was elected a Fellow of the British Academy and appointed CBE for services to risk reduction in healthcare.
