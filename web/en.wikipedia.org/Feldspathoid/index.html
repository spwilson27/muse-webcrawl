Feldspathoid

The feldspathoids are a group of anhydrous tectosilicate minerals that resemble the feldspars but have a different structure and a considerably lower proportion of silica. They form in silica-undersaturated igneous rocks in place of feldspars: feldspathoids and quartz are mutually exclusive in equilibrium, so a rock containing feldspathoids never contains primary quartz.

Chemistry and structure

Feldspathoids have frameworks of silicon and aluminum tetrahedra like the feldspars, but a Si:Al ratio closer to 1:1 rather than 3:1 or 2:1. Their relatively open structures contain cavities occupied by large cations (sodium, potassium, calcium) and often additional anions such as chloride, carbonate, or sulfate.

Members

  • Nepheline — (Na,K)AlSiO4; the most common feldspathoid, the main constituent of nepheline syenite.
  • Leucite — KAlSi2O6; occurs in potassium-rich volcanic rocks and is notable for its trapezohedral crystals.
  • Sodalite group — sodalite, nosean, and haüyne, containing chloride or sulfate.
  • Lazurite — the blue, sulfur-bearing mineral that gives lapis lazuli its color.
  • Cancrinite group — carbonate-bearing feldspathoids.
  • Scapolite — sometimes classified with the feldspathoids.
  • Analcime — a zeolite-like mineral often grouped with the feldspathoids.

Occurrence and uses

Feldspathoid-bearing rocks include nepheline syenites, phonolites, and some alkali basalts, found in rift zones and continental interiors — for example the Kola Peninsula in Russia, Ontario in Canada, and the Eifel district of Germany. Nepheline syenite is mined as a raw material for glass and ceramics, and as a source of alumina. Lapis lazuli, composed largely of lazurite, has been prized as a gemstone and pigment source (ultramarine) since antiquity.
