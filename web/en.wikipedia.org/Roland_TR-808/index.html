The Roland TR-808 Rhythm Composer, commonly known as the 808, is a drum machine manufactured by the Roland Corporation between 1980 and 1983. Unlike its main competitor, the more expensive Linn LM-1, the 808 generates its drum sounds entirely with analogue circuitry rather than digital samples, producing distinctive, deliberately unrealistic sounds — a deep booming bass drum, snappy snare, and sizzling hi-hats made from six square-wave oscillators. Initially a commercial disappointment, the 808 became one of the most influential instruments in music history, underpinning hip hop, house, techno, electro, and much of modern pop.

Development and release

The 808 was designed under Roland founder Ikutaro Kakehashi, with engineer Tadao Kikumoto leading the project. To keep costs low, Roland used defective transistors that the company could obtain cheaply; these gave the machine its unique sonic character, most famously in the cowbell and cymbal circuits. The 808 offered a step sequencer with 32 patterns, individual outputs and volume controls for each sound, and DIN sync to lock tempo with other Roland equipment. It retailed for $1,195, far less than the $5,000 Linn LM-1.

Professional studios largely dismissed its synthetic sounds as unrealistic, and production ended after about 12,000 units when a key semiconductor became unavailable. The machines flooded second-hand shops at low prices — which placed them in the hands of young producers in New York, Detroit, and Chicago.

Impact on music

One of the first hits built on the 808 was Marvin Gaye's "Sexual Healing" (1982). Afrika Bambaataa and Soulsonic Force's "Planet Rock" (1982) made it the heartbeat of electro and early hip hop; the Beastie Boys, Run-DMC, LL Cool J, and later the Miami bass scene used it extensively. Its bass drum could be tuned and its decay lengthened to create a pitched sub-bass note — a technique central to Miami bass, Atlanta trap, and contemporary hip hop production, where "808" now generically means that booming kick-bass sound, whatever instrument makes it. The machine also powered early house and techno records and remains a staple of electronic music; Kanye West's album 808s & Heartbreak (2008) put its name in a title.

Legacy

Original units became prized collector's items, routinely selling for several thousand dollars. Roland has revisited the design repeatedly: in the TR-8 and TR-8S rhythm performers, the Boutique TR-08 (2017), the cloud-based software 808, and the TR-1000 (2025). The 808's sounds are among the most sampled and imitated in recording history, and its step-sequencer layout helped define how electronic musicians compose rhythm.
