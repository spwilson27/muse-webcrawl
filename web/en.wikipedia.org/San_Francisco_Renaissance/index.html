San Francisco Renaissance

The San Francisco Renaissance was a literary and cultural flowering centred on San Francisco in the years after the Second World War, roughly from the mid-1940s through the 1950s and into the 1960s. It made the city, and especially the North Beach neighbourhood, a magnet for experimental poetry and laid the groundwork for the Beat Generation.

Origins

The movement grew out of the circle around Kenneth Rexroth, who hosted literary salons and anarchist discussion groups, and included poets such as Robert Duncan, Jack Spicer, Robin Blaser, Madeline Gleason, Philip Lamantia and William Everson (Brother Antoninus). Gleason organised the First Festival of Modern Poetry in 1947, a landmark event that brought together a generation of West Coast modernists.

The Six Gallery reading

On 7 October 1955, the Six Gallery reading in San Francisco introduced Allen Ginsberg, who performed the first part of Howl, alongside Gary Snyder, Michael McClure, Philip Whalen and Philip Lamantia, with Jack Kerouac in the audience. The event is often treated as the public debut of the Beat Generation, which overlapped with — but was distinct from — the broader Renaissance.

Legacy

Institutions of the era, notably Lawrence Ferlinghetti's City Lights Bookstore (founded 1953) and its Pocket Poets series, gave the movement a lasting infrastructure. The San Francisco Renaissance is credited with shifting American poetry toward open forms, spoken-word performance, ecological and Buddhist themes, and a distinctly Pacific, West Coast sensibility.
