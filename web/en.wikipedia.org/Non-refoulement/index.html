Non-refoulement

Non-refoulement
FieldInternational refugee and human rights law
Key sources1951 Refugee Convention, Art. 33; Convention Against Torture, Art. 3; ICCPR; ECHR jurisprudence
Core ruleNo state may return a person to a territory where they face persecution, torture or other serious harm
StatusWidely regarded as customary international law; non-derogable as to torture
Term originFrench refouler — "to drive back"

Non-refoulement (UK: /nɒn rəˈfuːlmɒ̃/) is a fundamental principle of international law that a person should not be returned to a country where they face serious threats to their life or freedom. It is the cornerstone of international refugee protection, codified in Article 33 of the 1951 Refugee Convention, which prohibits a contracting state from expelling or returning ("refouler") a refugee to the frontiers of territories where their life or freedom would be threatened on account of race, religion, nationality, membership of a particular social group or political opinion.

Scope and sources

The principle extends beyond the Refugee Convention. Article 3 of the UN Convention Against Torture absolutely forbids return to a state where there are substantial grounds for believing a person would be in danger of torture, with no exceptions for national security or criminal conduct. Human-rights bodies have read equivalent obligations into the ICCPR and the European Convention on Human Rights (Article 3, as in Soering v. United Kingdom and Hirsi Jamaa v. Italy, the latter applying the principle to interceptions at sea). Most scholars and the UNHCR regard non-refoulement as a rule of customary international law binding even on states not party to the Convention; the torture-related prohibition is often described as a peremptory norm (jus cogens).

Application and controversies

Practice raises contested questions: whether the principle applies extraterritorially (at embassies, in international waters, through "pushbacks" at borders); the legality of returning asylum seekers to "safe third countries"; accelerated border procedures; and diplomatic assurances against torture. Article 33(2) of the Refugee Convention itself excepts refugees reasonably regarded as a danger to the security or community of the host country — an exception absent from the Convention Against Torture.
