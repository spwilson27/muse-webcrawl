Dielectric spectroscopy

Dielectric spectroscopy (sometimes called impedance spectroscopy) measures the dielectric properties of a medium as a function of frequency. It is based on the interaction of an external electric field with the electric dipole moment of the sample, often expressed as the complex permittivity. The method can span an enormous frequency range, from below 10−6 Hz to above 1012 Hz.

Principles

When an alternating field is applied to a material, charges and molecular dipoles respond with characteristic relaxation times. Polarization mechanisms — electronic, atomic, dipolar, and interfacial — fall out of step with the field at progressively lower frequencies, producing steps in the real part of the permittivity and peaks in dielectric loss that can be modeled by the Debye, Cole–Cole, Cole–Davidson, and Havriliak–Negami relaxation functions.

Applications

Dielectric spectroscopy is used to study molecular dynamics in polymers and glass-forming liquids, charge transport in ionic conductors and battery materials, hydration and structure in biological tissue, and the curing of resins. In the time domain, the related technique of time-domain reflectometry is used to locate faults in cables and measure soil moisture.
