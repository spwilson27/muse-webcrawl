Concert of Europe

The Concert of Europe was a general agreement among the great powers of nineteenth-century Europe to act together to maintain the balance of power, political boundaries, and spheres of influence established at the Congress of Vienna (1814–15) after the Napoleonic Wars. Its members—initially Austria, Prussia, Russia, the United Kingdom, and later France—sought to preserve the conservative order and prevent the spread of revolutionary movements.

The Congress System

In its early phase the Concert operated through a series of congresses—Aix-la-Chapelle (1818), Troppau (1820), Laibach (1821), and Verona (1822)—at which the powers coordinated interventions against revolutions in Naples, Piedmont, and Spain. Britain, under Castlereagh and Canning, distanced itself from interventionist policies, weakening the system. The Holy Alliance of Austria, Prussia, and Russia represented its most conservative expression.

Decline and later phases

The revolutions of 1830 and 1848, the Crimean War (1853–56)—in which Britain and France fought Russia—and the wars of Italian and German unification eroded the original settlement. Nevertheless, habits of great-power consultation persisted in episodes such as the Congress of Berlin (1878) and conferences over the Balkans and Africa. Historians debate when the Concert ended; the system of managed equilibrium finally broke down with the First World War in 1914.
