Partition of the Ottoman Empire

The partition of the Ottoman Empire (30 October 1918 – 1 November 1922) was a geopolitical event that occurred after World War I and the occupation of Constantinople by British, French, and Italian troops in November 1918. The partitioning was planned in several agreements made by the Allied Powers early in the course of World War I, notably the Sykes–Picot Agreement, after the Ottoman Empire had joined Germany to form the Ottoman–German Alliance.

The huge conglomeration of territories and peoples that formerly comprised the Ottoman Empire was divided into several new states. The empire, founded in 1299, was succeeded by the Republic of Turkey, proclaimed in 1923, alongside a series of League of Nations mandates and new Arab states.

Background

During the war, Britain, France, Russia, and Italy concluded secret agreements dividing Ottoman territory among themselves. The Hussein–McMahon Correspondence promised Arab independence in exchange for revolt against the Ottomans, while the Balfour Declaration of 1917 supported a Jewish national home in Palestine—commitments that proved difficult to reconcile.

The settlement

The Armistice of Mudros ended hostilities in October 1918. The Treaty of Sèvres (1920) imposed a harsh settlement: the Levant and Mesopotamia became League of Nations mandates under Britain and France, the Hejaz gained independence, Armenia and Kurdistan were to be autonomous or independent, and Anatolia itself was divided into zones of influence with Greek control around Smyrna. Turkish nationalists under Mustafa Kemal rejected Sèvres; after the Turkish War of Independence, the Treaty of Lausanne (1923) recognised the Republic of Turkey within roughly its modern borders.

Legacy

The partition created the modern state system of the Middle East. Its borders, drawn largely by European officials, remain a source of political controversy, and the settlement's unresolved promises—particularly regarding Kurdish statehood and Palestine—shaped conflicts throughout the twentieth century and beyond.
