Coxswain

Role: Steers boat, commands crew
Contexts: Rowing, sailing, naval service
Pronunciation: "KOK-sən"

The coxswain (pronounced "kok-sən") is the person in charge of a boat, particularly its navigation and steering. In rowing, the coxswain sits in the bow or stern of an eight or four, steering, coordinating the rowers' rhythm, and executing race strategy, while in naval usage a coxswain is a petty officer responsible for a ship's boat.

Rowing

Coxed boats include eights (always coxed) and coxed fours and pairs. The coxswain uses a rudder controlled by cables, a microphone system (the "cox box") that amplifies commands and reports stroke rate and speed, and their voice alone in older traditions. Because the coxswain does not row, racing rules impose minimum weights — 55 kg for men and 50 kg for women in FISA competition — with deadweight added if the coxswain is lighter. Great coxswains are valued as tacticians, motivators, and precise helmsmen who can hold the shortest course.

Naval and other uses

In the Royal Navy and United States Navy, a coxswain is the senior sailor in charge of a small boat such as a launch, cutter, or lifeboat. In the U.S. Coast Guard, coxswain is a qualification for boat crews, certifying the member to command a boat in rescue and law-enforcement operations. The term derives from "cock", an old name for a small boat, plus "swain", a servant or attendant.

Sailing

In dinghy and keelboat sailing the word is sometimes used for the helmsman, though "helm" is the more common term; in lifeboat services, the coxswain commands the lifeboat and its crew during launches and rescues.
