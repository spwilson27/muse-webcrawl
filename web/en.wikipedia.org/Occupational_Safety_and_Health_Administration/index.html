Occupational Safety and Health Administration

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) is a regulatory agency of the United States Department of Labor that originally had federal visitorial powers to inspect and examine workplaces. Congress established the agency under the Occupational Safety and Health Act (OSH Act), which President Richard M. Nixon signed into law on 29 December 1970. OSHA's mission is to "ensure safe and healthful working conditions for workers by setting and enforcing standards and by providing training, outreach, education and assistance".

History

The OSH Act was passed after decades of campaigning on industrial accidents and occupational disease, energised by works such as Ralph Nader's reports and by disasters in coal mining. Congress declared its purpose to assure "so far as possible every working man and woman in the Nation safe and healthful working conditions". Workplace fatality rates in the United States have fallen substantially since OSHA's creation, from about 14,000 deaths in 1970 to roughly 5,000–5,500 annually in recent years.

Standards and enforcement

OSHA issues standards governing hazards such as falls, machine guarding, hazardous chemicals (the Hazard Communication Standard), respirable crystalline silica, confined spaces and lockout/tagout of machinery. Compliance officers conduct inspections — unannounced — triggered by complaints, accidents, referrals or programmed targeting of high-hazard industries, and may issue citations and fines. Employers must report fatalities within 8 hours and serious hospitalisations within 24 hours. About half the states operate OSHA-approved "state plans" covering private and public workers under standards at least as effective as federal ones.

Rights and responsibilities

Workers have the right to a workplace free from recognised hazards, to receive information and training about hazards, to report injuries, and to file complaints without retaliation; the Act's Section 11(c) prohibits retaliation against whistleblowers. The Act's General Duty Clause requires employers to keep workplaces free of recognised hazards even where no specific standard applies.

Criticism and reform debates

OSHA has been criticised for slow standard-setting (major standards can take a decade or more), modest penalty levels, and limited inspector numbers relative to the millions of workplaces covered; business groups have at times criticised its standards as burdensome. These debates recur in proposals to modernise the OSH Act.
