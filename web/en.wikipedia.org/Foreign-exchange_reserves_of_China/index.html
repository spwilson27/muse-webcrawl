Foreign-exchange reserves of China

China's reserves are managed principally by the State Administration of Foreign Exchange (SAFE) under the People's Bank of China.

The foreign-exchange reserves of China are the world's largest stock of official reserve assets held by a single country. They are held by the People's Bank of China (PBOC) and managed principally by the State Administration of Foreign Exchange (SAFE). The reserves peaked at just under US$4 trillion in June 2014 and have since fluctuated around US$3.1–3.3 trillion, standing at roughly US$3.2 trillion in the mid-2020s — approximately double those of the second-largest holder, Japan.

Accumulation

China's reserves grew rapidly after the mid-1990s as a result of large trade and current-account surpluses, substantial foreign direct investment inflows, and the PBOC's intervention to limit appreciation of the renminbi, which required buying incoming foreign currency. Reserves passed US$1 trillion in 2006, US$2 trillion in 2009, and US$3 trillion in 2011. Growth stalled after 2014: capital outflows in 2015–16 led the PBOC to sell reserves to support the currency, drawing the total down by roughly US$1 trillion from the peak, before stabilization around US$3 trillion under tighter capital controls.

Composition and investment

China does not publish a full currency breakdown. The reserves are believed to be held mainly in US dollars, with significant holdings of euros, Japanese yen, and pounds sterling, invested largely in highly liquid, low-risk securities — above all US Treasury securities and US agency debt, of which China has been one of the largest foreign holders. A portion is allocated through investment vehicles: the China Investment Corporation (CIC), the sovereign wealth fund created in 2007 with an initial US$200 billion drawn from reserves, and SAFE's Hong Kong-based investment arm. China has also substantially increased official gold holdings, rising above 2,200 tonnes in the mid-2020s.

Purpose and debate

The reserves serve as insurance against external shocks, support confidence in the exchange rate and financial system, and back China's ability to pay for imports and service foreign-currency debts. Economists debate the opportunity cost of holding such large low-yielding reserves, and analysts follow China's holdings as a factor in global bond markets and in the international role of the dollar.
