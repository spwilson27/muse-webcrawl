Gordon Tullock (13 February 1922 – 3 November 2014) was an American economist and professor of law and economics at George Mason University School of Law. He is best known, along with James M. Buchanan, as a co-founder of public choice theory, the application of economic methods to the study of politics and government.

Life and career

Tullock was born in Rockford, Illinois. He served in the United States Army during the Second World War, then studied at the University of Chicago, receiving a J.D. in 1947. After a brief career in private legal practice he joined the United States Foreign Service and was posted to Tianjin, China. He later taught at the University of South Carolina, the University of Virginia, Rice University, Virginia Polytechnic Institute, the University of Arizona, and finally George Mason University. With Charles Goetz and others he helped found the Center for Study of Public Choice, first at Virginia Tech and later at George Mason.

Contributions

The Calculus of Consent

With Buchanan, Tullock wrote The Calculus of Consent: Logical Foundations of Constitutional Democracy (1962), a foundational work of public choice that analysed voting rules, constitutions and collective decision-making using the assumption that political actors, like market actors, are self-interested.

Rent seeking

In a 1967 paper, "The Welfare Costs of Tariffs, Monopolies, and Theft", Tullock argued that the social cost of monopoly and protection includes not only the conventional deadweight-loss triangle but also the resources spent competing to obtain the privilege — the "Tullock rectangle". Anne Krueger later coined the term rent seeking for this behaviour, and the insight became one of the cornerstones of public choice.

Other work

Tullock's other books include The Politics of Bureaucracy (1965), The Logic of the Law (1971), The Vote Motive (1976) and The Economics of Special Privilege and Rent Seeking (1989). He also founded the journal Public Choice (originally Papers on Non-Market Decision Making) in 1966 and edited it for many years. His prolific and often provocative output ranged over voting paradoxes, the economics of crime, revolution, and even the biology of animal behaviour. Tullock was widely tipped as a possible Nobel laureate in economics but never received the prize.
