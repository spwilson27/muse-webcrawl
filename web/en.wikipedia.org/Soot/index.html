Soot

Aggregated soot particles

Soot is a mass of impure carbon particles resulting from the incomplete combustion of hydrocarbons, such as coal, wood, oil, and diesel fuel. It consists mostly of amorphous carbon, together with polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) and traces of metals and other compounds. Soot forms in flames when fuel-rich regions pyrolyse, producing tiny primary particles (typically 10–50 nm) that aggregate into chain-like clusters and deposit on cool surfaces as a black powdery or flaky residue.

Sources and effects

Major sources include diesel engines, residential wood and coal burning, industrial furnaces, wildfires, and shipping. Historically, soot blackened buildings and monuments in coal-burning cities and accumulated in chimneys, where chimney sweeps suffered the first described occupational cancer (scrotal cancer, identified by Percivall Pott in 1775).

Health and climate

Fine soot particles form part of PM2.5 air pollution and are linked to respiratory and cardiovascular disease and to lung cancer. As the atmospheric component called black carbon, soot absorbs sunlight, warming the air, and darkens snow and ice when deposited, accelerating melting; it is considered the second or third most important human-caused contributor to global warming after carbon dioxide and methane. Reducing soot emissions is therefore seen as a fast-acting climate and public-health measure, since black carbon remains in the atmosphere only days to weeks.

Uses

Controlled soot production yields carbon black, an industrial material used as a pigment and as a reinforcing filler in tyres and rubber products. Lampblack soot was for centuries the basis of inks and mascara.
