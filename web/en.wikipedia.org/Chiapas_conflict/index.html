The Chiapas conflict is the long-running confrontation between the Mexican government and indigenous peoples in the southern state of Chiapas, crystallised by the 1994 uprising of the Zapatista Army of National Liberation (EZLN). On 1 January 1994 — the day the North American Free Trade Agreement took effect — Zapatista rebels seized San Cristóbal de las Casas and several other towns, declaring war on the Mexican state in the name of indigenous rights, land, and democracy.

Course of the conflict

After twelve days of fighting, a ceasefire took hold and negotiations followed. The 1996 San Andrés Accords promised constitutional recognition of indigenous autonomy and culture, but the government declined to implement them, and a proposed law in 2001 was rejected by the EZLN as a betrayal. Tension was marked by paramilitary violence, most notoriously the December 1997 Acteal massacre, in which 45 Tzotzil civilians were killed.

Zapatista autonomy

From 2003 the EZLN governed de facto autonomous municipalities coordinated by regional centres called caracoles and Juntas de Buen Gobierno ("Good Government Councils"), running their own schools, clinics, and cooperatives outside state structures. In 2023 the EZLN announced a restructuring of its autonomous system in response to escalating organised-crime violence in Chiapas, while vowing to continue its civilian resistance.

Significance

Led publicly by the masked spokesman Subcomandante Marcos (later Galeano), the Zapatistas became an emblem of the global anti-globalisation movement, pioneering the use of the internet for solidarity campaigning. The conflict remains unresolved, with indigenous land disputes, militarisation, and cartel violence continuing in the region.
