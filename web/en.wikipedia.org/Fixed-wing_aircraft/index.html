Fixed-wing aircraft

A fixed-wing aircraft is a heavier-than-air flying machine whose wings are fixed in position and generate lift from the airflow produced by its forward speed. Aeroplanes are the most common type; the category also includes gliders.

Principles

Lift is produced as the wing's aerofoil section deflects air downward, and thrust from an engine (propeller or jet) or gravity (in gliders) provides the forward motion. Flight is controlled about three axes — pitch, roll, and yaw — using elevators, ailerons, and rudder, a system pioneered in practical form by the Wright brothers, whose 1903 Wright Flyer was the first powered, controlled fixed-wing aircraft.

Configurations

Fixed-wing aircraft are described by wing position (high-wing, low-wing, mid-wing), number of wing pairs (monoplane, biplane, triplane), and tail arrangement. Specialised forms include seaplanes, flying wings, and canard designs. They dominate civil air transport and most military aviation, in contrast to rotary-wing aircraft such as helicopters.
