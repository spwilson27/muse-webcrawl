Steam railways, born in northern England, reshaped transport, industry and time itself.

The history of rail transport began with primitive trackways — wooden rails laid in mines in 16th-century Europe, including German wagonways — and became one of the defining technologies of the Industrial Revolution. Guided ways of stone and wood existed in antiquity (such as the Diolkos of Corinth), but the modern railway emerged in Britain between 1800 and 1830.

Steam and the first railways

Richard Trevithick built the first steam locomotive to run on rails at Penydarren in Wales in 1804. Colliery railways in north-east England refined the technology; George Stephenson's Stockton and Darlington Railway (1825) was the first public railway to use steam locomotives, and the Liverpool and Manchester Railway (1830) — won by Stephenson's Rocket at the Rainhill Trials — was the first fully timetabled, double-track, steam-worked passenger line.

Expansion

Railway manias swept Britain in the 1840s; standard time ("railway time") and standard gauges followed. Networks spread across Europe, North America (where the First Transcontinental Railroad was completed in 1869) and the British Empire, transforming freight costs, urban growth, commuting and warfare. Brunel's broad gauge, the gauge wars, steel rails, block signalling and air brakes progressively raised speeds and safety. By 1900 electric tramways and underground lines (London, from 1863) served the cities.

Diesel, electric and high-speed eras

Diesel and electric traction displaced steam in most countries between the 1930s and 1970s. Road and air competition shrank many networks, but Japan's Shinkansen (1964) inaugurated high-speed rail, followed by France's TGV (1981) and China's vast high-speed network, the world's largest. Rail remains central to freight (especially bulk and container traffic) and urban transit worldwide, and is experiencing renewed growth driven by capacity and environmental concerns.
