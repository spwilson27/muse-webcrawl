Blood doping

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Blood doping is the practice of boosting the number of red blood cells in the bloodstream in order to enhance athletic performance. Because red blood cells carry oxygen from the lungs to the muscles, a higher concentration in the blood can improve an athlete's aerobic capacity (VO2max) and endurance. The three widely used types of blood doping are blood transfusions, injections of erythropoietin (EPO), and injections of synthetic oxygen carriers. Blood doping is considered unethical by most international sports organisations and is illegal under the World Anti-Doping Agency's prohibited list.

Methods

Transfusion

In autologous transfusion, an athlete's own blood is drawn weeks before competition, stored, and reinfused shortly before the event, raising haematocrit. Homologous transfusion uses blood from a matched donor; it is more readily detected and risks infection and transfusion reactions.

Erythropoietin

Recombinant human EPO, developed medically for anaemia, stimulates the bone marrow to produce red cells. Its abuse spread through professional cycling and endurance sports in the 1990s; a urine test distinguishing recombinant from natural EPO was introduced at the 2000 Sydney Olympics.

Synthetic oxygen carriers

Haemoglobin-based oxygen carriers and perfluorocarbons can temporarily transport oxygen but carry serious side-effect risks.

Detection and health risks

The Athlete Biological Passport, introduced by WADA in 2009, monitors longitudinal blood markers to detect doping indirectly. Risks of blood doping include dangerously increased blood viscosity, heart attack, stroke, pulmonary embolism, and infection.

Notable cases

Blood doping scandals have implicated numerous endurance athletes, notably in professional cycling during the EPO era, the Operación Puerto investigation in Spain (2006), and the disqualification of Olympic cross-country skiers at the 2002 Salt Lake City Games.
