The LM324 is a low-cost integrated circuit containing four independent operational amplifiers (a "quad op amp") in a single 14-pin package. Introduced by National Semiconductor in the early 1970s, it became one of the most widely used general-purpose analogue ICs in history and is manufactured by many semiconductor companies as a commodity part.

Characteristics

The LM324 is designed to run from a single power supply over a wide range (typically 3 V to 32 V, or split supplies of ±1.5 V to ±16 V), which made it unusually convenient for battery-powered and consumer equipment. Its inputs can operate down to the negative supply rail (ground in single-supply use) and its output can swing close to ground, properties that simplified circuit design before rail-to-rail op amps became common. Typical specifications include a gain-bandwidth product around 1 MHz, low supply current of roughly 700 µA for all four amplifiers, and internal frequency compensation.

Limitations and applications

The LM324's bipolar output stage exhibits crossover distortion in some audio and precision applications, and its speed and noise performance are modest by modern standards; nevertheless its low price and ruggedness have kept it in production for over fifty years. It is used in sensor amplifiers, filters, comparators, control loops, consumer electronics and countless hobbyist and industrial circuits. The dual version, the LM358, and the single LM321 share the same basic design.
