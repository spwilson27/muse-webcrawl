Cave

A cave is a natural void in the ground, specifically a space large enough for a human to enter. Caves often form by the weathering of rock and can extend deep underground. The scientific study of caves is called speleology.

Formation

Most caves are solutional caves, formed in soluble rock such as limestone by slightly acidic groundwater dissolving the rock along fractures — a process called karst. Other types include lava caves (formed in lava flows), sea caves (eroded by wave action), glacier caves, and erosional caves in sandstone.

Features

Caves commonly contain mineral deposits called speleothems, including stalactites hanging from the ceiling, stalagmites rising from the floor, and flowstones. Cave systems can extend for hundreds of kilometres; Mammoth Cave in Kentucky, USA, is the longest known, with more than 680 km of surveyed passages.

Ecology and human use

Caves harbour specialised organisms called troglobites, adapted to perpetual darkness. Humans have used caves since prehistory for shelter, burial, and ritual; famous cave paintings such as those at Lascaux and Altamira date back tens of thousands of years. Today caves are used for tourism, research, storage, and recreation (caving).
