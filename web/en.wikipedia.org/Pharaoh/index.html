Pharaoh

Pharaoh (from Egyptian pr-ꜥꜣ, "great house") was the common title of the monarchs of ancient Egypt from the First Dynasty (c. 3150 BCE) until the annexation of Egypt by the Roman Empire in 30 BCE, although the actual term "pharaoh" was not used contemporaneously for a ruler until about 1200 BCE, during the New Kingdom. In official documents the king held a fivefold titulary, and queens regnant such as Hatshepsut also bore the title.

Role and duties

The pharaoh was the political and religious leader of the Egyptian people, holding the titles "Lord of the Two Lands" and "High Priest of Every Temple". As a divine mediator between the gods and humans, the pharaoh was tasked with maintaining Ma'at — cosmic order, truth and justice — through rituals, law, and warfare against the forces of chaos (Isfet). The king owned all the land of Egypt, enacted laws, collected taxes, and served as commander-in-chief of the army.

Regalia and symbolism

The pharaoh's regalia included the Double Crown (pschent), combining the White Crown of Upper Egypt and the Red Crown of Lower Egypt; the striped nemes headdress; the false beard; and the crook and flail, symbols of kingship and fertility. The king's names were written within cartouches, and from the Fourth Dynasty the title "Son of Ra" linked the ruler to the sun god.

Crowns and ceremonies

Coronation and the Sed festival, traditionally celebrated after thirty years of reign, renewed the king's strength and legitimacy. After death, the pharaoh was believed to unite with Osiris, god of the afterlife, and was buried with grave goods in tombs such as those of the Valley of the Kings or, in the Old Kingdom, within pyramids.
