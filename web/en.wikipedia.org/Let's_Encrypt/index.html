Let's Encrypt is a non-profit certificate authority run by the Internet Security Research Group (ISRG) that provides X.509 certificates for Transport Layer Security (TLS) encryption at no charge. Launched in 2016, it is one of the world's largest certificate authorities and is credited with driving the web's transition to HTTPS by making certificates free and obtainable automatically.

History

The project grew out of work at the Electronic Frontier Foundation, Mozilla, and the University of Michigan, and was publicly announced in November 2014. It entered public beta in December 2015 and left beta in April 2016. Milestones included its 100 millionth certificate in 2017 and its billionth in 2020; it subsequently reported serving hundreds of millions of websites.

How it works

Let's Encrypt issues domain-validated certificates through the ACME protocol, which it helped develop and which was standardised by the IETF as RFC 8555. Server software such as Certbot proves control of a domain — typically by answering an HTTP or DNS challenge — and then requests, installs, and renews certificates without human intervention. Certificates are valid for 90 days, a deliberately short lifetime intended to encourage automation and limit the damage from key compromise.

The service issues no organisation-validation or extended-validation certificates, and it publishes every certificate it issues to Certificate Transparency logs. Funding comes from sponsors and donations; major backers have included Mozilla, the EFF, Cisco, Akamai, Google, and Facebook.

Impact

By removing cost and complexity as barriers, Let's Encrypt transformed HTTPS from a feature of large commercial sites into the default for the web: the share of pages loaded over HTTPS in major browsers rose from roughly 40 percent at its launch to well over 90 percent within several years.
