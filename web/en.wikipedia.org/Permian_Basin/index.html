Permian Basin

The Permian Basin is a large sedimentary basin in the southwestern United States, principally in West Texas and southeastern New Mexico. It is one of the world's most productive petroleum-producing regions, named for the thick sequence of Permian-age rocks deposited there when the area was covered by a shallow sea.

Geology

The basin formed as a downwarp during the late Palaeozoic. During the Permian period, the Delaware and Midland sub-basins flanked the shallower Central Basin Platform, and reef complexes such as the Capitan reef grew around their margins. Thick evaporites of the Castile and Salado formations later sealed the underlying reservoirs. Major producing formations include the Spraberry, Wolfcamp, Bone Spring and San Andres.

Oil and gas production

Commercial oil production began in the early 1920s, with the Santa Rita No. 1 well of 1923 a famous early strike on University of Texas lands. The basin powered much of the growth of Midland and Odessa. After a conventional-production peak in the 1970s, output revived dramatically after about 2010 through horizontal drilling and hydraulic fracturing of shale formations, making the Permian the largest oil-producing region in the United States and one of the largest in the world, with production exceeding five million barrels per day in the early 2020s.

Economy and environment

The region's economy is dominated by upstream oil and gas, pipelines, refining and petrochemicals. Rapid growth has brought concerns about flaring and methane emissions, induced seismicity linked to wastewater injection, water use, and road safety during boom cycles.
