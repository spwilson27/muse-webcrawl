The Strait of Malacca is a narrow stretch of water, about 930 km long, between the Malay Peninsula and the Indonesian island of Sumatra. It is one of the most important shipping lanes in the world, the main channel connecting the Indian Ocean with the Pacific Ocean and the economies of East Asia.

Importance

Roughly a quarter of the world's traded goods — and the great majority of China's and Japan's oil imports — pass through the strait each year; more than 15 million barrels of oil per day transit it. Its narrowest point, the Phillips Channel near Singapore, is only 2.8 km wide, making the strait a strategic chokepoint; China's leaders have spoken of a "Malacca dilemma" — dependence on a lane that could be blocked in a conflict. The largest ships that can transit ("Malaccamax") are limited by the strait's ~25 m depth.

History

The strait has carried trade since antiquity between India, China, the Middle East and Southeast Asia. Control of it made the fortunes of the Srivijaya empire, the Malacca Sultanate (whose port, Malacca, gives the strait its name), then the Portuguese (1511), Dutch (1641) and British, who founded Singapore in 1819 at its southern end. Today Singapore is the world's second-busiest container port and largest transshipment hub.

Security

Piracy was a serious problem in the early 2000s; coordinated patrols by Malaysia, Indonesia, Singapore and Thailand (the Malacca Straits Patrol, 2004) sharply reduced incidents. Navigation is managed under a Traffic Separation Scheme, and large vessels must report to the littoral states' vessel traffic systems.
