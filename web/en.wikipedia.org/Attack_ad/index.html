Attack ad

In political campaigns, an attack ad is a television, radio, print or online advertisement whose purpose is to damage an opponent's reputation or candidacy rather than to promote the sponsor's own record. Attack ads are a central feature of negative campaigning, particularly in the United States.

Attack ads typically use dramatic imagery and voiceover to criticise an opponent.

History

Although negative campaigning is as old as electoral politics, the television era transformed it. The famous "Daisy" advertisement of the 1964 United States presidential campaign, aired once by Lyndon B. Johnson's campaign, implied that electing Barry Goldwater risked nuclear war and is often regarded as the first modern attack ad. Later landmarks include the "Willie Horton" ad of 1988 and the Swift Boat Veterans advertisements of 2004.

Techniques

Attack ads commonly use selective quotations, unflattering photographs, ominous music, distorted records of votes or statements, and "push poll" style insinuation. Disclosure rules in the United States require candidates to appear in or approve their advertisements ("I'm [candidate] and I approve this message"), while independent groups such as super PACs — which are not subject to the same requirement — sponsor a large share of the harshest ads.

Effectiveness

Research on the effectiveness of attack ads is mixed. Studies find they can be memorable and can lower an opponent's support, but they can also provoke backlash against the sponsor and depress turnout. Meta-analyses have generally concluded that negative advertisements are no more effective on average than positive ones, though they attract disproportionate media attention.
