Franz Uri Boas (9 July 1858 – 21 December 1942) was a German-born American anthropologist, often called the "father of American anthropology". He founded the discipline's first PhD programme at Columbia University, trained a generation of leading anthropologists, and mounted a lifelong scientific challenge to theories of racial hierarchy.

Life and career

Boas studied physics and geography in Germany, earning his doctorate at Kiel in 1881. Fieldwork among the Inuit of Baffin Island in 1883–84 redirected him toward ethnology. After emigrating to the United States he worked at Clark University, organised exhibits for the 1893 World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago, and in 1896 joined the American Museum of Natural History and Columbia University, where he became a full professor in 1899. He led the Jesup North Pacific Expedition (1897–1902) and did extensive fieldwork among the Kwakiutl (Kwakwaka'wakw) of British Columbia.

Ideas

In works such as The Mind of Primitive Man (1911) and Race, Language, and Culture (1940), Boas argued that culture, not biological race, shapes human behaviour; that each culture must be understood in its own historical context (historical particularism); and that cultures should be judged by their own standards (cultural relativism). His study of the children of immigrants showed that even bodily form responds to environment, undermining fixed racial typologies. His students included Margaret Mead, Ruth Benedict, Alfred Kroeber, Edward Sapir and Zora Neale Hurston.
