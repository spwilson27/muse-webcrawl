Seneca the Younger

Lucius Annaeus Seneca the Younger (c. 4 BC – AD 65), usually known simply as Seneca, was a Stoic philosopher of Ancient Rome, a statesman, and a dramatist. As a statesman he served as imperial advisor to Nero and as Imperial Roman consul; he was forced to take his own life following accusations of having been part of the Pisonian conspiracy to assassinate Nero.

Life

Seneca was born in Cordoba in Hispania, the second son of Seneca the Elder, a famous rhetorician. He was educated in Rome in rhetoric and philosophy and rose to prominence as a senator under Claudius. In AD 41 he was exiled to Corsica on a charge of adultery with Julia Livilla; he was recalled in 49 by Agrippina the Younger to become tutor to her son, the future emperor Nero. When Nero became emperor in 54, Seneca, together with the praetorian prefect Burrus, effectively governed the empire during the celebrated first years of Nero's reign (the quinquennium Neronis). His influence waned after Burrus's death in 62, and in 65 he was implicated in the Pisonian conspiracy and ordered to commit suicide, which he did in the manner of Socrates.

Works

Seneca's surviving philosophical works include the Dialogues, the Letters to Lucilius (Epistulae Morales), the treatises De Clementia and De Beneficiis, and the Naturales Quaestiones. His nine tragedies, including Medea, Phaedra, and Thyestes, profoundly influenced the development of European drama, especially during the Renaissance. He also wrote the Apocolocyntosis, a satire on the deification of Claudius.

Philosophy

As a Stoic, Seneca emphasized ethics and the rational ordering of life in accordance with nature. His writings stress the brevity of life, the proper use of time, self-examination, and indifference to wealth and fortune — though his own immense wealth attracted criticism, both in antiquity and since.
