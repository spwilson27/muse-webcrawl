Sarcomere

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sarcomere
DefinitionSegment of a myofibril between two Z-lines
LengthAbout 2.2 μm at rest in vertebrate muscle
Main proteinsActin, myosin, titin, nebulin, tropomyosin, troponin
FunctionSmallest functional unit of striated muscle contraction

A sarcomere (Greek sarx, "flesh", and meros, "part") is the smallest functional unit of striated muscle tissue. It is the repeating unit between two Z-lines within a myofibril, and the orderly arrangement of thousands of sarcomeres gives skeletal and cardiac muscle their striated appearance under the microscope. Muscle contraction results from the concerted shortening of sarcomeres along the fiber.

Structure

Each sarcomere is bounded by Z-discs, to which thin filaments of the protein actin are anchored, extending toward the center. Thick filaments of myosin occupy the central A band; their cross-bridge heads project toward the actin. The I band contains only thin filaments, the H zone only thick filaments, and the M line at the center anchors the myosin. The giant elastic protein titin runs from the Z-disc to the M line and provides passive elasticity, while nebulin helps set thin-filament length.

Contraction

Under the sliding filament theory, contraction occurs when myosin heads bind actin and pull the thin filaments toward the sarcomere center: the Z-discs move closer together while the filaments themselves do not shorten. Calcium ions released from the sarcoplasmic reticulum bind troponin, moving tropomyosin to expose binding sites on actin; ATP powers the cross-bridge cycle. The length-tension relationship — maximal force at an optimal sarcomere length of about 2.2 μm — reflects the degree of filament overlap.
