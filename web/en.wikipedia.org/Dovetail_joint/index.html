Dovetail joint

A dovetail joint, or simply dovetail, is a woodworking joint technique most commonly used in furniture — particularly for drawers, cabinets, and boxes — that is noted for its resistance to being pulled apart. The joint consists of a series of trapezoid-shaped pins cut on the end of one board that interlock with a series of matching tails cut on the end of another board. The flared shape of the tails, resembling a dove's tail, mechanically locks the two boards together even before glue is applied.

History

The dovetail technique predates written history; examples are found in furniture entombed with mummies in ancient Egypt and in Chinese imperial tombs. In European furniture it became standard for drawer construction from the late 17th century onward, and the proportions and neatness of a craftsman's dovetails remain a traditional mark of skill.

Types

  • Through dovetail — the end grain of both boards is visible; the standard joint for boxes and carcasses.
  • Half-blind (single-lap) dovetail — the end grain is not visible from the front; commonly used to join drawer fronts to drawer sides.
  • Secret mitred dovetail — fully concealed, used in the finest cabinet work.
  • Dovetail slide — a mating tongue-and-groove-like form used for sliding parts such as table leaves and tool slides.

Making dovetails

Dovetails may be cut by hand with a backsaw and chisels, or with a router and jig. Typical slopes are 1:6 for softwoods and 1:8 for hardwoods. The joint requires little glue because of its mechanical interlock, and its visible interlocking figure is often left exposed as a decorative feature in contemporary furniture and timber framing.
