Legal systems of the world

The legal systems of the world are generally divided among five main types: civil law, common law, customary law, religious law, and mixed legal systems that combine elements of these. Civil law (derived from Roman law and codified in comprehensive codes) is the most widespread system worldwide, followed by common law (developed in England and based heavily on precedent). Many jurisdictions — including Louisiana, Quebec, Scotland, South Africa and most of sub-Saharan Africa and the Middle East — operate mixed systems.

Civil law

Civil law systems descend from Roman law, systematized in Justinian's Corpus Juris Civilis and revived in medieval universities. Their hallmark is comprehensive codification — civil codes (notably the French Napoleonic Code of 1804 and the German BGB of 1900) are the primary source of law, and precedent is formally less binding than in common law. Judges apply codes to facts in a comparatively inquisitorial procedure. Civil law dominates continental Europe, Latin America, much of Asia (Japan, South Korea, China in structure), and francophone Africa.

Common law

Common law developed in medieval England from the decisions of royal courts and was exported throughout the British Empire: it prevails in England and Wales, the United States (except Louisiana), Canada (except Quebec), Australia, India, Pakistan, Nigeria and other Commonwealth jurisdictions. Its distinguishing features are stare decisis (binding precedent), adversarial procedure, and historically the split between law and equity. Statutes have grown in importance, but case law remains a primary source.

Religious law

Religious legal systems derive their norms from sacred texts and traditions: Islamic law (Sharia) governs wholly or in part in countries such as Saudi Arabia and Iran and supplies family-law rules in many Muslim-majority states; Jewish Halakha and Christian canon law govern internal church matters and specific personal-status questions. In practice most such jurisdictions mix religious with civil or common law.

Customary law

Customary law consists of unwritten norms and practices recognized as binding within a community; it remains important in much of Africa, in Indigenous legal orders worldwide, and in international law, where customary international law is a formal source.

Comparative study

Comparative lawyers group systems into "legal families" by style — sources of law, procedure, concepts and ideology. Classifications vary: older schemes (René David) used families such as Romano-Germanic, common law, socialist and religious/traditional; modern scholarship stresses functional comparison and legal transplants, noting that systems converge and borrow constantly.
