Phase angle (astronomy)

In observational astronomy, the phase angle is the angle between the light incident onto an observed object (typically from the Sun) and the light reflected from the object toward the observer. More precisely, it is the angle at the object in the triangle formed by the Sun, the object, and the observer: the Sun–object–observer angle. It is conventionally denoted by the Greek letter φ or by the symbol g in asteroid photometry.

Relation to phases

A phase angle of 0° means the observer looks along the direction of illumination, so the object appears fully lit ("full"); at 90° roughly half the visible disc is illuminated; and at 180° the object's dark side faces the observer ("new"). The phases of the Moon and of the inferior planets Venus and Mercury follow directly from the changing phase angle over an orbit. For objects beyond Earth's orbit, the phase angle as seen from Earth is always small, so Mars, Jupiter and the other outer planets always appear nearly full.

Applications

Phase angle governs the observed brightness of planets, asteroids, and comets; the dependence of brightness on phase angle (the phase curve) carries information about surface texture and composition. The sharp brightening near zero phase angle, the opposition surge, is a key photometric phenomenon. Spacecraft imaging likewise plans observations around phase geometry.
