Coriolis force
TypeFictitious (inertial) force
Named afterGaspard-Gustave de Coriolis
FormulaF = −2m(Ω × v)

In physics, the Coriolis force is an inertial or fictitious force that acts on objects in motion within a frame of reference that rotates with respect to an inertial frame. In a reference frame with clockwise rotation, the force acts to the left of the direction of motion; in one with counter-clockwise (anticlockwise) rotation, to the right. Deflection of an object due to the Coriolis force is called the Coriolis effect. The French scientist Gaspard-Gustave de Coriolis described the force mathematically in 1835 in connection with water wheels.

Formula

For a body of mass m moving with velocity v in a frame rotating with angular velocity Ω, the Coriolis acceleration is −2(Ω × v). On Earth, the horizontal deflection depends on the Coriolis parameter f = 2Ω sin φ, where φ is latitude: the effect is zero at the equator and maximal at the poles.

Meteorology and oceanography

The Coriolis effect is essential to large-scale atmospheric and oceanic dynamics. It deflects moving air to the right in the Northern Hemisphere and to the left in the Southern Hemisphere, causing cyclones to rotate counter-clockwise in the north and clockwise in the south, and shaping trade winds, jet streams and ocean gyres. It underlies geostrophic balance, in which pressure-gradient forces are balanced by Coriolis deflection. Because the effect is proportional to the size and duration of motion, it is negligible for small, short-lived flows — contrary to the popular myth, it does not determine the direction water swirls down household drains.

Other applications

The effect matters in ballistics and long-range artillery, in the precession of Foucault pendulums, in the Eötvös effect on gravimetry, and in engineering devices such as Coriolis mass flow meters and vibrating gyroscopes used in navigation.
