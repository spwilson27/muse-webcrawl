Elektronische Musik

Elektronische Musik (German for "electronic music") is a term associated with the school of electronic composition that emerged in West Germany in the early 1950s, centred on the Studio for Electronic Music of the NWDR in Cologne.

Origins

Founded in 1951 with the involvement of Werner Meyer-Eppler, Robert Beyer and Herbert Eimert, the Cologne studio pursued music built entirely from electronically generated sounds, such as sine tones combined through additive synthesis — a deliberate contrast with the French musique concrète, which manipulated recorded sounds.

Composers

Karlheinz Stockhausen became the studio's most prominent figure, producing works such as Studie I and Studie II (the first electronic work published in score form) and Gesang der Jünglinge (1956), which combined electronic sounds with a recorded boy's voice. Other associated composers included Karel Goeyvaerts, Gottfried Michael Koenig and Henri Pousseur.

Legacy

The Cologne approach influenced serial composition and later studio practice, and its techniques helped lay groundwork for later electronic and computer music.
