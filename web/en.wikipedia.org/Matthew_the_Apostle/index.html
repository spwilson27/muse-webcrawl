Matthew the Apostle (Greek: Ματθαῖος, Matthaios) was, according to the New Testament, one of the twelve apostles of Jesus and the traditional author of the Gospel of Matthew. Before his calling he was a tax collector (telōnēs) at Capernaum, a profession despised for its collaboration with the Roman occupation.

In the New Testament

The Gospel of Matthew recounts that Jesus saw Matthew "sitting at the tax booth" and said "Follow me," and Matthew rose and followed, afterwards hosting a dinner at which Jesus ate with tax collectors and sinners. The Gospels of Mark and Luke tell the same story of a tax collector named Levi, son of Alphaeus; tradition identifies Levi and Matthew as the same person. Matthew appears in all the lists of the Twelve but is otherwise little mentioned in the New Testament.

Tradition and martyrdom

Later tradition held that Matthew preached in Judea and then in Ethiopia (or, in other accounts, Persia or Macedonia), where he was martyred. His relics are venerated at Salerno Cathedral in Italy, where they were said to have been brought in the 10th century.

Symbol and art

Matthew's evangelist symbol is the winged man or angel, because his Gospel begins with the genealogy of Jesus. His calling is a famous subject in art, notably in Caravaggio's The Calling of Saint Matthew in San Luigi dei Francesi, Rome.
