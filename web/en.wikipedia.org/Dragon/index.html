Dragon

A winged European dragon

A dragon is a magical legendary creature that appears in the folklore of multiple cultures worldwide. Beliefs about dragons vary considerably through time and region. In Western cultures since the High Middle Ages, dragons have typically been depicted as winged, horned, and capable of breathing fire. In Eastern cultures, such as Chinese culture, dragons are usually portrayed as wingless, four-legged, serpentine creatures with above-average intelligence. Common features — sometimes called "dragon traits" — include snake-like, reptilian or avian features.

Etymology

The word "dragon" entered English in the early 13th century from Old French dragon, from Latin draco (genitive draconis), from Ancient Greek δράκων (drákōn), "serpent" or "giant seafish", possibly from a verb meaning "to see clearly".

Western dragons

Greco-Roman traditions featured serpent-like dragon guardians such as Ladon of the Hesperides and the dragon of Colchis guarding the Golden Fleece. Germanic and Norse mythology gave the dragon-slayer legends of Sigurd and Fáfnir, and Beowulf's final battle with a fire-breathing dragon. In medieval Christian symbolism the dragon often represented Satan or sin; saints such as Saint George became famous dragon-slayers, while heraldic dragons — including the red dragon of Wales — signified valour and sovereignty. Literary dragons from Smaug in Tolkien's The Hobbit to modern fantasy continue the hoard-guarding, fire-breathing type.

Eastern dragons

The Chinese dragon (long) is a benevolent symbol of power, strength and good fortune, associated with water, rain, rivers and imperial authority; the emperor's throne was the Dragon Throne. Chinese dragons have features of nine animals, hold or chase a flaming pearl, and rank in elaborate celestial hierarchies. Related traditions include the Japanese ryū, Korean yong, and Vietnamese rồng. Dragon dances and dragon boat festivals remain major cultural celebrations.

Origins of the myth

Scholars have suggested that dragon myths arose from encounters with large reptiles (crocodiles, monitor lizards, pythons), from fossil bones interpreted as dragon remains (Protoceratops skulls have been linked to griffin legends), and from deep-seated primate fears of snakes, big cats and birds of prey combined into a composite predator.
