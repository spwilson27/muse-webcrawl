Classical logic

Classical logic (or standard logic) or Frege–Russell logic is the class of deductive systems most widely studied and used. It encompasses propositional logic and first-order (predicate) logic as developed from the work of Aristotle through Gottlob Frege, Bertrand Russell, and their successors. The term "classical" was coined in contrast to non-classical logics, which reject one or more of its principles.

Principles

Classical logic is characterised by several principles:

  • Law of excluded middle and double negation elimination: every proposition is either true or false (bivalence), and ¬¬p implies p.
  • Law of non-contradiction: a proposition and its negation cannot both be true.
  • Principle of explosion (ex falso quodlibet): from a contradiction, anything follows.
  • Monotonicity of entailment: adding premises can never invalidate a valid conclusion.
  • Truth-functionality: the truth value of a compound statement is determined by the truth values of its parts.

History

The foundations were laid by Aristotle's syllogistic logic, radically extended in the late nineteenth century by Frege's Begriffsschrift (1879), which introduced quantifiers and a formal language adequate to mathematics, and by Russell and Whitehead's Principia Mathematica (1910–1913). In the twentieth century, Gödel's completeness and incompleteness theorems, Tarski's semantics, and Gentzen's proof systems consolidated classical first-order logic as the standard framework for mathematics and analytic philosophy.

Alternatives

Non-classical logics modify classical assumptions for particular purposes: intuitionistic logic rejects excluded middle (motivated by constructive mathematics); relevance and paraconsistent logics tolerate contradictions without explosion; modal, temporal, and many-valued logics extend or alter the classical base.
