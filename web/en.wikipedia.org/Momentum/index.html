Momentum


Image placeholder: Newton's cradle

In Newtonian mechanics, momentum (more specifically linear momentum or translational momentum) is the product of the mass and velocity of an object. It is a vector quantity, possessing both magnitude and direction, measured in kilogram-metres per second (kg·m/s). Newton called it the "quantity of motion."

p = mv

Newton's second law of motion states that the rate of change of a body's momentum equals the net force acting on it: F = dp/dt, which reduces to F = ma when mass is constant. The change in momentum caused by a force acting over time is called impulse, J = FΔt = Δp.

Conservation

If no external force acts on a system, its total momentum remains constant — the law of conservation of momentum. Momentum is therefore conserved in collisions: in elastic collisions both momentum and kinetic energy are conserved; in inelastic collisions only momentum is. Conservation of momentum follows from the homogeneity of space via Noether's theorem, and it holds in relativistic mechanics and quantum mechanics as well, where momentum is defined with the Lorentz factor γ (p = γmv) or as an operator, respectively. Photons, though massless, carry momentum p = E/c = h/λ.

History

The concept developed from medieval theories of impetus (notably Jean Buridan's), through Descartes's "quantity of motion," to Leibniz's rival vis viva (proportional to mv²), a controversy resolved when both momentum and kinetic energy were recognised as distinct conserved-type quantities in different contexts.
