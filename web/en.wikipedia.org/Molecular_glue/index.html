Molecular glue


Class: Small-molecule drug modality
Examples: Cyclosporin, rapamycin, lenalidomide
Mechanism: Induced protein–protein interaction

Molecular glues are small molecules that stabilize the interaction between two proteins that would not otherwise bind strongly, or that induce a new interaction between them. By bringing proteins together, molecular glues can trigger downstream effects such as inhibition of a signalling protein or the targeted destruction of a disease-causing protein.

Mechanism

A molecular glue typically binds to one protein and creates or reshapes a surface that recruits a second protein, forming a ternary complex. Well-known natural examples include the immunosuppressants cyclosporin and rapamycin (sirolimus), which glue immunophilins to calcineurin and mTOR respectively. The immunomodulatory drugs lenalidomide and pomalidomide act as glues between the E3 ubiquitin ligase cereblon and zinc-finger transcription factors, marking them for degradation by the proteasome.

Drug discovery

Historically, molecular glues were discovered by chance rather than by design, and most known examples were found only after their clinical effects were observed. Interest has grown because glues can drug "undruggable" targets that lack conventional binding pockets, and because, unlike the related PROTAC degraders, they are small, single molecules with favourable drug-like properties. Rational discovery strategies using phenotypic screening, chemoproteomics, and structure-based design are an active area of pharmaceutical research.
