Chichén Itzá

Chichén Itzá
Location: Tinúm Municipality, Yucatán, Mexico
Culture: Maya
Peak period: c. 800–1200 CE
Notable structures: El Castillo (Temple of Kukulcán), Great Ball Court, Temple of the Warriors
Status: UNESCO World Heritage Site (1988)

Chichén Itzá was a large pre-Columbian city built by the Maya people of the Terminal Classic period, located in Tinúm Municipality, Yucatán State, Mexico. The name means "at the mouth of the well of the Itzá" in Yucatec Maya, referring to the natural sinkholes (cenotes) that supplied water. It was one of the largest and most powerful Maya cities, dominating northern Yucatán between roughly the 9th and 12th centuries CE.

Major structures

The most famous building is El Castillo, the step pyramid of the feathered-serpent god Kukulcán, whose stairways produce a shadow resembling a descending serpent at the equinoxes. Other major monuments include the Great Ball Court — the largest in Mesoamerica — the Temple of the Warriors with its colonnades and chacmool figures, the observatory known as El Caracol, and the Sacred Cenote, into which offerings of gold, jade, and human sacrifices were cast.

History

Chichén Itzá rose during the decline of the southern lowland Maya cities, and its architecture blends Puuc Maya styles with influences from central Mexico, long attributed to the Itzá people and to contacts with Toltec Tula. The city declined after about 1200 CE, though it remained a pilgrimage site into the Spanish colonial period. Excavations by the Carnegie Institution in the 1920s–30s restored many structures, and in 2007 El Castillo was named one of the New7Wonders of the World.
