Export processing zone

An export processing zone (EPZ) is a designated area within a country, usually near a port or airport, where goods may be imported, manufactured and re-exported without the intervention of customs authorities or payment of import duties. EPZs are created by governments to attract foreign investment, generate employment, earn foreign exchange and encourage industrialisation oriented toward export markets.

Features

Firms operating in an EPZ typically receive exemptions from import duties on machinery and raw materials, tax holidays, streamlined licensing and permission to repatriate profits. In return, they are generally required to export most or all of their output. Inputs brought into the zone are treated as being outside the national customs territory until they enter domestic commerce.

History and spread

One of the earliest modern zones was established at Shannon Airport in Ireland in 1959. The model spread to East and Southeast Asia — including Taiwan's Kaohsiung zone (1966) and sites in South Korea, Malaysia, the Philippines and later China — and to Latin America, where the Mexican maquiladora programme performs a similar function. By the early 2000s the International Labour Organization counted several thousand zones in more than 100 countries, employing tens of millions of workers, the majority of them women in garments, footwear and electronics assembly.

Criticism

EPZs are criticised for weak labour protections, restrictions on union organisation, low wages and limited technology transfer to the host economy. Supporters reply that zone jobs often pay more than local alternatives and can serve as a first step in industrial development, as in the East Asian economies and, later, Bangladesh's garment industry.
