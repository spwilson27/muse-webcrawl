Meningioma

A meningioma is a tumor that arises from the meninges, the membranes that surround the brain and spinal cord. Most meningiomas are benign (WHO grade I), slow-growing, and are the most common primary tumors of the central nervous system, accounting for roughly a third of all primary brain and spinal cord tumors. They occur more often in women than men and most frequently in middle-aged and older adults.

Signs and symptoms

Many meningiomas are found incidentally on imaging and cause no symptoms. Symptomatic tumors produce effects by pressing on adjacent brain or nerves: headaches, seizures, weakness, vision changes or personality changes, depending on location. Spinal meningiomas may cause pain and progressive weakness or numbness in the limbs.

Causes and diagnosis

Known risk factors include prior ionizing radiation to the head and the genetic disorder neurofibromatosis type 2, in which tumors arise from loss of the NF2 gene on chromosome 22. Hormonal influences are suspected because of the female predominance and the presence of progesterone receptors in many tumors. Diagnosis is by medical imaging, typically contrast-enhanced MRI, which classically shows a dural-based mass with a "dural tail"; confirmation is by histopathology after biopsy or resection. The World Health Organization classifies meningiomas into grades I, II (atypical) and III (anaplastic/malignant) based on histological features.

Treatment

Small, asymptomatic tumors may simply be observed with serial imaging. Symptomatic or growing tumors are usually treated with surgical resection; complete removal can be curative for grade I tumors. Radiotherapy or stereotactic radiosurgery is used for unresectable, recurrent or higher-grade tumors. Prognosis for benign meningioma is generally good, though recurrence is possible even after apparent complete removal.
