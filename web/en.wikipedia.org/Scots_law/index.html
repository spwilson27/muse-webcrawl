Scots law is the legal system of Scotland. It is a hybrid or mixed legal system containing civil law and common law elements, that traces its roots to a number of different sources. Together with English law and Northern Ireland law, it is one of the three legal systems of the United Kingdom. Scots law recognises four sources of law: legislation, legal precedent, specific academic writings, and custom. Legislation affecting Scotland and Scots law is passed by the Scottish Parliament on all areas of devolved responsibility, and the United Kingdom Parliament on reserved matters. Some legislation passed by the pre-1707 Parliament of Scotland is still also in force.

Early Scots law before the 12th century consisted of the different legal traditions of the various cultural groups who inhabited the country at the time, the Gaels in most of the country, with the Britons and Anglo-Saxons in the south-east, and the Norse in the north and the islands. The introduction of feudalism from the 12th century and the expansion of the Kingdom of Scotland established the modern roots of Scots law, which was gradually influenced by other, especially continental, legal traditions. Although there was some indirect influence from Roman law, in Scots law the direct influence of Roman law was slight up until around the middle of the 15th century. After this time, Roman law was often adopted in argument in court, and increasingly some of its principles were adopted into Scots law. After the Acts of Union 1707, Scotland shared a legislature with England and Wales, and although Scotland retained a fundamentally different legal system, the Union brought English influence on Scots law.

Courts

The judiciary of Scotland, the College of Justice, are organised into the Court of Session for civil matters, and the High Court of Justiciary for criminal matters. The supreme court for civil cases from Scotland is the Supreme Court of the United Kingdom. Sheriff courts deal with most civil and criminal cases, including conducting criminal trials with a jury, known as sheriff solemn court, or with a sheriff and no jury, known as sheriff summary court. Justices of the peace courts deal with minor offences, mostly traffic offences. Scotland's unique feature, the procurator fiscal, investigates all sudden, suspicious, accidental and unexplained deaths — a role carried out under the supervision of the Lord Advocate.

Branches of law

Scots law is divided into two main categories: private law, which covers areas such as contract, delict (the Scots equivalent of tort), property, family and succession; and public law, which includes constitutional law, administrative law and criminal law. The Scots criminal standard of proof and verdicts differ subtly from those in England: there are three verdicts — guilty, not guilty, and not proven — the last being an acquittal that has no equivalent elsewhere in the United Kingdom.

Sources of law

The institutional writers of Scots law — notably Viscount Stair (Institutions of the Law of Scotland, 1681), Erskine, Bell and Baron Hume — are recognised as formal sources of law where no other authority exists, a feature without parallel in English law.
