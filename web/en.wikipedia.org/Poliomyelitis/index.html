Poliomyelitis, commonly shortened to polio, is an infectious disease caused by the poliovirus. Approximately 75% of cases are asymptomatic; mild symptoms which can occur include sore throat and fever; in a proportion of cases more severe symptoms develop such as headache, neck stiffness, and paresthesia. These symptoms usually pass within one or two weeks. A less common symptom is permanent paralysis, and possible death in extreme cases.

Years after recovery, post-polio syndrome may develop, with a slow development of muscle weakness similar to that which the person had during the initial infection. Poliovirus is usually spread from person to person through infected fecal matter entering the mouth. It may also be spread by food or water containing human feces and less commonly from infected saliva.

History

Poliomyelitis has existed for thousands of years, depictions of which are visible in ancient Egyptian art. Major epidemics began to appear in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. The disease caused widespread panic in the United States during the first half of the 20th century, and people with the disease were sometimes placed in iron lungs to assist breathing.

Vaccine and eradication

Two vaccines are used throughout the world to combat poliomyelitis. The first was developed by Jonas Salk and first tested in 1952; announced to the world on April 12, 1955, the Salk vaccine consists of an injected dose of inactivated poliovirus. An oral vaccine was developed by Albert Sabin, coming into commercial use in 1961.

The Global Polio Eradication Initiative, launched in 1988 by the World Health Organization, Rotary International, the CDC, and UNICEF, has reduced the number of annual cases by more than 99.9%. Wild poliovirus types 2 and 3 have been certified eradicated, and type 1 transmission remains endemic only in Afghanistan and Pakistan, though vaccine-derived outbreaks remain a challenge in under-immunized communities.

Mechanism

Poliovirus infects the motor neurons of the spinal cord and brainstem. Destruction of these neurons prevents them from sending signals to muscles, leading to flaccid paralysis. The degree of paralysis depends on the number and location of neurons destroyed.
