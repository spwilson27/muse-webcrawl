Revolver

A revolver is a repeating handgun that has at least one barrel and uses a revolving cylinder containing multiple chambers (each holding a single cartridge) for firing. Because most revolver models hold six cartridges (in the past, some held five, while the earliest held seven), revolvers are commonly called six-shooters.

Before firing a round, cocking the hammer partially rotates the cylinder, indexing the next chamber and aligning it with the barrel. The revolver allows the user to fire multiple rounds without reloading after every shot, unlike older single-shot firearms. After firing all of the cartridges, the cylinder must be unloaded and reloaded manually.

History

Revolving firearms predate the modern revolver: revolving matchlock arquebuses were made in the 16th century, and Elisha Collier patented a flintlock revolver in Britain in 1818. The first practical and commercially successful revolver was Samuel Colt's 1836 Paterson, based on his patent for a revolving cylinder with a locking mechanism. Colt's later models — the Walker (1847), the Dragoon, the Navy Model 1851, and the Army Model 1860 — became standard sidearms of the American frontier and the Civil War. Smith & Wesson introduced bored-through cylinders for metallic cartridges with the Model 1 of 1857, and the Colt Single Action Army of 1873 ("Peacemaker") became an icon of the Old West. Double-action designs matured in the late 19th century, and the swing-out cylinder, pioneered by Colt (1889) and perfected by Smith & Wesson (1899 Model 10), set the pattern for modern revolvers.

Design

The revolver's defining component is the cylinder, rotated about a central axis pin by a pawl (hand) engaging a ratchet at its rear as the hammer is cocked or the trigger pulled. Locking is ensured by a bolt that engages notches in the cylinder so that a chamber aligns precisely with the barrel at the moment of firing. A small gap between cylinder and barrel vents some propellant gas, one reason revolvers cannot be effectively suppressed. Frames may be solid, top-break, or side-opening; calibers range from .22 rimfire to powerful magnum cartridges such as .357 Magnum, .44 Magnum, and .500 S&W Magnum. Because the revolver does not depend on cartridge energy to cycle an action, it can fire very powerful or very mild loads interchangeably and is largely immune to ammunition-related malfunctions.

Modes of operation

In a single-action revolver, the trigger performs only the single function of releasing the hammer, which must be cocked manually before each shot. In a double-action revolver, one pull of the trigger both cocks and releases the hammer, enabling faster fire with a longer, heavier pull; most double-action revolvers can also be thumb-cocked for single-action shooting. Double-action-only designs, with concealed or bobbed hammers, are intended for defensive carry.

Loading mechanisms

Early cartridge revolvers loaded through a side gate, one round at a time, with spent cases pushed out by an ejector rod. Top-break designs (such as the Smith & Wesson Schofield and the Webley) hinge open to eject all cases simultaneously. Swing-out cylinders likewise eject all chambers at once via a star extractor, and can be reloaded quickly with speedloaders or moon clips.

Modern role

Semi-automatic pistols displaced revolvers in most military and police service during the 20th century because of their greater capacity and faster reloading. Revolvers remain popular for concealed carry (small-frame "snub-nose" models), hunting with magnum calibers, sport shooting, and as backup weapons, and are valued for their mechanical simplicity and reliability.
