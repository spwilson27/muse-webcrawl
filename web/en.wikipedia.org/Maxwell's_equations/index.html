Maxwell's equations

Maxwell's equations are a set of four partial differential equations that form the foundation of classical electromagnetism, classical optics and electric circuits. Formulated by the Scottish physicist James Clerk Maxwell in the 1860s — building on the experimental work of Faraday, Ampère, Gauss and others — they describe how electric and magnetic fields are generated by charges and currents, and how they propagate. Together with the Lorentz force law, they constitute the complete classical theory of electromagnetism.

The equations

In differential form, using E for the electric field, B for the magnetic field, ρ for charge density and J for current density:

Gauss's law

∇ · E = ρ / ε₀

Electric field lines begin and end on electric charges; the flux out of a closed surface is proportional to the enclosed charge.

Gauss's law for magnetism

∇ · B = 0

There are no magnetic monopoles; magnetic field lines are closed loops.

Faraday's law of induction

∇ × E = −∂B/∂t

A changing magnetic field induces a curling electric field — the principle of the electric generator and transformer.

Ampère's law with Maxwell's addition

∇ × B = μ₀J + μ₀ε₀ ∂E/∂t

Magnetic fields are generated by electric currents and by changing electric fields (the displacement current term Maxwell added).

Consequences

In empty space the equations predict self-sustaining electromagnetic waves travelling at c = 1/√(ε₀μ₀), which Maxwell recognised from its numerical value as the speed of light — concluding that light itself is an electromagnetic wave. Heinrich Hertz experimentally confirmed radio waves in 1887–88. The equations are also consistent with special relativity — indeed their invariance under Lorentz transformations was a principal motivation for Einstein — and they underpin all of electrical engineering: motors, generators, radio, radar and telecommunications.
