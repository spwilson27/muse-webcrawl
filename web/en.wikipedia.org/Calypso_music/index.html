Calypso music

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Calypso is a style of Caribbean music that originated among Afro-Caribbean communities in Trinidad and Tobago during the early to mid-19th century and spread to the rest of the Caribbean Antilles by the mid-20th century. Its highly rhythmic, harmonic, and syncopated character is rooted in both West African traditions and French Creole influences brought by enslaved Africans and European settlers.

Origins

Calypso evolved from the kaiso singing tradition of West African griots, in which singers used song to chronicle events, satirise authority, and rally their communities. Under slavery in Trinidad, enslaved Africans were forbidden to speak to one another while working, and music became a vital channel of communication. After emancipation in 1834, calypso flourished during Carnival, developing into a vehicle for news, gossip, political commentary, and social satire in Trinidadian English Creole.

Carnival and the calypsonian

Calypso became inseparable from the Trinidad Carnival, where rival singers known as calypsonians competed in "tents" before the festival and battled in song contests. The title of Calypso Monarch, awarded during Carnival, became one of the most coveted honours in Trinidadian music. Legendary performers included Lord Invader, the Roaring Lion, Attila the Hun, and later the Mighty Sparrow and Lord Kitchener, who carried the music to postwar Britain and worldwide audiences.

Recording era and international success

Calypso was first recorded in the 1910s, and in 1944 the Andrews Sisters' version of Lord Invader's "Rum and Coca-Cola" became a major hit in the United States. In 1956 Harry Belafonte's album Calypso — featuring "Day-O (The Banana Boat Song)" — became the first LP by a solo artist to sell over a million copies, sparking a calypso craze in North America.

Legacy

Calypso laid the foundations for later Caribbean genres including soca, chutney, and dancehall, and its topical, witty songwriting influenced popular music far beyond the Caribbean. The tradition of the calypsonian as social commentator remains central to Carnival in Trinidad and Tobago.
