Gaeltacht

Gaeltacht

Country: Ireland

Counties: Donegal, Mayo, Galway, Kerry, Cork, Waterford, Meath

Language: Irish (Gaeilge)

Administered by: Údarás na Gaeltachta; Department of Tourism, Culture, Arts, Gaeltacht, Sport and Media

Population (Gaeltacht areas): approx. 96,000 (2016 census), of whom roughly two-thirds report ability in Irish

A Gaeltacht (plural Gaeltachtaí) is a district of Ireland, either individually or collectively, where the Irish government recognises that the Irish language is the predominant vernacular, or language of the home. The Gaeltacht districts were first officially recognised during the 1920s in the early years of the Irish Free State, following the Gaelic Revival, as part of government policy to restore the Irish language.

The Gaeltacht areas are mostly scattered along the western seaboard of Ireland, in counties Donegal, Mayo, Galway, Kerry, and Cork, with smaller districts in County Waterford in the south-east and County Meath in the east. They are characterised by concentrations of Irish speakers, though in most areas English is now widely spoken as well, and in many districts the majority of residents are English-dominant. The government body Údarás na Gaeltachta, established in 1980, is responsible for the economic and social development of the Gaeltacht, while Irish-language policy is overseen nationally by the department responsible for the Gaeltacht and by Foras na Gaeilge on an all-island basis.

History

Irish was the majority language of Ireland until the nineteenth century, when a combination of the Great Famine of the 1840s — which fell hardest on the poor, Irish-speaking districts of the west and south — mass emigration, and the English-only national school system drove a rapid shift to English. By the time of Irish independence in 1922, native Irish-speaking communities survived mainly in remote rural districts along the western seaboard.

The first Gaeltacht Commission, appointed in 1925 and reporting in 1926, recommended the designation of districts where Irish was spoken by the great majority of the population, with special measures for education, administration, and economic support. Official boundaries were fixed by order in 1956, substantially reducing the designated area compared with the commission's proposals; smaller adjustments followed. From the start, policy struggled with the tension between developing the Gaeltacht economically — so that its people would not be forced to emigrate — and preserving it as a reservoir of the traditional language and culture.

Demography and language use

According to census data, roughly 96,000 people live in Gaeltacht areas, of whom about two-thirds are able to speak Irish. However, the number of daily speakers of Irish outside the education system is much smaller — studies such as the Comprehensive Linguistic Study of the Use of Irish in the Gaeltacht (2007) found that daily, community-wide use of Irish survives strongly in only a minority of Gaeltacht electoral divisions, mostly in parts of Connemara in County Galway, west Donegal, and the Dingle Peninsula in County Kerry. The 2007 study warned that without intervention Irish might cease to be a community language in most of the Gaeltacht within a generation. Gaeltacht areas are conventionally categorised A, B, and C according to the proportion of daily Irish speakers, with category A areas having the strongest use.

Administration and development

Údarás na Gaeltachta, established under the Údarás na Gaeltachta Act 1979 and in operation from 1980, is the regional development authority for the Gaeltacht. It promotes job creation through industry, tourism, aquaculture, renewable energy, and the audiovisual sector, and its members include a board partly elected by Gaeltacht residents. Its predecessor was Gaeltarra Éireann, founded in 1957 to develop Gaeltacht industries such as tweed, knitwear, and seaweed processing.

The Official Languages Act 2003 placed obligations on public bodies to provide services through Irish, and the 20-Year Strategy for the Irish Language 2010–2030 set out a programme of support for the language in both the Gaeltacht and the rest of the country. The Gaeltacht Act 2012 introduced a system of language planning: Gaeltacht districts are required to prepare and implement local language plans in order to retain their status, and towns serving Gaeltacht areas may be recognised as Gaeltacht Service Towns, while Irish-language networks may be recognised outside the Gaeltacht altogether.

Education and media

Education is central to Gaeltacht life. Schools in the Gaeltacht teach through the medium of Irish, and thousands of English-speaking students from across Ireland attend summer colleges in Gaeltacht districts, staying with local families to learn Irish. The Irish-language media sector is anchored in the Gaeltacht: the national Irish-language radio station RTÉ Raidió na Gaeltachta, founded in 1972, broadcasts from Casla in Connemara, and the television station TG4, launched in 1996, has its headquarters at Baile na hAbhann in County Galway. Independent television and film production in Irish is a significant source of Gaeltacht employment.

Individual Gaeltacht areas

The largest Gaeltacht is in County Galway, centred on Connemara (Conamara), including the districts of Carna, Ros Muc, and the Aran Islands. The Donegal Gaeltacht includes the Rosses, Gweedore (Gaoth Dobhair), and Tory Island. In Kerry, the Gaeltacht centres on the Dingle Peninsula (Corca Dhuibhne), while the Mayo Gaeltacht includes parts of Erris, Achill Island, and Tourmakeady. The Cork Gaeltacht includes Múscraí and Cape Clear Island (Oileán Chléire). Smaller districts survive at Ring and Old Parish in County Waterford (Gaeltacht na nDéise) and at Ráth Cairn and Baile Ghib in County Meath, the latter founded by government resettlement of Connemara families in the 1930s.

Decline and revitalisation

Despite a century of state support, the sociolinguistic position of Irish in the Gaeltacht has continued to weaken, under pressure from English-language media, tourism, in-migration of English speakers, and the economic pull of English-speaking employment. At the same time, the Gaeltacht remains the symbolic heartland of the language, and language-planning efforts, Irish-medium education, and the creative industries continue to sustain communities of fluent speakers. The tension between the Gaeltacht as a living bilingual region and as a repository of Ireland's linguistic heritage remains at the centre of Irish language policy.
