Rerum novarum (from its incipit, Latin for "of new things") is an encyclical issued by Pope Leo XIII on 15 May 1891. It is an open letter, passed to all Catholic patriarchs, primates, archbishops and bishops, that addressed the condition of the working classes. The turning point in Catholic social teaching, it discussed the relationships and mutual duties between labor and capital, as well as government and its citizens.

Of primary concern was the need for improvements in "the misery and wretchedness pressing so unjustly on the majority of the working class." It supported the rights of labor to form trade unions, rejected both socialism and unrestrained capitalism, and affirmed the right to private property as natural.

Contents

The encyclical criticized the concentration of wealth and the treatment of workers in industrial societies, arguing that workers are entitled to a just wage sufficient to support a family, reasonable hours, rest on Sundays, and safe working conditions. It rejected the socialist remedy of abolishing private property, holding property to be a natural right and a safeguard of the family, but insisted that property carries social obligations. It assigned the state a role in protecting the weak while defending the principle later known as subsidiarity — matters should be handled by the smallest competent authority.

Legacy

Rerum novarum is considered the foundational document of modern Catholic social teaching and of Christian democratic political movements. Subsequent popes marked its anniversaries with major encyclicals building on it, including Pius XI's Quadragesimo anno (1931), John XXIII's Mater et magistra (1961), and John Paul II's Centesimus annus (1991). It influenced labor movements, welfare-state policies, and the development of distributism.
