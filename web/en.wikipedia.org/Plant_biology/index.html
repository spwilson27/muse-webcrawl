Plant biology

Plant biology, more commonly called botany, is the branch of biology that studies plants — living organisms of the kingdom Plantae — including their structure, growth, reproduction, metabolism, development, diseases, chemical properties, and evolutionary relationships. Modern plant biology encompasses genetics, physiology, ecology, morphology, and molecular biology, and it underpins agriculture, horticulture, forestry, and much of biotechnology.

Scope

Plant biology covers organisms from single-celled algae to the largest flowering trees. Core topics include photosynthesis, plant hormones, cell walls and tissues (xylem and phloem), roots, stems, leaves, flowers and seeds, and alternation of generations in the plant life cycle. Related disciplines such as phycology (algae), mycology (fungi, historically grouped with plants), and palaeobotany are often studied alongside.

History

Theophrastus (c. 371–287 BCE) wrote the earliest surviving systematic botanical works. Herbalists of the medieval and Renaissance periods compiled illustrated herbals; Carl Linnaeus's binomial nomenclature (1753) founded modern taxonomy. In the twentieth century, genetics and molecular biology transformed the field, leading to the Green Revolution and genetically modified crops.

Importance

Plants supply nearly all food, oxygen, medicines, fibres, timber, and fuels used by humanity, and plant biology informs conservation and responses to climate change.
