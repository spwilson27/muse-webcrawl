Health economics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Health economics is a branch of economics concerned with issues related to efficiency, effectiveness, value, and behaviour in the production and consumption of health and healthcare. Health economists study how scarce resources are allocated among alternative uses for the care of sickness and the promotion, maintenance, and improvement of health, including the functioning of health systems, health insurance markets, and the behaviour of patients, providers, and payers.

Scope

The field is often dated to Kenneth Arrow's seminal 1963 paper "Uncertainty and the Welfare Economics of Medical Care", which highlighted features distinguishing medical care from ordinary markets: uncertainty, asymmetric information between doctor and patient, insurance and moral hazard, adverse selection, and barriers to entry. Major subfields include the demand for health and healthcare (the Grossman model of health capital), the economics of health insurance, provider payment (fee-for-service, capitation, DRGs), pharmaceutical markets, and the economic evaluation of interventions.

Methods

Health economists perform cost-effectiveness analysis, cost-utility analysis (using quality-adjusted life years, QALYs), cost-benefit analysis, and budget-impact analysis to inform decisions by bodies such as the UK's National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE). The field also studies health system financing — taxation-funded, social insurance, and private insurance models — and why health spending, especially in the United States (roughly 17–18% of GDP), varies so much between countries and regions.
