Socially responsible investing

Socially responsible investing (SRI), or social investment, is any investment strategy which seeks to consider both financial return and social/environmental good. Modern SRI evolved from religious investors — Quakers and Methodists — who avoided "sin stocks" in alcohol, tobacco, gambling, and weapons, and expanded through campaigns for divestment from apartheid-era South Africa.

Strategies

Common approaches include negative screening (excluding sectors or companies), positive or best-in-class screening, shareholder activism and proxy voting, community investing, and impact investing targeting measurable social outcomes. Since the 2000s the related framework of ESG (environmental, social, and governance) integration has become mainstream in institutional finance.

Performance debate

Whether screened portfolios underperform is contested: excluding sin stocks may forfeit a documented "sin premium", while proponents argue ESG analysis captures real risks. Meta-analyses generally find no systematic penalty for SRI funds, though results vary by period and method.

Criticism

Critics charge some products with greenwashing, note inconsistent ratings between ESG agencies, and question whether divestment changes corporate behavior. In the 2020s SRI and ESG also became politically contested in the United States, prompting both regulatory requirements and anti-ESG legislation in different jurisdictions.
