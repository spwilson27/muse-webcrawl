Contraction mapping

In mathematics, a contraction mapping, or contraction or contractor, on a metric space (M, d) is a function f from M to itself, with the property that there is some nonnegative real number 0 ≤ k < 1 such that for all x and y in M,

d(f(x), f(y)) ≤ k·d(x, y).

The smallest such value of k is called the Lipschitz constant of f. Contractive maps are sometimes called Lipschitzian maps. If the above condition is instead satisfied for k ≤ 1, then the map is said to be a non-expansive mapping. More generally, the idea of a contractive map can be defined for maps between two different metric spaces.

Banach fixed-point theorem

The Banach fixed-point theorem states that every contraction mapping on a non-empty complete metric space has a unique fixed point, and that for any starting point x in M the iterated function sequence x, f(x), f(f(x)), ... converges to the fixed point. This gives a constructive method for finding (or approximating) solutions of equations, and underlies Picard's existence theorem for ordinary differential equations.

Contractions on Banach spaces

More generally, a contraction on a Banach space can be used in place of a contraction on a complete metric space, and the theorem extends to maps whose iterates eventually contract (eventually contractive maps). Contraction mappings play an important role in dynamic programming, fractal geometry (through iterated function systems), and numerical analysis.

Weakly contractive maps

A weakly contractive map satisfies d(f(x), f(y)) < d(x, y) for all distinct x and y. Such maps need not have fixed points on arbitrary complete metric spaces, but if the space is compact a weakly contractive map always has a unique fixed point.
