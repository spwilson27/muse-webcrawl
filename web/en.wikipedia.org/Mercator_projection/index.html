The Mercator projection is a cylindrical map projection presented by the Flemish geographer and cartographer Gerardus Mercator in 1569. It became the standard map projection for navigation because it is able to represent lines of constant course, known as rhumb lines, as straight segments that conserve the angles with the meridians. Although the linear scale is equal in all directions around any point, thus preserving the angles and the shapes of small objects, the Mercator projection distorts the size of objects as the latitude increases from the equator to the poles, where the scale becomes infinite. For example, Greenland appears much larger relative to land masses near the equator than it actually is.

Construction

The projection wraps a cylinder around the Earth tangent at the equator. A point at longitude λ and latitude φ maps to coordinates x = λ and y = ln[tan(π/4 + φ/2)]. Meridians are equally spaced vertical lines; parallels are horizontal lines whose spacing grows toward the poles. Because the projection is conformal, local shapes and bearings are preserved, which is precisely the property sailors needed: a course of constant bearing can be drawn as a straight line.

History

Mercator published his world map Nova et Aucta Orbis Terrae Descriptio ad Usum Navigantium Emendate Accommodata ("New and more complete representation of the terrestrial globe properly adapted for use in navigation") in 1569. He never fully explained his construction; the mathematics was first published by Edward Wright in 1599. The projection dominated nautical charts for centuries and was widely adopted for world maps in the 19th and 20th centuries, shaping popular perceptions of the relative size of continents.

Criticism and alternatives

From the 20th century onward the projection was criticised for exaggerating the size of Europe and North America relative to Africa and South America; the Peters projection debate of the 1970s made this a prominent public controversy. Cartographers generally recommend equal-area or compromise projections (such as Robinson) for general-purpose world maps, while retaining Mercator for navigation. A spherical variant, Web Mercator, is the de facto standard for online slippy maps (Google Maps, OpenStreetMap), even though it is not strictly conformal.
