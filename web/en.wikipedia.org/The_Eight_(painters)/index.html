The Eight (painters)

The Eight was a group of American painters who organised a landmark exhibition at the Macbeth Galleries in New York in February 1908, in protest against the conservative jury policies of the National Academy of Design. The Eight were Robert Henri, William Glackens, John Sloan, George Luks, Everett Shinn, Arthur B. Davies, Ernest Lawson and Maurice Prendergast.

Background

Henri, Glackens, Sloan, Luks and Shinn had worked together in Philadelphia as illustrators before moving to New York; their gritty scenes of modern urban life — street crowds, tenements, saloons and sporting events — earned them the derisive label "Ashcan school" from critics, though their art championed everyday working-class subjects the Academy considered improper. Davies, Lawson and Prendergast, more lyrical and Post-Impressionist in style, joined the exhibition to make common cause for artistic freedom rather than shared style.

The 1908 exhibition

After the Academy's 1907 exhibition rejected works by several of Henri's circle, the painters withdrew and mounted their own show. The exhibition drew large crowds and national publicity; though reviews were mixed, many works sold, and the show is remembered as a decisive blow against academic gatekeeping in America. Its success helped clear the way for the Armory Show of 1913 and for the growing independence of American modern art — some members later helped found the Association of American Painters and Sculptors.
