Complex number

In mathematics, a complex number is an element of a number system that extends the real numbers with a specific element denoted i, called the imaginary unit, satisfying i² = −1. Every complex number can be expressed in the form a + bi, where a and b are real numbers: a is the real part and b the imaginary part. The set of complex numbers is denoted ℂ. Because no real number satisfies x² = −1, complex numbers were once called "imaginary", but they are now fundamental throughout mathematics, physics, and engineering.

Arithmetic

Addition, subtraction, and multiplication proceed as for polynomials in i, replacing i² by −1. Division uses the complex conjugate ā = a − bi: dividing by z amounts to multiplying numerator and denominator by the conjugate, since z·z̄ = a² + b² is a non-negative real number. The modulus |z| = √(a² + b²) gives the distance from the origin in the complex plane.

Polar form

Writing z = r(cos θ + i sin θ) = reiθ (via Euler's formula) expresses a complex number by its modulus r and argument θ. Multiplication then corresponds to multiplying moduli and adding arguments, which yields De Moivre's theorem and makes roots easy to compute: every non-zero complex number has exactly n distinct nth roots.

Fundamental theorem of algebra

The complex numbers are algebraically closed: every non-constant polynomial with complex coefficients has a complex root — and hence, counting multiplicity, exactly as many roots as its degree. This theorem, first rigorously proved by Gauss, has no analogue for the reals and is a principal reason complex numbers are indispensable.

History and applications

Complex numbers arose in the sixteenth century in the work of Cardano and Bombelli on cubic equations, where they appeared even for equations with only real roots. Descartes coined the (originally dismissive) term "imaginary"; Euler introduced the symbol i and the formula eiθ = cos θ + i sin θ; and the geometric interpretation by Wessel, Argand, and Gauss secured acceptance. Today they underpin complex analysis, electrical engineering (phasors and impedance), signal processing, fluid dynamics, and quantum mechanics, whose wavefunctions are intrinsically complex.
