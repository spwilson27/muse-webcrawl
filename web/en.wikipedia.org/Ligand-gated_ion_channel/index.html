Ligand-gated ion channel

Ligand-gated ion channels (LICs), also called ionotropic receptors, are a group of transmembrane ion channel proteins that open in response to the binding of a chemical messenger (ligand), such as a neurotransmitter. Binding at a site distinct from the channel pore triggers a conformational change that allows ions such as Na+, K+, Ca2+ or Cl− to flow across the membrane, rapidly changing the cell's membrane potential.

Major families

The best-known family is the Cys-loop (pentameric) receptors, which include the nicotinic acetylcholine receptor, GABAA and GABAC receptors, glycine receptors and the 5-HT3 serotonin receptor. A second family comprises the tetrameric ionotropic glutamate receptors — AMPA, kainate and NMDA receptors — central to excitatory transmission and synaptic plasticity. A third family includes the trimeric ATP-gated P2X receptors.

Mechanism

Ligand-gated channels open within microseconds to milliseconds of transmitter binding, making them the mediators of fast synaptic transmission. Channel opening is typically followed by desensitisation, in which the channel closes despite continued ligand presence. Many clinically important drugs act on these channels, including benzodiazepines and general anaesthetics at GABAA receptors and neuromuscular blocking agents at nicotinic receptors. Mutations in ligand-gated channel genes cause channelopathies such as congenital myasthenic syndromes and some forms of epilepsy.
