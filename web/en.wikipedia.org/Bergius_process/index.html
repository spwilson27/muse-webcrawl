Bergius process

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Bergius process is a method of production of liquid hydrocarbons for use as synthetic fuel by hydrogenation of high-volatile bituminous coal at high temperature and pressure. It was first developed by Friedrich Bergius in 1913, and in 1931 Bergius was awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for this and other work on high-pressure chemistry.

Description

In the process, dried and crushed coal is mixed with heavy oil recycled from the process to form a slurry. A catalyst, typically based on iron, tungsten or tin compounds, is added, and the mixture is heated to around 450 °C under hydrogen at pressures of 200–700 bar. The coal is hydrogenated and cracked into liquid products — gasoline, synthetic oil and liquefied petroleum gas — together with gas and a solid residue of ash and unconverted coal.

History

The process was industrialized in Germany by IG Farben, and coal liquefaction plants using the Bergius process supplied a large share of the aviation fuel used by the Luftwaffe during the Second World War. After the war the process was largely abandoned in the West because of the abundance of cheap petroleum, though interest revived periodically during oil crises. It is an example of direct coal liquefaction, in contrast to the indirect Fischer–Tropsch process, which first gasifies the coal to synthesis gas.
