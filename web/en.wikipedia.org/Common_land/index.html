Common land

ConceptLand owned collectively or by one person over which others hold rights of use
Typical rightsGrazing (pasture), pannage, turbary, estovers, piscary
Extent (England)About 3% of land area
Key legislationCommons Act 2006 (England and Wales)

Common land is land owned by one person or by a community over which other people — known as commoners — hold traditional rights of use, called rights of common. The system originated in the manorial economy of medieval England and Wales, where the "waste" of the manor was subject to shared rights such as grazing livestock, collecting firewood, and cutting turf for fuel.

Rights of common

Traditional rights include pasture (grazing animals), pannage (turning pigs out to eat acorns and beechmast), turbary (cutting turf or peat), estovers (taking wood), piscary (fishing), and rights in the soil such as digging gravel or sand. Historically these rights were usually attached to particular properties ("appurtenant") rather than held personally, so they pass with the land when it is sold.

Enclosure and decline

From the sixteenth century onward, and especially under the parliamentary Inclosure Acts of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, much common land was enclosed and converted to exclusive private ownership, transforming English agriculture and driving rural displacement. Surviving commons are concentrated in upland areas such as Cumbria, Wales, and Dartmoor, and in urban fragments like Wimbledon Common and Clapham Common in London.

Modern law

The Commons Registration Act 1965 created registers of common land and rights; the Commons Act 2006 updated the regime, providing for commons councils and better management. The public gained a right of access on foot to most registered common land under the Countryside and Rights of Way Act 2000. Economists and ecologists continue to debate commons as examples of shared-resource governance, famously analyzed in Elinor Ostrom's work challenging the simple "tragedy of the commons" narrative.
