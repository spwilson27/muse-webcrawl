Ishango bone

Material: Baboon fibula, quartz

Created: c. 20,000 years ago (Upper Paleolithic)

Discovered: 1950, Ishango, Belgian Congo

Present location: Royal Belgian Institute of Natural Sciences, Brussels

The Ishango bone is a bone tool, dated to the Upper Paleolithic era, and roughly 10 cm long. It is a dark brown length of baboon fibula, with a sharp piece of quartz affixed to one end for engraving or writing, and a series of notches carved in three columns along its length. The bone was found in 1950 by Belgian geologist Jean de Heinzelin de Braucourt at the archaeological site of Ishango, near Lake Edward in what is now the Democratic Republic of the Congo.

The notches

The three columns of tally marks have attracted intense mathematical speculation. One column groups notches as 11, 13, 17 and 19 — the prime numbers between 10 and 20; another column shows 3, 6, 4, 8, 10, 5, 5, 7, suggesting doubling and halving; the third contains odd numbers 11, 21, 19, 9. From these arrangements it has been argued that the bone is the oldest evidence of mathematical knowledge, possibly a lunar calendar or an early counting aid.

Interpretation

More recent scholarship counsels caution: the notches may simply be tally marks for counting objects, and the apparent patterns could be coincidental. Nevertheless, the Ishango bone remains one of the most famous candidates for the earliest mathematical artefact, often cited alongside the older Lebombo bone from southern Africa. It is on permanent display in Brussels.
