Boiling point

The boiling point of a substance is the temperature at which the vapor pressure of a liquid equals the pressure surrounding the liquid and the liquid changes into a vapor. At this point bubbles of vapor form within the bulk of the liquid and rise to the surface. The boiling point at a pressure of one atmosphere is called the normal boiling point.

Pressure dependence

Boiling point varies strongly with ambient pressure. Water boils at 100 °C (212 °F) at sea level but at only about 71 °C (160 °F) on the summit of Mount Everest, where pressure is roughly a third of an atmosphere — which is why cooking at high altitude takes longer. Conversely, a pressure cooker raises the boiling point, allowing food to cook at temperatures around 120 °C. The relationship is described by the Clausius–Clapeyron equation.

Boiling versus evaporation

Evaporation occurs only at the surface of a liquid and takes place at any temperature, whereas boiling occurs throughout the liquid, but only at the boiling point. A liquid in a partial vacuum has a lower boiling point than at atmospheric pressure, and a liquid in a high-pressure environment has a higher boiling point.

Factors affecting boiling point

For organic compounds, boiling point rises with molecular weight and with the strength of intermolecular forces, such as hydrogen bonding. Impurities usually raise and broaden the boiling range of a liquid mixture; distillation separates components by exploiting differences in boiling point, and boiling point measurement is a standard test of a substance's identity and purity. Liquids can also be heated above their boiling point without boiling if nucleation sites are absent, a hazardous condition called superheating.
