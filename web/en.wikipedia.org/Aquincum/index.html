Aquincum was a major Roman city and military stronghold on the northeastern frontier of the province of Pannonia, situated on the west bank of the Danube in what is now Óbuda, Budapest, Hungary. Its name is thought to derive from the Celtic Eravisci tribe who inhabited the area before the Roman conquest.

History

Founded as a military camp in the 1st century AD, Aquincum grew into the capital of Pannonia Inferior after Trajan divided the province around 106 AD. A legionary fortress, eventually home to the Legio II Adiutrix, stood beside a military town (canabae), while a separate civil city developed to the north. At its height the settlement may have had 30,000–40,000 inhabitants, with aqueducts, amphitheatres, baths, temples, and forums. Marcus Aurelius is thought to have written part of his Meditations while campaigning in the region. The city declined in the late 4th century under pressure from migrating peoples.

Archaeology

Extensive ruins, including streets, houses with mosaic floors and wall paintings, a governor's palace on Hajógyári Island, and two amphitheatres, are preserved. The Aquincum Museum, opened in 1894, displays finds from the site.
