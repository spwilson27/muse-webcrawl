Cao Cao

Born155, Qiao County (modern Bozhou, Anhui)
Died15 March 220, Luoyang
OfficeChancellor of the Han dynasty; King of Wei

Cao Cao (155 – 15 March 220), courtesy name Mengde, was a Chinese statesman, warlord, and poet who rose to dominate northern China in the final years of the Han dynasty. As chancellor and later King of Wei, he ruled in the name of the powerless Emperor Xian while building the state of Cao Wei, which his son Cao Pi formally established as an empire shortly after his death. He was posthumously honoured as Emperor Wu of Wei.

A traditional portrait of Cao Cao.

Rise to power

Cao Cao began his career as a Han official and distinguished himself in campaigns against the Yellow Turban rebels. In the chaos following Dong Zhuo's seizure of the capital, he built his own army in Yan Province and, in 196, took custody of Emperor Xian, moving the court to Xu (Xuchang) and gaining legitimacy as the emperor's chancellor. At the Battle of Guandu in 200 he defeated the much larger forces of Yuan Shao, securing control of the North China Plain.

Red Cliffs and the northwest

In 208 Cao Cao moved south against Liu Bei and Sun Quan, but his fleet was destroyed at the Battle of Red Cliffs, forcing his retreat and fixing the division of China into three spheres. He later campaigned in the northwest against Ma Chao and annexed Hanzhong briefly before losing it to Liu Bei. In 216 he was enfeoffed as King of Wei. He declined to depose the emperor himself, famously remarking that if Heaven mandated it, he would be King Wen of Zhou — the father, not the founder.

Government and poetry

Cao Cao promoted agricultural colonies (tuntian) to feed his armies, recruited officials by merit rather than pedigree, and punished corruption severely. He was also one of the great poets of the era; his verse, and that of his sons Cao Pi and Cao Zhi, founded the Jian'an style. His tomb at Anyang was identified by archaeologists in 2009.

In culture

In the fourteenth-century novel Romance of the Three Kingdoms and in Chinese opera, Cao Cao is conventionally portrayed as a cunning, ruthless villain — a reputation modern historians regard as substantially unfair to a pragmatic and talented ruler.
