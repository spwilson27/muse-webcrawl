Somite

The somites (from Greek soma, "body") are a set of bilaterally paired block-like segments of paraxial mesoderm that form in the embryonic stage of all vertebrates, along the head-to-tail axis, and give rise to much of the axial skeleton, skeletal muscle and dermis. In the human embryo, around 42–44 pairs of somites form, appearing at a steady rate — roughly one pair every 90 minutes in the chick — under the control of a molecular "segmentation clock" of oscillating genes in the Notch, Wnt and FGF pathways.

Development

Somites form from the presomitic mesoderm on either side of the neural tube, beginning at the head end and progressing tailward. As each somite matures it differentiates into regions: the sclerotome, which forms vertebrae and ribs; the myotome, which forms skeletal muscle; and the dermatome, which contributes the dermis of the back. Cells of the myotome also migrate into the limb buds to form limb muscles.

Significance

Because somites appear in a regular sequence, counting them is a standard way to stage embryos. Their segmental pattern imposes the metameric organisation visible in the adult in the vertebrae, ribs, spinal nerves and segmental blood vessels. Defects of somitogenesis cause congenital vertebral malformations such as spondylocostal dysostosis.
