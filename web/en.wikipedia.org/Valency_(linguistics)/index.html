Valency (linguistics)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A verb's valency is the number and type of arguments it requires, like the bonding capacity of a chemical atom.

In linguistics, valency (or valence) is the number of arguments controlled by a verbal predicate. The notion was introduced by Lucien Tesnière in his dependency grammar, in explicit analogy with chemical valency: just as an atom has a fixed number of bonds it can form, a verb has a fixed number of "actants" (arguments) it can take. Verb valency is a central topic in syntax, typology and lexicography, closely related to the concepts of transitivity and subcategorization.

Types by number of arguments

  • Avalent (valency 0): no arguments — weather verbs in some languages (e.g., Latin pluit, "it rains", where English requires the dummy subject it).
  • Monovalent (valency 1): one argument, the subject — intransitive verbs such as sleep, arrive.
  • Divalent (valency 2): subject and one object — transitive verbs such as read (a book), see.
  • Trivalent (valency 3): subject, direct object and indirect object (or oblique) — ditransitive verbs such as give, send, put.

Valency and transitivity

Valency is broader than transitivity: it counts all obligatory arguments, including subjects and oblique complements, whereas transitivity traditionally counts objects only. It also distinguishes obligatory arguments from optional adjuncts (Tesnière's circonstants) — time, place and manner expressions that can attach to any predicate. Some frameworks also count arguments of nouns and adjectives (e.g., destruction of the city).

Valency-changing operations

Languages have grammatical devices that systematically alter a verb's valency:

  • Decrease: passive (demotes the subject), antipassive (demotes the object), reflexive and reciprocal constructions, object omission (John ate), and noun incorporation;
  • Increase: causatives (add a causer), applicatives (promote an oblique to object), and dative-shift alternations in some analyses.

Theoretical approaches

Tesnière's dependency grammar made valency the organizing principle of the sentence, with the verb as its structural centre. Fillmore's case grammar, Dik's functional grammar, and lexicalist frameworks such as LFG and HPSG all encode valency in lexical entries. Valency dictionaries — for German, English (the Valency Dictionary of English) and other languages — record the complement patterns of verbs for learners and computational use, and valency classes are a key parameter in cross-linguistic databases such as ValPaL (the Leipzig Valency Classes Project).
