Thomas Clarkson

Thomas Clarkson (28 March 1760 – 26 September 1846) was an English abolitionist, and a leading campaigner against the slave trade in the British Empire. He helped found the Society for Effecting the Abolition of the Slave Trade and helped achieve passage of the Slave Trade Act 1807, which ended British trade in slaves.

Clarkson was born in Wisbech, Cambridgeshire, and educated at St John's College, Cambridge. In 1785 he won the university's Latin essay prize with an essay on the question of whether it was lawful to enslave others against their will. Researching and translating the essay changed his life: he later recalled stopping on the road to London, dismounting from his horse, and resolving to devote himself to ending the trade.

In 1787 Clarkson joined with Granville Sharp, William Wilberforce and others in forming the Society for Effecting the Abolition of the Slave Trade. He travelled tens of thousands of miles around Britain collecting evidence against the trade — interviewing sailors, gathering artefacts such as shackles and thumbscrews, and obtaining the famous plan of the slave ship Brookes. His Essay on the Slavery and Commerce of the Human Species (1786) and later works provided the movement with much of its factual ammunition.

After the Slave Trade Act 1807, Clarkson continued to campaign for the complete abolition of slavery itself, which came with the Slavery Abolition Act 1833. In 1840, at the age of eighty, he gave the opening address at the World Anti-Slavery Convention in London. He died in 1846 and was buried at Playford, Suffolk.
