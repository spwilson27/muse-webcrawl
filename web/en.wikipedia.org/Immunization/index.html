Immunization

Immunization, or immunisation, is the process by which a person's immune system becomes fortified against an infectious agent (known as the immunogen). It can occur naturally, when the immune system encounters a pathogen during infection, or artificially, most commonly through vaccination, in which a vaccine stimulates the body's adaptive immune system to produce lasting protection without causing the disease.

Active and passive immunization

Active immunization induces the body to produce its own antibodies and memory cells, giving long-lived protection; it is the principle behind vaccines against diseases such as measles, polio, hepatitis B and influenza. Passive immunization transfers pre-formed antibodies — for example maternal antibodies through the placenta and breast milk, or therapeutic immunoglobulin preparations — and confers immediate but temporary protection.

Public health impact

Immunization is considered one of the most cost-effective public health interventions. The World Health Organization estimates that vaccination prevents several million deaths each year; mass campaigns eradicated smallpox (declared 1980) and brought poliomyelitis close to eradication. When coverage is high, herd immunity also protects those who cannot be vaccinated. National immunization schedules typically begin in infancy and continue with boosters through life.
