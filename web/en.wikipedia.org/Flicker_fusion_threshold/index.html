Flicker fusion threshold

The flicker fusion threshold (or flicker fusion rate) is the frequency at which an intermittent light stimulus appears to be completely steady to the average human observer. Above this critical flicker frequency (CFF), the visual system can no longer resolve individual flashes, and the light is perceived as continuous.

Values and factors

For most people the CFF lies roughly between 50 and 90 Hz depending on conditions. It rises with the logarithm of light intensity (the Ferry–Porter law), is higher in peripheral vision and for larger stimulus fields, and varies with colour, fatigue, and age. Cinema historically exploited persistence of vision at 24 frames per second, with each frame flashed two or three times to raise the effective flicker rate.

Applications

The threshold is relevant to the design of displays, LED lighting (where low-frequency flicker can cause eye strain and headaches), and clinical tests of visual and neurological function. Many animals, such as dogs and birds of prey, have substantially higher flicker fusion thresholds than humans.
