Low-temperature physics

A dilution refrigerator, used to reach temperatures of a few millikelvin.

Low-temperature physics is the branch of condensed matter physics dealing with the physical properties of matter at very low temperatures, typically from the liquefaction of gases down to a few thousandths of a degree above absolute zero. The field began with the liquefaction of air, oxygen and nitrogen in the late 19th century and the liquefaction of helium by Heike Kamerlingh Onnes in 1908.

Phenomena

Many striking quantum phenomena appear only at low temperatures: superconductivity, discovered in 1911 when mercury's electrical resistance vanished at 4.2 K; superfluidity in liquid helium-4 below the lambda point (2.17 K) and in helium-3 at still lower temperatures; and Bose–Einstein condensation. Low temperatures also suppress thermal noise, enabling precision measurements and devices such as superconducting magnets and cryogenic detectors.

Cooling techniques

Techniques include cryostats using liquid helium baths, Joule–Thomson expansion, helium-3/helium-4 dilution refrigeration (reaching a few millikelvin), adiabatic (nuclear) demagnetization, and laser cooling of atoms to microkelvin and nanokelvin temperatures. Cryocoolers such as Gifford-McMahon and pulse-tube refrigerators provide closed-cycle cooling without cryogenic liquids.
