Autotomy

[Image placeholder: a lizard that has shed its tail]

Autotomy (from the Greek auto-, "self", and tomē, "severing") is the behaviour whereby an animal sheds or discards one or more of its own appendages, usually as a self-defence mechanism to elude a predator's grasp or to distract the predator and thereby allow escape. Many species can later regenerate the lost part.

In reptiles

Many lizards, including geckos and skinks, can shed their tails along predetermined fracture planes within the vertebrae. The detached tail continues to thrash for several minutes, distracting the predator. The regenerated tail is supported by a rod of cartilage rather than bone and often differs in colour and pattern. Some salamanders show a similar ability.

In invertebrates

Autotomy is widespread among invertebrates. Crabs and other crustaceans can shed claws and legs at special breakage joints and regrow them over successive moults. Spiders may shed trapped legs; some octopuses can shed arms; sea stars can shed arms and, in a related process, reproduce asexually from a detached arm. Certain sea slugs even discard their entire body, regenerating it from the head.

Costs and benefits

Losing a body part carries costs: reduced mobility or feeding ability, lost fat reserves in a tail, and diminished social or mating status. Autotomy evolves where the immediate benefit — surviving a predator encounter — outweighs these costs.
