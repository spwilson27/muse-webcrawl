Day-care sex-abuse hysteria

Day-care sex-abuse hysteria was a moral panic that swept the United States during the 1980s and early 1990s, in which child care workers were accused of sexually abusing children in their care, frequently in elaborate and implausible rituals said to involve Satanism. A series of prosecutions rested on testimony obtained from young children through highly suggestive interviewing techniques; many convictions were later overturned, and the episode is now widely regarded as a miscarriage of justice driven by panic rather than evidence.

Background

The panic grew out of the broader Satanic panic of the era and rising public anxiety about child sexual abuse as more mothers entered the workforce and day care expanded. Social workers and therapists held conferences promoting the idea that organised ritual abuse was widespread, and unsubstantiated claims spread through media coverage.

Major cases

The most famous case was the McMartin preschool trial in California (1983–1990), then the longest and most expensive criminal trial in American history; it ended with no convictions. Other notable prosecutions included the cases against the Amirault family in Massachusetts, Kelly Michaels in New Jersey, and defendants in Kern County, California, most of which were reversed on appeal as the interviewing methods used with the children were discredited.

Aftermath

Research by psychologists such as Stephen Ceci and Maggie Bruck demonstrated how easily preschool children's testimony can be shaped by repeated, leading questioning, producing detailed but false accounts. The episode led to reforms in forensic interviewing of children, including protocols requiring open-ended questions and video recording of interviews.
