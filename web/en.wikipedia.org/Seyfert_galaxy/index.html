Seyfert galaxies are a class of active galaxies named after Carl Keenan Seyfert, who first described them in 1943. They have extremely bright, compact nuclei whose spectra show strong, often broad emission lines, produced by gas ionised by a central supermassive black hole accreting matter. Seyferts are the most common type of active galactic nucleus (AGN) in the nearby universe and are less luminous than quasars, with their host galaxies — nearly always spirals — readily visible.

Characteristics

The nucleus of a Seyfert galaxy can outshine the rest of the galaxy and varies in brightness over months or even days, implying a very small emitting region, light-days to light-years across. Emission lines of hydrogen, helium, and highly ionised species such as [O III] reveal gas clouds moving at thousands of kilometres per second close to the black hole (the broad-line region) and slower gas farther out (the narrow-line region). The central engines are believed to span black-hole masses of roughly 10⁶ to 10⁹ solar masses, accreting below the Eddington limit.

Classification

Seyferts are divided according to their spectra. In Type 1 Seyferts both broad and narrow emission lines are present, with hydrogen lines broadened to several thousand km/s. In Type 2 Seyferts only narrow lines appear. Intermediate types (1.5, 1.8, 1.9) show progressively weaker broad components. The prototype Type 2 is NGC 1068; a classic, highly variable Type 1 is NGC 4151.

Unification

In the unified model of AGN, the difference between Type 1 and Type 2 Seyferts is mainly one of viewing angle: a dusty, doughnut-shaped torus surrounds the central engine and broad-line region, so that when the torus is seen edge-on it hides the broad lines and continuum, and the galaxy appears as a Type 2. Support came from spectropolarimetry of NGC 1068, which revealed a hidden broad-line spectrum in scattered (polarised) light, and from the detection of ionisation cones aligned with radio structure in many Type 2 objects.

Examples

  • NGC 1068 (Messier 77) — the prototypical Seyfert 2, about 47 million light-years away.
  • NGC 4151 — one of the brightest and most studied Seyfert 1 galaxies.
  • Circinus Galaxy — a nearby Seyfert 2 only about 13 million light-years away.
  • Centaurus A (NGC 5128) — a nearby active galaxy with a prominent dust lane and jets.
