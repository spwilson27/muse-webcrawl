Adhesive

Adhesive, also known as glue, cement, mucilage, or paste, is any non-metallic substance applied to one or both surfaces of two separate items that binds them together and resists their separation. The use of adhesives offers certain advantages over other binding techniques such as sewing, mechanical fastenings, or welding, including the ability to join dissimilar materials and distribute stress across a joint.

History

Adhesives have been used since prehistory: Neanderthals produced birch bark tar, and ancient peoples used natural gums, beeswax, and animal glues made from collagen. The first commercial glue factory opened in Holland around 1690, and the modern era brought synthetic adhesives such as Bakelite-based glues, epoxies, and cyanoacrylates ("super glue", developed in the 1940s–50s).

Types

Adhesives may be classified by chemistry (epoxy, polyurethane, acrylic, silicone), by cure mechanism (drying, pressure-sensitive, heat-activated, UV-cured, or reactive two-part systems), or by origin (natural or synthetic). Pressure-sensitive adhesives, used in tapes and sticky notes, form a bond on light pressure without curing.

Mechanisms of adhesion

Theories of adhesion include mechanical interlocking, adsorption (wetting and secondary bonding forces at the interface), chemisorption, and diffusion of polymer chains across the joint. Surface preparation is critical to bond strength.
