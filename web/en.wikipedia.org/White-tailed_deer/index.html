White-tailed deer

The white-tailed deer (Odocoileus virginianus), also known simply as the whitetail, is a medium-sized deer native to North America, Central America, and South America as far south as Peru and Bolivia. It is one of the most widely distributed large mammals in the Americas and the most common deer species in North America.

Description

The deer's coat is reddish-brown in summer and greyer-brown in winter. Males (bucks) grow antlers each spring and shed them in winter; females (does) usually lack antlers. The species is named for the white underside of its tail, which it raises like a flag when alarmed. Fawns are born spotted, which provides camouflage.

Habitat and behaviour

White-tailed deer inhabit a wide range of habitats, from forests and farmland to suburbs. They are herbivores, browsing on twigs, leaves, acorns, grasses and crops, and are most active at dawn and dusk. They are strong swimmers and can run at speeds up to about 48 km/h (30 mph), leaping as high as 2.6 metres.

Population and management

After near-elimination by market hunting in the late 19th century, conservation and hunting regulation restored the North American population to roughly 30 million. In many areas deer are now overabundant, causing crop damage, vehicle collisions and over-browsing that harms forest regeneration; they are also a principal host of the ticks that spread Lyme disease.
