Nacre, also known as mother of pearl, is an organic–inorganic composite material produced by some molluscs as the inner lining of their shells. It is the material from which pearls are formed. Strong, resilient, and famously iridescent, nacre has been used for millennia in jewellery, inlay, buttons, and decorative objects.

Structure

Nacre consists of microscopic hexagonal platelets of aragonite (a form of calcium carbonate), each about half a micrometre thick, arranged in continuous sheets separated by thin layers of the elastic protein conchiolin — a "brick and mortar" architecture. Because the platelet thickness is comparable to the wavelength of visible light, interference between reflections produces nacre's shifting rainbow lustre.

Formation and toughness

The material is secreted by the epithelial cells of the mollusc's mantle tissue. If a foreign body becomes lodged in the mantle, the animal coats it in successive layers of nacre, forming a pearl. Despite being mostly brittle mineral, nacre is remarkably fracture-resistant — thousands of times tougher than aragonite alone — because cracks are deflected along the protein layers. This has made it a model for biomimetic materials design.

Uses

Historically used for inlay in furniture and musical instruments, watch faces, cutlery handles, and buttons, nacre is now often replaced by plastics, though genuine mother-of-pearl remains valued in lutherie and jewellery.
