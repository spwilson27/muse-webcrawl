Callable bond

A callable bond (also called a redeemable bond) is a type of bond that allows the issuer of the bond to retain the privilege of redeeming the bond at some point before the bond reaches its date of maturity. In other words, on the call date or dates, the issuer has the right, but not the obligation, to buy back the bonds from the bondholders at a defined call price. Technically speaking, the bonds are not really bought and held by the issuer, but are cancelled immediately.

Features

The main cause of refinance for a callable bond is falling interest rates: if rates fall below the bond's coupon, the issuer can redeem the old debt and reissue at a lower cost, much like a homeowner refinancing a mortgage. The call price is usually set at a premium above par value, and most callable bonds may only be called after an initial period of call protection. Some bonds are callable on discrete dates, while others (such as many United States Treasury-era issues and make-whole call bonds) may be redeemed at any time under a formula price.

Pricing and risk

Because the call option belongs to the issuer, a callable bond can be viewed as a straight bond minus an embedded call option belonging to the issuer. Investors therefore demand a higher yield than on an otherwise identical non-callable bond, and the bond's price appreciation is capped as rates fall — a property known as negative convexity. Common measures for pricing callable bonds include yield to call, yield to worst, and option-adjusted spread (OAS). Callable bonds are common among United States municipal bonds, corporate bonds, and agency securities; the opposite structure, a puttable bond, gives the investor the right to sell the bond back to the issuer.
