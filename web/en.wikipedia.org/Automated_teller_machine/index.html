Automated teller machine

ATMs allow customers to withdraw cash and perform other banking transactions without a human teller.

An automated teller machine (ATM), also known in British English as a cash machine or cashpoint, is an electronic telecommunications device that enables bank customers to perform financial transactions — principally cash withdrawal, balance enquiry, funds transfer and deposit — at any time and without direct interaction with bank staff.

History

The first cash dispenser was installed by Barclays Bank at Enfield, London, on 27 June 1967; the actor Reg Varney was photographed making the first withdrawal. The Scottish inventor John Shepherd-Barron is usually credited with the concept, and the machines initially used paper vouchers impregnated with carbon-14. In the United States, a machine installed by Chemical Bank in Rockville Centre, New York, in September 1969 is often cited as the first American ATM. The introduction of the personal identification number (PIN) and magnetic-stripe cards in the 1970s, followed by shared interbank networks in the 1980s, made ATM use universal.

Operation and security

A customer inserts a debit or credit card (or uses a contactless or mobile credential) and enters a PIN. The machine communicates with the bank's host processor to authorise the transaction, then dispenses cash from note cassettes. Security threats include card skimming, PIN capture, cash trapping and physical attacks such as ram raiding; countermeasures include chip (EMV) cards, encryption, anti-skimming devices and ink-staining of stolen notes.

Prevalence and change

There are around three million ATMs worldwide. In many developed countries their numbers are declining as cash use falls and banking moves to mobile channels, leading to debates about access to cash for rural communities and people who depend on it. Modern machines increasingly offer cardless access, video teller services, currency exchange and recycling of deposited notes.
