Niche construction

Niche construction
FieldEvolutionary biology, ecology
Key figuresRichard Lewontin, John Odling-Smee, Kevin Laland
Classic examplesBeaver dams, earthworm soil engineering
Related theoryExtended evolutionary synthesis

Niche construction is the process by which organisms, through their activities and choices, modify their own and each other's niches — the environmental conditions that affect their fitness. By building structures, altering resources, or changing physical conditions, organisms modify the selection pressures acting on themselves and their descendants, thereby playing an active role in their own evolution rather than merely adapting to a pre-existing environment.

Examples

Beavers dam streams, creating ponds that change habitats for whole communities. Earthworms restructure soil chemistry and texture — an example Charles Darwin documented — altering the conditions their descendants inherit. Termites build temperature-regulated mounds, cyanobacteria oxygenated the early Earth's atmosphere, and humans engage in niche construction on an unmatched scale, from agriculture to cities. The inheritance of such modified environments is termed ecological inheritance.

Theoretical significance

The concept, developed by Richard Lewontin and formalised by John Odling-Smee, F. John Odling-Smee's collaborators, and Kevin Laland, challenges the picture of adaptation as a one-way process. Niche construction theory has become a component of the extended evolutionary synthesis, which argues that development, plasticity, and extra-genetic inheritance should be integrated with population genetics. Critics respond that standard theory already accommodates these phenomena, while proponents hold that niche construction generates distinctive predictions, for example in the co-evolution of dairy farming and adult lactase persistence in humans.
