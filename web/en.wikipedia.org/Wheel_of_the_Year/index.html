The Wheel of the Year is an annual cycle of seasonal festivals observed by many modern pagans, particularly Wiccans. The wheel consists of eight festivals, known as sabbats, spaced at roughly six- to seven-week intervals through the year: the solstices and equinoxes and the cross-quarter days midway between them.

The eight sabbats

The four "greater sabbats" are drawn largely from Celtic fire festivals: Samhain (31 October–1 November), Imbolc (1 February), Beltane (1 May), and Lughnasadh or Lammas (1 August). The four "lesser sabbats" mark the astronomical turning points: Yule (winter solstice), Ostara (spring equinox), Litha (summer solstice or Midsummer), and Mabon (autumn equinox). In the Southern Hemisphere the dates are commonly shifted by six months so the festivals match the local seasons.

Origins

The eightfold wheel in its modern form was assembled in the mid-20th century within Wicca, the new religious movement founded by Gerald Gardner. Gardner's tradition combined the four Celtic fire festivals with solstice and equinox observances influenced by the work of Ross Nichols, founder of the Order of Bards, Ovates and Druids; the two men are said to have standardized the cycle between them in the 1950s. The names Ostara and Mabon are modern additions — Mabon, a Welsh mythological figure, was applied to the autumn equinox by Aidan Kelly in the 1970s.

Symbolism

In Wiccan mythology the wheel narrates the life cycle of the God — born at Yule, maturing through the year, dying at Samhain — and the Goddess's changing aspects as maiden, mother, and crone. The festivals celebrate the agricultural year: sowing, growth, harvest, and the fallow season.
