Folk art

Folk art
MediumVaried — wood, textiles, clay, metal, paint
CharacteristicsMade within a cultural community, often self-taught, decorative and utilitarian
Related termsOutsider art, naïve art, vernacular art

Folk art covers all forms of visual art made in the context of folk culture — by makers working within a tradition, often self-taught, and usually producing objects that are both decorative and useful. Folk art is rooted in the material culture of a community: pottery, quilts, weathercocks, painted furniture, carvings, embroidery, toys, and festival objects transmit designs, motifs, and techniques across generations rather than through academic training.

Characteristics

Definitions vary, but folk art is generally distinguished from academic "fine art" by its makers' position within a community tradition, its use of local materials, and its functions — household, religious, commemorative, or commercial. The work is often anonymous; where individual makers become known, as with American itinerant portraitists or the weavers of celebrated quilt traditions, they are still understood through their community's idiom. Folk art may be made for use within the community, for ritual, or increasingly for outside markets and tourists.

Regional traditions

Every culture has named traditions: Mexican alebrijes and Day of the Dead figures, Polish wycinanki paper cuttings, Ukrainian pysanky decorated eggs, Indian Madhubani painting, Japanese mingei folk crafts, Scandinavian rosemaling, and the quilts of Gee's Bend in Alabama. In the United States, the category has been shaped by institutions such as the Museum of International Folk Art in Santa Fe and the American Folk Art Museum in New York.

Relation to outsider and naïve art

Folk art overlaps with, but is distinct from, outsider art (work by self-taught makers outside cultural traditions, a category derived from Jean Dubuffet's art brut) and naïve art (work by untrained painters such as Henri Rousseau or Grandma Moses, who lack a communal tradition but share folk art's disregard for academic perspective and technique). Scholars debate whether these boundaries reflect the work itself or the institutions that collect and market it.

Preservation

Industrialisation eroded many folk crafts, but revival movements, heritage designations, craft fairs, and tourism sustain others. Critics note that commercial demand can standardise designs and detach objects from their original meanings, while supporters see adaptation as the tradition's natural continuation.
