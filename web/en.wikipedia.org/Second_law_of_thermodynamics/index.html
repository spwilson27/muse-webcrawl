Second law of thermodynamics

FieldThermodynamics, statistical mechanics
Key conceptEntropy of an isolated system never decreases
Key figuresSadi Carnot, Rudolf Clausius, Lord Kelvin, Ludwig Boltzmann
Related lawsZeroth, first and third laws of thermodynamics

The second law of thermodynamics is a fundamental physical law based on universal empirical observation concerning heat and energy interconversions. In its simplest statement, heat generally cannot flow spontaneously from a colder body to a hotter body. More generally, the law establishes the concept of entropy as a physical property of a thermodynamic system and states that the total entropy of an isolated system can never decrease over time.

Statements of the law

The Clausius statement declares that it is impossible for a self-acting machine to transfer heat from a colder to a hotter body without external work. The Kelvin–Planck statement holds that no heat engine can be perfectly efficient: it is impossible to construct a device operating in a cycle that converts all absorbed heat into work. These statements are equivalent.

Entropy

Rudolf Clausius introduced entropy in 1865, defining its change as the reversible heat transferred divided by absolute temperature. Ludwig Boltzmann later gave entropy a statistical interpretation: entropy measures the number of microscopic configurations (microstates) consistent with a system's macroscopic state, expressed in the famous formula S = kB log W inscribed on his tombstone.

Implications

The second law implies the irreversibility of natural processes, sets the maximum efficiency of heat engines (the Carnot efficiency), and underlies the arrow of time. It predicts that the universe tends toward a state of maximum entropy, sometimes called heat death. Apparent exceptions, such as life maintaining internal order, are resolved by noting that living organisms export entropy to their surroundings.
