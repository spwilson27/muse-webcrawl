Southern United States literature


Major works of Southern literature

Southern United States literature, often called Southern literature, is the body of fiction, poetry, drama, and nonfiction written by authors from the Southern United States or set in the region. It is defined in part by its engagement with Southern history, including slavery, the Civil War, Reconstruction, and the region's distinctive speech, landscape, and social customs.

Origins

Early Southern writing included the works of John Smith and William Byrd II in colonial Virginia. In the 19th century, humorists such as Augustus Baldwin Longstreet and George Washington Harris developed the "Southwestern humor" tradition, while Edgar Allan Poe, though born in Boston, was raised in Richmond, Virginia. Mark Twain, from Missouri, is often considered the first major Southern writer; his Adventures of Huckleberry Finn (1884) profoundly influenced American prose.

The Southern Renaissance

The Southern Renaissance of the 1920s and 1930s produced writers of international stature, above all William Faulkner, whose novels set in the fictional Yoknapatawpha County, including The Sound and the Fury (1929) and Absalom, Absalom! (1936), explored the burden of Southern history. Other figures included Thomas Wolfe, Katherine Anne Porter, Eudora Welty, and the Fugitive poets of Vanderbilt University, including John Crowe Ransom, Allen Tate, and Robert Penn Warren.

Southern Gothic

The Southern Gothic mode, marked by grotesque characters, decaying settings, and themes of violence and moral conflict, is represented by Flannery O'Connor, Carson McCullers, Truman Capote, and Tennessee Williams. Harper Lee's To Kill a Mockingbird (1960) became one of the most widely read American novels.

African American Southern literature

Black Southern writers have produced central works of the tradition, including the slave narratives of Frederick Douglass, the fiction of Richard Wright and Ralph Ellison, and the poetry and prose of Zora Neale Hurston, whose Their Eyes Were Watching God (1937) is set in Florida. Later writers include Alice Walker, Toni Morrison, and Ernest J. Gaines.
