Henoch–Schönlein purpura

Henoch–Schönlein purpura (IgA vasculitis)
SpecialtyRheumatology, nephrology, paediatrics
TypeSmall-vessel vasculitis (IgA-mediated)
Typical ageChildren, peak 4–6 years
Named forJohann Lukas Schönlein; Eduard Heinrich Henoch

Henoch–Schönlein purpura (HSP), now also called IgA vasculitis, is a disorder in which inflammation of small blood vessels is caused by deposition of immunoglobulin A (IgA) immune complexes. It is the most common systemic vasculitis of childhood and typically follows an upper respiratory tract infection. The condition is named after the German physicians Johann Lukas Schönlein, who described the joint and skin features in the 1830s, and his student Eduard Heinrich Henoch, who recognised the abdominal and kidney involvement.

Signs and symptoms

The classic tetrad comprises: (1) a purpuric rash — raised, palpable purple-red spots caused by bleeding into the skin, predominantly over the buttocks and legs — with normal platelet counts, distinguishing it from purpura due to low platelets; (2) arthritis or joint pain, especially of the knees and ankles; (3) abdominal pain, which can be severe and may be complicated by intussusception, a telescoping of the bowel; and (4) kidney involvement, ranging from microscopic blood and protein in the urine to nephritis, which determines the long-term prognosis.

Cause and diagnosis

The precise cause is unknown. Abnormally glycosylated IgA1 molecules form immune complexes that deposit in vessel walls and the kidney glomeruli, activating complement and recruiting inflammatory cells. Diagnosis is usually clinical, based on the characteristic rash plus at least one other feature; skin or kidney biopsy showing IgA deposition confirms it. Blood tests are used to exclude other causes of purpura and to assess kidney function, and urinalysis is monitored for months after presentation because kidney disease can develop late.

Treatment and prognosis

Most cases resolve spontaneously within a few weeks with supportive care, rest and simple analgesia; corticosteroids are used for severe abdominal pain and sometimes for joint symptoms. Children with significant kidney involvement require follow-up and may need immunosuppressive treatment. The overall prognosis is excellent, though a small minority develop chronic kidney disease, and the condition can recur. Adults with the disease tend to have more severe kidney involvement than children.
