Activation-induced cytidine deaminase

Activation-induced cytidine deaminase
Gene: AICDA
Chromosome: 12p13.31 (human)
Family: APOBEC deaminases
Function: DNA cytidine deamination

Activation-induced cytidine deaminase, commonly abbreviated AID, is a DNA-editing enzyme encoded by the AICDA gene. It is expressed primarily in activated B lymphocytes and is essential for the diversification of antibodies in the adaptive immune system. AID initiates both somatic hypermutation (SHM) and class-switch recombination (CSR) of immunoglobulin genes, processes that allow the immune system to produce high-affinity antibodies of different effector classes.

Mechanism

AID deaminates cytosine bases in single-stranded DNA, converting them to uracil. The resulting U:G mismatch is then processed by error-prone DNA repair pathways, including base excision repair and mismatch repair, which introduce point mutations into the variable regions of immunoglobulin genes (somatic hypermutation) or generate double-strand breaks that mediate class-switch recombination in the constant-region switch domains.

Regulation

Because AID can mutate DNA outside the immunoglobulin loci, its activity is tightly regulated at the levels of transcription, nuclear localization, and protein degradation. Off-target AID activity contributes to chromosomal translocations, such as those between the MYC gene and immunoglobulin loci found in Burkitt lymphoma, and to mutations in non-immunoglobulin genes such as BCL6.

Clinical significance

Mutations in the human AICDA gene cause hyper-IgM syndrome type 2, an immunodeficiency in which patients cannot switch from IgM to other antibody classes and lack efficient somatic hypermutation. AID is also a member of the APOBEC family of cytidine deaminases, several of which act as restriction factors against retroviruses.
