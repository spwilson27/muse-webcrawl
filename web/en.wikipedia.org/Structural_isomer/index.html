Structural isomer

In chemistry, a structural isomer (or constitutional isomer in IUPAC nomenclature) of a compound is another compound whose molecule has the same number of atoms of each element, but with logically distinct bonds between them. The term metamer was formerly used for the same concept.

For example, butane (C4H10) exists as the straight-chain isomer n-butane and the branched isomer isobutane (methylpropane); they have different boiling points (−0.5 °C and −11.7 °C respectively). Ethanol and dimethyl ether share the formula C2H6O but belong to different functional classes — an alcohol and an ether — and differ dramatically in properties.

Types

Skeletal (chain) isomerism differs in the carbon skeleton; positional isomerism differs in the position of a functional group or substituent (e.g., 1-propanol vs 2-propanol); functional isomerism differs in functional group entirely (e.g., cyclohexane vs 1-hexene). Structural isomers are contrasted with stereoisomers, which have the same connectivity but different spatial arrangements of atoms.

Enumerating isomers

The number of structural isomers grows explosively with molecular size — there are 75 isomers of decane (C10H22) and over 4 billion for C30 alkanes — a classic problem in enumerative combinatorics treated by Pólya's enumeration theorem. Structural isomers can differ in reactivity, stability, and biological activity, which matters in pharmaceuticals and materials chemistry.
