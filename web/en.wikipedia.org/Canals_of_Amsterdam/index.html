The canals of Amsterdam form a network of waterways totalling more than 100 kilometres, with around 90 islands and 1,500 bridges, that gives the Dutch capital its nickname "Venice of the North." The three main canals — the Herengracht, Keizersgracht, and Prinsengracht — were dug in the 17th century during the Dutch Golden Age in a concentric pattern known as the Grachtengordel (canal belt).

History

The earliest canals, including the Singel, were medieval moats. As Amsterdam prospered through trade, the city planned a major expansion in 1613, digging concentric canals lined with warehouses and the tall, narrow merchants' houses that remain the city's signature. Taxation based on facade width encouraged the slender plots; decorative gables and hoisting beams (used to lift goods into attic stores) became characteristic features.

Canals served transport, water management, and defence: Amsterdam sits below sea level, and the system of locks and sluices connects to the Amstel River and the IJsselmeer. In the 19th and 20th centuries some canals were filled in for roads and trams, but the core ring survived and was designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2010.

The canal ring

From innermost to outermost, the principal canals are the Singel, Herengracht (Gentlemen's Canal), Keizersgracht (Emperor's Canal), and Prinsengracht (Prince's Canal), the longest at over 3 km. The stretch of Herengracht between Leidsestraat and Vijzelstraat, the "Golden Bend," holds the grandest mansions. The Jordaan district's smaller canals and the Brouwersgracht are among the most photographed.

Canal life

About 2,500 houseboats are moored along Amsterdam's canals, some converted from historic barges. Events on the water include King's Day celebrations, the Amsterdam Light Festival, and the Canal Parade during Pride Amsterdam. The canals are regularly dredged — famously recovering thousands of bicycles each year — and ice skating on frozen canals is a beloved tradition in cold winters.
