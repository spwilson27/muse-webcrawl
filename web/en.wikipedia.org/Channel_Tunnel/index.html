The Channel Tunnel (French: Tunnel sous la Manche), sometimes referred to in the United Kingdom as the Chunnel, is a 50.46-kilometre (31.35 mi) underwater railway tunnel that connects Folkestone in Kent, England, with Coquelles in Pas-de-Calais, France, beneath the English Channel at the Strait of Dover. It is the only fixed link between the island of Great Britain and the European mainland.

Structure

The tunnel consists of two main rail tunnels 7.6 metres in diameter, spaced 30 metres apart, with a smaller 4.8-metre service tunnel between them connected by cross-passages every 375 metres. The tunnels lie an average of 40 metres beneath the seabed, bored through a layer of chalk marl. Trains run on standard-gauge track with overhead electrification at 25 kV AC.

History

Proposals for a cross-Channel link date to 1802. An attempt begun in the 1880s was abandoned over military fears, and a 1974 attempt was cancelled in 1975 on cost grounds. The 1986 Treaty of Canterbury authorized the present project, built by the Anglo-French Eurotunnel consortium at a cost of about £9.5 billion — some 80% over budget. British and French tunnel boring machines met beneath the Channel on 1 December 1990. The tunnel was formally opened on 6 May 1994 by Queen Elizabeth II and President François Mitterrand.

Services

Eurostar high-speed passenger trains run between London St Pancras and Paris, Brussels, and Amsterdam. LeShuttle services carry cars, coaches, and freight vehicles between the Folkestone and Calais terminals in about 35 minutes. Through freight trains also use the tunnel. Journey time between London and Paris by Eurostar is about 2 hours 15 minutes.

Incidents

Fires in 1996, 2006, and 2008 caused closures and damage but no fatalities among passengers. The tunnel has also been a focus of attempts by migrants to reach the United Kingdom, particularly around the Calais camps.
