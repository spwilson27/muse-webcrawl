The Trinidad and Tobago Carnival is an annual festival held on the Monday and Tuesday before Ash Wednesday in Trinidad and Tobago. This event is well known for participants' colourful costumes and exuberant celebrations. It is often described as "the greatest show on earth" and is the signature cultural event of the twin-island republic, drawing tens of thousands of visitors each year.

Origins

Trinidad's carnival draws on French pre-Lenten masquerade traditions introduced by planters in the 1780s, transformed after emancipation by freed Africans whose Canboulay processions, drumming, and stick-fighting injected African performance traditions into the festival. British colonial ordinances restricting drums and torchlight processions provoked riots in 1881 and 1884 but could not suppress the festival, which instead evolved into the modern national celebration.

Elements

The season builds through months of fetes and preliminary competitions: calypso and soca tents, the Panorama steelband championship, the National Calypso Monarch, extempo contests, stick-fighting, and the elaborate King and Queen of the Bands costume competition. The final two days begin with J'ouvert at 4 a.m. on Monday — revellers covered in mud, oil, paint, and cocoa — and continue with "pretty mas" as costumed bands parade through Port of Spain and other towns. Traditional mas characters such as the Midnight Robber, Dame Lorraine, Jab Jab, and Blue Devils preserve older Carnival forms alongside the modern bikini-and-beads bands.
