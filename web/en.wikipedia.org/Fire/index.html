Fire

Fire is the rapid oxidation of a material (the fuel) in the exothermic chemical process of combustion, releasing heat, light, and various reaction products. Flames, the most visible portion of a fire, are produced when the fuel reaches its ignition temperature and vaporised fuel reacts with oxygen, emitting light from excited molecules and incandescent soot particles.

Fire is one of the four classical elements of ancient Greek philosophy and science. The mastery of fire by early humans — for warmth, protection from predators, cooking, and later for metallurgy and steam power — was a turning point in human cultural evolution. Fire is also a destructive force, responsible for wildfires, structural fires, and burns, and is managed through fire prevention, firefighting, and controlled burning.

Chemistry

Combustion requires four components, often drawn as the fire tetrahedron: fuel, an oxidising agent (typically atmospheric oxygen), heat to reach the ignition temperature, and a self-sustaining chemical chain reaction. Removing any one component extinguishes a fire — the principle behind fire extinguishers, which cool, smother, coat, or interrupt the chain reaction. Common fuels include wood, hydrocarbons, and hydrogen; the idealised combustion of a hydrocarbon yields carbon dioxide and water, while incomplete combustion produces carbon monoxide, soot, and other pollutants.

Flames

A flame is the visible, gaseous part of a fire. Its colour and temperature depend on the fuel and the completeness of combustion: a well-aerated natural gas flame is blue and can exceed 1,500 °C, while a wood fire's orange-yellow glow comes from incandescent soot at roughly 600–1,000 °C. In microgravity, flames form spheres because buoyancy-driven convection is absent.

Human control of fire

Evidence for controlled fire extends back at least 400,000 years, with some claims for Homo erectus sites around one million years old, such as Wonderwerk Cave in South Africa. Fire enabled cooking (which increases the energy available from food), extended activity into the night, allowed settlement of colder climates, and later powered pottery, metalworking, steam engines, and electricity generation. Most of the world's electricity is still produced by burning fuel to boil water for steam turbines.

Fire and ecology

Many ecosystems are fire-adapted: some plants, such as certain pines, have serotinous cones that open only after fire, and grasslands and savannas are maintained by periodic burning. Indigenous peoples practised cultural burning for millennia. Modern wildfires, intensified by fuel accumulation and climate change, cause extensive loss of life and property and release large quantities of carbon dioxide.
