Metrical psalter

A metrical psalter is a collection of the biblical Psalms rendered into metrical verse so that they can be sung to hymn tunes by a congregation. Metrical psalmody became a hallmark of Reformed Protestant worship after the Reformation, when John Calvin promoted congregational singing of psalms in the vernacular.

History

The Genevan Psalter (completed 1562), with texts by Clément Marot and Théodore de Bèze and tunes associated with Louis Bourgeois, set the model and was translated across Europe. In English, the Sternhold and Hopkins psalter (1562) — "Old Version" — dominated for over a century, followed by the Bay Psalm Book (1640), the first book printed in British North America. The Scottish Psalter of 1650, a revision prized for accuracy, remains in use in some Presbyterian churches. Nahum Tate and Nicholas Brady's "New Version" (1696) and Isaac Watts's freer imitations (1719) gradually displaced strict psalmody with hymnody.

Meters

Most metrical psalms use a handful of stanza forms — common meter (8.6.8.6), long meter (8.8.8.8), and short meter (6.6.8.6) — allowing any psalm to be sung to many interchangeable tunes, a practical system for congregations with few books.
