Spontaneous order

Spontaneous order, also named self-organization in some contexts, is the spontaneous emergence of order out of seeming chaos. It is a social theory that describes processes such as the evolution of language, customary law, and market economies, in which order arises from the voluntary actions of individuals rather than from central design.

History of the concept

The idea reaches back to thinkers such as Zhuangzi, Ibn Khaldun, and Adam Smith, whose "invisible hand" described how markets coordinate activity. Carl Menger applied evolutionary explanation to institutions such as money. The economist Friedrich Hayek became the concept's most influential modern proponent, arguing that law, language, and markets are "the results of human action, but not the execution of any human design" — a phrase he borrowed from Adam Ferguson.

Examples

Languages, common law, money, scientific communities, the Internet, and free markets are frequently cited as spontaneous orders. Michael Polanyi applied the idea to the self-coordination of science.

Criticism

Critics note that spontaneous processes can also produce undesirable outcomes and that the theory can be invoked to oppose beneficial regulation; defenders respond that the theory is descriptive and comparative, emphasizing the knowledge problem faced by central planners.
