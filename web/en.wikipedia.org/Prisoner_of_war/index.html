A prisoner of war (POW) is a person held captive by a belligerent power during or immediately after an armed conflict. The status applies chiefly to captured members of the armed forces, and to certain accompanying persons such as war correspondents and supply contractors. Prisoners of war are protected under international humanitarian law, principally the Geneva Convention relative to the Treatment of Prisoners of War of 1949.

Legal protections

The Third Geneva Convention requires that prisoners be treated humanely, protected against violence, insults, and public curiosity, and provided with adequate food, shelter, and medical care. They may be required to give only their name, rank, date of birth, and service number, and may not be tortured or coerced for further information. Prisoners may work under regulated conditions, correspond with their families, and receive visits from the International Committee of the Red Cross. They must be released and repatriated without delay after the cessation of active hostilities.

History

In ancient warfare captives were commonly enslaved or killed. Customary restraints developed unevenly through the medieval and early modern periods; the modern codification began with the Lieber Code of 1863, the Hague Conventions of 1899 and 1907, and the Geneva Convention of 1929. The Second World War saw tens of millions of prisoners: treatment varied drastically by captor, with very high mortality among Soviet prisoners in German hands and among Allied prisoners of Japan, experiences that drove the comprehensive revision of 1949. In later conflicts, including the Korean War and the Vietnam War, repatriation questions — such as whether prisoners could refuse return — became major diplomatic issues.

Qualifying persons

Article 4 of the Third Convention extends POW status to regular armed forces, militias and volunteer corps meeting specified conditions, crews of merchant marine and civil aircraft, and inhabitants who spontaneously take up arms on the approach of the enemy (levée en masse). Persons who do not qualify may still be protected as civilians under the Fourth Geneva Convention.
