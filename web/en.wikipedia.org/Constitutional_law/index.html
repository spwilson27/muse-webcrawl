Constitutional law

Constitutional law is a body of law which defines the role, powers, and structure of different entities within a state, namely, the executive, the parliament or legislature, and the judiciary; as well as the basic rights of citizens and, in federal countries such as the United States and Canada, the relationship between the central government and state, provincial, or territorial governments.

State constitutions

Most national constitutions are codified in a single written document, such as the Constitution of the United States or the Constitution of India. Others, notably the constitution of the United Kingdom, are uncodified and consist of statutes, case law and constitutional conventions accumulated over centuries. A constitution may be entrenched, meaning its amendment requires a special procedure beyond ordinary legislation, or flexible.

Judicial review

Constitutional courts and supreme courts commonly exercise judicial review: the power to invalidate laws and governmental acts that conflict with the constitution. The doctrine was famously asserted by the United States Supreme Court in Marbury v. Madison (1803). In other systems, specialized constitutional courts, such as Germany's Federal Constitutional Court, perform this function.

Rights

Modern constitutional law is closely concerned with the protection of fundamental rights, including freedom of speech, due process, equality before the law and freedom of religion. Instruments such as the United States Bill of Rights, the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms and the European Convention on Human Rights supply standards against which state action is measured.

Federalism and separation of powers

Two structural themes dominate constitutional design: federalism, the division of power between levels of government, and the separation of powers among legislative, executive and judicial branches, each checking the others. Constitutional lawyers study how these arrangements operate in practice as well as in text.
