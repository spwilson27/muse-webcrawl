Mapp v. Ohio

CourtSupreme Court of the United States
Full case nameDollree Mapp v. State of Ohio
DecidedJune 19, 1961
Citation367 U.S. 643

HoldingEvidence obtained in violation of the Fourth Amendment is inadmissible in state court prosecutions; the exclusionary rule applies to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment.

Mapp v. Ohio, 367 U.S. 643 (1961), is a landmark decision of the United States Supreme Court in which the Court held that the exclusionary rule — which forbids prosecutors from using evidence obtained in violation of the Fourth Amendment's protection against unreasonable searches and seizures — applies to the states through the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. The decision overruled Wolf v. Colorado (1949), in which the Court had held the Fourth Amendment applicable to the states but declined to impose the exclusionary rule on them.

Background

On May 23, 1957, Cleveland police officers, acting on a tip that a bombing suspect and illegal betting paraphernalia were hidden in the home of Dollree Mapp, forced their way into her house despite her demand to see a search warrant; the paper an officer waved at her was never produced in court. The officers found no suspect, but in the basement they discovered allegedly obscene books and pictures. Mapp was convicted under Ohio law of possessing obscene materials. The Ohio Supreme Court upheld the conviction. On appeal to the U.S. Supreme Court, the case was argued largely as a First Amendment obscenity case, but the Court decided it on Fourth Amendment grounds.

Decision

Justice Tom C. Clark, writing for a 6–3 majority, reasoned that without the exclusionary rule the Fourth Amendment guarantee would be reduced to "a form of words", and that the rule was an essential part of both the Fourth and Fourteenth Amendments. The Court held that "all evidence obtained by searches and seizures in violation of the Constitution is, by that same authority, inadmissible in a state court." Justice Hugo Black concurred, and Justice William O. Douglas also wrote a concurrence. Justice John Marshall Harlan II dissented, joined by Justices Felix Frankfurter and Charles Evans Whittaker, arguing that the majority had reached an issue the parties had not fully briefed and that federalism counseled leaving remedies to the states.

Significance

Mapp is one of the pillars of the Warren Court's "criminal procedure revolution", alongside Gideon v. Wainwright (1963) and Miranda v. Arizona (1966). It made the exclusionary rule a uniform national standard binding every state and local police force, reshaping American policing by compelling attention to search warrants and constitutional procedure. Later Courts have carved exceptions to the rule — notably the good-faith exception of United States v. Leon (1984) — and have debated its deterrent rationale, but Mapp itself remains good law.
