Weber–Fechner law

The Weber–Fechner law combines two related hypotheses in psychophysics: Weber's law and Fechner's law. Together they propose that the perceived intensity of a stimulus grows as the logarithm of its physical intensity, so that equal ratios of stimulus change produce equal increments in sensation.

Weber's law

Ernst Heinrich Weber observed in the 1830s and 1840s that the just-noticeable difference between two stimuli is proportional to the magnitude of the stimuli: ΔI / I = k, where k is a constant (the Weber fraction) that varies by sense modality.

Fechner's law

Gustav Fechner, building on Weber's work in his Elements of Psychophysics (1860), integrated the Weber relation to obtain a logarithmic scale of sensation, S = k ln(I / I0). The law holds approximately for many senses in mid-range intensities but breaks down near thresholds and at high intensities; Stevens's power law later offered a competing description.
