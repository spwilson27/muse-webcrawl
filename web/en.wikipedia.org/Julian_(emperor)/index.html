Julian (emperor)

Reign: 361 – 363 AD (Caesar in Gaul from 355)
Born: 331 or 332, Constantinople
Died: June 26, 363, near Samarra, Mesopotamia
Predecessor: Constantius II
Successor: Jovian
Known as: Julian the Apostate

Julian (331/332 – 26 June 363) was Roman emperor from 361 to 363, as well as a notable philosopher, satirist, and author in Greek. A member of the Constantinian dynasty, he was the last non-Christian ruler of the Roman Empire, and his rejection of Christianity — which earned him the epithet Julian the Apostate — and his promotion of Roman religious traditions and Neoplatonic Hellenism made him one of antiquity's most controversial figures.

Early life

Julian was a nephew of Constantine the Great. His father and much of his family were murdered in the purges following Constantine's death in 337; Julian and his half-brother Gallus survived under surveillance. Raised a Christian under the supervision of bishops, he secretly turned to pagan philosophy, studying Neoplatonism at Pergamon and Ephesus and being initiated into the Eleusinian and Mithraic mysteries. In 355 his cousin, Emperor Constantius II, appointed him Caesar and sent him to govern Gaul.

Gaul and usurpation

In Gaul, Julian proved an able administrator and general, defeating the Alemanni decisively at the Battle of Strasbourg in 357 and restoring the Rhine frontier. When Constantius ordered his best troops east in 360, the Gallic legions proclaimed Julian Augustus at Paris. Civil war was averted only by Constantius's death in November 361, which left Julian sole emperor.

Religious policy

As emperor, Julian proclaimed general religious toleration while working to revive traditional Roman religion: he reopened temples, restored sacrifices, reorganized a pagan priesthood modelled partly on the Church, and wrote treatises including Against the Galileans. He removed Christians' privileges, barred them from teaching classical literature, and favoured pagans in appointments, while generally avoiding outright persecution and martyrdoms that might strengthen the Church. He also authorized the rebuilding of the Jewish Temple in Jerusalem, a project abandoned after his death.

Persian campaign and death

In 363 Julian invaded Persia with a large army, advancing down the Euphrates to the walls of Ctesiphon. Unable to take the city and harassed on the retreat, he was mortally wounded in a skirmish near Samarra on 26 June 363 — legend later gave him the dying words "You have won, Galilean." His successor Jovian ceded territory to Persia. Julian's brief reign was the last attempt to arrest the Christianization of the empire, and his writings — orations, letters, the satire Misopogon ("Beard-Hater") — survive as major sources for the period.
