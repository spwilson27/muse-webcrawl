Life and death (Go)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In the game of Go, life and death (shikatsu in Japanese) refers to the status of a group of stones: whether the group can avoid capture and remain on the board permanently, or whether it can be captured and removed. A group is "alive" if it cannot be captured under optimal play, and "dead" if it inevitably can be. Life and death is one of the central tactical themes of Go, and its study in the form of composed problems is called tsumego.

Eyes and false eyes

The fundamental principle is the eye: an empty point (or connected region) fully surrounded by a group. Because a move inside the opponent's eye is normally a self-capture (prohibited unless it captures), a group with two separate eyes cannot be captured: the opponent can never fill both eyes, since playing in the last remaining eye would be an illegal self-capture unless it captures the whole group, which requires filling the other eye first — an impossibility. Hence the maxim "two eyes live."

An apparent eye may be a false eye if the surrounding stones are themselves weak — for example, if some of the stones bordering the eye are in atari and can be captured, opening the shape. Judging whether an eye is real or false is a basic skill in reading life and death.

Status categories

Between unconditional life and unconditional death there are intermediate statuses:

  • Ko: the group's survival depends on winning a ko fight.
  • Seki (mutual life): two weak groups live side by side because neither player can capture the other without being captured first; typically neither has an eye, but the shared liberties prevent attack.
  • Unsettled: the group can be made alive by its owner or killed by the opponent, depending on who plays first.

Common shapes

Certain interior shapes in a corner or on the edge are proverbially classified: the "straight three" is unsettled, the "bent four in the corner" is alive under Chinese and Korean rules but is a famous source of dispute under Japanese rules, where it is treated as dead in most circumstances, and a "six-point eye space" patterns vary by symmetry. Classic collections of tsumego such as the Gengen Gokyo catalogue these statuses.

Rules differences

At the end of a game, disputed groups are normally resolved by agreement; if the players disagree, some rulesets require play to resume. Japanese scoring traditionally avoids playing out capture sequences, which gives rise to special rulings on shapes like bent four in the corner, whereas Chinese-style area scoring allows such questions to be settled by simply playing the moves.
