The NOVA classification is a framework for grouping edible products according to the extent and purpose of food processing they undergo. Developed by researchers at the University of São Paulo, Brazil, and championed by epidemiologist Carlos Augusto Monteiro, it assigns foods to one of four groups. The system is particularly known for defining and popularising the category of ultra-processed foods.

The four groups

Group 1 — Unprocessed or minimally processed foods: edible parts of plants and animals as found in nature, and foods altered only by drying, grinding, pasteurisation, refrigeration, freezing, and similar processes that add no substances. Examples include fresh fruit, vegetables, grains, milk, and fresh meat.

Group 2 — Processed culinary ingredients: substances obtained from Group 1 foods or from nature by pressing, refining, or extraction, used in cooking — oils, butter, sugar, and salt.

Group 3 — Processed foods: relatively simple products made by adding Group 2 ingredients to Group 1 foods, such as canned vegetables in brine, salted nuts, cheese, and freshly made bread.

Group 4 — Ultra-processed foods: industrial formulations made mostly or entirely from extracted substances and additives — soft drinks, packaged snacks, reconstituted meat products, instant noodles, and many ready-to-heat meals.

Use and reception

NOVA has been adopted in dietary guidelines by Brazil (2014), several other countries, and reports of the Food and Agriculture Organization. Epidemiological studies using NOVA associate high ultra-processed food consumption with obesity, cardiovascular disease, and mortality. Critics argue the categories are broad, can group nutritionally dissimilar products, and that processing per se is not always a reliable proxy for healthfulness.
