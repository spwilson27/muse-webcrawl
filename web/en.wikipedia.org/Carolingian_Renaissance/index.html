The Carolingian Renaissance was a period of intellectual and cultural revival in the Frankish Empire during the late 8th and 9th centuries, under Charlemagne and his successors. It is the first of three medieval "renaissances" (followed by the Ottonian and 12th-century renaissances) and saw major reforms in education, scholarship, literature, art, and architecture.

Reforms

Charlemagne gathered scholars from across Europe and beyond — including Alcuin of York, Peter of Pisa, and Theodulf of Orléans — to his palace school at Aachen. Royal capitularies such as the Admonitio generalis (789) ordered monasteries and cathedrals to maintain schools and to teach correct Latin. Scribes developed Caroline minuscule, a clear, standardised book hand that greatly improved legibility and became an ancestor of modern lowercase type.

Achievements

Carolingian monks systematically copied and preserved classical Latin literature: the oldest surviving manuscripts of most Roman authors are Carolingian copies. Scholarship flourished in grammar, theology, computus (calendar science), and music theory, while art and architecture — such as the Palatine Chapel at Aachen and illuminated gospel books like the Utrecht Psalter — revived classical forms. The revival faded amid the political fragmentation and Viking, Magyar, and Saracen invasions of the later 9th and 10th centuries, but its preservation of texts and standardisation of writing permanently shaped European culture.
