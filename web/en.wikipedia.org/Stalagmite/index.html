Stalagmite

Stalagmites on the floor of a limestone cave.

A stalagmite is a type of speleothem—a rock formation that rises from the floor of a cave due to the accumulation of material deposited on the floor from ceiling drippings. Stalagmites are typically composed of calcium carbonate or other minerals precipitated from mineralized water dripping through the cave ceiling.

Formation

As water rich in dissolved carbon dioxide percolates through limestone, it dissolves calcium carbonate. When the water emerges into a cave and releases carbon dioxide, it can no longer hold as much mineral in solution, and calcite is deposited. Water dripping from the ceiling builds a stalactite downward; water striking the floor splashes and deposits calcite in a mound that grows upward as a stalagmite. Growth rates are slow, typically a fraction of a millimetre per year. Given enough time, a stalactite and stalagmite may join to form a column.

Distinction from stalactites

A common mnemonic is that stalagmites "mite" rise from the ground and have a 'g' for ground, while stalactites have a 'c' for ceiling. Unlike stalactites, which often end in a sharp point, stalagmites usually have rounded or flattened tips because the falling drops splash on impact.

Other types

Stalagmite-like formations occur in lava tubes, formed of congealed lava, and as icicles of mineral salts in mines. Stalagmites are valuable to paleoclimatologists: their growth layers record past rainfall and temperature, complementing other natural archives studied with statistics such as measures of variability.
