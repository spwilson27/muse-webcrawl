Nucleotide

A nucleotide is an organic molecule composed of three subunit molecules: a nitrogenous base, a five-carbon (pentose) sugar, and one or more phosphate groups. Nucleotides are the monomeric building blocks of the nucleic acids DNA and RNA, in which they are joined into chains by phosphodiester bonds between the sugar of one nucleotide and the phosphate of the next.

Structure

The nitrogenous bases are of two types: the double-ringed purines — adenine (A) and guanine (G) — and the single-ringed pyrimidines — cytosine (C), thymine (T, in DNA) and uracil (U, in RNA). The sugar is deoxyribose in DNA and ribose in RNA. A base joined to a sugar alone is called a nucleoside; addition of one, two or three phosphates gives a nucleoside mono-, di- or triphosphate.

Functions beyond nucleic acids

Nucleotides serve many roles outside DNA and RNA. Adenosine triphosphate (ATP) is the principal energy currency of the cell; GTP drives protein synthesis and signal transduction; cyclic AMP and cyclic GMP act as second messengers; and nucleotide derivatives form parts of coenzymes such as NAD and coenzyme A.

Synthesis and diet

Cells synthesise nucleotides both de novo from amino acids, carbon dioxide and other small precursors, and by salvaging bases and nucleosides recycled from nucleic acid turnover. Dietary nucleotides are largely broken down in the gut. Defects in nucleotide metabolism underlie several diseases, including gout (overproduction of uric acid from purine breakdown) and Lesch–Nyhan syndrome (defective salvage of purines).
