Machiavellianism (psychology)

Machiavellianism is a personality trait characterised by interpersonal manipulation, a cynical disregard for conventional morality, a focus on self-interest, and a tendency toward strategic, calculating behaviour. In personality psychology it forms one third of the so-called Dark Triad, alongside narcissism and psychopathy. The construct takes its name from the Italian Renaissance political philosopher Niccolò Machiavelli, whose work The Prince (1532) was read by later interpreters as endorsing deceitful and amoral statecraft.

Machiavellianism is measured as a dimension of normal personality variation.

History and measurement

The modern construct was developed in the 1960s by social psychologists Richard Christie and Florence L. Geis, who derived a set of statements from Machiavelli's writings and from observations of manipulative behaviour. Their 1970 book Studies in Machiavellianism introduced the Mach-IV, a 20-item self-report inventory scored on a Likert scale. Respondents who endorse items such as "Never tell anyone the real reason you did something unless it is useful to do so" score as "high Machs"; those who reject them are "low Machs". A shorter form, the Mach-V, was designed to reduce social-desirability bias.

Characteristics

High scorers tend to be pragmatic, emotionally detached in negotiations, and skilled at reading social situations for personal advantage. Experimental studies found that high Machs manipulate more, win more in bargaining tasks, and persuade more effectively than low Machs, particularly in face-to-face interactions with few rules and scope for improvisation. They tend to hold a cynical view of human nature, believing others are weak, selfish, and manipulable. Research also links the trait to lower empathy, lower agreeableness, and a strategic rather than impulsive style of dishonesty — distinguishing it from the reckless impulsivity typical of psychopathy.

The Dark Triad and Dark Tetrad

In 2002 Delroy Paulhus and Kevin Williams grouped Machiavellianism with subclinical narcissism and subclinical psychopathy as the Dark Triad of personality. Some researchers have questioned whether Machiavellianism and psychopathy are empirically distinguishable in self-report measures, since the scales correlate strongly and share items about callousness and deceit. Others argue the two differ in temporal orientation: Machiavellian individuals are planful and reputation-conscious, whereas psychopathic individuals are impulsive and thrill-seeking. Adding everyday sadism yields the proposed "Dark Tetrad".

Workplace and organisational behaviour

Machiavellianism has been studied extensively in organisational settings. High Machs are over-represented in competitive, loosely structured environments and may attain leadership positions through coalition-building and credit-claiming, though their teams often report lower trust and job satisfaction. Meta-analyses associate the trait with counterproductive work behaviour, knowledge hiding, and abusive supervision when combined with low honesty–humility.
