Ground plane

A large copper area on a circuit board forms a ground plane.

In electrical engineering, a ground plane is an electrically conductive surface, usually connected to electrical ground, that serves as a reference potential and a return path for currents. Ground planes appear in two main contexts: as part of an antenna system, and as a layer of a printed circuit board.

In antennas

For a monopole antenna — a single vertical radiator such as a quarter-wave whip — a ground plane (the earth itself, a set of radial wires, or a metal surface such as a car roof) forms the "missing half" of the dipole by the method of images: the plane reflects the monopole so that it behaves like a full dipole. The size and quality of the ground plane strongly affect the antenna's impedance, radiation pattern and efficiency; artificial ground planes of radial conductors are used when the earth is a poor conductor.

In printed circuit boards

On a multilayer printed circuit board, an entire layer of copper is often devoted to ground (a "power plane" may similarly distribute a supply voltage). A solid ground plane provides a low-impedance return path for signals, which reduces noise and electromagnetic emissions, improves signal integrity by controlling trace impedance, and acts as a shield between signal layers. Good practice places the plane unbroken beneath high-speed traces so return currents can flow directly under their signal paths; splits in the plane can force return currents to detour, increasing loop area and interference.
