Jackrabbit

Jackrabbits are North American hares classified in the genus Lepus. Despite the name they are true hares, not rabbits, and are generally larger, with longer ears and hind legs. Six species are commonly recognised, including the white-tailed jackrabbit (Lepus townsendii), the black-tailed jackrabbit (Lepus californicus) and the antelope jackrabbit (Lepus antelope).

Description

Jackrabbits range from about 2 to 7 kilograms in weight. Their very long ears help dissipate heat, an adaptation to the hot deserts and open plains they inhabit. They are powerful runners, capable of bursts of speed up to about 64 km/h (40 mph) and leaps of several metres in a zigzag pattern to evade predators.

Habitat and behaviour

They live in open country — deserts, shrublands, prairies and farmland — across western and central North America. Largely solitary and nocturnal, they rest by day in shallow scrapes called forms, relying on camouflage. They feed on grasses, forbs, shrubs and, in winter, bark and twigs.

Reproduction

Unlike rabbits, hares give birth to precocial young — fully furred, eyes open, and able to move shortly after birth — in the open rather than in burrows. Females may raise several litters per year in mild climates.

Relationship with humans

Jackrabbits figure in Native American folklore, including trickster tales, and in the legendary "jackalope". They are hunted as game and can be agricultural pests, though they are also an important prey species for eagles, coyotes and other predators.
