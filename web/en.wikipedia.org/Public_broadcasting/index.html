Public broadcasting

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Public broadcasting (or public service broadcasting) involves radio, television and other electronic media outlets whose primary mission is public service. Public broadcasters receive funding from diverse sources including license fees, individual contributions, commercial sponsorships and government funding, and are generally operated independently of direct government editorial control.

The model is often contrasted with state media, in which the government exercises direct editorial control, and with commercial broadcasting, which is funded primarily by advertising. Public broadcasters typically aim to provide programming that informs, educates and entertains, serving audiences that commercial media may neglect, including children, minorities and rural communities.

History

The British Broadcasting Corporation (BBC), founded in 1922 and reconstituted as a public corporation in 1927, became the archetype of the public service model under its first director-general, John Reith, whose mission was to "inform, educate and entertain". The licence fee funding model adopted in the United Kingdom was widely imitated, notably in Germany, Japan (NHK) and the Nordic countries.

In the United States, public broadcasting developed later and on a decentralised model: the Public Broadcasting Act of 1967 created the Corporation for Public Broadcasting, leading to the creation of PBS for television and NPR for radio.

Funding models

Public broadcasters are funded through a variety of mechanisms: a household licence fee or media tax (United Kingdom, Germany, Japan), direct government appropriation (Australia, Canada, Spain), voluntary listener and viewer contributions (United States), and limited advertising or sponsorship. Funding arrangements are a recurring subject of political debate, centring on independence, value for money and competition with commercial media.

Examples

  • BBC (United Kingdom)
  • PBS and NPR (United States)
  • CBC/Radio-Canada (Canada)
  • ABC and SBS (Australia)
  • NHK (Japan)
  • ARD and ZDF (Germany)
  • France Télévisions and Radio France (France)

Criticism

Critics argue that public broadcasters can exhibit institutional or political bias, duplicate commercial offerings, and that compulsory funding is difficult to justify in an era of abundant media choice. Supporters respond that public media sustain civic information, cultural production and universal access in ways markets alone do not.
