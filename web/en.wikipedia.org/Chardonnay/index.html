Chardonnay

Chardonnay
SpeciesVitis vinifera
OriginBurgundy, France
ParentagePinot noir × Gouais blanc
Notable regionsBurgundy, Champagne, California, Australia

Chardonnay is a green-skinned grape variety used in the production of white wine. The variety originated in the Burgundy wine region of eastern France, but is now grown wherever wine is produced, from England to New Zealand. For new and developing wine regions, growing Chardonnay is seen as a "rite of passage" and an easy entry into the international wine market.

The Chardonnay grape itself is neutral, with many of the flavors commonly associated with the wine being derived from influences such as terroir and oak. It is vinified in many different styles, from the lean, crisply mineral wines of Chablis to the fuller-bodied, oak-aged wines of California and Australia with tropical fruit flavors. It is also one of the principal grapes used in Champagne and other sparkling wines.

History and parentage

DNA fingerprinting research at the University of California, Davis, showed that Chardonnay is the result of a cross between Pinot noir and Gouais blanc, a Croatian variety rarely grown today. The cross likely occurred in eastern France where both parent varieties were planted. Chardonnay takes its name from the village of Chardonnay in the Mâconnais region of Burgundy.

Viticulture

Chardonnay is an early-budding variety, which makes it vulnerable to spring frosts — a risk famously managed in Chablis with smudge pots and water sprinklers. It is a vigorous, adaptable vine that ripens early. In cool climates it produces wines with high acidity and flavors of green apple and citrus; in warm climates the wines become fuller with melon and tropical fruit character. The variety is susceptible to powdery mildew, botrytis, and coulure.

Winemaking styles

Winemakers may ferment and age Chardonnay in stainless steel for a crisp, unoaked style, or in oak barrels, which add vanilla and spice notes and permit malolactic fermentation that softens acidity and imparts buttery flavors. Lees stirring (bâtonnage) adds texture. The variety's versatility contributed to its dominance in the 1980s and 1990s, and also to a later backlash summarized by the "ABC" slogan — "Anything But Chardonnay" — as some consumers turned to lighter styles.
