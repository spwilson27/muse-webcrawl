Portico

A portico is a porch leading to the entrance of a building, consisting of a roof structure supported by columns or enclosed by walls. It is a defining element of classical architecture, originating in ancient Greek temples, where it formed a covered walkway (the stoa being a related, longer form) and a ceremonial threshold between the outside world and the sanctuary.

Ancient origins

Greek temples characteristically placed a portico of columns before the cella, often at both ends. The number of columns in the front row gives rise to standard terms: a portico with four columns is tetrastyle, six hexastyle, eight octastyle, and ten decastyle. The Roman world adopted and monumentalised the form; the Pantheon in Rome, rebuilt under Hadrian around AD 118–125, retains a great octastyle portico of Egyptian granite columns supporting a pediment.

Revival and later use

The portico returned to prominence with Renaissance architecture, notably in the works of Andrea Palladio, whose villas and churches applied temple-front porticoes to secular and sacred buildings alike. Neoclassical architecture of the 18th and 19th centuries made the portico a standard feature of civic buildings, churches, country houses, and universities across Europe and North America. In the United States, the Greek Revival style applied porticoes to plantation houses, courthouses, and banks; the White House is fronted by the columned North Portico (completed 1830). The British Museum in London presents a grand Ionic portico with 44 columns.

Related forms

  • Pronaos — the inner portico or vestibule of a temple, between the outer colonnade and the cella.
  • Stoa — a long covered colonnade, often freestanding, used in Greek public spaces.
  • Loggia — a gallery or arcade open on one side, often at an upper level.
  • Porte-cochère — a porch large enough for vehicles to pass beneath, sheltering arriving passengers.
