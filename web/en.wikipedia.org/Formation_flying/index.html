Formation flying

Aircraft in a tight fingertip formation.

Formation flying is the flight of multiple aircraft in a coordinated manner, maintaining set positions relative to a leader. Birds fly in formation naturally — most famously in the V formation of migrating geese, which saves energy by exploiting the upwash from the wingtip vortices of the bird ahead — and military aviators adopted disciplined formations from the First World War onwards for mutual defence, concentration of firepower and ease of command.

Standard formations

  • V formation (vic) — aircraft arranged in a V behind the leader; the standard interwar bomber and fighter pattern.
  • Echelon — aircraft stepped diagonally up or down to one side of the leader.
  • Fingertip (finger-four) — four aircraft positioned like the fingertips of an outstretched hand; developed into the Luftwaffe's Schwarm and the Allied finger-four of the Second World War.
  • Fluid four / tactical formations — modern spread formations (wall, line abreast, combat spread) giving each pilot room to manoeuvre and check blind spots.
  • Diamond — used by display teams such as the Thunderbirds and Blue Angels, along with arrowhead, delta and trail formations.

Technique

Wingmen hold position by visual reference on the leader's aircraft, making small, smooth power and control corrections; only the leader navigates and communicates, which reduces radio traffic and lets the formation pass through airspace as a single unit under air traffic control. Formation takeoffs, joins (rendezvous), cross-unders and breakaways for landing are standardized manoeuvres taught in military flight training.

Uses

Beyond combat, formation flying is used for aerial refuelling, mass transport and parachute drops, search-and-rescue patterns, ceremonial flypasts and aerobatic displays. In spaceflight, the term also applies to formation-flying satellites, whose relative positions are maintained to centimetre precision for interferometry and other missions.
