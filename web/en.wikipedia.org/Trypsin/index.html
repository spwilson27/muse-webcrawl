Trypsin is an enzyme in the digestive system and a vital component of pancreatic juice, where it is secreted in its inactive precursor form, trypsinogen, and activated in the small intestine. It is a serine protease that hydrolyses proteins into smaller peptides during digestion, cleaving peptide bonds on the carboxyl side of the basic amino acids lysine and arginine.

Activation

To protect the pancreas from digesting itself, trypsin is produced as the inactive zymogen trypsinogen. On reaching the duodenum, the enzyme enteropeptidase converts trypsinogen to active trypsin; trypsin then activates more trypsinogen and the other pancreatic zymogens, including chymotrypsinogen and procarboxypeptidases. Premature activation within the pancreas is a cause of acute pancreatitis.

Properties

Trypsin works best at a slightly alkaline pH, around 8, matching conditions in the small intestine. It is one of the most widely used enzymes in the laboratory: in proteomics it digests proteins into peptides for mass spectrometry ("tryptic peptides"), and in cell culture it is used to detach adherent cells from culture vessels.

Discovery

Trypsin was identified in pancreatic juice in the nineteenth century, notably by Wilhelm Kühne, who coined the term "enzyme". Bovine trypsin was among the early enzymes to be crystallised, by John Howard Northrop and Moses Kunitz in the 1930s.
