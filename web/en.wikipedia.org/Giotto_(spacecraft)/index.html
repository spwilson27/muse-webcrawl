Giotto (spacecraft)

Giotto was a European robotic spacecraft built and launched by the European Space Agency (ESA) to study Halley's Comet. On 14 March 1986 it passed within about 596 kilometres of the comet's nucleus — the closest encounter achieved by the international fleet of probes known as the "Halley Armada" — and returned the first close-up images of a comet's nucleus.

Mission

Giotto was launched on 2 July 1985 by an Ariane 1 rocket from French Guiana. The spacecraft was named after the Italian painter Giotto di Bondone, who had observed Halley's Comet in 1301 and is believed to have depicted it as the Star of Bethlehem in his fresco Adoration of the Magi in Padua's Scrovegni Chapel.

The cylindrical spacecraft, based on the GEOS satellite design, carried ten instruments including a camera, mass spectrometers, and dust detectors. Because it met the comet at a relative velocity of about 68 kilometres per second, it was protected by a Whipple dust shield. Seconds before closest approach, a large dust particle struck the spacecraft and knocked its antenna off Earth-pointing, interrupting data transmission; remarkably, the probe stabilised itself and resumed transmitting. Its images revealed a dark, peanut-shaped nucleus roughly 15 by 8 kilometres, covered in craters and venting jets of gas and dust.

Extended mission

After a period of hibernation and a swing by Earth, Giotto was retargeted to Comet Grigg–Skjellerup, which it flew past on 10 July 1992 at a distance of about 200 kilometres — the first spacecraft to visit two comets. The mission was switched off later that month.
