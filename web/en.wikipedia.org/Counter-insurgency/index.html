Counter-insurgency

Counter-insurgency (also written counterinsurgency and abbreviated COIN) comprises the comprehensive civilian and military efforts taken to simultaneously defeat and contain an insurgency and to address its root causes. An insurgency is a rebellion against an established authority — or, in cases of foreign occupation, against an occupying power — by irregular forces. Counter-insurgency doctrine holds that because insurgents rely on the population for support, recruits, and concealment, success depends less on destroying enemy forces than on securing and winning over the population.

Doctrine

Classical COIN theory, developed by writers such as David Galula (from French experience in Algeria), Robert Thompson (from the Malayan Emergency), and later codified in the United States Army and Marine Corps field manual FM 3-24 (2006), emphasizes political primacy, legitimacy, intelligence-driven operations, minimum necessary force, and the protection of civilians. The population-centric approach is summarized in the maxim of "clear, hold, build": clear an area of insurgents, hold it with security forces, and build government services and legitimacy.

Historical campaigns

Notable counter-insurgency campaigns include the British effort in the Malayan Emergency (1948–1960), often cited as a success; the French defeat in Algeria; the American-led efforts in Vietnam; and the wars in Iraq and Afghanistan, where the 2007 Iraq "surge" coincided with a sharp but temporary fall in violence. Critics note that COIN campaigns are typically long, expensive, and dependent on the host government's competence and legitimacy.

Criticism

Scholars have questioned whether population-centric COIN can deliver strategic success, pointing to the recurrence of insurgency after withdrawals and the difficulty of building legitimate partner governments. Enemy-centric approaches, focused on killing or capturing insurgent leadership, remain a competing tradition.
