Deontological ethics

Deontological ethics (from Greek déon, "duty, obligation") is the normative ethical theory that the morality of an action should be judged by whether the action itself conforms to a set of rules or duties, rather than by its consequences. On deontological views, some acts — lying, killing the innocent, breaking promises — are wrong in themselves and may not be done even to bring about more good overall. Deontology is standardly contrasted with consequentialism and with virtue ethics.

Kantian deontology

The paradigm of deontological theory is the ethics of Immanuel Kant, set out in the Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals (1785). Kant held that the only thing good without qualification is the good will, acting from duty rather than inclination, and that rational agents must obey the categorical imperative: act only on maxims that you can at the same time will to become universal laws, and always treat humanity, in yourself and others, as an end in itself and never merely as a means. Because the moral law is derived from reason itself, Kantian duties bind categorically, without exception for desired outcomes.

Other forms

W. D. Ross defended a pluralist deontology of prima facie duties — fidelity, reparation, gratitude, justice, beneficence, non-maleficence, and self-improvement — which can conflict and must be weighed judgementally. Divine command theory grounds duties in the commands of God. Rights-based theories (from Locke to Robert Nozick) treat individual rights as side-constraints that may not be violated for aggregate benefit. Contractualism, notably T. M. Scanlon's account of actions being wrong if they would be disallowed by principles no one could reasonably reject, is also commonly classed as deontological.

Features and criticism

Deontological theories typically draw distinctions such as acting versus allowing harm, and intending harm versus merely foreseeing it (the doctrine of double effect), and they hold that agents have special obligations to particular people (family, promisees, compatriots) that impartial consequentialism cannot easily accommodate. Critics object that rigid rule-following can produce perverse results — Kant himself appeared to hold that one must not lie even to a murderer asking after his victim — and that deontology struggles to say what to do when duties conflict or when obeying a duty leads to catastrophe. "Threshold deontologists" reply that constraints hold until consequences become bad enough to override them.

Applied contexts

Deontological reasoning pervades law and professional ethics: prohibitions on torture and cruel punishment, patients' rights to refuse treatment, lawyers' duties of confidentiality, and rules of armed conflict all function as duties that are not simply traded off against good outcomes.
