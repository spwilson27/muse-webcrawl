The geography of Fiji is dominated by its archipelago of more than 330 volcanic and coral islands in the South Pacific Ocean, lying roughly 2,000 kilometres north of New Zealand. The total land area is about 18,274 square kilometres, within an exclusive economic zone spread over some 1.3 million square kilometres of ocean. The 180th meridian passes through the island group, near Taveuni.

Main islands

Two large islands account for most of the land area and population: Viti Levu, home to the capital Suva and the main international gateway at Nadi, and Vanua Levu to the north. Other significant groups include the Lomaiviti, Lau, Yasawa, and Mamanuca islands, and the large islands of Taveuni, Kadavu, and Vanua Balavu. About 110 islands are permanently inhabited.

Terrain and climate

The larger islands are volcanic in origin, with rugged mountainous interiors — Mount Tomanivi (1,324 m) on Viti Levu is the highest point — dissected by river systems such as the Rewa, Navua, and Sigatoka. Fiji has a tropical maritime climate: the southeast trade winds make the eastern (windward) sides wet and lush, while the western (leeward) sides are markedly drier. The cyclone season runs from November to April, and Fiji is exposed to damaging tropical cyclones, sea-level rise, and coastal flooding.

Environment

Fiji's isolation has produced distinctive ecosystems, including lowland rainforest, mangroves, and extensive fringing coral reefs such as the Great Sea Reef off Vanua Levu. Environmental pressures include deforestation, reef degradation, and the climate vulnerability typical of Pacific small island developing states.
