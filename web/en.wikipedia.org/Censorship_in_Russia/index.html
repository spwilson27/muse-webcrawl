Censorship in Russia

Censorship in the Russian Federation is the exercise of control and suppression by the state over what can be published, broadcast and accessed. Although the Russian Constitution prohibits censorship in Article 29, a body of laws on extremism, "fake news", protection of children and state secrets has been used to restrict independent media, and Russia ranks near the bottom of international press-freedom indices.

Imperial era

Censorship was institutionalised under Catherine the Great and systematised under Nicholas I, whose 1826 censorship statute was notoriously strict. Alexander II's reforms of 1865 relaxed pre-publication censorship for many works, but revolutionary literature was driven abroad or underground.

Soviet era

The Bolsheviks established Glavlit in 1922, which censored all print, theatre and later broadcast media for seventy years; samizdat — self-published dissident literature — circumvented controls. Soviet censorship suppressed religious, nationalist and "anti-Soviet" material and enforced socialist realism.

Post-Soviet era

The 1990s saw an explosion of independent media under Boris Yeltsin. Under Vladimir Putin the state and allied companies regained control of national television, and in the 2010s internet regulation expanded: the 2012 blacklist law, the "sovereign internet" law of 2019, and the labelling of independent outlets as "foreign agents". After the full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022, laws criminalising "false information" about the armed forces (punishable by up to 15 years' imprisonment) caused the closure or exile of most remaining independent outlets, and access to Facebook, Instagram and many foreign news sites was blocked.
