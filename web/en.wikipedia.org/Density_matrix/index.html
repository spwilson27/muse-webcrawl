Density matrix

In quantum mechanics, a density matrix (or density operator) is a matrix that describes the quantum state of a physical system, allowing for the calculation of the probabilities of the outcomes of any measurement performed upon it. Unlike a wave function, a density matrix can describe mixed states — statistical ensembles of different quantum states — as well as pure states.

Definition

For a system that is in state |ψi⟩ with probability pi, the density operator is ρ = Σ pi |ψi⟩⟨ψi|. A density matrix is Hermitian, positive semi-definite, and has trace equal to one. A state is pure if and only if Tr(ρ²) = 1; for mixed states Tr(ρ²) < 1.

Motivation

Density matrices are needed when a system's exact state is unknown and only statistical information is available, and when describing a subsystem of an entangled pair: tracing over the other subsystem's degrees of freedom yields a reduced density matrix that is generally mixed.

Time evolution and measurement

The evolution of a closed system is given by the von Neumann equation, iħ dρ/dt = [H, ρ]. The expectation value of an observable A is ⟨A⟩ = Tr(ρA). The von Neumann entropy S = −Tr(ρ log ρ) measures the mixedness of the state, generalizing classical information entropy.

The formalism was introduced independently in 1927 by John von Neumann and by Lev Landau.
