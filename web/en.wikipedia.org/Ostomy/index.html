Ostomy

An ostomy is a surgical procedure that creates an artificial opening (a stoma, from the Greek for "mouth") in the body for the discharge of wastes or for feeding. Ostomies may be temporary or permanent and are performed when part of the digestive or urinary tract is diseased, injured, or removed. Body waste passes through the stoma into an external pouch worn over the opening.

Types

A colostomy brings part of the colon to the abdominal wall; an ileostomy does the same for the ileum and is common after total colectomy for ulcerative colitis or familial adenomatous polyposis. A urostomy diverts urine, typically using an ileal conduit after bladder removal. A gastrostomy (such as a PEG tube) provides feeding access to the stomach, while a tracheostomy is an opening in the windpipe for breathing. Common reasons for bowel ostomies include colorectal cancer, inflammatory bowel disease, diverticulitis, trauma, and congenital conditions such as Hirschsprung disease.

Living with a stoma

Modern pouching systems are discreet and odour-proof, and most people with stomas resume normal work, exercise, and travel. Stoma care nurses (enterostomal therapists) teach management and help with complications such as skin irritation, retraction, prolapse, and parastomal hernia. Continent alternatives such as the ileal pouch–anal anastomosis (J-pouch) and Kock pouch suit some patients, avoiding an external appliance.
