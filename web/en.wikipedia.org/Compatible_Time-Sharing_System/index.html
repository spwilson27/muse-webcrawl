Compatible Time-Sharing System (CTSS)

The Compatible Time-Sharing System (CTSS), first demonstrated in November 1961, was one of the first time-sharing operating systems and a landmark in the history of computing. It was developed at the MIT Computation Center under the direction of Fernando J. Corbató. "Compatible" referred to its ability to run standard IBM batch-processing software (the Fortran Monitor System) in a background partition alongside interactive time-sharing use.

Features

CTSS allowed multiple users at teleprinter terminals to share one mainframe computer interactively, each receiving the illusion of having the machine to themselves. It introduced or pioneered many features now taken for granted: user passwords for security (one of the first computer password systems), an online file system, inter-user messaging (a precursor of electronic mail, implemented with the MAIL command in 1965), text editing and formatting programs (TYPSET and RUNOFF), and the first computerized bulletin-board and instant-messaging facilities. Computer scientist Tom Van Vleck maintained a CTSS system that was the last of its kind to run, on an IBM 7094, until 1973.

Influence

CTSS directly inspired MIT's Project MAC and the Multics project (a collaboration of MIT, Bell Labs, and General Electric); Multics in turn profoundly influenced Unix, whose creators Ken Thompson and Dennis Ritchie had worked on Multics. Corbató received the Turing Award in 1990 in part for his work on CTSS and Multics.
