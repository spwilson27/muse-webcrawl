The Proto-Indo-Europeans are the hypothetical prehistoric population of Eurasia who spoke Proto-Indo-European (PIE), the reconstructed common ancestor of the Indo-European languages — today the world's largest language family, including English, Spanish, Hindi, Persian and Russian. No direct records of them survive; their society is inferred from comparative linguistics, archaeology and, more recently, ancient DNA.

Homeland and migrations

The leading Kurgan hypothesis, developed by Marija Gimbutas and supported by David Anthony, places the homeland on the Pontic–Caspian steppe north of the Black Sea in the 5th–4th millennia BCE, associating the speakers with the Yamnaya culture: mobile pastoralists with wheeled wagons, domesticated horses, herds of cattle and sheep, and burials under mounds (kurgans). From about 3000 BCE, steppe populations expanded westward into Europe and eastward and southward toward Central and South Asia — movements now documented genetically by large influxes of "steppe ancestry" into both Europe and India. An alternative Anatolian hypothesis links the dispersal to the spread of farming from Anatolia around 7000 BCE; a hybrid "steppe–Anatolian" model has also been proposed.

Reconstructed society

Reconstructed PIE vocabulary suggests a patrilineal, kin-based society organised in clans, practising pastoralism supplemented by some agriculture, with knowledge of the wheel, wool, copper and bronze. Scholars have reconstructed elements of a common religion — including a sky father (*Dyḗus), a dawn goddess and divine twins — and poetic formulas, and a social ideology sometimes analysed as trifunctional (priests, warriors, producers). Descendant groups include the ancestors of the Proto-Indo-Iranians, Greeks, Celts, Germanic, Slavic, Baltic and Italic peoples.
