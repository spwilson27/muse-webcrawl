End of the Han dynasty

The end of the Han dynasty refers to the period of Chinese history from 189 to 220 CE, during which the Eastern Han dynasty disintegrated into warlordism before its formal end with the abdication of Emperor Xian. The collapse ushered in the Three Kingdoms period, as the empire split into the rival states of Cao Wei, Shu Han, and Eastern Wu.

Background

By the late 2nd century, the Han court was weakened by factional struggles between consort clans, eunuchs, and scholar-officials, by corruption, and by heavy taxation. In 184 the Yellow Turban Rebellion, a vast peasant uprising led by the religious leader Zhang Jue, erupted across eastern China. Although the rebellion was suppressed, the generals and provincial governors who raised armies to fight it retained their forces and became largely autonomous.

Warlord era

In 189, after the court massacred the eunuchs, the general Dong Zhuo seized the capital Luoyang and installed the young Emperor Xian as a puppet. A coalition of warlords campaigned against Dong, who burned Luoyang and moved the capital to Chang'an before his assassination in 192. The empire then fragmented among competing warlords including Yuan Shao, Yuan Shu, Lü Bu, Liu Bei, Sun Ce, and Cao Cao. Cao Cao, holding the emperor from 196, defeated Yuan Shao at Guandu in 200 and unified the north, but his failure at Red Cliffs in 208 against the allied forces of Sun Quan and Liu Bei confirmed the division of China into north and south.

Abdication

Cao Cao died in 220, and his son Cao Pi compelled Emperor Xian to abdicate that winter, ending four centuries of Han rule and founding the state of Wei. Liu Bei and Sun Quan subsequently proclaimed their own dynasties, Shu Han (221) and Eastern Wu (229), completing the Three Kingdoms division. The period is immortalized in the 14th-century novel Romance of the Three Kingdoms.
