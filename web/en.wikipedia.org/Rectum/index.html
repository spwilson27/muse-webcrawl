Part of: Large intestine

The rectum is the final straight segment of the large intestine, ending at the anal canal.

The rectum (Latin: rectum intestinum, "straight intestine") is the final segment of the large intestine in humans and some other mammals, and the gut in other organisms. In adult humans it is about 12 centimetres (4.7 inches) long, beginning at the rectosigmoid junction, where the sigmoid colon ends, and terminating at the anal canal. It receives faeces from the descending colon, stores them, and — through stretch receptors in its wall — signals the need for defecation.

Structure

Despite its name, the rectum follows the curve of the sacrum and coccyx and has three lateral flexures, corresponding internally to folds called the transverse folds of the rectum (Houston's valves). Its lower part, the rectal ampulla, dilates to hold faeces before defecation. The wall has the usual layers of the gut but lacks the taeniae, haustra and appendices epiploicae that characterise the colon. Blood is supplied by the superior, middle and inferior rectal arteries, and its venous drainage is clinically important because anastomoses between the portal and systemic circulations here can swell in portal hypertension. Relations differ between the sexes: the rectum lies behind the bladder and prostate in males, and behind the uterus and vagina in females.

Function

The rectum is usually empty until defecation approaches. Mass movements of the colon propel faeces into it; the resulting distension triggers the defecation reflex, relaxing the internal anal sphincter and producing the conscious urge to defecate. Continence is maintained by the internal and external anal sphincters and the puborectalis muscle, and voluntary control allows defecation to be deferred until a suitable time.

Clinical significance

Diseases of the rectum include rectal cancer, proctitis (inflammation, sometimes part of ulcerative colitis), rectal prolapse, and haemorrhoids of the adjacent anal canal. The rectum can be examined by a gloved finger (digital rectal examination), by rigid or flexible sigmoidoscopy, and imaged with MRI for cancer staging. Some medicines are given rectally as suppositories or enemas, and body temperature is traditionally measured rectally in infants.
