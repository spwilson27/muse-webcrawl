The Waldensians (also known as Waldenses or Vaudois) are adherents of a Christian movement traditionally founded by Peter Waldo in Lyon around 1173. Emphasising apostolic poverty, lay preaching and the authority of Scripture in the vernacular, the movement was condemned as heretical by the Roman Catholic Church and subjected to centuries of persecution, yet survived in the alpine valleys of Piedmont in north-western Italy. The Waldensians are regarded as forerunners of the Protestant Reformation.

Origins

According to tradition, Waldo, a wealthy merchant of Lyon, gave away his property after hearing the parable of the rich young man, commissioned translations of the New Testament into the vernacular, and began preaching. His followers, the "Poor of Lyon", were refused authorisation to preach and were excommunicated in 1184.

Persecution and survival

Waldensian communities spread through France, Italy, Germany and Bohemia. In 1487 Pope Innocent VIII called a crusade against them. The Piedmontese Easter massacre of 1655, in which troops of the Duke of Savoy killed many Waldensians, provoked international protest, including John Milton's sonnet "On the Late Massacre in Piedmont". Civil rights were finally granted in 1848 by King Charles Albert of Sardinia.

Union with the Reformation

At the Synod of Chanforan in 1532 the Waldensians voted to join the Reformed tradition influenced by John Calvin and the Swiss Reformation. Today the Waldensian Evangelical Church in Italy is united with the Methodist churches, and Waldensian congregations also exist in South America and the United States, where the town of Valdese, North Carolina, was founded by Waldensian settlers in 1893.
