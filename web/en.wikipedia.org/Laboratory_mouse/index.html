The laboratory mouse or lab mouse is a small mammal of the order Rodentia bred and used for scientific research and experiments. It is a domesticated form of the house mouse, Mus musculus, most often the subspecies Mus musculus domesticus. Laboratory mice are by far the most commonly used mammalian research model and have contributed to the majority of modern biomedical discoveries.

Why mice?

Mice are used because they are small, inexpensive to keep, easy to breed, genetically very similar to one another within inbred strains, and share about 99% of their genes with functional counterparts in humans. They have short generation times (about ten weeks) and lifespans of roughly two years, allowing ageing and multigeneration studies. Their genome was sequenced in 2002, and tools such as transgenics and targeted "knockout" mutations allow individual genes to be added or disabled.

Strains

Inbred strains are produced by at least 20 generations of brother–sister mating, making all individuals effectively genetically identical. Famous strains include C57BL/6 ("black 6"), the most widely used strain and the reference for the mouse genome; BALB/c, an albino strain used in immunology and hybridoma production; and immunodeficient lines such as the nude mouse and the NOD/scid mouse, which can host human tumour grafts. Outbred stocks such as CD-1 are used where genetic diversity is preferred.

Applications and ethics

Lab mice are used in genetics, immunology, oncology, neuroscience, toxicology, and drug development, and featured in the work behind numerous Nobel Prizes, including the development of monoclonal antibodies via hybridoma technology. Tens of millions are used annually worldwide. Their use is regulated under animal-welfare frameworks emphasising the "3Rs" — replacement, reduction, and refinement — and the extent to which mouse results translate to humans remains an active scientific debate.
