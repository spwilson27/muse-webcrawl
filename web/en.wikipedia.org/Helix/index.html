Helix

A helix (plural: helices or helixes) is a three-dimensional curve that winds around an axis at a constant distance while advancing at a constant rate along that axis — the shape of a coiled spring or a spiral staircase. The word comes from the Greek helix, "twisted, curved". A helix is characterised by its radius, its pitch (the axial distance per complete turn), and its handedness: a helix that turns clockwise as it recedes is right-handed, like an ordinary screw.

Mathematical description

In Cartesian coordinates, a circular helix around the z-axis can be written parametrically as x = a cos t, y = a sin t, z = bt, where a is the radius and 2πb is the pitch. The tangent to a helix makes a constant angle with the axis, and a helix is a geodesic of the cylinder on which it lies: unrolling the cylinder maps the helix to a straight line.

In nature and technology

Helical structures are ubiquitous. In biology, the DNA molecule forms a double helix of two intertwined strands, and many proteins fold into single-helix motifs such as the alpha helix. Tendrils, horns, shells, and vines adopt helical forms. In engineering, the helix underlies screws, bolts, springs, drills, and spiral staircases; a filled-in helix traced by a rotating line describes devices such as the Archimedes' screw.
