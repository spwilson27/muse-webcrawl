Laccolith

A laccolith is a type of igneous intrusion: a sheet-like body of magma that has been injected between two layers of sedimentary rock, forcing the overlying strata upward into a dome or lens shape while its base remains relatively flat. The word comes from the Greek lakkos (a cistern or pond) and lithos (stone).

Formation

Laccoliths form when viscous magma, unable to flow far sideways, accumulates between sedimentary layers at relatively shallow depths (usually less than about 3 km). As more magma arrives, its pressure lifts the overlying rock into a characteristic mushroom- or dome-shaped body with a planar floor. Because the intrusion cools slowly underground, laccoliths are typically composed of coarse-grained rocks such as granite or diorite.

The concept was introduced by Grove Karl Gilbert, who studied the Henry Mountains of Utah in the 1870s and described the intrusions that arch the strata there. Well-known laccoliths include those of the Henry Mountains, Devil's Tower in Wyoming (often interpreted as the eroded remnant of a laccolith or volcanic neck), and intrusions in the Black Hills of South Dakota.

Related intrusions

A laccolith resembles a sill, but a sill does not dome the overlying layers. A much larger, deeper body is a batholith; a downward-sagging, saucer-shaped intrusion is a lopolith; and a small laccolith-like body fed from below whose overlying layers are tilted rather than domed is sometimes called a bysmalith. When erosion removes the softer overlying sedimentary rock, the resistant laccolith may stand out as a dome-shaped mountain.
