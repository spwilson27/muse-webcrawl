Toleration Act 1688

The Toleration Act 1688 (1 Will. & Mar. c. 18), formally "An Act for exempting their Majesties Protestant Subjects dissenting from the Church of England from the Penalties of certain Laws", was an Act of the Parliament of England passed in 1689, in the aftermath of the Glorious Revolution. It granted freedom of worship to Protestant Nonconformists — Dissenters such as Baptists, Congregationalists, and Presbyterians — who took oaths of allegiance and supremacy and subscribed to a declaration against transubstantiation.

Provisions and limits

The Act exempted qualifying Dissenters from the penal laws against non-attendance at Anglican worship and allowed them to hold licensed meetings for worship, with doors unlocked. It did not extend toleration to Roman Catholics or to anti-Trinitarian Protestants such as Unitarians, and Dissenters remained excluded from many public offices by the Test and Corporation Acts until their repeal in 1828; tithes to the Church of England were still required.

Significance

Though limited, the Act marked a decisive step toward religious pluralism in England, ending the attempt to enforce uniform Anglican worship on all Protestants and recognising a permanent place for organised dissent.
