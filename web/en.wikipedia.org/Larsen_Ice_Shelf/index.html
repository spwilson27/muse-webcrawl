Larsen Ice Shelf

The Larsen Ice Shelf is a long ice shelf in the Weddell Sea, extending along the eastern coast of the Antarctic Peninsula. It is named after Captain Carl Anton Larsen, the Norwegian whaler and Antarctic explorer who sailed along the ice front in 1893. The shelf was formerly divided into sections known as Larsen A, B, C, and D; the northernmost sections made headlines for their dramatic collapses, which are widely cited as evidence of regional climate warming on the Antarctic Peninsula.

Sections and collapses

Larsen A

Larsen A, the smallest and northernmost section, disintegrated in January 1995, in an event that destroyed about 1,600 square kilometres of ice shelf within days.

Larsen B

Larsen B was larger and much older — ice core and marine sediment evidence suggested it had been stable for up to 12,000 years, throughout the Holocene. Between 31 January and 7 March 2002, about 3,250 square kilometres of its area shattered into thousands of icebergs. Glaciologists attributed the collapse to strong regional warming (the Antarctic Peninsula warmed by roughly 2.5 °C over the 20th century, several times the global average), which produced surface melt ponds that wedged open crevasses — a process called hydrofracturing — in combination with thinning from warmer ocean water below. After the collapse, the glaciers that had fed Larsen B accelerated markedly toward the sea, demonstrating the buttressing role of ice shelves.

Larsen C

Larsen C, the fourth-largest ice shelf in Antarctica, remained mostly intact but lost a huge iceberg, designated A-68, in July 2017. The calving removed about 5,800 square kilometres — roughly 12% of the shelf's area — and reduced Larsen C to its smallest recorded extent. Scientists continue to monitor rifts in Larsen C and the stability of the remaining shelf, as well as the smaller Larsen D to the south.

Significance

Because floating ice shelves do not directly raise sea level when they melt, the Larsen collapses matter mainly for what follows: the removal of buttressing ice allows the grounded glaciers behind them to flow faster and discharge more ice into the ocean, contributing to sea-level rise. The events also transformed scientific understanding of how quickly ice shelves can disintegrate and helped spur intensive study of similar processes in West Antarctica.
