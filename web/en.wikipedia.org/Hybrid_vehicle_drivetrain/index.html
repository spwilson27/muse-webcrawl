Hybrid vehicle drivetrain

A hybrid vehicle drivetrain transmits power to the driving wheels of a hybrid vehicle — one that combines an internal combustion engine with one or more electric motors and a battery. Hybrid drivetrains are designed to let the engine run nearer its efficient operating range, recover braking energy through regenerative braking, and shut the engine off at standstill, improving fuel economy and reducing emissions relative to conventional vehicles.

Architectures

  • Series hybrid: the engine drives only a generator; an electric motor alone drives the wheels. Used where simplicity of control and electric-only traction are favoured, e.g. diesel-electric locomotives and range-extended vehicles.
  • Parallel hybrid: both the engine and the motor can mechanically drive the wheels, typically through a shared transmission.
  • Series-parallel (power-split): a planetary gear set blends engine and motor power, allowing both mechanical and electrical paths — the arrangement popularized by the Toyota Prius's Hybrid Synergy Drive.

Degrees of hybridization

Mild hybrids add a small motor-generator (often an integrated starter-generator) for start-stop and torque assist but cannot drive the car electrically alone. Full hybrids can propel the vehicle on electric power at low speeds. Plug-in hybrids (PHEVs) have larger batteries that can be charged from the grid, giving tens of kilometres of electric-only range. Common components include inverters, DC-DC converters, high-voltage traction batteries (nickel-metal hydride or lithium-ion), and electronic control units coordinating torque delivery.
