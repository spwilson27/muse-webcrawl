The history of Florence, a city in Tuscany, Italy, spans from a Roman colony on the Arno to the birthplace of the Renaissance and, briefly, capital of the Kingdom of Italy. Florence's wealth rested on wool, silk, and banking; its republican commune became a great power of late medieval Italy, and under Medici patronage it produced an unmatched concentration of art and learning.

Origins and commune

Florentia was founded as a Roman colony, traditionally under Julius Caesar in 59 BCE, on an Etruscan site near Fiesole. After Lombard and Frankish rule, Florence grew as a free commune from the 11th century. The gold florin, first minted in 1252, became a standard European currency. Factional strife between Guelphs and Ghibellines, then between Black and White Guelphs — which exiled Dante Alighieri in 1302 — marked the turbulent politics of the medieval republic, organised around its guilds and the Signoria seated in the Palazzo Vecchio.

The Medici and the Renaissance

The Medici bank made the family the city's dominant force; Cosimo de' Medici's return from exile in 1434 began a century of Medici ascendancy. Under Cosimo and Lorenzo the Magnificent, Florence became the cradle of the Renaissance: Brunelleschi raised the cathedral dome (1420–1436), and Donatello, Botticelli, Leonardo, and Michelangelo worked in the city. After episodes of republican restoration — including Savonarola's fiery theocracy (1494–1498) — the Medici returned and became Dukes of Florence (1532) and Grand Dukes of Tuscany (1569).

Later history

The Medici line ended in 1737 and Tuscany passed to the Habsburg-Lorraine. Florence joined the Kingdom of Italy in 1861 and served as its capital from 1865 to 1871. The city suffered severe flooding in 1966, which damaged countless works of art and spurred an international rescue effort. Its historic centre is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
