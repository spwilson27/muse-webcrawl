East India Company

The East India Company (EIC), formally the Honourable East India Company, was an English (later British) joint-stock company chartered by Elizabeth I on 31 December 1600 to trade in the Indian Ocean region. Over two and a half centuries it grew from a merchant venture into a territorial power that ruled large parts of the Indian subcontinent, maintained one of the world's largest private armies, and at its height accounted for around half of world trade in commodities such as cotton, silk, indigo, saltpetre, tea, and opium.

From trade to rule

The company established factories at Surat, Madras, Bombay, and Calcutta, and after victories at Plassey (1757) under Robert Clive and Buxar (1764) it acquired the diwani — the right to collect revenue — in Bengal, the richest province of the declining Mughal Empire. Through subsidiary alliances, wars against Mysore, the Marathas, and the Sikhs, and doctrines such as lapse, the company expanded until it governed India directly or indirectly with an army of some 260,000 soldiers, twice the size of the British Army itself.

Decline and dissolution

Parliament progressively curbed the company's autonomy (Regulating Act 1773, India Act 1784) and stripped its commercial monopolies in 1813 and 1833. The Indian Rebellion of 1857 ended company rule: the Government of India Act 1858 transferred its territories and armies to the Crown, inaugurating the British Raj. The company lingered as a financial shell managing the tea trade and stockholders' dividends until it was formally dissolved on 1 June 1874.
