In linguistics, an argument is an expression that completes the meaning of a predicate, most typically a verb. The number and type of arguments a predicate takes is called its valency. Verbs may be intransitive (one argument, the subject), transitive (two arguments, subject and object) or ditransitive (three arguments, including an indirect object).

Arguments versus adjuncts

Arguments are distinguished from adjuncts — optional modifiers of time, place or manner — by tests such as obligatoriness, iterability and ordering: adjuncts can usually be repeated and omitted freely, whereas arguments are licensed by the predicate itself. In "Kim gave the book to Lee yesterday", the arguments are Kim, the book and to Lee, while yesterday is an adjunct.

Thematic roles and linking

Semantically, arguments bear thematic roles assigned by the predicate: agent, patient or theme, experiencer, recipient, location and others. A central problem of grammar — the linking problem — is how these semantic roles map onto syntactic positions across active and passive sentences and across languages; theories differ on whether argument structure is projected from lexical semantics (projectionist approaches) or built by syntactic structure itself (constructionist approaches).

Argument structure alternations

Many predicates participate in alternations that rearrange their arguments: the passive removes or demotes the agent, the dative alternation converts "give the book to Lee" into "give Lee the book", and causative–inchoative pairs such as "Kim broke the glass" / "The glass broke" alter valency. Argument structure is a key topic in lexical semantics and theories including case grammar, lexical functional grammar and construction grammar.
