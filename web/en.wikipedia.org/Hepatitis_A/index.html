Hepatitis A

Hepatitis A is an infectious disease of the liver caused by Hepatovirus A (HAV). Many cases have few or no symptoms, especially in the young. When symptoms occur, the time between infection and symptoms is between two and six weeks. Symptoms usually last eight weeks and may include nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, jaundice, fever, and abdominal pain. Around 10–15% of people experience a recurrence of symptoms during the six months after the initial infection. Acute liver failure may rarely occur, with this being more common in the elderly.

Transmission

It is usually spread by eating food or drinking water contaminated with the feces of an infected person. Shellfish which have not been sufficiently cooked are a relatively common source. It may also be spread through close personal contact with an infectious person.

Signs and symptoms

While children often do not have symptoms, adults are more likely to. Early symptoms can be mistaken for the flu: fatigue, nausea, vomiting, fever, and right-sided abdominal pain. As the disease progresses, jaundice — yellowing of the skin and the whites of the eyes — dark urine, and pale stools may appear. Following the initial infection, a person is generally immune for the rest of their life. Diagnosis requires blood testing, as the symptoms are similar to those of a number of other diseases.

Prevention

The hepatitis A vaccine is effective for prevention. Some countries recommend it routinely for children and those at higher risk who have not previously been vaccinated. It appears to be effective for life. Other preventive measures include hand washing and properly cooking food. Immune globulin can also be used for post-exposure prophylaxis within two weeks of exposure.

Treatment

No specific treatment is available; rest and medications for nausea or diarrhea are recommended as needed. Infections usually resolve completely and without ongoing liver disease. Treatment of acute liver failure, should it occur, is with liver transplantation.

Epidemiology

Globally, around 1.4 million symptomatic cases occur each year, with well over 100 million total infections including asymptomatic cases; the disease killed about 11,200 people in 2015. It is more common in regions of the world with poor sanitation and not enough safe water. In the developing world, about 90% of children have been infected by age 10, and thus are immune as adults. Improved hygiene and childhood vaccination have sharply reduced incidence in many countries, though outbreaks continue to occur, sometimes paradoxically because more adults remain susceptible.

Virology

Hepatovirus A is a small, unenveloped, single-stranded positive-sense RNA virus in the family Picornaviridae. It is unusually stable in the environment, resisting detergents, acid, and moderate heat, which facilitates its fecal–oral transmission.
