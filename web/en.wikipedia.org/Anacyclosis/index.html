Anacyclosis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Anacyclosis (Ancient Greek: ἀνακύκλωσις, "recycling" or "revolving") is a cyclical theory of political evolution, most fully developed by the Greek historian Polybius in Book VI of his Histories. According to the theory, constitutions evolve through a fixed sequence of stages: each "good" form of rule degenerates into a corrupt counterpart, which is overthrown and replaced by the next form, until the cycle eventually returns to its beginning.

The cycle

StageRule byDegenerates into
MonarchyOne virtuous rulerTyranny
AristocracyThe virtuous fewOligarchy
DemocracyThe many, law-abidingOchlocracy (mob rule)

Polybius held that primitive monarchy, founded on a leader's strength and justice, decays into tyranny; the tyrant is expelled by the noblest citizens, who found an aristocracy that in turn decays into oligarchy; the people overthrow the oligarchs and establish democracy, which degenerates into ochlocracy — lawless mob rule — from which a single strong leader again emerges, restarting the cycle.

Influence

Polybius praised Rome's mixed constitution — consuls (monarchical), Senate (aristocratic), and assemblies (democratic) — as a brake on the cycle. The theory influenced later thinkers including Cicero, Machiavelli (in the Discourses on Livy), and the framers of the United States Constitution, whose system of checks and balances echoes the mixed-constitution remedy. Related cyclical ideas appear in Plato's Republic and Aristotle's Politics.
