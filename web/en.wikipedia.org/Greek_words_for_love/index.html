Greek words for love

Ancient Greek distinguishes several kinds of love where English uses one word.

The ancient Greek language distinguishes several senses in which the word "love" can be used in English, employing distinct terms whose meanings partly overlap. The principal words are agapē, erōs, philia, and storgē, supplemented by terms such as philautia, ludus, and pragma in later discussions. These distinctions became widely known in modern times partly through C. S. Lewis's book The Four Loves (1960).

The principal terms

  • Agapē (ἀγάπη) — selfless, charitable, unconditional love; used in the Septuagint and New Testament for divine and brotherly love, and the source of the Latin caritas ("charity").
  • Erōs (ἔρως) — passionate, romantic, or sexual desire; named for the god Eros and central to Plato's philosophy of desire in the Symposium.
  • Philia (φιλία) — affectionate friendship and loyalty between equals; Aristotle analysed it extensively in the Nicomachean Ethics.
  • Storgē (στοργή) — natural familial affection, especially of parents for children.

Other terms

Further distinctions drawn by ancient and modern authors include philautia (self-love, healthy or vain), xenia (guest-friendship and ritual hospitality), ludus (playful love, a Latin term often paired with the Greek list), and pragma (enduring, practical love). Modern Greek usage differs considerably from the classical senses: agápi is the everyday word for love, while érōtas retains strongly romantic connotations.
