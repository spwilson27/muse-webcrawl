The siege of Vienna of 1529 was the first attempt by the Ottoman Empire, led by Sultan Suleiman the Magnificent, to capture the city of Vienna, Austria. The Ottoman failure, caused by heavy rains, supply difficulties, disease and fierce resistance, is traditionally seen as the high-water mark of Ottoman expansion into Central Europe.

Background

After the Hungarian defeat at the Battle of Mohács (1526), Hungary was divided between the Ottoman client John Zápolya and the Habsburg Ferdinand I, brother of Emperor Charles V. In 1529 Suleiman marched northwest with a huge army — estimates range from 100,000 to over 300,000 including camp followers — reclaiming Buda and advancing on Vienna. Torrential rain delayed the army, flooding roads; the Ottomans abandoned much of their heavy artillery and many camels died on the muddy route.

The siege

Vienna was defended by roughly 20,000 soldiers and armed citizens under the veteran commander Niklas Graf Salm. The garrison strengthened the medieval walls, razed suburbs to clear fields of fire, and made frequent sorties. Ottoman miners tunnelled under the walls, and several attacks were launched, but the defenders counter-mined and repelled assaults. Arriving late in the campaign season, short of supplies, and facing early snow, Suleiman ordered a final failed assault and withdrew on the night of 14–15 October 1529, leaving baggage and prisoners behind.

Aftermath

The siege confirmed Vienna as a bulwark of Central Europe against Ottoman advance. Fighting over Hungary continued for more than a century; Suleiman returned in 1532 (halted at Güns/Kőszeg) and the Ottomans besieged Vienna again, unsuccessfully, in 1683 — the famous second siege, broken at the Battle of Vienna.
