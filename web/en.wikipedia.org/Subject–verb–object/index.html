Subject–verb–object

The SVO order: subject first, then verb, then object.

In linguistic typology, subject–verb–object (SVO) is a sentence structure where the subject comes first, the verb second, and the object third. Languages that use SVO word order include English, French, Spanish, Italian, Swahili, Yoruba, Malay/Indonesian, Vietnamese, Thai and Mandarin Chinese, among many others. SVO is the second most common word order among the world's languages, after subject–object–verb (SOV), and is the dominant order in Europe and in languages of West Africa and Southeast Asia.

Properties

SVO languages commonly place modifiers after the words they modify in some constructions, use prepositions rather than postpositions, and put auxiliary verbs before main verbs. However, these are tendencies rather than universals: Mandarin, for example, is SVO in many clauses yet has some SOV characteristics in subordinate and topic-prominent structures.

Examples

In English, "Sam ate oranges" is SVO; permuting it gives different orders such as the SOV "Sam oranges ate" (ungrammatical in English but normal in Japanese or Turkish). Typologists classify languages by their basic, most neutral order for declarative transitive clauses, while acknowledging that many languages permit flexibility for emphasis or information structure.
