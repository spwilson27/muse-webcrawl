Table salt

Table salt — refined salt containing about 97–99% sodium chloride, usually with anti-caking agents and often iodine. Main sources: evaporated seawater and mined rock salt (halite).

Table salt is a mineral composed primarily of sodium chloride (NaCl), a chemical compound belonging to the larger class of salts; salt in the form of a natural crystalline mineral is known as rock salt or halite. Salt is present in vast quantities in seawater. Table salt is among the most ubiquitous household items, used as a condiment and food preservative.

Production

Table salt is produced from salt mines or by the evaporation of seawater or mineral-rich spring water in shallow pools. Its major industrial products are caustic soda and chlorine; salt is used in many industrial processes including the manufacture of polyvinyl chloride, plastics, and paper pulp. Of the annual global production of around two hundred million tonnes of salt, only about 6% is used for human consumption.

Additives

Most table salt sold for consumption today is iodized salt: potassium iodide is added, a practice begun in the 1920s to prevent iodine deficiency, which can cause goitre and intellectual disability. Anti-caking agents such as calcium silicate or sodium ferrocyanide are added in small amounts to keep the crystals free-flowing in humid conditions.

Health

Sodium is an essential nutrient, but excessive salt intake is associated with hypertension and cardiovascular disease, and health authorities recommend moderating consumption. Unrefined alternatives, such as sea salt and kosher salt, differ mainly in crystal size and trace mineral content rather than in sodium content.
