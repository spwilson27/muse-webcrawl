Enteroendocrine cell

Enteroendocrine cells are specialized cells of the gastrointestinal tract and pancreas with endocrine function. They produce gastrointestinal hormones or peptides in response to various stimuli and release them into the bloodstream to exert systemic effects, or diffuse them as local messengers, or spread them to the enteric nervous system to evoke nervous responses. Enteroendocrine cells of the intestine are the most numerous endocrine cells of the body, even though they constitute only about 1% of the intestinal epithelium. Together they are sometimes described as forming the body's largest endocrine organ.

Types

Classical types are designated by letters according to their principal hormone:

  • G cells (antrum of the stomach) secrete gastrin, stimulating acid secretion;
  • D cells secrete somatostatin, an inhibitory hormone;
  • I cells (upper small intestine) secrete cholecystokinin (CCK);
  • S cells secrete secretin in response to acid;
  • K cells secrete gastric inhibitory peptide (GIP);
  • L cells (ileum and colon) secrete glucagon-like peptides GLP-1 and GLP-2 and peptide YY;
  • Enterochromaffin (EC) cells secrete serotonin and substance P;
  • Enterochromaffin-like (ECL) cells in the gastric fundus secrete histamine.

Function

These cells sense luminal nutrients, acidity and microbial products through apical receptors and respond by releasing hormones that regulate digestion, appetite, insulin secretion and gut motility. The incretin hormones GIP and GLP-1, which enhance glucose-dependent insulin release, underpin the physiology exploited by modern diabetes and obesity medicines.

Clinical significance

Tumours arising from enteroendocrine cells are the neuroendocrine tumours (NETs) of the gut and pancreas; serotonin-secreting midgut NETs can cause the carcinoid syndrome of flushing and diarrhoea. GLP-1 receptor agonists such as semaglutide have become major therapies for type 2 diabetes and obesity.
