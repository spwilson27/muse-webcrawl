Abu Abdullah Muhammad ibn Muhammad ibn Abdullah ibn Idris al-Qurtubi al-Hasani as-Sabti, commonly Muhammad al-Idrisi (c. 1100 – c. 1165), was a Muslim geographer and cartographer who lived and worked in Sicily at the court of King Roger II. His world map, the Tabula Rogeriana, was among the most advanced maps of the medieval world, and his accompanying geography, the Nuzhat al-Mushtaq fi Ikhtiraq al-Afaq ("The Excursion of One Who Yearns to Penetrate the Horizon", often called the Book of Roger), remained a standard reference for centuries.

Life

Al-Idrisi was born in Ceuta, in what is now Morocco, into a noble family claiming descent from the Prophet Muhammad through the Idrisids and Hammudids of al-Andalus. He was educated at Córdoba and travelled widely in Spain, North Africa, Anatolia and possibly beyond — his geography claims information drawn from as far as Scandinavia, West Africa and the sources of the Nile.

Invited to Palermo by Roger II, al-Idrisi spent some fifteen years compiling his great work. For it, he interviewed travellers, collated the works of earlier Arab and Greek geographers (especially Ptolemy), and dispatched draftsmen to gather reports. The original centrepiece was a planisphere engraved on a great disc of silver, accompanied by seventy regional maps dividing the known world into the traditional seven "climates", each split into ten sections.

The work

Completed in 1154, shortly before Roger's death, the Nuzhat al-Mushtaq describes the peoples, cities, distances, climates, products and marvels of the inhabited world from the Atlantic to China. It preserves invaluable information about 12th-century Europe — including early descriptions of the British Isles, Scandinavia, Slavic lands and the Baltic — much of it unavailable in Latin sources. Al-Idrisi remained in Sicily under William I, for whom he produced a second, larger geographic compilation; copies of his maps circulated in the Islamic world and were later printed in Rome (1592) and translated in Europe.
