Karel Čapek

Karel Čapek (9 January 1890 – 25 December 1938) was a Czech writer, playwright, critic and journalist, widely regarded as one of the most influential Czech authors of the 20th century. He is best known internationally for his pioneering science fiction and for introducing the word robot to the world in his play R.U.R. (1920).

Life

Čapek was born in Malé Svatoňovice in Bohemia, studied philosophy in Prague, Berlin and Paris, and earned a doctorate in 1915. With his brother Josef — painter and writer, with whom he collaborated on many works — he became a central figure of interwar Czechoslovak culture, a close associate of President Tomáš Garrigue Masaryk, whose conversations he published. He edited the newspaper Lidové noviny and was an outspoken anti-fascist in the 1930s. He died of pneumonia in Prague on Christmas Day 1938, weeks after the Munich Agreement dismembered Czechoslovakia; the Gestapo later arrested Josef, who died in Bergen-Belsen in 1945.

Works

R.U.R. (Rossum's Universal Robots), premiered in 1921, coined "robot" (suggested by Josef, from Czech robota, forced labour) and set enduring themes of artificial beings and technological hubris. Other major works include the play The Makropulos Affair (1922), the philosophical novel trilogy Hordubal, Meteor and An Ordinary Life (1933–34), the dystopian novels The Absolute at Large (1922) and Krakatit (1924), the anti-totalitarian satire War with the Newts (1936), the children's classic Dashenka, and the beloved gardening memoir The Gardener's Year (1929). He was repeatedly nominated for the Nobel Prize in Literature but never received it.
