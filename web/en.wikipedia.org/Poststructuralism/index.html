Poststructuralism

Poststructuralism is a philosophical and literary movement that emerged in France in the mid-to-late 1960s, developing out of and reacting against structuralism. Where structuralism sought the underlying structures that govern language and culture, poststructuralists argued that meaning is unstable, that structures cannot stand outside the systems they describe, and that the author's intention does not fix a text's meaning. Key figures include Jacques Derrida, Michel Foucault, Gilles Deleuze, Jean-François Lyotard, Julia Kristeva and Roland Barthes.

Key ideas

Derrida's deconstruction examines how texts rely on binary oppositions (speech/writing, presence/absence) that they simultaneously undermine, and his concept of différance captures how meaning arises through difference and deferral. Barthes proclaimed the "death of the author", relocating meaning-making to the reader. Foucault analysed how knowledge is bound up with power (power/knowledge) and traced the historical formation of discourses in works such as The Order of Things and Discipline and Punish. Lyotard's The Postmodern Condition (1979) defined the postmodern as "incredulity toward metanarratives".

Origins and influence

A symbolic starting point is Derrida's 1966 lecture "Structure, Sign, and Play in the Discourse of the Human Sciences" at Johns Hopkins University. Poststructuralism profoundly influenced literary theory, critical theory, feminist and postcolonial studies, and the humanities generally, though many of its supposed members rejected the label itself. It is closely associated with, but not identical to, postmodernism.

Criticism

Critics have charged poststructuralist writing with obscurantism, relativism and political quietism, and philosophers such as Jürgen Habermas and John Searle disputed its claims about language and truth. Defenders reply that its difficulty is deliberate and that its analyses of power and meaning remain indispensable.
