Early Netherlandish painting

Early Netherlandish painting refers to the work of artists active in the Burgundian and Habsburg Netherlands during the 15th- and early 16th-century Northern Renaissance. The period, roughly 1420 to 1520, began with Robert Campin and Jan van Eyck and is famed for its advances in oil painting, naturalistic detail and complex iconography.

Characteristics

Early Netherlandish painters exploited the slow drying time of oil paint to build up translucent glazes, producing luminous colour and minutely observed surfaces — fur, glass, metal and reflected light. Unlike Italian contemporaries, they worked largely without a theoretical tradition of perspective, instead achieving spatial depth through colour and detail.

Major works

The Ghent Altarpiece (completed 1432) by Hubert and Jan van Eyck is the era's monumental landmark. Rogier van der Weyden's Descent from the Cross and the Portinari Altarpiece of Hugo van der Goes show the emotional intensity the school achieved; Hans Memling dominated Bruges painting in the later 15th century.

Patronage

Patrons included the Valois dukes of Burgundy, wealthy Italian merchant-bankers resident in Bruges, and an increasingly prosperous local middle class commissioning portraits and small devotional panels for private worship.

Legacy

The tradition fed directly into Dutch Golden Age painting and Flemish Baroque art, and its works were widely collected across Europe.
