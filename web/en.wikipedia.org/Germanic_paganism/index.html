Germanic paganism denotes the pre-Christian religious traditions of the Germanic peoples of northern and central Europe, encompassing the Norse religion of Scandinavia, the Anglo-Saxon religion of early England, and the continental Germanic traditions. It was a polytheistic system centred on gods such as Odin, Thor, and Freyja, on ritual sacrifice (blót), and on beings including elves, dwarves, and land spirits.

Sources

Little was written down by practitioners themselves. Knowledge derives from outside observers such as Tacitus, whose Germania (c. 98 CE) describes sacred groves and divination; from archaeology — votive deposits, bog bodies, runestones, and temple sites like Uppsala; and from medieval Icelandic literature, above all the Poetic Edda and Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda, recorded in the thirteenth century, after Christianisation, but preserving much older myth.

Beliefs and practice

Cosmology featured a world tree, Yggdrasil, linking multiple worlds, and a prophesied end, Ragnarök, in which gods and giants destroy one another. Worship centred on seasonal sacrificial feasts, offerings at groves, springs, and bogs, and rites conducted by chieftains or priests. Fate (wyrd) and honour within the kin group were central values.

Christianisation and legacy

Conversion proceeded gradually between the sixth and twelfth centuries, kingdom by kingdom, often by royal decision. Elements of the old religion survived in folklore, weekday names (Wednesday from Woden, Thursday from Thor), and literature, and the myths were revived in the modern era, including by contemporary neopagan movements.
