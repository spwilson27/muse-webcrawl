Cryogenian

The Cryogenian (from Ancient Greek κρύος krýos, meaning "cold", and γένεσις génesis, meaning "birth") is a geologic period that lasted from about 720 to 635 million years ago. It forms the second geologic period of the Neoproterozoic Era, preceded by the Tonian Period and followed by the Ediacaran Period. It is best known for two of the most severe glaciations in Earth's history, episodes so extreme that ice may have covered the entire planet in what is called a "Snowball Earth."

Glaciations

The Sturtian glaciation, beginning around 717 million years ago and lasting until about 660 million years ago, and the shorter Marinoan glaciation, ending about 635 million years ago, deposited glacial sediments at paleolatitudes near the equator, implying global or near-global ice cover. These events are thought to have been triggered by a combination of continental configuration, weathering-driven drawdown of atmospheric carbon dioxide, and possibly the breakup of the supercontinent Rodinia.

Climate and oceans

During Snowball episodes, global mean temperatures may have plunged to around −50 °C, the hydrologic cycle largely shut down, and photosynthesis in the oceans was severely restricted by sea ice. Deglaciation likely required enormous buildups of volcanic carbon dioxide over millions of years. The dramatic cap carbonate rocks that overlie glacial deposits record the rapid return to a hothouse climate after the ice melted.

Life

Despite the harsh conditions, life persisted. Fossil evidence suggests that sponges or sponge-grade organisms may have existed during the Cryogenian, and molecular clock studies hint that several major animal lineages began diverging in this period. Surviving refugia may have included cracks in the ice, open-water polynyas, and hydrothermal environments. The end of the Cryogenian opened the way for the Ediacaran biota, the first large complex organisms in the fossil record.
