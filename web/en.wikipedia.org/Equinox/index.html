Equinox

An equinox is the instant when the plane of Earth's equator passes through the geometric centre of the Sun's disc. It occurs twice a year, around 20 March and 23 September. On these days the Sun rises exactly in the east and sets exactly in the west, and day and night are of nearly equal length across the planet — hence the name, from Latin aequus (equal) and nox (night).

The two equinoxes

The March equinox marks the beginning of spring in the Northern Hemisphere and autumn in the Southern Hemisphere; the September equinox reverses the seasons. The March equinox is historically significant as the reference point for the Easter computus and as the new year (Nowruz) in Persian and related calendars. The point on the celestial sphere where the Sun crosses the equator northward is called the First Point of Aries or vernal equinox point, the origin of the equatorial coordinate system.

Day length

Because the Sun is a disc rather than a point and because atmospheric refraction bends its light, the day on which daylight and darkness are most nearly equal (the "equilux") falls a few days before or after the equinox itself, and at the equinox itself day is slightly longer than night at most latitudes. At the poles, the equinoxes mark the single annual sunrise and sunset.

Precession

Owing to the precession of the equinoxes, the equinoctial points drift westward along the ecliptic with a period of about 26,000 years, which is why the First Point of Aries now lies in the constellation Pisces.
