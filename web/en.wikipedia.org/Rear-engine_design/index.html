Rear-engine design

In automobile design, a rear-engine design places the engine behind the rear axle, as distinct from a rear mid-engine layout where the engine sits ahead of the rear axle but behind the cabin. The layout is written RR (rear-engine, rear-wheel drive). It concentrates mass over the driven wheels, giving strong traction and compact packaging without a propshaft, since engine and transaxle form one unit.

History and examples

The layout was popularised by Ferdinand Porsche's Volkswagen Beetle (1938), whose air-cooled flat-four hung behind the rear axle; derivatives included the VW Bus and the Porsche 356 and 911. The Porsche 911 remains the most famous rear-engined car, still in production with its flat-six behind the axle, its handling refined over decades of development. Other examples include the original Fiat 500, Renault 4CV and Dauphine, Chevrolet Corvair, Hillman Imp, Škoda's rear-engined models, the DeLorean DMC-12 and, in buses and coaches, almost universal rear engines. The Tata Nano revived the layout for low cost in 2008.

Characteristics

Advantages include excellent traction, a flat cabin floor, light steering (little weight on the front wheels) and easy engine cooling-air routing in air-cooled designs. Disadvantages include a tendency to oversteer — the heavy rear mass acts like a pendulum in corners ("trailing-throttle oversteer" was notorious in early 911s and the Corvair, criticised in Ralph Nader's Unsafe at Any Speed) — limited luggage space, and heating/cooling plumbing complexity for front radiators. Modern electronic stability controls have tamed the layout's vices.
