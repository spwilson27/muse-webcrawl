Appellation of origin

An appellation of origin is a legally defined and protected geographical indication used on products — most famously wines — that have qualities or characteristics essentially due to their place of origin. The best-known system is France's Appellation d'origine contrôlée (AOC), administered by the INAO since 1935.

Principles

An appellation fixes the boundaries of a production area and the rules for making the product within it: permitted grape varieties, maximum yields, minimum alcohol, pruning and ageing practices, and so on. The underlying idea is terroir — that soil, climate and local practice give a product a recognisable identity. Only producers inside the delimited area who follow the rules may use the name.

International systems

The French model inspired similar regimes worldwide: Italy's DOC and DOCG, Spain's denominación de origen, the European Union's PDO and PGI schemes, and American Viticultural Areas in the United States, which regulate place names but not production methods. Famous protected names include Champagne, Bordeaux, Rioja and Parmigiano-Reggiano (for cheese).
