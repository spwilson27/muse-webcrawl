Marlon Brando

Marlon Brando Jr. (April 3, 1924 – July 1, 2004) was an American actor. Widely regarded as one of the greatest actors of the 20th century, he received numerous accolades throughout his career, including two Academy Awards, two Golden Globe Awards, and three British Academy Film Awards. Brando is credited with being one of the first actors to bring the Stanislavski system of acting and method acting to mainstream audiences.

Early life and theatre

Brando was born in Omaha, Nebraska. After being expelled from military school, he moved to New York, studied with Stella Adler, and appeared on Broadway in I Remember Mama (1944). His breakthrough came as Stanley Kowalski in Tennessee Williams's A Streetcar Named Desire (1947), directed by Elia Kazan — a raw, naturalistic performance that changed American acting.

Film career

Brando made his film debut in The Men (1950) and reprised Kowalski in the 1951 film of Streetcar, receiving the first of four consecutive Academy Award nominations. He starred in Viva Zapata! (1952), Julius Caesar (1953) and The Wild One (1953), and won his first Oscar for playing Terry Malloy in Kazan's On the Waterfront (1954), famous for the "I coulda been a contender" scene. His later 1950s work included Guys and Dolls, Sayonara and The Young Lions.

After a comparatively uneven 1960s — including directing and starring in One-Eyed Jacks (1961) and the troubled Mutiny on the Bounty (1962), after which he bought an atoll, Tetiaroa, in French Polynesia — Brando's career was revived by his portrayal of Vito Corleone in Francis Ford Coppola's The Godfather (1972), for which he won a second Academy Award, which he declined in protest at Hollywood's treatment of Native Americans, sending activist Sacheen Littlefeather to refuse it on his behalf. The same year he starred in Bernardo Bertolucci's Last Tango in Paris. Later roles included Jor-El in Superman (1978) and Colonel Kurtz in Coppola's Apocalypse Now (1979).

Later life and legacy

Brando acted sporadically in the 1980s and 1990s (A Dry White Season, The Freshman, Don Juan DeMarco), published the memoir Songs My Mother Taught Me (1994), and died of respiratory failure in Los Angeles in 2004. The American Film Institute ranked him the fourth-greatest male screen legend; his mumbling naturalism and emotional intensity influenced generations of actors from James Dean to Robert De Niro.
