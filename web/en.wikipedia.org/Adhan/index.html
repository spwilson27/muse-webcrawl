Arabicأَذَان‎
MeaningThe Islamic call to prayer
Recited byMuezzin

The adhan (Arabic: أَذَان) is the Islamic call to public prayer in a mosque, recited by a muezzin at prescribed times of the day. Its purpose is to summon Muslims for the five obligatory (fard) prayers and to proclaim the central tenets of Islam, including the shahada. A second, shorter call, the iqama, is recited inside the mosque immediately before the prayer begins.

History

According to Islamic tradition, the adhan was instituted in Medina in the first or second year after the Hijra (c. 622–624 CE). The form of the call was said to have been shown in a dream to a companion, Abdullah ibn Zayd, and approved by the Prophet Muhammad, who appointed Bilal ibn Rabah, a freed Abyssinian slave renowned for his voice, as the first muezzin. The call was traditionally delivered from a rooftop or minaret; today loudspeakers are widely used.

Text

The Sunni adhan consists of phrases repeated in a fixed order: Allāhu akbar ("God is greatest", four times), the two testimonies of faith, ḥayya ʿala ṣ-ṣalāh ("hasten to prayer"), ḥayya ʿala l-falāḥ ("hasten to salvation"), and for the dawn (fajr) prayer the added line aṣ-ṣalātu khayrun min an-nawm ("prayer is better than sleep"). Shia versions include additional phrases, including the testimony to Ali's wilaya, and differ in repetitions. The adhan is always recited in Arabic, melodically, with styles varying by region and school.

Practice and regulation

The adhan marks the soundscape of Muslim-majority cities. Its public broadcast has occasionally been regulated or disputed — experiments with broadcast volume in Turkey, municipal rules in parts of Europe and Israel, and Sweden's first permitted amplified adhan in 2013 drew attention. The adhan is also whispered into the ears of newborns and performed at other solemn occasions.
