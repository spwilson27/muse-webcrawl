Maximum likelihood estimation

In statistics, maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) is a method of estimating the parameters of an assumed probability distribution, given some observed data. This is achieved by maximizing a likelihood function so that, under the assumed statistical model, the observed data is most probable. The point in the parameter space that maximizes the likelihood function is called the maximum likelihood estimate. The logic of maximum likelihood is both intuitive and flexible, and as such the method has become a dominant means of statistical inference.

Principles

Given a statistical model with parameters θ and observed data x, the likelihood function L(θ; x) is the joint probability (or probability density) of the data viewed as a function of θ. The MLE θ̂ is the value that maximises L. In practice the log-likelihood is usually maximised instead, since it is monotonic and converts products into sums. For many models the estimate can be found analytically; otherwise numerical optimization methods such as Newton–Raphson or Fisher scoring are used.

Properties

Under regularity conditions, MLEs are consistent (converging to the true parameter as the sample grows), asymptotically normal, and asymptotically efficient, attaining the Cramér–Rao lower bound. Their large-sample variance is approximated by the inverse of the Fisher information. MLE is closely connected to Bayesian inference: with a uniform prior, the MLE coincides with the maximum a posteriori estimate.

History

The method was recommended and popularised by Ronald Fisher between 1912 and 1922, though earlier forms appear in the work of Gauss, Laplace, and others. Fisher's formulation underlies much of modern inferential statistics, including logistic regression, generalized linear models, and survival analysis.
