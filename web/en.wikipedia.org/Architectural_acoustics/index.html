Architectural acoustics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


A concert hall designed for favourable acoustics

Architectural acoustics (also known as room acoustics and building acoustics) is the science and engineering of achieving a good sound within a building. Architectural acoustics can be about achieving good speech intelligibility in a theatre, restaurant or railway station, enhancing the quality of music in a concert hall or recording studio, or suppressing noise to make offices and homes more productive and comfortable places to work and live.

History

The scientific study of room acoustics began with Wallace Clement Sabine at Harvard University around 1895, who was hired to improve the acoustics of the Fogg Museum lecture hall. Sabine established the relationship between a room's volume, its total sound absorption, and its reverberation time — expressed in Sabine's formula, T = 0.161 V/A in metric units — founding the modern discipline.

Key concepts

  • Reverberation time (RT60) — the time for sound to decay by 60 dB after the source stops; concert halls typically target roughly 1.5–2 seconds, lecture rooms much less.
  • Absorption, reflection and diffusion — materials and surface shapes control how sound energy is absorbed or scattered; porous materials, panels and diffusers are used to tune a space.
  • Speech intelligibility — measured by indices such as the Speech Transmission Index (STI); early reflections help, late reverberation and background noise hinder.
  • Sound insulation — the control of airborne and impact sound between rooms, governed by mass, separation and sealing of constructions.
  • Noise control — managing building services noise (HVAC), external traffic noise, and vibration.

Applications

Acousticians work on performance venues, schools, hospitals, open-plan offices, housing, places of worship, and transport buildings, using measurement, simulation (ray tracing and wave-based models), and auralisation to predict how spaces will sound before they are built.
