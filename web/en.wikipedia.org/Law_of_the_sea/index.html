Law of the sea

Maritime zones defined by UNCLOS

The law of the sea is a body of international law governing the rights and duties of states in maritime environments. It concerns matters such as navigational freedoms, territorial waters, exclusive economic zones, and the exploitation of marine resources. It is distinct from admiralty law, which concerns private maritime transactions.

United Nations Convention

The modern framework is the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS), concluded in 1982 and in force since 1994, often described as a constitution for the oceans. It defines internal waters, territorial seas (up to 12 nautical miles), contiguous zones, exclusive economic zones (up to 200 nautical miles), and continental shelf rights, and guarantees transit passage through international straits.

Institutions

The International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea, seated in Hamburg, adjudicates disputes under the Convention. The International Seabed Authority regulates mineral activities in areas beyond national jurisdiction, and the high seas are governed by principles of freedom of navigation and fishing subject to treaty obligations.

Historical development

Earlier doctrines ranged from the closed-sea (mare clausum) claims of Spain and Portugal to Hugo Grotius's 1609 argument for the freedom of the seas (mare liberum), which became the dominant principle for the high seas in customary law.
