Culture shock

Culture shock is an experience a person may have when one moves to a cultural environment that is different from one's own; it is also the personal disorientation a person may feel when experiencing an unfamiliar way of life due to immigration or a visit to a new country, a move between social environments, or simply a transition to another type of life.

The term was introduced by anthropologist Kalervo Oberg in 1954, who described culture shock as "anxiety that results from losing all our familiar signs and symbols of social intercourse." The phrase has since passed into general usage.

Phases

Oberg's model, later elaborated by others, describes four phases of adjustment, often depicted as a U-curve:

  1. Honeymoon phase — differences are seen in a romantic, exciting light, and the new culture is approached with fascination.
  2. Crisis (culture shock proper) — differences in language, values, and daily routines produce frustration, anxiety, homesickness, and sometimes hostility toward the host culture.
  3. Recovery — the person gains fluency in the language and customs, develops coping strategies and friendships, and the crisis gradually resolves.
  4. Adjustment — the individual accepts and enjoys the new culture, functioning effectively within it; some models add a stage of bicultural mastery.

Returning home can produce reverse culture shock, in which the re-entry environment, assumed to be familiar, feels unexpectedly alien; combined with initial adjustment abroad, this yields the "W-curve" model.

Causes and management

Contributing factors include language barriers, loss of familiar cues and status, value clashes, and the cognitive effort of constant interpretation. Preparation, language study, maintaining connections to home, and building local support networks can ease the transition. Culture shock is a normal process of acculturation rather than a medical condition, though severe cases can contribute to depression or isolation.
