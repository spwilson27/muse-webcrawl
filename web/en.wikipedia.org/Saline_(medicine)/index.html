Saline (medicine)

Saline (also saline solution) is a mixture of sodium chloride (salt) and water. It has a number of uses in medicine, including cleaning wounds, removal and storage of contact lenses, and help with dry eyes. By injection into a vein, it is used to treat dehydration such as that from gastroenteritis and diabetic ketoacidosis. Large amounts may result in fluid overload, swelling, acidosis, and high blood sodium; long-term or overuse of normal saline can also cause these in people with kidney problems.

Normal saline

The commonly used concentration, normal saline (NSS), is 0.9% weight/volume sodium chloride — 9 grams per litre — with an osmolarity of about 308 mOsm/L, close to that of blood. It is one of the most widely used intravenous fluids in the world: it is used for fluid resuscitation in hypovolaemia, shock, metabolic alkalosis, and mild sodium deficiency, and as the diluent for many intravenous drugs.

Other concentrations

Half-normal saline (0.45%) is hypotonic and used for certain forms of dehydration and hypernatraemia. Hypertonic saline (3%, 7.5%, or 23.4%) is used to treat severe symptomatic hyponatraemia and, in neurocritical care, to reduce raised intracranial pressure. Lower concentrations are used in nasal sprays and irrigation fluids for their gentleness on mucous membranes.

History

Saline infusions were pioneered during the cholera epidemics of the 1830s, notably by Thomas Latta, whose intravenous salt solutions dramatically revived some moribund patients. The 0.9% concentration was later fixed by the Dutch physiologist Hartog Hamburger's studies of red-cell haemolysis in the 1880s–90s, from which the term "physiological saline" derives. Saline is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines. See also Intravenous therapy and Ringer's lactate.
