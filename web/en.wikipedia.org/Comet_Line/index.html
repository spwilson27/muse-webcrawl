Comet Line

The Comet Line (French: Réseau Comète) was a World War II Resistance escape line in occupied Western Europe. Operated by Belgian and French civilians and supported by the British intelligence service MI9, it helped Allied airmen shot down over Belgium and France to evade capture and return to Britain via France, the Pyrenees, Spain, and Gibraltar.

Origins

The line was founded in 1941 by Andrée de Jongh, a young Belgian woman known as "Dédée", together with Arnold Deppé and her father Frédéric de Jongh. De Jongh personally escorted airmen from Brussels through France and across the Pyrenees on foot, delivering them to the British consulate in Bilbao. The network's southern section relied on Basque guides, including the smuggler Florentino Goikoetxea, to cross the mountains.

Operations and losses

At its height the Comet Line involved several hundred helpers who sheltered, fed, clothed, and forged documents for evaders. Roughly 800 Allied airmen are estimated to have been exfiltrated along the route. The work was extraordinarily dangerous: the Gestapo repeatedly infiltrated and broke sections of the line, and hundreds of helpers were arrested; many were executed or died in concentration camps. Andrée de Jongh was arrested in January 1943 and survived imprisonment at Ravensbrück; the line was rebuilt under other leaders, including Jean-François Nothomb, and continued operating until liberation.

Legacy

After the war, de Jongh was awarded the George Medal, the United States Medal of Freedom, and the Légion d'honneur, among other honours. The Comet Line remains one of the best-known escape organisations of the war, and its route across the Pyrenees is commemorated by annual walks.
