Evidentialism

W. K. Clifford's dictum that it is wrong "always, everywhere, and for anyone, to believe anything upon insufficient evidence" is the classic statement of evidentialism.

Evidentialism is a thesis in epistemology which states that a belief is epistemically justified if and only if it is supported by a person's evidence. The view is most often associated with the contemporary formulation by Earl Conee and Richard Feldman: a doxastic attitude (belief, disbelief or suspension of judgment) is justified when it fits the evidence the subject has.

Historical roots

Classical statements include John Locke's insistence in the Essay Concerning Human Understanding that assent should be proportioned to the grounds of probability, David Hume's maxim that a wise person proportions belief to the evidence, and above all W. K. Clifford's 1877 essay "The Ethics of Belief", written against religious faith unsupported by evidence. William James's 1896 reply, "The Will to Believe", defended the legitimacy of belief ahead of evidence in certain forced, live and momentous options, setting up a famous debate.

The contemporary view

Conee and Feldman's evidentialism is typically combined with mentalism (justification supervenes on the subject's mental states) and is defended through thought experiments such as the "new evil demon problem". Evidentialism about justification is compatible with different views of what evidence consists in — experiences, beliefs or both. It is one component of the older ethics of belief debate, concerning whether there are moral as well as epistemic norms governing belief formation.

Criticisms

Critics raise several objections: that people may have justified beliefs based on evidence they have forgotten; that evidentialism cannot easily explain the basing relation between a belief and the evidence that supports it; and, from "pragmatic encroachment" theorists, that practical stakes seem to affect justification in ways a purely evidential theory cannot capture. Others object that some beliefs (such as basic perceptual or memory beliefs) seem justified without the subject possessing articulable evidence for them.
