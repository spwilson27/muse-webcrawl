External beam radiotherapy (EBRT), or teletherapy, is the most common form of radiotherapy, in which a beam of ionising radiation generated outside the body — usually by a linear accelerator — is directed at a tumour or other target. It contrasts with brachytherapy, where a radioactive source is placed inside or beside the target, and with systemic radionuclide therapy.

How it works

High-energy photons damage DNA directly and through free radicals generated in water; cells with defective DNA repair, including many cancer cells, die when they attempt to divide. Treatment is planned from CT (and often MRI and PET) imaging: the clinical target volume is drawn, margins added for microscopic spread and motion, and beams arranged to conform the dose to the tumour while sparing organs at risk. Dose is prescribed in grays (Gy) and typically fractionated — divided into daily sessions over several weeks — to exploit differences in repair between tumour and normal tissue, though hypofractionated and single-session stereotactic treatments are increasingly common.

Techniques

Three-dimensional conformal radiotherapy (3D-CRT) shapes beams with multileaf collimators. Intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) and its arc form VMAT vary beam intensity across the field, sculpting concave dose distributions. Image-guided radiotherapy (IGRT) uses daily imaging to correct positioning. Stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) delivers a few very high doses with millimetre precision. Proton therapy exploits the Bragg peak to deposit dose at depth with little exit dose, and is particularly valued for paediatric tumours and tumours near critical structures.

Side effects

Side effects depend on the site treated and include fatigue, skin reaction, mucositis, nausea, and site-specific late effects such as fibrosis, hypothyroidism or infertility; there is a small lifetime risk of a second malignancy. Modern conformal techniques have substantially reduced toxicity.
