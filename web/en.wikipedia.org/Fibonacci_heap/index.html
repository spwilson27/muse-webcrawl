A Fibonacci heap is a data structure for priority queue operations, consisting of a collection of heap-ordered trees. It was developed by Michael Fredman and Robert Tarjan in 1984 and achieves exceptionally fast amortised running times, especially for the decrease-key operation, which runs in constant amortised time.

Structure

The heap is a forest of trees obeying the min-heap property, with roots joined in a circular doubly linked list and a pointer to the minimum root. Operations are deliberately lazy: insertion simply adds a one-node tree, and merging two heaps concatenates their root lists. Consolidation of trees of equal degree is postponed until a delete-min operation forces a cleanup pass.

Decrease-key and cascading cuts

To decrease a key, the node is cut from its parent if heap order is violated and placed in the root list. A marking scheme triggers cascading cuts: a parent that loses a second child is itself cut, and so on up the tree. This rule bounds the degree of any node to O(log n), with maximum degree governed by the golden ratio — the property from which the Fibonacci heap takes its name, since a node of degree k has at least Fk+2 descendants, where F is the Fibonacci sequence.

Applications

Fibonacci heaps improve the asymptotic running time of graph algorithms that use many decrease-key operations, notably Dijkstra's shortest-path algorithm (to O(E + V log V)) and Prim's minimum spanning tree algorithm. In practice their large constant factors and pointer-heavy structure often make simpler heaps faster on real inputs.
