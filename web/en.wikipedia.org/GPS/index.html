The Global Positioning System (GPS; originally Navstar GPS) is a satellite-based radionavigation system owned by the United States government and operated by the United States Space Force. It is one of the global navigation satellite systems (GNSS) that provides geolocation and time information to a GPS receiver anywhere on or near the Earth where there is an unobstructed line of sight to four or more GPS satellites.

How it works

Each GPS satellite continuously transmits a signal carrying its position and a precise timestamp generated by onboard atomic clocks. A receiver computes the distance to each satellite from the signal travel time, and by trilateration using signals from at least four satellites, fixes its own position and the time offset of its clock. Corrections for ionospheric and tropospheric delay, satellite clock error and relativistic effects (gravitational and velocity time dilation, together about 38 microseconds per day) are applied to achieve metre-level accuracy.

History

GPS grew out of earlier systems such as the Navy's TRANSIT and Timation satellites and the Air Force's Project 621B. The programme was approved in 1973; the first Block I satellite was launched in 1978. Following the Soviet downing of Korean Air Lines Flight 007 in 1983, the US announced GPS would be made available for civilian use. The constellation reached full operational capability in 1995. "Selective Availability", the deliberate degradation of the civilian signal, was switched off in 2000, improving civilian accuracy from about 100 m to a few metres. Successive satellite blocks (IIR, IIF and the GPS III series first launched in 2018) added new civil signals such as L2C and L5.

Applications

Beyond military navigation and weapons guidance, GPS is embedded in transport (aviation, shipping, road navigation), agriculture, surveying, telecommunications network timing, banking timestamp synchronisation, emergency services and consumer smartphones and wearables. Other GNSS systems include Russia's GLONASS, China's BeiDou, the European Union's Galileo and regional systems such as India's NavIC and Japan's QZSS; modern receivers commonly combine several.
