Trade route from the Varangians to the Greeks

The trade route from the Varangians to the Greeks was a medieval waterway connecting Scandinavia, Kievan Rus', and the Byzantine Empire. Running from the Baltic Sea along the rivers of Eastern Europe — chiefly the Neva, Lake Ladoga, the Volkhov, Lake Ilmen, the Lovat, and the Dnieper — to the Black Sea and Constantinople, it was described in the Primary Chronicle and was the axis along which the Rus' state formed in the ninth and tenth centuries.

The route

Varangian (Norse) traders from Sweden crossed the Baltic to Lake Ladoga, ascending the Volkhov past Staraya Ladoga and Novgorod to Lake Ilmen. From there boats were taken up the Lovat and portaged to the headwaters of the Dnieper near Smolensk and Gnezdovo. The Dnieper carried them past Kiev to the Black Sea, a leg made hazardous by nomad Pechenegs and by the Dnieper rapids, which the emperor Constantine VII described in detail in De Administrando Imperio, listing the rapids' Norse and Slavic names. From the Black Sea coast the ships sailed to Constantinople.

Trade and significance

Southbound cargoes included furs, wax, honey, amber, weapons, and slaves; northbound came Byzantine wine, silk, glassware, spices, and silver. The route underpinned the rise of Kiev, whose princes exacted tribute and negotiated trade treaties with Byzantium in 907, 911, and 944, and Varangians travelling it formed the core of the Byzantine emperor's Varangian Guard. The route declined in the twelfth century as Kiev weakened, the Crusades shifted Mediterranean trade to Italian cities, and the Mongol invasions disrupted the steppe.
