Metz is a city in north-eastern France, the prefecture of the Moselle department in the Grand Est region. It stands at the confluence of the Moselle and Seille rivers, near the borders with Luxembourg and Germany, and has a history spanning more than two thousand years.

History

Metz began as Divodurum, chief town of the Celtic Mediomatrici and then a prosperous Roman city. In the early Middle Ages it was a capital of the Frankish kingdom of Austrasia and a favoured city of the Carolingians; Arnulf of Metz, an ancestor of Charlemagne, was its bishop. It later became a free imperial city and a wealthy merchant republic before being annexed by France under Henri II in 1552 and confirmed by the Treaty of Westphalia. Because of its strategic position it was heavily fortified by Vauban and others.

After the Franco-Prussian War, Metz was annexed to the German Empire in 1871 and surrounded by a ring of modern forts. French forces and their allies besieged it in 1870 (when Marshal Bazaine surrendered an entire army there). It returned to France in 1918, was annexed again by Nazi Germany in 1940, and was liberated in late 1944 after the bitter Battle of Metz against American forces under General Patton.

Landmarks

The Gothic Cathedral of Saint-Étienne is famous for its immense expanse of stained glass — the largest in France — including windows by Marc Chagall, earning the city the nickname La ville lumière in glass. Other landmarks include the Porte des Allemands, the imperial railway station built under German rule, the Temple Neuf on an island in the Moselle, and the Centre Pompidou-Metz, a shingle-roofed contemporary art museum opened in 2010 as the first decentralised outpost of the Paris Pompidou Centre.

Economy and culture

Metz is a garrison town, university city and commercial centre close to the Luxembourg financial sector, drawing many cross-border workers. Its mirabelle plum is celebrated locally, and its Christmas markets and the Mirabelle festival mark the year. The football club FC Metz plays at the Stade Saint-Symphorien.
