Five Ways (Aquinas)

The Five Ways (Latin: Quinque viae) are five logical arguments for the existence of God summarised by the 13th-century Catholic philosopher and theologian Thomas Aquinas in his book Summa Theologiae (Part I, Question 2, Article 3). They are: the argument from motion, the argument from causation, the argument from contingency, the argument from degree, and the argument from purpose (teleology). Aquinas did not present them as a complete proof of the Christian God, but as demonstrations that "God exists" is knowable by natural reason, distinct from what is known by faith and revelation.

The five arguments

First Way — motion. Things in the world are in motion (change); whatever is moved is moved by another; an infinite regress of movers is impossible; therefore there must be a first unmoved mover.

Second Way — causation. Every effect has a cause, and causes are ordered in series; there cannot be an infinite regress of efficient causes; therefore there is a first efficient cause.

Third Way — contingency. Contingent beings exist but might not have existed; if everything were contingent, at some time nothing would have existed, and nothing would exist now; therefore a necessary being exists.

Fourth Way — degree. Things are more or less good, true and noble; gradations imply a maximum — that which is maximally good and the cause of all perfections.

Fifth Way — purpose. Natural bodies without knowledge act for ends, as shown by their regular behaviour; whatever lacks knowledge cannot move toward an end unless directed by a knowing intelligence, as an arrow is directed by the archer.

Reception and criticism

The Five Ways became cornerstones of natural theology and were influential in Catholic thought; the First Vatican Council (1870) affirmed that God's existence can be known with certainty by reason. Philosophers from David Hume and Immanuel Kant to Bertrand Russell have criticised the arguments — questioning, for example, the rejection of infinite regress, the leap from a first cause to God, and the inference from order to a designer. Modern defenders, including Thomists, respond that the arguments are frequently misunderstood when read as simple empirical hypotheses rather than metaphysical demonstrations.
