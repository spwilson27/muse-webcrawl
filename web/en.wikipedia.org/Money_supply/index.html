Money supply

Currency in circulation is only a small part of the total money supply

The money supply is the total amount of money in circulation or in existence in a country at a specific time. Because "money" can be defined at different levels of liquidity, central banks publish several monetary aggregates. Most of the money supply in modern economies consists not of physical currency but of deposits created by commercial banks through lending.

Monetary aggregates

AggregateComponents
M0 / monetary baseCurrency in circulation plus bank reserves at the central bank
M1Currency held by the public plus demand (checking) deposits and other liquid deposits
M2M1 plus savings deposits, small time deposits, and retail money market funds
M3M2 plus large time deposits and institutional money funds (no longer published by the Federal Reserve)

Creation of money

The central bank creates base money; commercial banks expand broad money when they lend, since loans create deposits. The older textbook "money multiplier" model treats this as reserve-constrained, while modern central banks describe banks as lending first on the basis of profitable opportunities and capital, with reserves supplied as needed. The relationship between money growth and inflation — central to monetarism, summarised in the quantity theory equation MV = PY — is stable over very long horizons but proved unreliable as a short-run policy guide, leading most central banks to target interest rates instead of aggregates.

Measurement and recent history

US M2 grew unusually fast in 2020–21 during pandemic-era stimulus and quantitative easing, then contracted in 2022–23 — the first sustained decline since the Great Depression era — as the Federal Reserve tightened monetary policy.
