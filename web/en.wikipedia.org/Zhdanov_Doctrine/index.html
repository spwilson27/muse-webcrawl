Zhdanov Doctrine

The Zhdanov Doctrine was a Soviet cultural and foreign-policy doctrine formulated by Andrei Zhdanov, a close associate of Joseph Stalin, in the early Cold War period. It declared that the world was divided into two irreconcilable camps: an imperialist camp led by the United States and a democratic, anti-imperialist camp led by the Soviet Union.

Foreign policy

The doctrine was announced in Zhdanov's report at the founding conference of the Cominform in September 1947, responding to the Truman Doctrine and the Marshall Plan. It obliged communist parties to oppose American influence and framed the Cold War as a struggle between two camps.

Cultural policy

Domestically, "Zhdanovism" (Zhdanovshchina) denoted the ideological tightening of 1946–1948: decrees condemned the journals Zvezda and Leningrad and writers such as Anna Akhmatova and Mikhail Zoshchenko, and a 1948 resolution attacked composers including Shostakovich and Prokofiev for "formalism". Soviet art, literature, and scholarship were required to serve party-minded socialist realism.
