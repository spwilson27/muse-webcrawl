Option for the poor

The preferential option for the poor, or simply the option for the poor, is a principle of Catholic social teaching holding that, in moral and social decision-making, the needs of the poor and vulnerable should be given special consideration — God, in the biblical tradition, shows a particular concern for the poor, and the moral test of a society is how it treats its most vulnerable members. The phrase emerged from Latin American theology in the late 1960s and became central to liberation theology.

Origins

The idea draws on biblical themes — the Exodus, the prophets' denunciations of injustice, the Magnificat, and Jesus's ministry to the marginalised — as filtered through modern social teaching from Rerum novarum (1891) onward. The term was used by Jesuit superior general Pedro Arrupe in 1968 and taken up that year by the Latin American bishops' conference at Medellín, Colombia, which spoke of the Church's "preference" for the poorest sectors. Peruvian theologian Gustavo Gutiérrez developed it in A Theology of Liberation (1971).

Development in official teaching

Pope John Paul II affirmed the "option or love of preference for the poor" in the encyclical Sollicitudo rei socialis (1987), while Vatican instructions of the 1980s criticised versions of liberation theology that adopted Marxist class analysis. The Compendium of the Social Doctrine of the Church (2004) states that the option "is a special form of primacy in the exercise of Christian charity." Pope Francis made it a signature of his pontificate — "a Church which is poor and for the poor" — in Evangelii gaudium (2013) and in his teaching on economic exclusion and inequality.

Meaning and debate

Interpretations range from charitable works (feeding, housing and advocating for the poor) to structural analysis of the political and economic causes of poverty — "structures of sin" in John Paul II's phrase — including land reform, labour rights and critique of unregulated markets. The principle has influenced Protestant and ecumenical social ethics as well, and is invoked in debates on welfare, migration, development aid and environmental justice (where it extends to "the cry of the earth" alongside "the cry of the poor" in Laudato si').
