Cleromancy

Cleromancy is a form of divination based on the casting of lots. It rests on the belief that the outcome of a seemingly random process — the fall of dice, bones, sticks, stones, or marked objects — is directed by divine will and can therefore reveal answers to questions or guidance for decisions. The word derives from the Greek klēros, a lot or allotment, and manteia, divination.

In the ancient world

Casting lots was widespread in the ancient Near East and Mediterranean. In ancient Israel the Urim and Thummim kept in the high priest's breastplate were probably a lot oracle, and the Hebrew Bible records lots being cast to choose a scapegoat, divide land, and select Matthias as an apostle in the New Testament book of Acts. The Greeks cast lots at some oracles, and Roman practice included the sortes, lots drawn from an urn.

Forms

Related practices include astragalomancy, divination with knucklebones or dice; the drawing of short and long straws; bibliomancy and the sortes biblicae, in which a sacred text is opened at random; and various systems of casting sticks or coins, of which the Chinese I Ching yarrow-stalk method is a highly developed relative. In Tibet, dough-ball lots have traditionally been used in monasteries to decide religious questions.

Attitudes

Religious authorities have often been ambivalent about cleromancy: lot-casting sanctioned by scripture was accepted in some traditions, while unauthorised fortune-telling was condemned. Medieval and early modern theologians debated whether casting lots to settle disputes was lawful when no better means existed, generally permitting it for dividing goods but not for probing hidden matters of faith.
