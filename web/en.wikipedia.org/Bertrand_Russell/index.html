Bertrand Russell

Bertrand Arthur William Russell, 3rd Earl Russell (18 May 1872 – 2 February 1970), was a British philosopher, logician, and social critic. As an academic he worked in philosophy, mathematics, and logic; his writing on a broad range of subjects made him a populariser of philosophy and a leading public intellectual and activist of the twentieth century. He is generally considered one of the founders of analytic philosophy.

Work in logic and philosophy

Russell's paper "On Denoting" (1905) is a landmark of analytic philosophy. With Alfred North Whitehead he wrote Principia Mathematica (1910–1913), an attempt to derive all of mathematics from a small set of logical axioms; in the course of this work he had earlier discovered Russell's paradox, a contradiction in naive set theory. His History of Western Philosophy (1945) reached a wide readership, and he received the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1950 "in recognition of his varied and significant writings in which he champions humanitarian ideals and freedom of thought".

Activism

Russell was a prominent anti-war activist: he was imprisoned in 1918 for pacifist writing during the First World War, campaigned for nuclear disarmament, co-authored the Russell–Einstein Manifesto (1955), and chaired the Russell Tribunal on alleged American war crimes in Vietnam. His 1927 lecture "Why I Am Not a Christian" became a classic statement of religious scepticism.
