Bioprospecting

Bioprospecting (also known as biodiversity prospecting) is the exploration of natural sources for small molecules, macromolecules and biochemical and genetic information that could be developed into commercially valuable products for the agricultural, aquaculture, bioremediation, cosmetics, nanotechnology, or pharmaceutical industries. In the pharmaceutical industry, for example, almost one third of all small-molecule drugs approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) between 1981 and 2014 were either natural products or compounds derived from natural products.

Screening plant extracts for pharmaceutical activity.

Process

Bioprospecting typically involves collecting samples — plants, fungi, marine organisms, soil microbes — followed by extraction and screening against biological targets such as enzymes, receptors or pathogens. Promising "hits" are fractionated to isolate the active compound, whose structure is determined and optimized, often synthetically, before preclinical and clinical development.

Examples

Well-known products originating in bioprospecting include the cancer drugs derived from the rosy periwinkle (vincristine, vinblastine) and the Pacific yew (paclitaxel), artemisinin from sweet wormwood for malaria, and enzymes from extremophile microbes used in industry and in the polymerase chain reaction (Taq polymerase).

Legal and ethical issues

The practice has been criticized as "biopiracy" when genetic resources or traditional knowledge are patented without consent or fair compensation to source countries and indigenous communities. The Convention on Biological Diversity (1992) and the Nagoya Protocol (2010) established frameworks for access and benefit-sharing governing the use of genetic resources.
