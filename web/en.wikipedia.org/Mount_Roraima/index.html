Mount Roraima (Spanish: Monte Roraima) is the highest of the Pakaraima chain of tepui plateaus in South America, marking the triple border point of Venezuela, Brazil and Guyana. Its flat summit of about 31 km² is bounded on all sides by cliffs up to 1,000 metres high.

Geology and ecology

Roraima is composed of Proterozoic quartz arenite sandstones nearly two billion years old, remnants of a vast plateau eroded into isolated tepuis. Its summit hosts a unique ecosystem with a high level of endemism, including carnivorous plants and the tiny black Roraima bush toad, amid rock formations, valleys of crystals and constant mist.

Exploration and culture

The mountain was first described by Sir Walter Raleigh in 1596 and first climbed in 1884 by Everard im Thurn. It is an important inspiration for Sir Arthur Conan Doyle's 1912 novel The Lost World. For the Pemón people the tepui is a sacred place, the stump of a mighty tree that once bore all the world's fruits and vegetables.
