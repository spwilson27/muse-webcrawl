Medical malpractice

Medical malpractice is a legal cause of action that occurs when a medical or health care professional, through a negligent act or omission, deviates from standards in their profession, thereby causing injury or death to a patient. The negligence might arise from errors in diagnosis, treatment, aftercare or health management. An act or omission is not, in itself, malpractice; it must be shown to have caused injury, and it must fall below the accepted standard of care.

Medical malpractice claims are litigated as a specialized form of negligence law.

Elements of a claim

To succeed in a medical malpractice claim, a plaintiff generally must prove four elements:

  1. Duty — a legal duty existed, typically established by the doctor–patient relationship.
  2. Breach — the provider failed to conform to the accepted standard of care, usually proven through expert testimony.
  3. Causation — the breach directly caused the injury.
  4. Damages — the injury resulted in compensable harm, such as medical costs, lost income, or pain and suffering.

Frequency and consequences

Studies of medical error, including the influential 1999 Institute of Medicine report To Err Is Human, have drawn attention to the scale of preventable harm in hospitals. Malpractice litigation is controversial: proponents argue it deters negligence and compensates victims, while critics contend it raises healthcare costs through insurance premiums and "defensive medicine" — tests and procedures ordered mainly to protect against lawsuits. Many jurisdictions have enacted tort reform measures, such as caps on non-economic damages and statutes of limitation specific to malpractice.

Common types of claims

  • Misdiagnosis or delayed diagnosis
  • Surgical errors
  • Medication and anesthesia errors
  • Childbirth injuries
  • Failure to obtain informed consent
