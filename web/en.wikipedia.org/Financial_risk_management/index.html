Financial risk management is the practice of protecting economic value in a firm by managing exposure to risk — principally market risk and credit risk. Financial risk managers identify risks, measure them, and develop strategies to manage them, frequently using financial instruments such as derivatives to hedge exposures.

Financial risk management is an interdisciplinary subject, drawing on financial economics, statistics, and operations research. It is practised by banks, insurers, asset managers, and the corporate treasuries of non-financial firms, and is overseen at the international level by regulatory frameworks such as the Basel Accords.

Types of financial risk

Market risk

Market risk is the risk of losses arising from movements in market prices, including interest rates, foreign exchange rates, equity prices, and commodity prices. A standard measure is value at risk (VaR), which estimates the maximum loss over a given horizon at a given confidence level.

Credit risk

Credit risk is the risk of loss due to a debtor's non-payment of a loan or other line of credit. It is managed through credit analysis, exposure limits, collateral, and credit derivatives such as credit default swaps.

Liquidity risk

Liquidity risk arises when an institution cannot meet its short-term obligations, either because it cannot liquidate assets quickly without a substantial loss in value (market liquidity risk) or because it cannot obtain funding (funding liquidity risk).

Operational risk

Operational risk is the risk of loss resulting from inadequate or failed internal processes, people, and systems, or from external events, including legal risk and fraud.

Risk management process

The standard process comprises identifying the risks faced by the firm, measuring and quantifying exposures, deciding on a strategy (avoidance, reduction, transfer, or acceptance), implementing controls and hedges, and monitoring and reporting results. Hedging with derivatives — forwards, futures, options, and swaps — allows firms to lock in prices or rates without selling the underlying exposure.

Regulation

Following the 2008 financial crisis, regulation of financial risk management tightened considerably. The Basel III framework raised capital and liquidity requirements for banks, introduced stress testing, and imposed leverage limits. Central clearing of standardised derivatives and trade reporting were mandated in many jurisdictions under reforms such as the Dodd–Frank Act in the United States and EMIR in the European Union.
