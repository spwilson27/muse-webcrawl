César Milstein CH FRS (8 October 1927 – 24 March 2002) was an Argentine biochemist in the field of antibody research. Milstein shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1984 with Niels Kaj Jerne and Georges J. F. Köhler for developing the hybridoma technique for the production of monoclonal antibodies.

Career

Milstein studied at the University of Buenos Aires and later in Cambridge. After a period back in Argentina, where political turmoil under the military government disrupted his institute, he returned to Cambridge permanently in the 1960s, joining the Medical Research Council Laboratory of Molecular Biology under Fred Sanger's influence. His early work concerned enzyme mechanisms, including phosphoglucomutase, before he turned to antibodies.

Monoclonal antibodies

In 1975, working with the German postdoctoral researcher Georges Köhler, Milstein fused antibody-producing B lymphocytes with myeloma cells to create hybridomas — immortal cell lines, each producing a single ("monoclonal") antibody of predefined specificity. The technique, published in Nature in 1975, transformed diagnostics, research and therapeutics; monoclonal antibodies are now widely used medicines for cancer and autoimmune diseases. Milstein famously did not patent the method, a decision later regretted by British officials.
