The Walls of Benin, also known as the Benin Moat (Edo: Iya), are a vast series of earthworks — ramparts and ditches — built around Benin City and the surrounding territory of the historic Kingdom of Benin in what is now Edo State, Nigeria. They are considered among the largest man-made structures in the world by length, and one of the most extensive earthwork constructions ever undertaken.

Description

The Benin earthworks consist of a network of moats and banks enclosing the capital and outlying districts. The combined system has been estimated at some 16,000 kilometres of boundaries, covering roughly 6,500 square kilometres. The inner wall of Benin City itself, described by Portuguese visitors in the sixteenth century, stood about 20 metres high including its ditch. Construction proceeded in phases between roughly 800 and 1500 AD, with major works attributed to Oba Oguola (c. 1280) and Oba Ewuare the Great (c. 1440s), who expanded the city's fortifications.

History and destruction

European visitors were astonished by the scale and order of Benin City, comparing it favourably with Lisbon and Amsterdam. Much of the inner wall system was destroyed during and after the British punitive expedition of 1897, which looted and burned the royal palace and its famous bronzes; further losses have followed from modern road building and urban growth. Surviving sections of the moat remain visible within Benin City and are protected as a Nigerian national monument.
