Humanism

Humanism
A philosophical stance emphasising the value and agency of human beings, individually and collectively.

Humanism is a philosophical stance that emphasises the individual and social potential, and agency of human beings, whom it considers the starting point for serious moral and philosophical inquiry. The term has been applied to several distinct movements across history.

Renaissance humanism

Renaissance humanism was a cultural and intellectual movement in Europe during the Renaissance, originating in 14th-century Italy with figures such as Petrarch. Its scholars — the umanisti — revived the study of classical Greek and Latin texts, rhetoric, history and moral philosophy (the studia humanitatis), shifting scholarship from scholastic theology toward human experience and civic life. Erasmus of Rotterdam became its most famous representative north of the Alps.

Enlightenment and secular humanism

Enlightenment thinkers extended humanist ideas through reason, science and individual rights. In the 20th century, secular humanism emerged as a life stance rejecting supernatural and religious dogma while affirming ethics grounded in human welfare; the Humanist Manifestos (1933, 1973, 2003) articulated its principles, and Humanists International coordinates affiliated organisations worldwide. Its commitments include democracy, human rights and the scientific method.

Other varieties

Religious humanism, Marxist humanism, existentialist humanism and literary humanism adapt the core concern with human flourishing to different frameworks. Critics, including posthumanists and some religious traditions, question humanism's anthropocentrism and its confidence in reason.
