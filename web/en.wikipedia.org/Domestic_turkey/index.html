The domestic turkey (Meleagris gallopavo domesticus) is a large domesticated bird descended from the wild turkey, raised worldwide for its meat. Turkeys were first domesticated by Indigenous peoples of Mesoamerica at least 2,000 years ago; Spanish conquistadors brought them to Europe in the early sixteenth century, from where they spread globally — and, curiously, were re-imported to North America by English colonists.

Commercial production

Modern commercial turkeys are overwhelmingly Broad Breasted Whites, bred for rapid growth, a large proportion of breast meat, and white pinfeathers that leave a cleaner-looking carcass. They are so heavily breasted that they cannot mate naturally and are reproduced by artificial insemination. The United States is the world's largest turkey producer; turkey is the centrepiece of the American Thanksgiving dinner and of Christmas meals in Britain and elsewhere.

Heritage breeds

Heritage varieties — Narragansett, Bourbon Red, Royal Palm, Standard Bronze, Midget White and others — retain the ability to fly short distances, mate naturally, and forage, and are recognised by the American Poultry Association as varieties of a single breed. Interest in heritage turkeys has grown with the slow food and livestock-conservancy movements.

Biology and behaviour

Adult males (toms or gobblers) display with fanned tail feathers, drooping wings, and engorged red, white, and blue head skin; the fleshy appendage over the beak is the snood, and the projection below the throat is the wattle. Females (hens) lay clutches of 10–14 eggs with an incubation period of about 28 days. Domestic turkeys retain the wild species' gobbling call in males and social flocking behaviour.
