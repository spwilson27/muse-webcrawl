Indo-European ablaut

In linguistics, the Indo-European ablaut (from German Ablaut, "sound gradation") is a system of apophony — regular vowel alternations — in the Proto-Indo-European language (PIE). An example in modern English is the strong verb sing–sang–sung, which continues the PIE pattern of alternating root vowels. In PIE, a root's basic vowel was *e (the "e-grade"), which could alternate with *o (the "o-grade"), with a long vowel (the "lengthened grade"), or with no vowel at all (the "zero grade"), depending on accent and grammatical function.

The five ablaut grades of a typical PIE root.

Grades

The standard paradigm of grades for a root such as *bʰer- "carry" comprises the e-grade *bʰer-, the o-grade *bʰor-, the zero grade *bʰr̥-, and lengthened grades *bʰēr- and *bʰōr-. Grades were originally conditioned by the position of the PIE accent: unstressed syllables lost their vowel, producing zero grades. Survivals include the English alternations in foot/feet patterns of derivation and noun–verb pairs such as Greek patḗr (e-grade) versus genitive patrós (o-grade).

Descendants

Ablaut survives most visibly in the Germanic strong verbs, where it was systematised into seven classes, and in remnants of ancient Greek and Indo-Iranian verb and noun morphology. Although obscured in many daughter languages by analogy and sound change, ablaut remains a cornerstone of Indo-European historical linguistics, first described systematically in the 19th century and given the name Ablaut by Jacob Grimm.
