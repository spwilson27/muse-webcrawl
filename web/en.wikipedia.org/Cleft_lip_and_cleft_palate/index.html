Cleft lip and cleft palate

Cleft lip and cleft palate are openings or splits in the upper lip, the roof of the mouth (palate), or both, that result when facial structures developing in an unborn baby do not close completely. They are among the most common birth defects, occurring in roughly 1 in 700 live births, with rates varying by population.

Causes

In most cases the cause is unknown and is believed to be a combination of genetic and environmental factors, including certain medications, maternal smoking and diabetes, and folate status during early pregnancy. Clefts may occur as part of a syndrome, such as van der Woude syndrome or 22q11.2 deletion syndrome, or in isolation. A cleft lip can be one-sided (unilateral) or two-sided (bilateral); a cleft palate may involve the hard palate, the soft palate, or both.

Complications and treatment

Children with clefts may have difficulty feeding, recurrent ear infections and hearing loss, dental problems, and speech difficulties. Treatment is centred on surgery: a cleft lip is usually repaired in the first months of life and a cleft palate before about 18 months, timed to support speech development. Care is typically multidisciplinary, involving surgeons, speech therapists, audiologists, orthodontists and psychologists, and charitable organisations such as Operation Smile and Smile Train provide surgery in low-resource settings where access is otherwise limited.
