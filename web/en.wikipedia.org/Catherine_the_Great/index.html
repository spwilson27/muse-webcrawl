Catherine II (born Princess Sophie of Anhalt-Zerbst; 1729–1796), known as Catherine the Great, was Empress of Russia from 1762 until her death in 1796 — the longest-reigning female ruler in Russian history. Born in Stettin, Prussia, she came to power after the overthrow and death of her husband, Peter III, and presided over a golden age of the Russian Empire: Russia expanded enormously, was recognised as one of the great powers of Europe, and experienced a flowering of arts and education known as the Russian Enlightenment.

Rise to power

Sophie arrived in Russia in 1744 as the chosen bride of the heir, Grand Duke Peter. She converted to Orthodoxy as Catherine, mastered Russian, and cultivated allies at court and among the Guards regiments, while her marriage remained cold. In July 1762, weeks after Peter III's accession, she led a coup d'état with the Orlov brothers; Peter abdicated and died days later in custody, almost certainly murdered.

Domestic policy

Catherine corresponded with Voltaire and Diderot and cast herself as an enlightened absolutist. Her Nakaz (Instruction) of 1767, drawing on Montesquieu and Beccaria, guided a Legislative Commission that ultimately produced little reform. She reorganised provincial government (1775), issued the Charter to the Nobility (1785), founded the Smolny Institute for girls, introduced smallpox inoculation (undergoing it herself), and greatly expanded the Hermitage art collection. Yet her reign also entrenched serfdom, extending it into Ukraine, and she crushed Pugachev's Rebellion (1773–75), the largest peasant revolt in Russian history.

Foreign policy and expansion

Under Catherine, Russia defeated the Ottoman Empire in two wars, annexed Crimea (1783), won a Black Sea coast, and participated in the three Partitions of Poland (1772, 1793, 1795), erasing Poland from the map. Her "Greek Project" envisaged a restored Byzantine state under Russian tutelage. The empire gained some 520,000 km² of territory during her reign.

Legacy

Catherine's reputation mixes genuine achievement as a state-builder and patron with the darker record of serfdom and expansionism. Famous for her many favourites — most consequentially Grigory Potemkin — she herself promoted salacious myths about her with characteristic irony. She remains one of the most written-about monarchs of the 18th century.
