World Heritage List

The World Heritage List is a list of sites maintained by UNESCO under the World Heritage Convention, adopted in 1972. It comprises cultural, natural and mixed properties around the world considered to be of "outstanding universal value" to humanity, which the international community has pledged to help protect.

As of the mid-2020s, the list contains more than 1,200 properties in over 160 countries. Italy and China have the largest numbers of inscribed sites, each with around 60. The first twelve sites were inscribed in 1978, among them Yellowstone National Park, the Galápagos Islands, Aachen Cathedral and the Old City of Kraków.

Nomination and selection

To be inscribed, a site must first appear on a country's Tentative List. The state party then prepares a nomination dossier, which is evaluated by advisory bodies — ICOMOS for cultural properties and IUCN for natural properties. The World Heritage Committee, made up of 21 elected member states, makes the final decision at its annual session.

Sites must meet at least one of ten selection criteria, demonstrate integrity and (for cultural sites) authenticity, and have adequate legal protection and management systems. Six criteria are cultural (i–vi) and four are natural (vii–x).

Categories

  • Cultural properties — monuments, groups of buildings and sites, including historic cities, archaeological sites and cultural landscapes; these form the large majority of the list.
  • Natural properties — areas of exceptional natural beauty, geological significance, or importance for biodiversity and ecosystems.
  • Mixed properties — sites meeting both cultural and natural criteria, such as Machu Picchu and Mount Athos.

Danger list and delisting

Properties threatened by conflict, natural disaster, pollution or uncontrolled development may be placed on the List of World Heritage in Danger, which mobilises international attention and assistance. Only a few sites have ever been removed from the list entirely: the Arabian Oryx Sanctuary in Oman (2007), the Dresden Elbe Valley in Germany (2009), and Liverpool – Maritime Mercantile City in the United Kingdom (2021), in each case after development was judged to have destroyed the site's outstanding universal value.

Transboundary sites

Some properties span several countries, such as the Frontiers of the Roman Empire, the Primeval Beech Forests of the Carpathians and other regions of Europe, and the Struve Geodetic Arc, which crosses ten countries.
