A water gap is a geographical feature where a river cuts through a mountain ridge. Water gaps often provide natural corridors for roads and railways through otherwise impassable terrain, and they create sites of striking scenery. The term contrasts with a wind gap, a similar notch in a ridge that is no longer occupied by flowing water.

Formation

Water gaps form when a river's erosive power keeps pace with, or exceeds, the uplift of a mountain ridge crossing its path. Several mechanisms have been proposed. In antecedence, the river is older than the uplift and incises downward as the ridge rises. In superimposition, the river originally flowed over a covering surface (such as a coastal plain) and, as it cut down, became "let down" onto buried resistant rocks, maintaining its course across the structure. Headward erosion and stream capture can also breach ridges. Debates over the origins of individual gaps — most famously the Delaware Water Gap — engaged geologists for much of the twentieth century.

Notable examples

The Delaware Water Gap, where the Delaware River cuts through the Kittatinny Ridge between Pennsylvania and New Jersey, is now a national recreation area. Other celebrated examples include the Iron Gate gorge of the Danube between Serbia and Romania, the Columbia River Gorge through the Cascade Range, the Manawatu Gorge in New Zealand, and the Cumberland Gap region's gaps in the Appalachians. Many Appalachian rivers — the Susquehanna, Potomac, and James — pass through prominent water gaps in the Ridge-and-Valley province.

Human use

Because they offer low-gradient routes through mountains, water gaps have concentrated migration, commerce, and military movement for centuries. Highways, rail lines, and canals frequently thread through them, and their constriction of rivers has also made gaps attractive sites for dams — the Iron Gate now hosts one of Europe's largest hydroelectric complexes.
