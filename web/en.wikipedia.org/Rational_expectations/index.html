Rational expectations

Rational expectations is an economic hypothesis holding that agents' predictions of future economic variables are not systematically wrong: on average, forecasts equal the true values generated by the economic model itself, given the information available. The idea was first proposed by John Muth in 1961 and was developed in the 1970s by Robert Lucas Jr., Thomas Sargent, and others, becoming a cornerstone of the new classical economics.

Formulation

Formally, the rational expectation of a variable is its mathematical expectation conditional on the agent's information set, including knowledge of the structure of the economy. Forecast errors still occur, but they are random and uncorrelated with anything the agent knew when the forecast was made.

Implications

The hypothesis underlies the policy ineffectiveness proposition: anticipated monetary policy cannot systematically affect real output, because workers and firms incorporate expected inflation into wages and prices. It also transformed macroeconomic modelling, leading to dynamic stochastic general equilibrium (DSGE) models in which expectations are model-consistent.

Criticism

Critics argue that real agents lack the information and computational capacity the theory assumes, that learning takes time, and that surveys of expectations often show systematic biases. Approaches such as adaptive expectations, behavioural economics, and models of learning offer alternatives.
