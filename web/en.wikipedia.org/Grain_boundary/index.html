Grain boundary

In materials science, a grain boundary is the interface between two grains, or crystallites, in a polycrystalline material. Grain boundaries are two-dimensional defects in the crystal structure, and tend to decrease the electrical and thermal conductivity of a material, while often increasing its strength. Most grain boundaries are preferred sites for the onset of corrosion and for the precipitation of new phases from the solid.

Description

Grain boundaries are the regions where crystals of different orientation meet. A boundary can be described by the misorientation between the adjacent crystals and the orientation of the boundary plane — five macroscopic degrees of freedom in total. Boundaries with a small misorientation (low-angle boundaries, roughly below 10–15°) can be described as arrays of dislocations; high-angle boundaries contain more disordered atomic arrangements and higher energies. Certain special boundaries, such as coherent twin boundaries and those matching coincidence site lattice (CSL) geometries, have notably low energies and distinctive properties.

Effects on properties

Strength: Grain boundaries impede the motion of dislocations, so reducing grain size strengthens a metal — the Hall–Petch relationship. Conversely, at high temperatures boundaries can slide and allow creep, so single-crystal alloys are used for the hottest turbine blades.

Transport: Boundaries scatter electrons and phonons, lowering electrical and thermal conductivity, but provide fast diffusion paths ("short-circuit" diffusion) for atoms.

Chemical behaviour: Impurities and alloying elements segregate to boundaries, which can cause embrittlement (e.g., sulfur in steel, bismuth in copper) or be exploited beneficially in grain boundary engineering.

Grain boundary energy and migration

Because the atoms at a boundary are less well bonded, boundaries possess an interfacial free energy, typically 0.3–1.0 J/m² for metals. This energy drives grain growth during annealing as the system reduces total boundary area; solutes and second-phase particles can pin boundaries and retard growth (Zener pinning). Grain boundaries are also central to recrystallization and texture development in processed materials.
