Christiaan Huygens

Christiaan Huygens, Lord of Zeelhem (14 April 1629 – 8 July 1695) was a Dutch mathematician, physicist, engineer, astronomer and inventor, generally regarded as one of the greatest scientists of all time and a major figure in the Scientific Revolution. His work spanned early telescopic astronomy, the wave theory of light, the mathematics of probability and games of chance, and the invention of the pendulum clock.

Biography

Huygens was born in The Hague into a wealthy and influential family; his father Constantijn Huygens was a diplomat and secretary to the Princes of Orange, and a friend of Descartes and Rembrandt. Christiaan studied law and mathematics at Leiden University and the College of Orange in Breda. He soon turned wholly to science, publishing early work on squaring the circle and the laws of elastic collision. In 1666 he moved to Paris as a founding member of Louis XIV's Académie Royale des Sciences, remaining there for fifteen years before ill health and the changing religious climate brought him back to The Hague, where he died in 1695.

Astronomy

Huygens ground his own lenses with his brother Constantijn and built long "aerial telescopes". In 1655 he discovered Titan, Saturn's largest moon. After observing Saturn with improved instruments he solved the riddle of its "handles", announcing in Systema Saturnium (1659) that the planet is surrounded by a thin flat ring. He also described the Orion Nebula and several interstellar nebulae, and was the first to attempt a measurement of the distance to a star (Sirius).

Horology and mechanics

In 1656 Huygens invented the pendulum clock, a breakthrough that transformed timekeeping; his treatise Horologium Oscillatorium (1673) analysed the mathematics of pendulums, introduced the cycloid as the tautochrone curve, and developed the theory of evolutes. He also derived the law of centrifugal force for uniform circular motion and worked on the balance-spring watch, a priority he disputed with Robert Hooke.

Optics and mathematics

His Traité de la Lumière (1690) set out the wave theory of light: every point of a wavefront is a source of secondary wavelets — now known as the Huygens–Fresnel principle — with which he explained reflection, refraction and double refraction in Iceland spar. In mathematics, his De Ratiociniis in Ludo Aleae (1657) was the first printed treatise on probability. His posthumous Cosmotheoros speculated on extraterrestrial life.

Legacy

The European Space Agency's Huygens probe, which landed on Titan in 2005 as part of the Cassini–Huygens mission, was named in his honour, as are an asteroid, a lunar crater and mountains on the Moon.
