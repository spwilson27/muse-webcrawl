The London School of Economics and Political Science (LSE) is a public research university in London, England, and a member institution of the federal University of London. Founded in 1895 by Fabian Society members Sidney Webb, Beatrice Webb, Graham Wallas and George Bernard Shaw, it specialises in the social sciences, including economics, politics, law, sociology, anthropology, international relations and finance.

History

LSE was founded with the aim of bettering society through the scientific study of poverty, inequality and related issues; it joined the University of London in 1900. Economist William Beveridge, later author of the Beveridge Report that inspired the British welfare state, served as director from 1919 to 1937. Under Friedrich Hayek and Lionel Robbins in the 1930s, the school became famous for its debates with Cambridge over economic theory. The school's campus is centred on Houghton Street in Aldwych, in the City of Westminster.

Academics and reputation

LSE is consistently ranked among the world's leading social science institutions, and its economics department is particularly renowned. The school has one of the most international student bodies of any university, with students from over 150 countries. Its alumni and faculty include numerous heads of state and government, and 18 Nobel laureates in economics, peace and literature (as of recent counts), including George Bernard Shaw, Ralph Bunche, Hayek, John Hicks, James Meade, Merton Miller, Robert Mundell, Arthur Lewis, Leonid Hurwicz, Paul Krugman and Christopher Pissarides.

The British Library of Political and Economic Science, housed in the Lionel Robbins Building, is the world's largest social-science library. LSE's public events programme hosts lectures by world leaders and academics, open to the general public.
