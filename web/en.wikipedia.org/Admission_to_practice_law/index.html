Admission to practice law

Admission to the bar in the United States is the granting of permission by a particular court system to a lawyer to practice law in the jurisdiction. Admission to practice law generally is the process by which a person becomes authorized to work as a legal practitioner; requirements typically include a law degree, passing a bar examination, and meeting character and fitness standards.

United States

In the U.S., admission is by state (and territory), not federal: each state's highest court or bar authority licenses attorneys. Most applicants must earn a Juris Doctor from an accredited law school, pass the state's bar examination — many states use the Uniform Bar Examination — and pass the Multistate Professional Responsibility Examination. Admitted attorneys may later gain admission to federal courts, including the Supreme Court bar, separately. Lawyers in good standing in one state may be admitted in another on motion or by reciprocity, or appear temporarily under pro hac vice rules.

Other countries

Elsewhere the path differs: in England and Wales, solicitors qualify through the Solicitors Qualifying Examination and barristers through pupillage after being called to the bar; in many civil-law countries, graduates complete a state examination and practical traineeship (such as the German Referendariat) before admission.
