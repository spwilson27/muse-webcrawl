Christian Jürgensen Thomsen (29 December 1788 – 21 May 1865) was a Danish antiquarian and museum curator who is best known for devising the three-age system — the division of prehistory into the Stone Age, Bronze Age and Iron Age — which became the foundational framework for the scientific study of human prehistory.

The son of a wealthy Copenhagen merchant, Thomsen developed an early passion for coin collecting and antiquities. In 1816 he was appointed secretary of the Danish Royal Commission for the Preservation of Antiquities and took charge of arranging the commission's growing collection, which formed the basis of the National Museum of Denmark, opened in 1819 with Thomsen as its first curator.

The three-age system

Tasked with organising the museum's artefacts, Thomsen sorted objects according to the material from which cutting tools were made — stone, bronze or iron — and argued that these materials succeeded one another chronologically. He set out the system publicly in his Ledetraad til Nordisk Oldkyndighed ("Guide to Scandinavian Antiquities", 1836; English translation 1848). Although similar ideas had been hinted at by earlier writers, Thomsen was the first to demonstrate the system empirically through closed finds and artefact associations, and his museum arrangement presented the argument physically to visitors, whom he personally guided through the galleries.

Later career and legacy

Thomsen also made significant contributions to numismatics, particularly the study of bracteates, and helped establish modern museum practice, insisting on careful documentation of find contexts. His framework was refined by his successor Jens Jacob Asmussen Worsaae and remains, in broad outline, the standard chronology of Old World prehistory.
