Lucius Cassius Dio Cocceianus (c. 155 – c. 235 AD), known in English as Cassius Dio, was a Roman historian and senator of Greek descent from Nicaea in Bithynia. He published a monumental Roman History in 80 books in Greek, covering the period from the legendary founding of Rome by Aeneas down to AD 229 — nearly a thousand years of history, the fruit of some 22 years of research and writing.

Life and career

Dio's father, Cassius Apronianus, was a Roman senator and governor of Dalmatia and Cilicia. Dio followed the senatorial career in Rome, becoming quaestor, praetor under Septimius Severus, and suffect consul around 204. He later served as proconsul of Africa and legate in Dalmatia and Upper Pannonia, where his strict discipline made him unpopular with the troops. In 229 he held the ordinary consulship as colleague of the emperor Severus Alexander, who then advised him to retire to Nicaea, where he died.

Roman History

Of the 80 books, Books 61–80 survive substantially complete, covering 47 BC to AD 229; earlier books survive in fragments and in Byzantine epitomes, notably that of the 11th-century monk John Xiphilinus. Dio is a crucial source for the last century of the Republic and for his own era of the Principate, including an eyewitness account of the reign of Commodus. His history is modelled on Thucydides, with invented speeches, annalistic structure, and a recurring interest in the tension between senate and emperor — he regarded monarchy as the necessary, if regrettable, government for the empire.

Assessment

Modern historians value Dio for his insider's view of Severan politics while treating his speeches and portents sceptically. His work underpins much of the standard narrative of the late Republic where Livy's text is lost.
