Farewell Pilgrimage

EventMuhammad's only full Hajj pilgrimage
Date632 CE (10 AH), Dhu al-Hijjah
RouteMedina to Mecca
Key momentsFarewell Sermon at Arafat and (in Shia tradition) Ghadir Khumm
AttendanceTens of thousands of followers (traditional accounts)

The Farewell Pilgrimage (Arabic: Ḥajjat al-Wadāʿ) was the only Hajj pilgrimage that Muhammad, the founder of Islam, performed in full, in the final year of his life, 10 AH (632 CE). Having intended the pilgrimage, he left Medina with a vast company of followers and performed the rites at Mecca, establishing by his example the sequence of rituals that Muslims follow in the Hajj to this day.

The pilgrimage

Arriving in Mecca, Muhammad performed the circumambulation (tawaf) of the Kaaba, the running (saʿi) between Safa and Marwah, and travelled to Mina. On 9 Dhu al-Hijjah he delivered the Farewell Sermon in the Uranah valley of the plain of Arafat, addressing a crowd traditionally numbered in the tens of thousands or more. According to hadith and sira literature, the sermon sanctified the lives and property of Muslims, abolished usury and the blood feuds of the pre-Islamic era, enjoined kind treatment of women, affirmed the equality of believers — "no Arab has superiority over a non-Arab" — and urged adherence to the Qur'an and the Prophet's example (in Sunni versions) or to the Qur'an and his household, the Ahl al-Bayt (in Shia versions). It was during the pilgrimage that Qur'an 5:3 — "This day I have perfected for you your religion" — is traditionally held to have been revealed.

Ghadir Khumm and aftermath

On the return journey, at the pond of Ghadir Khumm, Muhammad is reported in both Sunni and Shia sources to have declared of Ali ibn Abi Talib, "Whoever I am the mawla of, Ali is his mawla" — interpreted by Shia Islam as the designation of Ali as Muhammad's successor, and by Sunni Islam as an affirmation of Ali's standing. Muhammad died in Medina a few months later, in June 632.

Significance

The Farewell Pilgrimage fixed the Hajj's rites and stands, through the Farewell Sermon, as one of Islam's foundational ethical texts, frequently cited on human rights, equality, and the sanctity of life and property.
