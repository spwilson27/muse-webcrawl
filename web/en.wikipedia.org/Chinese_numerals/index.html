Chinese numerals


Rod numerals, an ancient positional system

Chinese numerals are words and characters used to denote numbers in Chinese, a system with a history of more than three thousand years, extending back to the oracle bone script of the Shang dynasty. Today, speakers of Chinese use two principal written systems: the everyday characters (such as 一 二 三 for one, two, three) and a formal "financial" set of more complex characters used on cheques and official documents to prevent fraud by alteration. Arabic numerals are also widely used in modern contexts.

Structure

Standard Chinese numerals are written with characters for the digits one through nine together with characters for powers of ten: 十 (ten), 百 (hundred), 千 (thousand), 萬/万 (ten thousand) and 億/亿 (one hundred million). Unlike Western grouping by thousands, traditional large numbers are grouped by myriads (units of 10,000), so that a million is "one hundred ten-thousands" (一百萬). The character 零 (líng) serves as zero. A separate formal set — 壹, 貳, 參, 肆, 伍, 陸, 柒, 捌, 玖, 拾, 佰, 仟, 萬, 億 — is used in banking and legal documents because the ordinary characters can be too easily altered.

History

Ancient Chinese computation used counting rods in a decimal positional system, described in texts such as the Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art. The Suzhou numerals (花碼), a descendant of rod numerals, were long used in commerce and markets in southern China and Hong Kong. Fractions, negative numbers and decimal notation for metrology all developed within the Chinese tradition, and the system influenced neighbouring cultures, including the Japanese, Korean and Vietnamese numeral systems.

Cultural notes

Number symbolism plays a role in Chinese culture: eight (八) is considered lucky because it sounds like the word for prosperity, while four (四) is avoided in many contexts because its pronunciation resembles the word for death.
