Charles David Keeling (April 20, 1928 – June 20, 2005) was an American scientist whose measurements of carbon dioxide at the Mauna Loa Observatory first alerted the world to the anthropogenic contribution to the "greenhouse effect" and global warming. The Keeling Curve, named after him, is a graph plotting the continuing change in atmospheric carbon dioxide concentration since 1958.

Early life and education

Keeling was born in Scranton, Pennsylvania. He earned an undergraduate degree in chemistry from the University of Illinois in 1948 and a doctorate in chemistry from Northwestern University in 1954. He then worked at the California Institute of Technology, where, under the mentorship of Harrison Brown, he developed precise methods for measuring carbon dioxide in air and seawater samples.

Mauna Loa measurements

In 1956 Keeling joined the Scripps Institution of Oceanography and in 1958, as part of the International Geophysical Year, established a measuring station at the Mauna Loa Observatory in Hawaii. His careful, continuous measurements revealed both a seasonal cycle — carbon dioxide dipping each northern spring and summer as plants grow — and a steady year-on-year increase, from about 315 parts per million in 1958 to over 380 ppm by the time of his death. Parallel measurements at the South Pole confirmed the rise was global.

Legacy

The Keeling Curve became one of the most iconic datasets in environmental science and foundational evidence for human-caused climate change. Keeling received numerous honours, including the National Medal of Science in 2002. The measurement programme has been continued at Scripps by his son, Ralph Keeling.
