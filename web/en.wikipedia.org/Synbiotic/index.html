Synbiotic

A synbiotic is a mixture comprising live microorganisms (probiotics) and one or more substrates selectively utilised by host microorganisms (prebiotics) that confers a health benefit on the host. The term, from the Greek syn meaning "together," was introduced by Glenn Gibson and Marcel Roberfroid in 1995, and the definition was refined by an expert consensus panel of the International Scientific Association for Probiotics and Prebiotics (ISAPP) in 2020.

Types

The ISAPP consensus distinguishes two categories. A complementary synbiotic combines an established probiotic and an established prebiotic, each meeting the minimum criteria for its category and dosed independently of each other. A synergistic synbiotic contains a substrate designed to be selectively used by the specific microorganism(s) co-administered with it — for example, pairing a Bifidobacterium strain with the particular oligosaccharide it can ferment but competitors cannot — so the components need not individually qualify as probiotic or prebiotic.

Rationale and evidence

The rationale is that a prebiotic substrate can improve the survival, colonisation, and metabolic activity of the administered microbe in the gastrointestinal tract, and stimulate beneficial resident gut bacteria, producing short-chain fatty acids and other metabolites. Synbiotic products, common in yoghurts, infant formula, and dietary supplements, are marketed for digestive and immune health; clinical evidence varies by indication, with promising results in some trials for necrotising enterocolitis prevention in preterm infants and for certain functional bowel complaints, though regulators such as the European Food Safety Authority have authorised few specific health claims.
