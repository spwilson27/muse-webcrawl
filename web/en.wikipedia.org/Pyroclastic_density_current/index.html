Pyroclastic density current

A pyroclastic density current (commonly called a pyroclastic flow, or nuée ardente, "burning cloud") is a fast-moving current of hot gas and volcanic matter (tephra) that flows along the ground away from an erupting volcano at speeds that can exceed 100 km/h (60 mph) and, in the largest events, reach 700 km/h (430 mph). Temperatures of 200–1,000 °C make these currents the most destructive and lethal of all volcanic phenomena.

Formation and behavior

Pyroclastic density currents form by the gravitational collapse of an eruption column, by the collapse of a growing lava dome, or by explosive lateral blasts. The current consists of a dense basal flow of gas and rock fragments sliding under gravity, often accompanied by a dilute, turbulent ash-cloud surge that can detach and travel beyond the main flow, over ridges and even across water. Being driven by density, the currents follow valleys but powerful surges may overtop barriers.

Deposits

When the current stops, it leaves deposits called ignimbrites when they are thick, pumice-rich, and laterally extensive; some ignimbrites were emplaced hot enough to weld. Fine ash lifted from the flow ("co-ignimbrite" plumes) can contribute to widespread ashfall.

Historical eruptions

Pyroclastic flows destroyed Pompeii and Herculaneum during the eruption of Vesuvius in 79 CE—Pliny the Younger's account gave rise to the term "Plinian"—and annihilated the city of Saint-Pierre, Martinique, in 1902, killing about 28,000 people in minutes during the eruption of Mount Pelée. The 1980 eruption of Mount St. Helens, the 1991 eruption of Mount Pinatubo, the 1995–2010 activity at Soufrière Hills (Montserrat), and flows from Japan's Mount Unzen in 1991, which killed 43 people including volcanologists Katia and Maurice Krafft, are among the best documented modern examples. Because the currents cannot be outrun, hazard mitigation depends on exclusion zones and early evacuation.
