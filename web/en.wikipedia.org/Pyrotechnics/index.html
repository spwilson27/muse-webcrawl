Pyrotechnics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Pyrotechnics is the science and craft of creating self-contained and self-sustained exothermic chemical reactions to produce heat, light, gas, smoke and/or sound. The field includes the manufacture of fireworks, safety matches, oxygen candles, explosive bolts and fasteners, airbags for automobiles, and effects for entertainment and industry.

The word derives from the Greek pyr (fire) and tekhnikos (made by art). Pyrotechnic compositions typically combine a fuel with an oxidiser — classically potassium nitrate in black powder — plus binders and additives that produce coloured flames: strontium salts for red, barium for green, sodium for yellow and copper compounds for blue.

Categories

Pyrotechnic devices are classified by function and hazard. Display fireworks and consumer fireworks dominate the commercial market; proximate pyrotechnics are devices used close to audiences in concerts and theatre; and special-effects pyrotechnics simulate explosions and bullet hits in film. Industrial and military applications include signalling flares, decoy flares, gas generators and initiation systems. A distinction is drawn between pyrotechnics, which burn in a controlled, propagated reaction, and high explosives, which detonate.

Safety and regulation

Because pyrotechnic materials are energetic, their manufacture, storage, transport and use are tightly regulated in most countries, and professional display operators are generally licensed. Accidents — in factories, in storage (such as the 2000 Enschede fireworks disaster in the Netherlands) and among consumers — remain a significant cause of injury, particularly around festivals such as New Year, Diwali, Bonfire Night and the Fourth of July.
