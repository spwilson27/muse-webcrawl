Slipstream

A slipstream is a region behind a moving object in which a wake of fluid (typically air or water) is moving at velocities comparable to the moving object, relative to the ambient fluid through which the object is moving. In other words, it is the reduced-pressure, reduced-drag zone trailing the object. The phenomenon is exploited in drafting in motorsport, cycling and speed skating, and it also occurs in nature, in the wake of boats, aircraft and flying birds.

Physics

As a body moves through a fluid, it displaces air and creates a turbulent wake. Within the wake immediately behind the body, the air is dragged along with the body, so a following object experiences reduced relative airflow and thus lower aerodynamic drag. The benefit depends on the shape of the lead object, the gap between objects, and speed; at very close distances the following vehicle may also gain a push from the pressure field around the lead vehicle. Large vehicles traveling in convoy ("platooning") can measurably reduce fuel consumption for all vehicles in the group.

Uses

In motor racing, drivers use the slipstream of the car ahead to gain speed and overtake, a maneuver central to racing at oval tracks and in series such as NASCAR and Formula One, where the drag reduction system (DRS) complements it. Cyclists in a peloton shelter in each other's slipstreams, saving substantial energy. Migrating birds flying in V formation exploit the upwash at the edges of the preceding bird's wake, a related aerodynamic effect, and speed skaters and swimmers also benefit from drafting positions.

Hazards

Slipstreams can be dangerous: wake turbulence from large aircraft can upset smaller following aircraft, which is why air-traffic rules prescribe separation distances. The turbulent air behind a race car can also rob a following car of downforce in corners, making it harder to stay close through turns even while it helps on straights.
