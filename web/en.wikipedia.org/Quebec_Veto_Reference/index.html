Quebec Veto Reference

Case nameReference re: Objection by Quebec to a Resolution to amend the Constitution
CourtSupreme Court of Canada
Decided1982
Citation[1982] 2 S.C.R. 793

The Quebec Veto Reference, formally Reference re: Objection by Quebec to a Resolution to amend the Constitution, [1982] 2 S.C.R. 793, is a landmark decision of the Supreme Court of Canada. The Court held that Quebec did not possess a conventional constitutional veto over amendments to the Constitution of Canada.

Background

Following the election of the Parti Québécois government of René Lévesque and the 1980 Quebec referendum on sovereignty-association, Prime Minister Pierre Trudeau moved to patriate the Canadian Constitution with an amending formula and the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms. Quebec objected that it held a veto under a constitutional convention, grounded in the principle of duality between English and French Canada.

Question referred

The question referred to the Court was whether the consent of Quebec was required by constitutional convention for the House of Commons and Senate to pass a resolution requesting patriation and amendment of the Constitution.

Decision

The Court answered no. Although a substantial measure of provincial consent was required by convention, as the Court had found in the earlier Patriation Reference of 1981, no single province — including Quebec — held a veto. The Court examined historical instances Quebec put forward and concluded they did not establish a convention recognizing a Quebec veto. The Constitution Act, 1982 was proclaimed shortly afterwards, which Quebec has never formally endorsed.

Significance

The decision remains central to debates about Quebec's place in the Canadian federation and informed later discussions during the Meech Lake and Charlottetown accords.
