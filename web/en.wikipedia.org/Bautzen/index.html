Bautzen (Upper Sorbian: Budyšin) is a hilltop town in eastern Saxony, Germany, about 50 km east of Dresden. It is the historic and cultural capital of the Sorbs, a West Slavic minority whose language and institutions are centred in Lusatia, and it is noted for its remarkably preserved old town of medieval towers, its mustard (Bautzner Senf), and its role as the site of the infamous Bautzen II prison of the East German Stasi.

History

First mentioned in 1002 in the chronicle of Thietmar of Merseburg, the settlement grew on a granite plateau above the river Spree at the crossing of important trade routes. Bautzen received town privileges in the thirteenth century and was a member of the Lusatian League of six cities. In 1813 the Battle of Bautzen was fought nearby, when Napoleon defeated the Russo-Prussian armies after his retreat from Russia. The town suffered heavy fighting again in April 1945, in one of the last battles of the Second World War in Europe.

Under East Germany, Bautzen's prisons became bywords for political repression: Bautzen I, the "Gelbes Elend" (Yellow Misery), and the secret Bautzen II facility for special political prisoners. A memorial now occupies the Bautzen II site.

Sorbian culture

Bautzen is home to the Domowina (the federation of Sorbian associations), the Sorbian National Ensemble, a Sorbian museum and bilingual German–Sorbian street signs. Easter riding processions and other Sorbian customs remain prominent in the surrounding Catholic villages.

Landmarks

The old town crowns a rocky spur above the Spree, with the Ortenburg castle, St Peter's Cathedral — a rare Simultaneum shared by Catholics and Protestants since the Reformation — the leaning Reichenturm tower, and a ring of medieval fortifications. The Alte Wasserkunst (Old Waterworks, 1558) is a symbol of the town.
