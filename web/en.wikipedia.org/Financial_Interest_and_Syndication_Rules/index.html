Financial Interest and Syndication Rules

The Financial Interest and Syndication Rules, widely known as the fin-syn rules, were a set of United States Federal Communications Commission (FCC) regulations adopted in 1970 that restricted the involvement of the major television networks in the financial interest and syndication of the programmes they aired. The rules were a major force in shaping the American television industry until their repeal in the 1990s.

Provisions

The rules prohibited the ABC, CBS, and NBC networks from holding a financial stake in most independently produced programming shown in prime time, and from engaging in the domestic syndication of programmes — the lucrative business of reselling reruns to local stations. The intent was to prevent the three networks from using their gatekeeper power over prime-time schedules to dominate programme production and distribution, and to encourage a diversity of programme sources.

Effects

Fin-syn transferred bargaining power and syndication profits to independent production companies. Studios such as MTM Enterprises, Norman Lear's Tandem Productions, and later Carsey-Werner prospered by retaining ownership of hit series and selling reruns themselves. Critics argued the rules weakened the networks and became obsolete as cable television eroded their dominance.

Repeal

The FCC began relaxing the rules in 1991 and repealed them in stages, with the last restrictions expiring in 1995. Repeal enabled the wave of vertical integration that followed, in which networks merged with major studios — such as Disney's acquisition of ABC and Viacom's ownership of CBS and Paramount — and in-house production came to dominate network schedules.
