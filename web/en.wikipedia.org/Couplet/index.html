Couplet

In poetry, a couplet is a pair of successive lines of verse, typically rhyming and of the same length. Couplets may be "closed" (self-contained in meaning) or "open" (running on into the next couplet). They are one of the oldest and most widespread verse units, appearing in traditions from classical Greek and Latin poetry to Chinese regulated verse and Persian mathnawi.

In English poetry

The heroic couplet — two rhyming lines of iambic pentameter — became the dominant form of English narrative and satirical verse in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, used by Chaucer before them and perfected by John Dryden and Alexander Pope. Pope's Essay on Criticism and The Rape of the Lock exemplify the closed heroic couplet's balanced, epigrammatic style. Shakespeare frequently ended scenes and sonnets with a concluding couplet.

Other traditions

In classical Tamil poetry, the kural couplet of the Tirukkural compresses ethical teaching into seven-word couplets. In Urdu and Persian poetry, the couplets (sher) of a ghazal are semi-autonomous units linked by rhyme and refrain. Chinese duilian are antithetical couplets displayed on scrolls and doorways, especially at New Year.
