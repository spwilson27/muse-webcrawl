Enzyme kinetics

Key equationv = Vmax[S] / (Km + [S])
Key constantsKm, Vmax, kcat
FoundersLeonor Michaelis, Maud Menten (1913)

Enzyme kinetics is the investigation of how an enzyme binds its substrate and turns it into product, and of how reaction rates are affected by factors such as substrate concentration, temperature, pH, and the presence of inhibitors or activators. It is a central part of biochemistry, since enzymes catalyse nearly all reactions in living cells.

Michaelis–Menten kinetics

In 1913 Leonor Michaelis and Maud Menten proposed a model in which the enzyme (E) first binds substrate (S) reversibly to form an enzyme–substrate complex (ES), which then breaks down to release product (P). Under the steady-state assumption developed by Briggs and Haldane, the reaction velocity follows the Michaelis–Menten equation: v = Vmax[S] / (Km + [S]). The Michaelis constant Km is the substrate concentration at which the rate is half-maximal, and Vmax is the maximum rate approached at saturating substrate.

Catalytic efficiency

The turnover number kcat describes how many substrate molecules one enzyme molecule converts per second at saturation. The ratio kcat/Km, the specificity constant, measures catalytic efficiency; values near the diffusion limit (108–109 M−1s−1) denote "catalytically perfect" enzymes such as triose phosphate isomerase.

Inhibition

Enzyme inhibitors are classified as competitive (binding the active site, raising apparent Km), non-competitive, uncompetitive, and mixed. Inhibition kinetics underpin much of drug design: many pharmaceuticals, from statins to HIV protease inhibitors, act as enzyme inhibitors. Graphically, Lineweaver–Burk double-reciprocal plots were historically used to extract constants and diagnose inhibition type, though non-linear regression is now preferred.
