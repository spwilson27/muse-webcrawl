Anagnorisis

Anagnorisis (from Ancient Greek ἀναγνώρισις, "recognition") is a moment in a play or other fictional work when a character makes a critical discovery about their own identity or situation. The term was introduced by Aristotle in his Poetics, where he defined it as "a change from ignorance to knowledge, producing love or hate between the persons destined by the poet for good or bad fortune." Aristotle regarded the finest anagnorisis as one coinciding with peripeteia, a reversal of fortune.

Examples

The classic example is Sophocles' Oedipus Rex, in which Oedipus discovers that he himself is the murderer he seeks, and that he has killed his father and married his mother; the recognition and the reversal arrive together and precipitate the catastrophe. Other famous recognition scenes include Odysseus revealing himself in the Odyssey (where recognitions are often by tokens such as his scar), the discovery of parentage in Greek New Comedy and Shakespearean romance, and Othello's realization of Iago's deception and Desdemona's innocence, which comes too late.

Comedy and other forms

In comedy, anagnorisis typically resolves the plot happily — long-lost relatives are identified and marriages become possible — while in tragedy it usually brings catastrophe. The device persists in modern drama, novels, and film, often as the "reveal" of a hidden identity or relationship.
