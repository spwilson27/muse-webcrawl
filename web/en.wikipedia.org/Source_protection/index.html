Source protection

Source protection
FieldMedia law, journalistic ethics
ConceptConfidentiality of journalists' sources
Key US caseBranzburg v. Hayes (1972)
InstrumentsShield laws; Article 10 ECHR jurisprudence

Source protection, also called the confidentiality of sources or reporter's privilege, is the principle that journalists should not be compelled to reveal the identity of confidential sources. It is recognised in the professional ethics of journalism and, to varying degrees, in the law of many democracies, on the ground that whistleblowers and informants would be deterred—and public-interest reporting chilled—if anonymity could not be promised.

Legal status

In the United States, the Supreme Court held in Branzburg v. Hayes (1972) that the First Amendment does not exempt reporters from grand jury subpoenas, but most states have enacted shield laws, and federal courts have recognised qualified protections. In Europe, the European Court of Human Rights held in Goodwin v. United Kingdom (1996) that ordering a journalist to disclose a source violates Article 10's freedom of expression absent an overriding public interest. Many countries extend protection to unpublished notes and outtakes as well as identities.

Famous cases

The Watergate investigation rested on the anonymous source "Deep Throat", revealed in 2005 as FBI official Mark Felt. In 2005 New York Times reporter Judith Miller spent 85 days in jail for contempt rather than reveal a source in the Valerie Plame affair. Digital-era source protection has expanded to include secure communications tools, such as encrypted messaging and SecureDrop submission systems operated by news organisations.

Limits

Protection is rarely absolute: courts commonly balance it against compelling needs in criminal trials, national security, or preventing serious harm, and standards vary widely between jurisdictions, including some where journalists face routine compulsion.
