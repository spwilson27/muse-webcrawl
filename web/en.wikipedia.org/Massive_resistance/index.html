Massive resistance

Massive resistance
Type: Political strategy
Context: Opposition to school desegregation in the U.S. South
Period: 1954–1960s
Key figure: Senator Harry F. Byrd Sr. of Virginia

Massive resistance was a strategy declared by U.S. Senator Harry F. Byrd Sr. of Virginia in 1956 to unite white politicians and leaders in the Southern United States in resistance to the desegregation of public schools and other public facilities, which the Supreme Court had ordered in Brown v. Board of Education (1954). The strategy called on Southern states to pass laws and constitutional amendments designed to block, delay or circumvent integration, under slogans such as "interposition" and "nullification".

The Stanley Plan and school closings

In Virginia, the General Assembly adopted the Stanley Plan in 1956, which required the governor to close any school ordered to integrate and provided state tuition grants for private "segregation academies". Schools were shut in Charlottesville, Norfolk and Front Royal in 1958. Most notoriously, Prince Edward County closed its entire public school system from 1959 to 1964 rather than integrate, leaving Black children without public education for five years, until the Supreme Court ordered the schools reopened in Griffin v. County School Board of Prince Edward County (1964).

Decline

Federal courts struck down the core laws of massive resistance in January 1959, and business leaders, fearing economic damage, increasingly pressed for token compliance. Although active defiance faded by the mid-1960s, the movement shaped Southern politics for a generation, delayed desegregation across the region and left lasting educational harm in communities like Prince Edward County.
