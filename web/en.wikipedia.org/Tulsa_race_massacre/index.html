Tulsa race massacre

Greenwood burning during the massacre, May 31 – June 1, 1921

The Tulsa race massacre took place on May 31 and June 1, 1921, when mobs of white residents, some of them deputised by city officials, attacked black residents and destroyed homes and businesses in the Greenwood District of Tulsa, Oklahoma. The event is considered one of the single worst incidents of racial violence in American history.

Background

Greenwood was a prosperous African American community, sometimes referred to as "Black Wall Street" because of its thriving businesses, hotels, theatres, doctors' offices and shops. Oklahoma at the time was a segregated state with a strong Ku Klux Klan presence, and racial tensions were high.

The massacre

The violence began after Dick Rowland, a young Black shoeshiner, was accused of assaulting Sarah Page, a white elevator operator, and a sensationalised newspaper report inflamed tensions. A lynch mob gathered at the courthouse; armed Black residents, including many World War I veterans, arrived to protect Rowland, and a confrontation followed. Over the next hours, white mobs looted and burned Greenwood. More than 35 square blocks were destroyed, more than 800 people were admitted to hospitals, and as many as 300 people are estimated to have died. Thousands of Black residents were left homeless or detained in internment camps; no one was ever prosecuted for the violence.

Aftermath and remembrance

The massacre was largely omitted from official histories for decades. Survivors rebuilt much of Greenwood, but the community never fully recovered, and insurance claims were denied. A state commission in 2001 documented the event and recommended reparations. The centennial in 2021 brought renewed national attention, archaeological searches for mass graves, and continuing legal efforts on behalf of survivors and descendants.
