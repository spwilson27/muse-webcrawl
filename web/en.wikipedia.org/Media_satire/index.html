Media satire

Media satire is a form of satire directed at the media themselves — their practices, biases, formats, and conventions. It encompasses works that parody news reporting, journalism, television formats, advertising, and social media in order to expose or ridicule perceived failures such as sensationalism, bias, superficiality, and the blurring of news and entertainment.

Satirical news programmes mimic the form of real broadcasts to critique media and politics.

Forms

Media satire appears in many formats:

  • Satirical news — fake-news outlets and television programmes that imitate real journalism, such as The Onion, The Daily Show, and Britain's The Day Today and Brass Eye.
  • Parody of formats — works that satirize talk shows, reality television, documentaries, and current-affairs formats.
  • Films and novels — such as Network (1976), which satirized television's pursuit of ratings, and Wag the Dog (1997), about manufactured news.
  • Cartoons and columns — satirical cartoons commenting on the press itself.

Function and effects

Scholars of media studies note that media satire can serve as a form of media criticism accessible to broad audiences, promoting media literacy and skepticism toward news framing. At the same time, researchers debate whether political satire informs or merely entertains, and whether satirical "fake news" risks being mistaken for genuine reporting — a concern that has grown in the era of social media misinformation. The critic Neil Postman argued in Amusing Ourselves to Death (1985) that television had turned public discourse into entertainment, a thesis frequently echoed by media satire itself.
