Inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry

Inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) is a type of mass spectrometry that uses an inductively coupled plasma to ionize the sample. It atomizes the sample and creates atomic and small polyatomic ions, which are then detected. It is known for its ability to detect metals and several non-metals in liquid samples at very low concentrations — down to parts per trillion — and to measure isotope ratios.

Principles

A solution sample is nebulized into an argon plasma sustained by a radio-frequency induction coil, reaching temperatures of roughly 6,000–10,000 K, which dry, vaporize, atomize, and ionize the analytes. Ions are extracted through sampler and skimmer cones into a vacuum system, focused by ion optics, separated by mass-to-charge ratio — typically with a quadrupole mass filter, or by sector-field or time-of-flight analyzers — and counted by an electron multiplier detector.

Interferences and advances

Polyatomic ions formed in the plasma (for example 40Ar35Cl+ overlapping 75As+) create spectral interferences. Collision and reaction cells, high-resolution instruments, and cool plasma conditions are used to reduce them. Laser ablation sampling (LA-ICP-MS) allows direct analysis of solids, used extensively in geochronology such as U–Pb dating of zircons.

Applications

  • Environmental monitoring of trace metals in water and soil
  • Geochemistry and isotope geochemistry
  • Clinical and pharmaceutical analysis
  • Semiconductor manufacturing quality control
  • Forensic and food-safety testing
