Mihirakula

Mihirakula (c. 502–542 CE) was a ruler of the Alchon Huns (Hephthalite-related Huna) in northwestern India, the second and most powerful king of his line after his father Toramana. From his capital at Sagala (modern Sialkot), his empire at its height extended over much of present-day Afghanistan, Pakistan and north India as far as Gwalior.

Sources and reputation

Mihirakula is remembered in Indian and Chinese sources as a fierce persecutor of Buddhism. The Chinese pilgrim Xuanzang (writing a century later) describes his hostility to Buddhist monasteries, and Kalhana's Rajatarangini portrays him as cruel. He was himself a devotee of Shiva, and his coins bear Shaivite symbols such as the bull and trident alongside the Iranian sun-name Mihira ("Mithra").

Defeat and decline

Around 528 CE Mihirakula was defeated by an Indian confederacy — credited variously to the Gupta emperor Narasimhagupta Baladitya and to Yashodharman of Malwa, whose Mandasor inscriptions boast of victory over him. After his defeat his power contracted to the northwest, in Kashmir and Gandhara, where the Gwalior inscription of his 15th regnal year still records a temple to Surya built earlier in his reign. The Huna power in India collapsed within a generation of his death.
