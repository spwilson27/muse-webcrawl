Celluloid

Celluloid
Type: Thermoplastic
Invented: 1869, John Wesley Hyatt
Composition: Nitrocellulose + camphor

Celluloid is a class of materials produced from nitrocellulose and camphor, generally considered the first thermoplastic. Developed in the 1860s–1870s, it was widely used for photographic and motion-picture film, billiard balls, combs, collars and toys before safer plastics replaced it.

History

Alexander Parkes created Parkesine in 1856. John Wesley Hyatt patented celluloid in 1870 while seeking an ivory substitute for billiard balls. Eastman adopted celluloid film base in 1889, enabling cinema.

Properties and hazards

Celluloid is mouldable and can imitate ivory, tortoiseshell and mother-of-pearl, but it is highly flammable and decomposes over time, releasing nitric acid. Most film production switched to cellulose acetate by the 1950s.

Legacy

The word became a metonym for cinema. Table-tennis balls remained celluloid until 2014. See plastic and film stock.
