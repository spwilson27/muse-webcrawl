Hawthorne effect

The Hawthorne effect is the phenomenon in which individuals modify their behaviour in response to being observed or to awareness that they are participating in a study. The term derives from studies conducted at the Western Electric Hawthorne Works in Cicero, Illinois, between 1924 and 1932, and is a foundational concept in industrial psychology and research methodology, illustrating a form of reactivity that can threaten the validity of experiments.

The Hawthorne studies

The original research examined how working conditions affected productivity. In the illumination experiments (1924–1927), output rose in both test and control groups even when lighting was changed in unexpected ways. In the Relay Assembly Test Room (1927–1932), a small group of women assembling telephone relays increased their output through most changes in rest breaks, hours and incentives — and even when conditions reverted. Elton Mayo and his Harvard colleagues interpreted the results as evidence that social factors, group norms and the attention of investigators mattered more than physical conditions. The later Bank Wiring Observation Room study documented informal group norms that restricted output.

Interpretation and criticism

Henry A. Landsberger coined the term "Hawthorne effect" in 1958 while re-analysing the data. Later scholars have questioned the original conclusions: re-analysis of recovered data by economists Steven Levitt and John List (2011) found the productivity patterns were largely consistent with ordinary weekly rhythms rather than a pure observation effect, and critics have noted confounding variables such as feedback, small-group incentives and the replacement of uncooperative workers. The episode is now commonly used to teach the importance of demand characteristics, experimenter effects and careful control groups.

Applications

The concept is invoked in clinical trials, education research, workplace monitoring and software analytics — anywhere observation itself may change outcomes. Related ideas include the observer effect, the placebo effect and John Henry effects in control groups. Researchers mitigate the Hawthorne effect through unobtrusive measurement, blinding and longer observation periods that let behaviour normalise.
