Distributed amplifier

Type: Electronic amplifier circuit

Known for: Very wide bandwidth

Invented: Patented by William S. Percival, 1936

Applications: Oscilloscopes, radar, optical communications

Distributed amplifiers are circuit designs that incorporate transmission line theory into traditional amplifier design to obtain a larger gain–bandwidth product than is realizable by conventional circuits. Instead of a single amplifying device, several devices are arranged along artificial transmission lines so that their parasitic capacitances are absorbed into the lines themselves.

Operating principle

In a distributed amplifier, the input signal travels along a transmission line (the gate or grid line) that is tapped at intervals by the inputs of successive amplifying devices, such as vacuum tubes or field-effect transistors. Each device amplifies the signal onto a second transmission line (the drain or plate line). By arranging the propagation delays of the two lines to be equal, the amplified contributions add in phase in the forward direction while cancelling in reverse, producing gain over a very wide frequency range — potentially several octaves or more.

History

The design was first patented in 1936 by William S. Percival of EMI in Britain, but remained largely unused until the Second World War, when it was needed for radar and electronic countermeasures. After the war the topology was widely used in the vertical amplifiers of high-performance oscilloscopes, notably by Tektronix.

Modern use

With the advent of monolithic microwave integrated circuits (MMICs), distributed amplifiers became practical in gallium arsenide and CMOS technologies. They are used as broadband amplifiers in optical fibre communication systems, instrumentation, and electronic warfare receivers, where bandwidths of tens of gigahertz are required. The trade-offs are higher power consumption, larger area, and lower efficiency compared with narrowband designs.
