Two truths doctrine

The two truths doctrine in Buddhism differentiates between two levels of truth: conventional truth (Sanskrit saṃvṛti-satya) and ultimate truth (paramārtha-satya). According to the doctrine, the everyday world of persons, objects, and events is truly described by conventional truth, while ultimate truth reveals that all phenomena are empty (śūnya) of inherent, independent existence.

Origins and development

Early Buddhist texts distinguish teachings expressed in conventional language from those expressing the Buddha's direct insight. The doctrine was given systematic form by the Madhyamaka school, especially Nāgārjuna (c. 2nd century CE), who wrote in the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā that the Buddha's teaching relies on two truths: worldly conventional truth and truth in the highest sense. Crucially, Nāgārjuna held that the two truths are not two separate realities: emptiness itself does not exist independently, and without relying on convention, the ultimate cannot be taught, and without understanding the ultimate, liberation (nirvana) cannot be attained.

Interpretations

Different schools developed the doctrine in different directions. In Yogācāra, the two truths relate to the distinction between imagined, dependent, and perfected natures. In Tibetan Buddhism, the Prāsaṅgika and Svātantrika sub-schools of Madhyamaka debated how conventional phenomena function and how emptiness should be established. The doctrine also influenced East Asian Buddhism, where Tiantai thinker Zhiyi expanded it into a "three truths" scheme — emptiness, provisional existence, and the middle — held to be perfectly integrated.
