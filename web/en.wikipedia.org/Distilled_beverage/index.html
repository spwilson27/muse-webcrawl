Distilled beverage

A distilled beverage, spirit, or liquor is an alcoholic drink produced by distillation of grains, fruits, vegetables or sugar that have already gone through alcoholic fermentation. Distilled beverages include whisky, vodka, gin, rum, tequila, brandy and baijiu. Their alcohol content is typically far higher than that of beer or wine, usually between 20% and 50% alcohol by volume, although some reach much higher levels.

History

The distillation of alcohol was practised in China and the Islamic world by the early medieval period; the Persian alchemist Jabir ibn Hayyan (Geber) described early stills. Distilled aqua vitae spread through Europe in the 12th–15th centuries, initially as medicine. By the early modern period whisky, brandy and gin were commercial products, and excise taxation of spirits became a major source of state revenue — and of smuggling and illicit distilling.

Production

A fermented mash or wine is heated in a still; alcohol vaporises at a lower temperature than water, so the vapour is enriched in ethanol, then condensed. Pot stills operate in batches and retain more flavour compounds; column (continuous) stills allow higher purity. Distillers discard the initial "heads" and final "tails", keeping the "heart" of the run. Many spirits are then aged in wooden casks, diluted to bottling strength, or flavoured with botanicals, as in gin.

Terminology and measurement

Strength is expressed as alcohol by volume (ABV) in most of the world, or as "proof" in the United States (twice the ABV). Rules of origin govern names such as Scotch whisky, Cognac and Tequila.

Health and society

Distilled beverages are associated with higher risks of intoxication and alcohol-related harm per serving than weaker drinks, and are regulated through licensing, taxation and minimum-age laws in most countries.
