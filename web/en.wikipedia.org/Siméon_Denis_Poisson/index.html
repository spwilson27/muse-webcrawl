Baron Siméon Denis Poisson (21 June 1781 – 25 April 1840) was a French mathematician and physicist who worked on statistics, complex analysis, partial differential equations, the variational calculus, analytical mechanics, electricity and magnetism, thermodynamics, elasticity, and fluid mechanics. Moreover, he predicted the arrival of the Poisson distribution when studying a well-known problem of probability theory. He was a professor at the École Polytechnique and one of the most influential French scientists of his generation.

Life

Poisson was born in Pithiviers, in the Loiret department, the son of a soldier. In 1798 he entered the École Polytechnique in Paris as first in his class, and immediately attracted the notice of his teachers, among them Joseph-Louis Lagrange and Pierre-Simon Laplace, whose friendship he retained for life. On leaving the school he was appointed répétiteur there, a post he had in fact occupied while still a pupil, and in 1806 he succeeded Jean-Baptiste Joseph Fourier as professor when Fourier left for Grenoble.

Contributions

In pure mathematics Poisson's most important work concerned definite integrals and Fourier series, paving the way for the researches of Dirichlet and Riemann. His name is attached to the Poisson integral formula, Poisson's equation in potential theory, the Poisson brackets in differential equations, and Poisson's ratio in elasticity. In probability theory he introduced the distribution that bears his name in his work Recherches sur la probabilité des jugements (1837), applying it to the probability of convictions by juries.

In mathematical physics he developed the theory of electricity and magnetism, including the equation relating the electrostatic potential to the charge distribution, now called Poisson's equation, a generalisation of Laplace's equation. He also famously predicted, as an intended refutation of the wave theory of light, the bright spot at the centre of a circular shadow — the Poisson spot — whose experimental confirmation by François Arago became a celebrated vindication of Augustin-Jean Fresnel's theory.
