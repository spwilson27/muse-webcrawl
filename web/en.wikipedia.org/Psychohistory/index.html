Psychohistory

Psychohistory is the study of the psychological motivations of historical events, combining the insights of psychotherapy with the research methodology of the social sciences to examine the emotional origins of the behaviour of individuals, groups and nations in the past. It has generated controversy within academia, where many historians regard its methods as speculative.

Origins

The field draws on Sigmund Freud's applied psychoanalysis, including his study of Leonardo da Vinci, and on Erik Erikson's psychobiographies such as Young Man Luther (1958). Lloyd deMause, founder of the Journal of Psychohistory in 1973, developed one of the field's most influential — and contested — theories, proposing that the history of childhood is central to understanding historical change.

Methods and criticism

Psychohistorians analyse diaries, speeches, child-rearing practices and group fantasies for evidence of unconscious motivations. Critics argue that psychohistory often lacks falsifiability, applies modern psychological categories anachronistically, and reduces complex social causes to individual pathology. Defenders respond that all historical interpretation involves assumptions about human motivation and that psychohistory makes those assumptions explicit.
