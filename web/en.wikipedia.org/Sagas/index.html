Sagas are prose narratives written in Old Norse, principally in medieval Iceland, about historical and legendary subjects: kings, heroes, saints, Icelandic families and the settlement of the North Atlantic world. The word saga derives from the Old Norse verb segja, "to say, to tell." The sagas are among the most celebrated achievements of medieval European literature.

Categories

Sagas of Icelanders (family sagas)

The Íslendingasögur recount the lives of Icelandic settlers and their descendants during the "Saga Age" (c. 870–1030): feuds, voyages, love and law. Masterpieces such as Njáls saga, Egils saga (attributed to Snorri Sturluson) and Laxdæla saga combine terse dialogue, genealogies and a fatalistic tone, and preserve information — of debated reliability — about Viking Age society.

Kings' sagas and other genres

The konungasögur narrate the lives of Norwegian and Danish kings; the greatest collection is Snorri Sturluson's Heimskringla (c. 1230). Other genres include legendary sagas (fornaldarsögur) set in a mythic past, such as the Völsunga saga; bishops' and saints' sagas; chivalric romances (riddarasögur); and contemporary sagas describing 12th- and 13th-century Icelandic politics, notably the Sturlunga compilation.

Origins and transmission

The sagas drew on oral tradition, skaldic verse and earlier written sources, and survive mainly in vellum manuscripts such as Möðruvallabók. Long considered straightforward history, they are now read as literary works that blend fact, memory and invention.

Influence

Sagas shaped modern knowledge of the Viking Age and influenced writers from William Morris and J. R. R. Tolkien to contemporary historical novelists; they remain central to Icelandic national identity. The related Icelandic word gives English "saga" for any long, involved story.
