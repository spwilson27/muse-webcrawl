SDS-PAGE

SDS-PAGE (sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis) is a discontinuous electrophoretic system developed by Ulrich K. Laemmli that is used to separate proteins according to their electrophoretic mobility, which in this method depends primarily on molecular weight. It is one of the most widely used techniques in biochemistry and molecular biology for analysing protein mixtures and estimating protein sizes.

Principle

Samples are boiled with the anionic detergent sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) and a reducing agent such as β-mercaptoethanol or dithiothreitol. SDS denatures proteins, breaks non-covalent interactions, and coats them with a roughly uniform negative charge per unit mass, while the reducing agent cleaves disulfide bonds. When an electric field is applied across a porous polyacrylamide gel, the coated proteins migrate toward the anode at rates inversely related to the logarithm of their molecular mass.

The Laemmli system

The discontinuous gel consists of a low-percentage stacking gel (pH 6.8) layered over a higher-percentage resolving gel (pH 8.8). Differences in pH and pore size concentrate proteins into sharp bands in the stacking gel before they enter the resolving gel, where separation by size occurs. Acrylamide concentration is chosen according to the size range of interest; gradient gels separate a wide range at once.

Detection and applications

After electrophoresis, proteins are visualised by staining with Coomassie Brilliant Blue or silver stain, or transferred to a membrane for Western blotting. SDS-PAGE is used to assess protein purity, determine subunit composition, estimate molecular weights against standards, monitor expression and purification, and as the first dimension of two-dimensional gel electrophoresis.
