Subordination (linguistics)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Example tree of a sentence containing a subordinate clause.

In linguistics, subordination is a principle of the hierarchical organisation of linguistic units and the ranking of units on a scale of subordination. Most prominently, it describes the embedding of one clause inside another, producing a complex sentence in which a subordinate (dependent) clause is governed by a main (independent) clause.

Subordinate clauses

Subordinate clauses typically function as arguments or adjuncts of the main clause: as subjects or objects ("That he left surprised us"), as modifiers of nouns (relative clauses, "the book that I read"), or as adverbial modifiers of time, reason, condition or concession ("Because it rained, we stayed home"). They are often introduced by subordinating conjunctions (such as because, although, if, when) or complementizers.

Contrasts

Subordination contrasts with coordination, in which units of equal rank are linked (by and, but, or), and with parataxis, in which clauses are simply juxtaposed. Subordinate clauses may themselves contain further subordinate clauses, yielding recursive structures characteristic of human language.

Cross-linguistic notes

Languages mark subordination in varied ways: non-finite verb forms (infinitives, participles, converbs), special subordinating morphology, verb-final subordinate word order (as in German), or dedicated complementizer particles.
