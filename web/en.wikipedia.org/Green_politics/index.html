Green politics

Green politics, or ecopolitics, is a political ideology that aims to foster an ecologically sustainable society often, but not always, rooted in environmentalism, nonviolence, social justice and grassroots democracy. It began taking shape in the western world in the 1970s; since then, green parties have developed and established themselves in many countries across the globe and have achieved some electoral success.

Supporters of green politics share many ideas with the ecology, conservation, environmentalism, feminism, and peace movements. In addition to democracy and ecological issues, green politics is concerned with civil liberties, social justice, nonviolence, and sometimes localism.

Core tenets

The Global Greens Charter of 2001 sets out six guiding principles: ecological wisdom, social justice, participatory democracy, nonviolence, sustainability, and respect for diversity. Earlier, the German Greens articulated the "Four Pillars" of green politics — ecology, social responsibility, grassroots democracy, and nonviolence — which were adopted by many green parties around the world. Greens commonly advocate renewable energy, public transport, protection of biodiversity, and measures against climate change, and many support policies such as carbon taxes, a basic income, and the precautionary principle.

History

The first political party ever to campaign on a predominantly environmental platform is often considered to be the United Tasmania Group, formed in 1972 to oppose the flooding of Lake Pedder. In Europe, the German Green Party (Die Grünen), founded in 1980, became the most influential early green party, entering the Bundestag in 1983 and joining a federal government as coalition partner in 1998. The Global Greens federation, founded in 2001, coordinates green parties in more than 90 countries.

Criticism

Critics have charged green movements with being single-issue, internally divided between "fundamentalist" and "realist" wings, and in some cases hostile to economic growth and technological solutions. Greens respond that ecology necessarily encompasses economics and social organization rather than being a single issue.
