Battle of Ipsus

The Battle of Ipsus was fought between the Diadochi, the rival successors of Alexander the Great, in 301 BC near the village of Ipsus in Phrygia (modern Turkey). The alliance of Seleucus I Nicator, Lysimachus and Cassander crushed the forces of Antigonus I Monophthalmus and his son Demetrius Poliorcetes. Antigonus, seeking to reunify Alexander's empire, was killed, ending any realistic hope of restoring its unity and confirming the partition of the Hellenistic world.

Course of the battle

Demetrius, commanding the Antigonid right-wing cavalry, routed the cavalry of Seleucus's son Antiochus but pursued too far. On returning, he found his path back to the battlefield blocked by a line of Seleucus's Indian war elephants — more than 400, obtained from Chandragupta Maurya in exchange for eastern territories. Meanwhile the allied phalanx pressed the Antigonid infantry, many of whom defected or surrendered to Seleucus. Antigonus, 81 years old, stood his ground expecting his son's return and was killed by javelins. Demetrius escaped with part of his force, and the victors divided Antigonus's Asian realm: Seleucus took Syria and eastern Anatolia, Lysimachus western Asia Minor, while Ptolemy seized Coele-Syria — the settlement that fixed the map of the Hellenistic kingdoms.
