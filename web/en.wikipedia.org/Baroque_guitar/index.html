Baroque guitar


An ornate Baroque guitar with intricate soundhole rosette.

The Baroque guitar (c. 1600–1750) is a string instrument with five courses of gut strings and moveable gut frets. The first (highest) course sometimes used a single string, but usually doubled as well. It replaced the Renaissance guitar and was in turn succeeded by the modern guitar in the late 18th century.

Construction

Baroque guitars were often lavishly decorated with inlaid wood, ivory, and mother-of-pearl, and elaborate carved rosettes in the soundhole. The body was smaller and shallower than a modern classical guitar, with a vaulted or flat back. Strings were of gut, tied to a fixed bridge, and frets of gut were tied around the neck, allowing adjustment.

Tuning and technique

Re-entrant tunings were common, in which one or both strings of the lower courses were tuned an octave higher than expected, giving the instrument a bright, campanella-like effect when scales crossed courses. Playing techniques combined strumming (rasgueado, battute) with plucked counterpoint (punteado). Composers used the alfabeto system of chord notation for strummed accompaniment.

Repertoire

Important composers and authors for the instrument include Francesco Corbetta, who brought the guitar to prominence at the French court, Gaspar Sanz in Spain, Robert de Visée, and Santiago de Murcia. The instrument was popular across Spain, Italy, France, and Spanish America, both in art music and popular dance accompaniment.
