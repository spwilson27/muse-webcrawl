The mixing desk as musical instrument is central to dub

Dub is a musical style that grew out of reggae in Jamaica in the late 1960s and early 1970s. It is commonly considered a subgenre of reggae, though its development is stylistically distinct: dub consists predominantly of remixes of existing recordings, in which engineers manipulate the master tapes — dropping vocals in and out, emphasizing the drum and bass (the "riddim"), and applying studio effects such as echo, reverb and delay in real time.

History

Dub emerged from the Jamaican sound system culture, in which selectors demanded exclusive instrumental versions of songs for their deejays to toast over. Ruddy Redwood is credited with one of the first accidental dub mixes in 1967. Engineer King Tubby (Osbourne Ruddock) became the style's defining pioneer, building custom electronics and treating the mixing board as an instrument. Other foundational figures include Lee "Scratch" Perry, Errol Thompson, Scientist and Prince Jammy. Landmark albums include Perry's Super Ape (1976) and King Tubby's collaborations with Augustus Pablo.

Characteristics

Classic dub foregrounds bass weight and space: the lead vocal of the original song is typically reduced to fragments and echoes, while the rhythm section is pushed forward and subjected to dramatic drops, filters and reverberant effects. Live dub mixing, often credited on record sleeves to the engineer as artist, made the producer a creative figure in his own right.

Influence

Dub's remix techniques profoundly shaped popular music: hip hop pioneers in the Bronx drew on Jamaican sound system practice; British post-punk, jungle, garage and dubstep absorbed its bass culture; and its studio methods underpin modern remix and electronic dance music production generally.
