Scattering

In physics, scattering is a wide range of physical processes where moving particles or radiation of some form, such as light or sound, are forced to deviate from a straight trajectory by localized non-uniformities (including particles and radiation) in the medium through which they pass. In conventional use, this also includes deviation of reflected radiation from the angle predicted by the law of reflection. Reflections of radiation that undergo scattering are often called diffuse reflections and unscattered reflections specular (mirror-like) reflections. Originally, the term was confined to light scattering (going back at least as far as Isaac Newton in the 17th century), but it is now applied to many other phenomena.

Single and multiple scattering

Scattering can be single, when a photon or particle interacts with one localized scatterer, or multiple, when it is scattered many times before detection; multiple scattering randomizes direction and underlies the milky appearance of clouds and the operation of integrating spheres. It can also be elastic, with no change in the energy of the scattered radiation, or inelastic, where energy is exchanged with the medium.

Examples

Rayleigh scattering, the scattering of light by particles much smaller than its wavelength, explains the blue colour of the sky and the red of sunsets. Mie scattering describes the regime where particle size is comparable to the wavelength. Rutherford scattering of alpha particles by gold nuclei revealed the atomic nucleus. Compton scattering of X-rays by electrons demonstrated the particle nature of light. In particle physics, scattering experiments—described by cross sections and scattering amplitudes—remain the primary tool for probing the structure of matter, formalized in scattering theory and the S-matrix. Sound and ocean waves scatter from bubbles, sediment, and rough surfaces; neutron and X-ray scattering probe the structure of materials in condensed-matter physics.

Theory

The mathematics of scattering uses concepts such as the differential cross-section, which gives the probability of scattering into a given solid angle, and phase functions describing angular distributions. In quantum mechanics scattering is treated with partial-wave expansions and the Born approximation; in classical wave theory, solutions of the Helmholtz and Maxwell equations around obstacles give the scattered field. Scattering processes are characterized by a scattering coefficient and mean free path in transport theory.
