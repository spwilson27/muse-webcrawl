Notary public

OccupationPublic officer / legal official
DutiesWitnessing signatures, administering oaths, certifying documents
Appointed byVaries by jurisdiction (e.g., US state governments)

A notary public (also rendered public notary) is a public officer constituted by law to serve the public in non-contentious matters, usually by witnessing signatures, administering oaths and affirmations, taking affidavits and acknowledgements, and certifying copies, in order to deter fraud. The office descends from the scribes and notaries of Roman law.

Common-law and civil-law notaries

The role differs sharply between legal traditions. In common-law jurisdictions such as the United States, England, and India, a notary is typically a lay official (in the US, commissioned by a state) whose powers are limited mainly to authenticating signatures and oaths; American notaries are generally not lawyers and may not give legal advice. In civil-law countries, by contrast, the notaire or Notar is a highly trained lawyer who drafts and authenticates legal instruments — such as conveyances, wills, and marriage contracts — which carry special evidentiary force.

Notarial acts

Typical acts include acknowledgements, jurats, oaths, copy certifications, and protests of negotiable instruments. Notaries authenticate documents with a signature and an official seal or stamp. Documents notarised in one country may be used in another under the Hague Apostille Convention. In recent decades some jurisdictions have authorised electronic notarisation and remote online notarisation by video conference.
