Grigori Yefimovich Rasputin (21 January [OS 9 January] 1869 – 30 December [OS 17 December] 1916) was a Russian mystic and faith healer. He is best known for having befriended the imperial family of Nicholas II, the last Emperor of Russia, through whom he gained considerable influence in late Imperial Russia. Born a peasant in Siberia, Rasputin's religious conversion in the 1890s led him to wander as a pilgrim (strannik), acquire a reputation as a holy man (starets), and enter St. Petersburg society by 1905.

Early life

Rasputin was born in the village of Pokrovskoye on the Tura River in Siberia, one of several children of a prosperous peasant farmer. After a religious experience around 1897 — linked in some accounts to a vision of the Virgin Mary — he became a wandering pilgrim, traveling to Mount Athos and Jerusalem and joining (formally or not) the milieu of the banned Khlysts sect, with which he was later (probably unfairly) associated. His preaching and reputed gifts of healing and prophecy drew clergy attention, including that of Bishop Theofan, who introduced him to the imperial court.

Influence at court

Rasputin met Nicholas II and Empress Alexandra in 1905 and became indispensable after he appeared to relieve the suffering of the hemophiliac Tsarevich Alexei — most famously during the 1912 crisis at Spała, when the boy's bleeding stopped after Rasputin's telegram urged the tsarina to let the doctors withdraw and trust in prayer (modern speculation holds that stopping the aspirin then administered aided recovery). Alexandra's devotion ("Our Friend") made Rasputin a channel of court influence and a magnet for petitioners and resentment.

War years and scandal

During World War I, with Nicholas at the front from 1915, Alexandra's reliance on Rasputin coincided with a "ministerial leapfrog" of appointments and dismissals that scandalized the Duma and press. Rumors — many exaggerated or fabricated — portrayed him as a debauched, pro-German puppet-master, fatally corroding the monarchy's prestige. Police reports documented heavy drinking and womanizing, though historians such as Douglas Smith argue the darkest charges (espionage, systematic orgies, control of policy) were propaganda.

Assassination

On the night of 29–30 December 1916 (OS 16–17 December), a group of nobles led by Prince Felix Yusupov and Grand Duke Dmitri Pavlovich murdered Rasputin at the Yusupov Palace on the Moika. Yusupov's famous account — poisoned cakes and wine failing to kill him, followed by shooting, beating and drowning — is embroidered: the autopsy evidence indicates Rasputin was killed by gunshots (a final close-range shot to the forehead) and that the drowning story is doubtful. The killers were exiled; the monarchy they hoped to save fell two months later in the February Revolution.

Legacy

Rasputin endures as a symbol of decadence and occult influence over power — the "Mad Monk" of countless books, films, and songs (including Boney M.'s 1978 hit "Rasputin"). His children emigrated; his daughter Maria became a lion tamer and memoirist in the United States. His alleged prophecy letters and relic stories (including a notorious preserved "member") remain staples of popular myth rather than history.
