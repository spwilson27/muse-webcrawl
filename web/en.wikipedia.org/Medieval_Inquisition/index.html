Medieval Inquisition

An inquisitorial tribunal in medieval Languedoc.

The Medieval Inquisition was a series of inquisitions (Catholic Church bodies charged with suppressing heresy) from around 1184 until the 1230s and later. It was a response by the Roman Catholic Church to the growth of heretical movements in medieval Europe, above all Catharism in southern France and Waldensianism in France and Italy.

Origins

The Episcopal Inquisition, established in 1184 by Pope Lucius III's bull Ad abolendam, required bishops to investigate heresy in their dioceses. It proved uneven, and after the murder of the papal legate Pierre de Castelnau in 1208 and the Albigensian Crusade (1209–1229), the Church moved to a more centralised system.

The Papal Inquisition

In the 1230s Pope Gregory IX entrusted inquisitions to trained friars, mostly Dominicans and some Franciscans, who answered directly to the papacy. Inquisitors travelled through regions, preaching, granting a "period of grace" for voluntary confessions, and then conducting trials based on denunciations and witness testimony. Sentences ranged from pilgrimages, wearing crosses of cloth, and public penance, to imprisonment and — for the impenitent or relapsed — relaxation to the secular arm, which could impose burning at the stake.

Procedure and records

Inquisitorial procedure (inquisitio) allowed judges to initiate cases without a formal accuser. Names of witnesses were usually withheld from the accused, and legal representation was limited. Pope Innocent IV's bull Ad extirpanda (1252) permitted torture under restrictions. Inquisitors compiled meticulous registers; manuals such as Bernard Gui's Practica officii inquisitionis (c. 1323) and records like the interrogations preserved from Jacques Fournier (the future Pope Benedict XII) underpin modern scholarship, including the village study of Montaillou.

Legacy

The medieval inquisitions faded in much of Europe by the 15th century as episcopal and secular courts took over heresy prosecution. They are distinguished from the later, separate Spanish Inquisition (1478) and Roman Inquisition (1542). Historians continue to debate the scale of executions, with modern estimates generally far below the figures of popular legend.
