Asado

RegionSouthern Cone (Argentina, Uruguay, Paraguay, southern Brazil, Chile)
TypeBarbecue / social event
Main ingredientsBeef, chorizo, morcilla, offal
GrillParrilla

Asado (Spanish: [aˈsaðo]) is the technique and social event of having or attending a barbecue in various South American countries, especially Argentina, Paraguay, Uruguay, Chile and southern Brazil. An asado typically features a sequence of grilled meats cooked on a grill called a parrilla, and is as much a social ritual as a meal, often lasting several hours among family and friends.

Preparation

The fire, usually of wood or charcoal, is prepared in a firebox beside the grill, and embers are raked beneath the parrilla as needed. The person in charge of the grilling is the asador. Meat is typically salted — sometimes only with coarse salt — and cooked slowly. A traditional asado begins with offal and sausages (achuras): chorizo, morcilla (blood sausage), chinchulín (intestines), mollejas (sweetbreads) and riñones (kidneys), often served with provoleta, grilled provolone cheese. Larger cuts of beef follow, such as vacío (flank), tira de asado (short ribs), entraña (skirt steak) and lomo (tenderloin), accompanied by salads and red wine.

Cultural significance

Asado is deeply embedded in the culture of the Río de la Plata region, with roots in the gaucho traditions of the pampas, where cattle were plentiful. Sunday asados are a common family gathering, and the dish is often considered the national dish of Argentina and Uruguay.

Regional variants

In Chile, asados are common for Fiestas Patrias celebrations. In Paraguay, asado is a staple of weekend gatherings, frequently accompanied by sopa paraguaya and mandioca (yuca). In southern Brazil the equivalent tradition is the churrasco of Rio Grande do Sul.
