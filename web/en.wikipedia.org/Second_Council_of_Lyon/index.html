Second Council of Lyon

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The council met in Lyon Cathedral in 1274 under Pope Gregory X.

The Second Council of Lyon was the fourteenth ecumenical council of the Roman Catholic Church, convoked by Pope Gregory X and held in Lyon, France, from 7 May to 17 July 1274. It is recognized as an ecumenical council by the Catholic Church. The council was attended by about 560 bishops and abbots, as well as envoys from the Byzantine Empire and the Mongol Ilkhanate.

Purposes and proceedings

Pope Gregory X summoned the council to address three great concerns: the recovery of the Holy Land through a new crusade, the reunion of the Greek (Orthodox) and Latin churches, and the reform of the Church. The council promulgated constitutions on the crusade, including a tax on Church revenues to fund it, and enacted important reforms on papal elections.

Union with the Greeks

Envoys of the Byzantine Emperor Michael VIII Palaiologos accepted union with Rome, professing the Latin doctrine on the procession of the Holy Spirit and papal primacy — largely for political reasons, as Michael sought Western support against Charles of Anjou. This "Union of Lyon" proved deeply unpopular in Byzantium and was repudiated by Michael's successor Andronikos II in 1282.

Papal election reform

The council's most durable act was the constitution Ubi periculum, which formalized the papal conclave: cardinals were to be locked in seclusion, with progressively reduced food, until they elected a pope. The rule arose from the long and chaotic election of Gregory X himself, which had lasted nearly three years (1268–1271). The Franciscan theologian Bonaventure died during the council, and Thomas Aquinas died on his way to attend it.
