Calmodulin

Calmodulin (CaM) (an abbreviation for calcium-modulated protein) is a multifunctional intermediate calcium-binding messenger protein expressed in all eukaryotic cells. It is an intracellular target of the secondary messenger Ca²⁺, and its activation is required for the proper functioning of many crucial cellular processes.

Structure

Calmodulin is a small, highly conserved protein of 148 amino acids (about 16.7 kDa). It consists of two globular domains, each containing two EF-hand motifs, for a total of four Ca²⁺-binding sites, connected by a flexible central helix. In the absence of calcium it adopts a compact "closed" conformation; binding of Ca²⁺ exposes hydrophobic surfaces that allow it to wrap around and regulate target proteins. In humans it is encoded by three separate genes (CALM1, CALM2, CALM3) that produce an identical amino acid sequence.

Mechanism and targets

When intracellular calcium rises, Ca²⁺/calmodulin activates a wide array of enzymes and channels, including Ca²⁺/calmodulin-dependent protein kinases (CaMKs such as CaMKII, central to synaptic plasticity and memory), the phosphatase calcineurin (key to T-cell activation and the target of immunosuppressant drugs such as ciclosporin), nitric oxide synthases, phosphorylase kinase, myosin light-chain kinase, and various ion pumps and channels.

Clinical significance

Mutations in calmodulin genes cause calmodulinopathies — severe early-onset cardiac arrhythmias including long QT syndrome and catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia. Calmodulin is also essential to apoptosis, muscle contraction, metabolism, and neuronal signalling.
