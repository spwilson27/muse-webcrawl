Kundalini
Kundalini as a coiled serpent at the muladhara chakra
Sanskritकुण्डलिनी, kuṇḍalinī ("coiled one")
TraditionsTantra, Hatha yoga, Shaktism, Kundalini yoga
Location (dormant)Muladhara chakra, base of the spine
Goal of awakeningUnion with the divine; moksha (liberation)

In Hinduism, kundalini (Sanskrit: कुण्डलिनी, "coiled one") is a form of divine feminine energy — a manifestation of Shakti — believed to lie dormant, coiled like a serpent three and a half times around itself, at the base of the spine in the muladhara (root) chakra. Through spiritual practices its awakening and ascent through the chakras along the central channel (sushumna nadi) is said to culminate in enlightenment and liberation (moksha).

Concept and history

The concept developed within Tantric traditions of the first millennium CE and was elaborated in texts of hatha yoga, such as the Hatha Yoga Pradipika and the later Shat-chakra-nirupana, which described the system of channels (nadis), winds (prana) and energy centres (chakras). The awakened kundalini is said to pierce each chakra in turn — muladhara, svadhisthana, manipura, anahata, vishuddha and ajna — until it unites with Shiva at the sahasrara, the thousand-petalled lotus at the crown of the head.

Practices

Traditional methods of awakening include guru-initiated transmission (shaktipat), mantra, pranayama (breath control), asana, bandhas (body locks), mudras and meditation. The 20th century saw kundalini popularised in the West: Carl Jung lectured on its psychology in 1932, and from 1968 Yogi Bhajan taught a systematised "Kundalini Yoga" in the United States combining postures, rapid breathing (breath of fire), chanting and meditation. Sahaja Yoga, founded by Nirmala Srivastava in 1970, teaches a method of spontaneous awakening through self-realisation.

Experiences and cautions

Accounts of awakening describe heat, currents of energy along the spine, spontaneous movements (kriyas), bliss and expanded awareness; traditions also warn that premature or forced awakening can cause physical and psychological distress, a cluster of difficulties sometimes called "kundalini syndrome" in modern literature. Classical texts emphasise purification, ethical preparation and the guidance of a teacher.
