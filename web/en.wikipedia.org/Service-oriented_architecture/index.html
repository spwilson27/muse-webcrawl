Service-oriented architecture

In SOA, discrete services communicate over a network, often via an enterprise service bus

Service-oriented architecture (SOA) is a style of software design where services are provided to the other components by application components, through a communication protocol over a network. An SOA service is a discrete unit of functionality that can be accessed remotely and acted upon and updated independently, such as retrieving a credit card statement online. SOA is intended to allow different applications to exchange data and participate in business processes regardless of the operating systems or programming languages underlying them.

Principles

Core principles of SOA include loose coupling between services, service contracts that define how services interact, abstraction of underlying implementation, reusability, autonomy, statelessness and discoverability of services through a registry. In the 2000s, SOA implementations were commonly built on web services standards such as SOAP, WSDL and UDDI, frequently coordinated through an enterprise service bus (ESB).

History

The concepts of SOA emerged in the mid-1990s and became a dominant enterprise-architecture fashion in the 2000s, promoted by major vendors. Critics later argued that many SOA projects became over-engineered and bureaucratic. From the 2010s, lighter-weight approaches — RESTful APIs and microservices — absorbed many of SOA's ideas while discarding heavyweight standards and centralized governance.

Related concepts

Microservices are often described as a refinement of SOA with smaller, independently deployable services. Other related patterns include event-driven architecture and API-led connectivity.
