Holy Roman Empire

The Holy Roman Empire, also known as the Holy Roman Empire of the German Nation after 1512, was a polity in Central and Western Europe, usually headed by a Holy Roman Emperor. It developed in the Early Middle Ages — conventionally from Otto I's imperial coronation in 962, building on Charlemagne's coronation in 800 — and continued until its dissolution in 1806 during the Napoleonic Wars.

At its greatest extent the Empire comprised the Kingdom of Germany, Bohemia, Italy (at times), Burgundy, and a vast patchwork of duchies, prince-bishoprics, free imperial cities and other territories in what is now Germany, Austria, the Low Countries, Czechia, Switzerland, eastern France, northern Italy and western Poland. Voltaire famously quipped that it was "neither holy, nor Roman, nor an empire."

Origins and early emperors

Otto I, king of the East Franks, defeated the Magyars at Lechfeld (955) and was crowned emperor in Rome in 962, founding the Ottonian dynasty. His successors the Salians built imperial authority but clashed with the papacy in the Investiture Controversy (1075–1122), which weakened the crown and strengthened the princes.

High and late Middle Ages

Under the Hohenstaufen — Frederick Barbarossa and Frederick II — the Empire reached a medieval peak of prestige while becoming more decentralized. The Golden Bull of 1356 fixed the electoral college of seven prince-electors who chose the emperor. From 1438 the Habsburg family held the imperial title almost continuously. Imperial reform around 1500 created institutions such as the Reichstag (Imperial Diet), the Imperial Chamber Court (Reichskammergericht) and the Imperial Circles.

Reformation and confessional division

Martin Luther's Reformation split the Empire religiously. After war between Charles V and the Protestant Schmalkaldic League, the Peace of Augsburg (1555) let each prince choose his territory's religion (cuius regio, eius religio). Religious tension exploded in the Thirty Years' War (1618–1648), ended by the Peace of Westphalia, which confirmed the near-sovereignty of the imperial estates and reduced imperial power further.

Decline and dissolution

By the 18th century the Empire was a loose confederation of hundreds of polities, overshadowed by its two great powers, Habsburg Austria and rising Prussia. Revolutionary and Napoleonic France shattered its structure; after defeat at Austerlitz, Emperor Francis II abdicated the imperial crown on 6 August 1806, dissolving the Empire. Most of its German territories were reorganized into the Confederation of the Rhine and later the German Confederation of 1815.
