Social proof


People in crowds look to others to decide how to act

Social proof (or informational social influence) is a psychological and social phenomenon wherein people copy the actions of others in an attempt to undertake behaviour in a given situation. The term was coined by Robert Cialdini in his 1984 book Influence: Science and Practice. Social proof is used in ambiguous social situations where people are unable to determine the appropriate mode of behaviour, and is driven by the assumption that the surrounding people possess more knowledge about the current situation.

Mechanisms

Social proof is strongest when the situation is ambiguous, when the others are perceived as similar or expert, and when many people are behaving the same way. It underlies phenomena from canned laughter on television sitcoms and applause at concerts to the popularity displayed by bestseller lists, product ratings, and social-media follower counts. Psychologist Solomon Asch's conformity experiments demonstrated the power of others' judgments to shape an individual's stated perceptions.

Applications

Marketers use social proof in testimonials, endorsements, reviews, and "most popular" labels. In public policy, descriptive-norm messages—for example, telling households that most neighbours use less energy—have reduced consumption. Online, metrics such as likes, shares, and download counts function as social proof and can drive herd behaviour, sometimes amplifying misinformation or bubbles.

Negative effects

Social proof can promote harmful conformity, bystander apathy (because each bystander assumes others will act), and the spread of undesirable behaviours. Cialdini's work at Petrified Forest National Park showed that signs emphasising how many visitors stole petrified wood actually increased theft, leading instead to injunctive messaging.
