Urartu was an Iron Age kingdom centered around Lake Van in the Armenian Highlands, in what is now eastern Turkey, Armenia, northwestern Iran and part of Azerbaijan. It flourished from roughly the mid-9th century BC until the early 6th century BC and was, for a time, the principal rival of the Neo-Assyrian Empire.

The name derives from Assyrian records, where the region is called Uruatri and later Urartu. In their own inscriptions the Urartian kings called their land Biainili. Urartu is also widely identified with the biblical land of Ararat.

History

Loose tribal confederations in the Van region are first attested in Assyrian campaigns of the 13th century BC. A unified kingdom emerged around 860 BC under Aramu, and Sarduri I (r. c. 834–828 BC) established the capital at Tushpa, on the shore of Lake Van, building its great citadel. Under Menua (r. 810–786 BC) and Argishti I (r. 786–764 BC), Urartu expanded north into the Araxes valley, founding fortresses such as Erebuni (modern Yerevan, founded 782 BC) and Argishtihinili.

Urartu reached its greatest extent in the 8th century BC, controlling routes for trade and raw materials — especially metals — across the highlands. Conflict with Assyria was recurrent; in 714 BC Sargon II sacked the Urartian temple city of Musasir in a famous campaign. Under Rusa II (r. c. 685–645 BC) the kingdom enjoyed a final period of building and stability, but it declined in the 7th century and was destroyed by the Medes around 590 BC.

Culture and economy

The Urartians wrote in cuneiform adapted from Assyrian, recording royal annals, building inscriptions and administrative texts in the Urartian language, a member of the Hurro-Urartian family. They were accomplished metalworkers in bronze and iron, hydraulic engineers who built canals and reservoirs (including the Menua Canal, parts of which still function), and builders of massive cyclopean fortresses. Their religion centered on the god Ḫaldi, with Teisheba (storm god) and Shivini (sun god) completing the chief triad.

Legacy

Urartian material culture — bronze cauldrons, belts, helmets and distinctive pottery — is preserved in museums in Van, Yerevan, Ankara and elsewhere. The Armenian name of Mount Ararat and the continuity of settlement in the highlands connect Urartu to later regional history.
