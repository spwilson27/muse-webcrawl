Talmud


A Talmud page: Mishnah and Gemara at the centre, Rashi and Tosafot commentaries in the margins.

The Talmud (Hebrew: Talmūd, "instruction, learning") is the central text of Rabbinic Judaism and the primary source of Jewish religious law (halakha) and Jewish theology. It consists of the Mishnah — a written compilation of the Oral Torah, redacted around 200 CE under Rabbi Judah the Prince — and the Gemara, an elaboration and analysis of the Mishnah and related traditions composed over the following three centuries. Nearly all forms of Judaism take the Talmud as authoritative, the major exception being Karaite Judaism, which accepts only the written Tanakh.

The two Talmuds

Two recensions exist. The Jerusalem Talmud (Talmud Yerushalmi) was compiled in the academies of Roman Palestine, principally Tiberias, and reached its form around 400 CE. The Babylonian Talmud (Talmud Bavli) was produced in the academies of Sasanian Babylonia — Sura, Pumbedita, and Nehardea — and completed around 500–600 CE; it is longer, more thoroughly edited, and became normative for later Jewish law. Both are written in a mixture of Hebrew and Jewish Aramaic (Jewish Babylonian Aramaic in the Bavli, Jewish Palestinian Aramaic in the Yerushalmi).

Content and method

The Mishnah is organised into six orders (sedarim) covering agriculture, festivals, family law, civil and criminal law, sacrifices, and ritual purity, divided into 63 tractates. The Gemara records debates among the rabbis (the amoraim), ranging across law, exegesis, ethics, medicine, folklore, and narrative (aggadah) alongside legal (halakhic) analysis. Its dialectical, argument-preserving style — rarely issuing a single uncontested conclusion — made it the foundation of the yeshiva curriculum and of later commentaries, above all those of Rashi (11th century) and the Tosafists (12th–13th centuries), printed around the text in the standard editions.

History and study

The Talmud was transmitted in manuscripts through the Middle Ages, periodically targeted by censorship and public burnings — most notoriously Paris in 1242 — and first printed in full in Venice by Daniel Bomberg (1520–1523), whose pagination remains standard. The Vilna edition (1880s) fixed the familiar page layout. Study of a page a day (Daf Yomi), a cycle instituted in 1923, completes the Babylonian Talmud's 2,711 folios in about seven and a half years and is now followed by tens of thousands of students worldwide.
