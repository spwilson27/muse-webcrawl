Talmud

The Talmud (Hebrew: תּלְמוּד, Talmūd, "instruction, learning") is the central text of Rabbinic Judaism and the primary source of Jewish religious law (halakha) and Jewish theology. Until modernity, in nearly all Jewish communities, the Talmud was the centrepiece of Jewish cultural life and the foundation for all codes of Jewish law. It is a record of centuries of rabbinic discussion pertaining to Jewish law, ethics, philosophy, customs and history.

Composition

The Talmud has two components. The Mishnah (c. 200 CE), a written compendium of the Oral Torah redacted by Judah the Prince (Yehudah HaNasi), is organised into six orders (Sedarim): Zeraim (seeds), Moed (festival), Nashim (women), Nezikin (damages), Kodashim (holy things) and Tohorot (purities), comprising 63 tractates (masekhtot). The Gemara is the record of rabbinic analysis of and commentary on the Mishnah. The rabbis whose views are recorded in the Mishnah are known as Tannaim; those in the Gemara, as Amoraim.

The two Talmuds

Two collections of Gemara survive. The Jerusalem Talmud (Talmud Yerushalmi), compiled in the academies of the Land of Israel, was redacted around 400 CE. The Babylonian Talmud (Talmud Bavli), produced in the academies of Babylonia (notably Sura and Pumbedita) and redacted around 500–600 CE, is longer, more extensively edited and generally regarded as the more authoritative of the two; when "the Talmud" is spoken of without qualification, the Babylonian Talmud is usually meant.

Content and style

The Talmud interweaves halakha (legal discussion) with aggadah — narrative, parable, ethics, folklore and historical anecdote. Its characteristic method is dialectical: arguments are presented, challenged, refuted and reformulated, frequently leaving debate unresolved. The standard printed edition (the Vilna Shas, 19th century) runs to 2,711 double-sided folios (dappim) for the Babylonian Talmud; a page-a-day study cycle, Daf Yomi, completes the whole in about seven and a half years. The margins of the standard page carry the indispensable commentaries of Rashi (11th-century France) and the Tosafists.

History and influence

Manuscript Talmuds were periodically burned in medieval Europe — most notoriously in Paris in 1242 — and the work was censored or attacked in disputations and polemics across the centuries. At the same time it shaped Jewish life decisively: medieval codifiers from Alfasi and Maimonides to the Shulchan Aruch rested their law codes on it, and yeshiva study centred on it remains at the heart of traditional Jewish education. Translations into English, notably the Soncino edition and the Steinsaltz and ArtScroll (Schottenstein) editions, have made it widely accessible to modern readers.
