Mitzvah

Mitzvah (Hebrew: מִצְוָה, plural mitzvot) literally means "commandment" and denotes the precepts and commandments of Jewish law (halakha). In everyday usage, the word has also come to mean an individual good deed or act of kindness performed in accordance with Jewish teaching.

The 613 commandments

Traditional rabbinic enumeration counts 613 mitzvot in the Torah: 248 positive commandments ("thou shalt") and 365 prohibitions ("thou shalt not") — numbers traditionally correlated with the parts of the body and the days of the solar year. The most famous medieval enumeration is Maimonides' in his Sefer Hamitzvot. The commandments cover ritual matters (Sabbath, festivals, dietary laws, prayer) and ethical obligations (charity, honesty in business, visiting the sick, loving one's neighbour).

Bar and bat mitzvah

A Jewish boy becomes a bar mitzvah ("son of the commandment") at age 13, and a girl a bat mitzvah at 12 or 13, at which point they are considered morally responsible for observing the mitzvot. The milestone is commonly marked by being called to the Torah in synagogue and by family celebration.

Meaning

Performing mitzvot is understood in Judaism as the central way of sanctifying daily life and emulating God. Rabbinic tradition adds that the reward of a mitzvah is itself a mitzvah — the opportunity to perform another — and modern usage of "a mitzvah" for any good deed reflects the ethical expansion of the term.
