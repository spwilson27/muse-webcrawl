Tzedakah

Tzedakah (Hebrew: צְדָקָה) is the Jewish religious obligation of charitable giving. The word derives from the root meaning "righteousness" or "justice," reflecting the idea that giving to the poor is not merely generosity but a duty of justice — one of the 613 commandments. In common usage, tzedakah refers to monetary charity, while gemilut hasadim covers acts of loving-kindness more broadly.

Biblical and rabbinic foundations

The Torah commands leaving the corner of the field (pe'ah) and forgotten produce for the poor, lending to the needy, and opening one's hand to the poor (Deuteronomy 15). The rabbis institutionalised communal charity through the kuppah (fund) and tamhui (food distribution), and fixed norms of giving: ordinarily a tenth of one's income (ma'aser kesafim), with a ceiling of a fifth to prevent self-impoverishment.

Maimonides' ladder

Maimonides famously ranked eight levels of giving, from reluctant giving at the bottom to the highest level: helping a person earn a livelihood through a loan, partnership, or employment so that charity becomes unnecessary. Anonymous giving, where neither donor nor recipient knows the other, ranks near the top.

Practice

Tzedakah is commonly given before holidays and the Sabbath, in memory of the dead, and in times of trouble; many homes keep a pushke (charity box) into which coins are dropped regularly. Jewish communal life is organised around charitable institutions — free-loan societies, food banks, hospitals, and welfare federations — and philanthropy remains a central feature of Jewish identity across denominations.
