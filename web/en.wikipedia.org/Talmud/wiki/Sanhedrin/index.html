Sanhedrin

The Sanhedrin (Hebrew: סַנְהֶדְרִין, from Greek synedrion, "council") was the supreme rabbinic court and legislative assembly of ancient Israel. The Great Sanhedrin, composed of 71 sages headed by the nasi (president), met in the Chamber of Hewn Stone in the Temple in Jerusalem and later in various locations in the Galilee after the Temple's destruction.

Functions

The Sanhedrin served as the highest court for capital and religious cases, interpreted the Torah, ordained judges, supervised the calendar by declaring new months and leap years based on witnesses' testimony about the new moon, and ruled on matters affecting the whole people, such as war, the king, and the high priesthood.

History

Rabbinic tradition traces the institution to the seventy elders appointed by Moses. During the Second Temple period the Sanhedrin operated under Persian, Hellenistic, and Roman rule, its composition and powers varying with the political situation. After the destruction of the Temple in 70 CE it was reconstituted at Yavneh under Rabban Yochanan ben Zakkai, then moved successively to Usha, Beit She'arim, Sepphoris, and Tiberias. The institution was abolished by the Byzantine emperor Theodosius II in 425 CE, when the patriarchate was ended.

The tractate

Tractate Sanhedrin in the Mishnah and Talmud describes judicial procedure, the composition of courts, capital offences and punishments, and theological topics such as resurrection and the world to come.
