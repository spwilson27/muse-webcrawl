Synagogue

A synagogue (from Greek synagōgē, "assembly"; Hebrew: בית כנסת, beit knesset, "house of assembly") is a Jewish house of prayer, study, and communal gathering. Synagogues have served as the central institution of Jewish communal life since antiquity.

Origins

Synagogues arose during the Babylonian exile and Second Temple period as local places of assembly for prayer and Torah reading alongside the Temple cult in Jerusalem. After the Temple's destruction in 70 CE, the synagogue — with prayer conceived as a substitute for sacrifice — became the focus of Jewish religious life throughout the diaspora.

Layout and furnishings

The focal point of a synagogue interior is the Torah ark (aron ha-kodesh), a cabinet on the wall facing Jerusalem containing the Torah scrolls. Before the ark hangs an eternal light (ner tamid). The bimah is the raised platform from which the Torah is read; in Orthodox synagogues it is typically central, while Reform congregations place it at the front. A quorum of ten adults (minyan) is traditionally required for full public prayer.

Services and functions

Prayer services are held daily, with the principal congregational services on the Sabbath and festivals. Services are led by a cantor (hazzan) and often include a sermon by the rabbi. Beyond prayer, synagogues house schools, social halls, and study rooms (beit midrash), and in Yiddish are commonly called shul ("school").

Architecture

Synagogue architecture has adapted to the styles of host cultures — from the ancient stone synagogues of Galilee, to Moorish Revival and Byzantine Revival buildings of 19th-century Europe and America, to modernist designs — while retaining the ark, bimah, and orientation toward Jerusalem.
