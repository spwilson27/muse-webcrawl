Rabbi

A rabbi (Hebrew: רַבִּי, "my master") is a spiritual leader and religious teacher in Judaism. The title, from the root rav ("great" or "master"), arose in the rabbinic period following the destruction of the Second Temple in 70 CE, when the sages who preserved and interpreted the Oral Torah became the central authorities of Jewish life.

Role and ordination

Rabbis are ordained through semikhah, historically the laying on of hands in an unbroken chain from Moses, and today conferred by seminaries and individual scholars after years of study of Torah, Talmud, and halakha (Jewish law). Their functions include teaching, preaching, pastoral care, officiating at life-cycle events, supervising kosher food production, and ruling on questions of Jewish law. Congregational rabbis lead synagogues; other rabbis serve as teachers, judges (dayanim) on rabbinical courts, chaplains, or communal officials.

Historical development

The early rabbis — the tannaim who produced the Mishnah and the amoraim of the Talmud — reshaped Judaism around prayer and study in place of Temple sacrifice. In medieval Europe, great scholars such as Rashi and Maimonides set enduring standards of learning. The modern rabbinate took shape in the nineteenth century with the founding of rabbinical seminaries (Breslau, Cincinnati, New York, London) for the Reform, Conservative, and modern Orthodox movements.

Contemporary practice

Since the twentieth century, Reform, Reconstructionist, Conservative, and some Orthodox communities have ordained women as rabbis — Sally Priesand (Reform, 1972) was the first woman ordained by a seminary in the United States. Chief rabbis serve as official religious authorities in Israel and in many national communities.
