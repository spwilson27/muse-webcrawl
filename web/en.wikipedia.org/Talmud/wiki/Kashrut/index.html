Kashrut

Kashrut (Hebrew: כַּשְׁרוּת) is the body of Jewish dietary laws governing what food may be eaten and how it must be prepared. Food that conforms to these laws is called kosher ("fit" or "proper"). The laws derive principally from the Torah (especially Leviticus 11 and Deuteronomy 14) as elaborated by rabbinic tradition.

Permitted and forbidden animals

Land animals must both chew the cud and have fully split hooves — cattle, sheep, and goats are kosher; pigs, though split-hoofed, do not chew the cud and are forbidden. Of water creatures, only fish with both fins and removable scales are permitted, excluding shellfish and catfish. Most birds of prey are forbidden; permitted birds are identified by tradition (chicken, turkey, duck, goose). All insects (with a few historical exceptions) and reptiles are forbidden.

Slaughter and preparation

Meat must come from animals slaughtered by shechita — a swift severing of the throat with an extremely sharp knife by a trained slaughterer (shochet) — and the carcass is inspected for defects. Because consuming blood is prohibited, meat is salted and soaked (or broiled) to draw out blood.

Meat and milk

From the thrice-repeated command "You shall not boil a kid in its mother's milk," rabbinic law prohibits cooking, eating, or deriving benefit from mixtures of meat and dairy, and observant households keep separate dishes, utensils, and waiting periods between eating meat and dairy. Neutral (pareve) foods such as fish, eggs, fruit, and vegetables may be eaten with either.

Modern observance

Today, packaged kosher food is certified by agencies whose symbols (hekhshers) — such as OU, OK, and Star-K — appear on products worldwide. Levels of observance vary widely among Jewish communities and movements; kashrut remains one of the most widely practised elements of Jewish religious life.
