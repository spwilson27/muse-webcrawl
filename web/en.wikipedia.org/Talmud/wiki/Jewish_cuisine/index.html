Jewish cuisine

Jewish cuisine refers to the diverse cooking traditions of Jewish communities worldwide, shaped over centuries by Jewish dietary laws (kashrut), festival and Sabbath customs, and the local ingredients and techniques of the many lands in which Jews have lived. Major traditions include Ashkenazi, Sephardi, and Mizrahi cuisines, as well as the modern cuisine of Israel.

Shaping influences

Kashrut prohibits pork and shellfish, requires the separation of meat and dairy, and prescribes ritual slaughter, profoundly affecting dish composition. Sabbath laws forbidding cooking from Friday sunset led to slow-cooked stews such as cholent (Ashkenazi) and hamin (Sephardi) that simmer overnight. Festival foods carry symbolism: matzah at Passover, honey and apples at Rosh Hashanah, latkes and sufganiyot at Hanukkah.

Ashkenazi cuisine

The food of Central and Eastern European Jews features gefilte fish, chicken soup with matzah balls, kugel, chopped liver, kishke, tzimmes, bagels, and rye breads, as well as preserved fish such as pickled herring and lox. Many of these dishes were born of poverty, stretching modest ingredients.

Sephardi and Mizrahi cuisine

Jews of Iberian, Mediterranean, and Middle Eastern origin cook with olive oil, rice, legumes, spices, and stuffed vegetables. Signature dishes include bourekas, couscous preparations, shakshuka (now broadly popular), kubbeh soups, and elaborate rice dishes. Yemenite cuisine is known for jachnun, malawach, and fiery zhug.

Israeli cuisine

Modern Israeli food fuses these traditions with Levantine Arab cooking — falafel, hummus, sabich, and shawarma — alongside innovations such as Israeli salad, schnitzel, and "Israeli breakfast." It continues to evolve with global influences while retaining the framework of kashrut in much public food service.
