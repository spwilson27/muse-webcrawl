Midrash

Midrash (Hebrew: מִדְרָשׁ, "investigation, exposition"; plural midrashim) is a genre of rabbinic literature and a method of biblical interpretation in Judaism. Midrashic works expound the Hebrew Bible through close reading, filling narrative gaps, resolving contradictions, and drawing out legal and moral lessons, often by way of parable, wordplay, and imaginative elaboration of the biblical story.

Types

Scholars distinguish halakhic midrash, which derives legal norms from the Torah (such as the Mekhilta on Exodus and the Sifra on Leviticus), from aggadic midrash, which explores narrative, theology, and ethics. Major aggadic collections include Genesis Rabbah, Lamentations Rabbah, and the Midrash Tanhuma, compiled between roughly the third and the early medieval centuries, many arranged as homilies on the synagogue lectionary.

Method

Midrash treats Scripture as inexhaustibly meaningful: a superfluous word, an unusual spelling, or a repeated phrase becomes the hook for interpretation. Techniques range from the plain-sense rules associated with Hillel and Rabbi Ishmael to gezerah shavah (analogy between verses sharing a term) and, in later and mystical works, letter-based methods such as gematria and notarikon. Aggadic stories expand the lives of biblical figures — Abraham smashing his father's idols is a famous midrashic, not biblical, episode.

Influence

Midrash preserved centuries of Jewish preaching and storytelling and deeply influenced Jewish liturgy, medieval Bible commentary (Rashi draws heavily on it), and later literature. In modern times "midrash" is sometimes used loosely for creative retellings of biblical stories in fiction, poetry, and feminist and literary scholarship.
