Kosher

Kosher (Hebrew: כָּשֵׁר, kasher, "fit") describes food that conforms to the Jewish dietary laws known as kashrut. The laws, derived from the Torah and elaborated in the Talmud and later codes, govern which animals may be eaten, how they must be slaughtered and prepared, and the separation of meat and dairy products.

Permitted and forbidden animals

Land animals must both chew the cud and have fully split hooves — permitting cattle, sheep, and goats while forbidding pigs, camels, and hares. Birds are permitted by tradition (chicken, turkey, duck) while birds of prey are forbidden. Fish must have fins and scales, excluding all shellfish. Insects are generally forbidden, and meat and milk may not be cooked or eaten together.

Slaughter and preparation

Meat must come from animals slaughtered by shechita (see Shechita), performed by a trained shochet with a swift cut, followed by inspection, removal of certain fats and the sciatic nerve, and salting or broiling to remove blood, since consuming blood is prohibited.

Supervision and certification

Because industrial food production is complex, kosher food is commonly certified by agencies whose symbols (hechsherim) — such as OU, OK, and Star-K — appear on packaging. Supervisors (mashgichim) oversee production in factories, restaurants, and caterers. Standards vary among communities and movements.

Observance

Levels of observance vary widely: many Jews keep kosher fully, others only at home or in part, and Reform Judaism leaves the practice to individual choice. Kosher food also serves markets beyond the Jewish community, including consumers who regard certification as a mark of quality or oversight.
