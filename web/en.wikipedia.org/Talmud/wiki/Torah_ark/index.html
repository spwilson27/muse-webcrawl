Torah ark

The Torah ark (Hebrew: אֲרוֹן הַקֹּדֶשׁ, aron ha-kodesh, "Holy Ark"; in Sephardi usage heikhal) is the sacred cabinet in a synagogue that houses the Torah scrolls. It is usually located on the wall that worshippers face during prayer — in most of the Diaspora, the wall oriented toward Jerusalem — and is considered the holiest place in the synagogue.

Design and customs

Arks range from simple cupboards to monumental carved and gilded structures. Doors or a curtain (parochet), often embroidered with verses and symbols such as crowns, lions, or the Tablets of the Law, conceal the scrolls. Above many arks burns the ner tamid (eternal light), recalling the lamps of the Temple. Opening the ark is an honour during services, and congregants rise when the scrolls are carried to the reading desk (bimah).

History

In ancient synagogues scrolls were kept in portable chests or in niches; fixed monumental arks developed in late antiquity and the Middle Ages. Famous historical examples include the carved wooden arks of Eastern European wooden synagogues and the marble arks of Italian synagogues. The name echoes the Ark of the Covenant, which held the Tablets in the Tabernacle and Temple.

Symbolism

Verses commonly inscribed include "Know before whom you stand" and "I have set the Lord always before me" (Psalm 16:8). The ark is the destination of the Torah procession and the focus of especially solemn prayers, such as those of the High Holy Days, when it may remain open through parts of the service.
