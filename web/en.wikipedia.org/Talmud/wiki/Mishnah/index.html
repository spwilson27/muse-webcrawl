The Mishnah (Hebrew: מִשְׁנָה, "study by repetition") is the first major written compilation of Judaism's Oral Torah and the foundational work of rabbinic literature. It was redacted around 200 CE by Rabbi Judah ha-Nasi (Judah the Prince) in Roman Palestine, drawing on centuries of oral legal tradition in the aftermath of the destruction of the Second Temple (70 CE) and the Bar Kokhba revolt (135 CE).

Structure

The Mishnah is organised into six orders (sedarim), subdivided into 63 tractates (masekhtot) and some 525 chapters:

  • Zeraim (Seeds) — agriculture and blessings
  • Moed (Festival) — Sabbath and holidays
  • Nashim (Women) — marriage and family law
  • Nezikin (Damages) — civil and criminal law, courts, ethics (including Pirkei Avot)
  • Kodashim (Holy Things) — sacrifices and Temple service
  • Tohorot (Purities) — ritual purity

Style and authority

Written in a terse, elegant Mishnaic Hebrew, the Mishnah typically states rules without citing scriptural proof, often recording disputes between named sages (the tannaim) such as Hillel and Shammai and their schools. Together with its later commentary, the Gemara, it forms the Talmud. Related tannaitic material not included in the Mishnah survives in the Tosefta and in halakhic midrashim.

Legacy

The Mishnah marks the transition of Judaism to a text-centred, study-centred civilisation able to survive without the Temple, and it remains a core text of Jewish learning, studied daily in traditional academies (yeshivot) to the present day.
