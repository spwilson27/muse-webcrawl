Shechita

Shechita (Hebrew: שחיטה) is the ritual method of slaughtering animals for food in Judaism, as prescribed by Jewish law for meat to be kosher. It is performed by a shochet, a specially trained slaughterer, who severs the trachea and oesophagus (and, in practice, the major blood vessels of the neck) with a single swift motion of an extremely sharp, perfectly smooth knife called a chalaf.

Procedure and rules

The shochet must be observant and trained in the laws of slaughter, and recites a blessing before beginning. Five errors invalidate the act: pausing, pressing, digging the knife, cutting outside the prescribed region of the neck, and tearing. After slaughter, the lungs and other organs are inspected for defects that would render the animal terefah (unfit); the strictest standard, glatt kosher, requires the lungs to be free of adhesions.

Basis and rationale

The Torah commands slaughter "as I have commanded you" (Deuteronomy 12:21), which rabbinic tradition understands as referring to an orally transmitted method. Defenders of the practice argue that the cut causes rapid loss of blood pressure and consciousness and is intended to minimise suffering; the method also serves the prohibition on consuming blood, which is drained and removed by salting.

Controversy and law

Shechita — like Islamic dhabihah slaughter — is contested in debates over slaughter without prior stunning. Several European countries have restricted or banned non-stun slaughter, while Jewish and Muslim communities defend it as a protected religious practice; some authorities accept post-cut stunning. The debate continues in courts and legislatures.
