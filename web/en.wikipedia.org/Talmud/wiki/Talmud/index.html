Talmud

The Talmud (Hebrew: תַּלְמוּד, "instruction, learning") is the central text of Rabbinic Judaism and the primary source of Jewish religious law (halakha) and theology. It consists of the Mishnah (c. 200 CE), the first written redaction of the Oral Torah, and the Gemara (c. 500 CE), a commentary and analysis of the Mishnah and related traditions.

The two Talmuds

Two versions exist. The Jerusalem Talmud (Talmud Yerushalmi) was compiled in the academies of the Galilee, principally Tiberias, in the 4th–5th centuries. The Babylonian Talmud (Talmud Bavli), produced in the academies of Sura and Pumbedita in Mesopotamia, is longer, more thoroughly edited, and became the authoritative version for later Jewish law.

Contents and structure

The Mishnah is organised into six orders (sedarim) covering agriculture, festivals, marriage and damages, civil and criminal law, sacrifices, and ritual purity, subdivided into 63 tractates. The Gemara records dialectical discussions of the Tannaim and later Amoraim, combining legal analysis (halakha) with narrative, folklore, and ethics (aggadah). Tractate Berakhot opens the work, and discussions range from prayer and blessings to torts and Temple ritual.

Study and influence

Talmud study is the centrepiece of traditional Jewish education and of the yeshiva curriculum; the seven-and-a-half-year cycle of one page a day (Daf Yomi) is followed worldwide. The classic printed page surrounds the text with the commentaries of Rashi and the Tosafists. Talmudic dialectic profoundly influenced Jewish law codes such as Maimonides' Mishneh Torah and the Shulchan Aruch.

History of the text

The Talmud survived medieval censorship, burnings (notably Paris in 1242), and disputes, and was first printed in full in Venice (1520–23) by Daniel Bomberg, whose pagination remains standard. Modern critical scholarship studies its redaction and sources, and translations such as the Steinsaltz and ArtScroll editions have broadened access.
