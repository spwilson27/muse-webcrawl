Beit She'arim

Beit She'arim (Hebrew: בית שערים, "House of Gates") is an ancient Jewish town and necropolis in the Lower Galilee, Israel, near modern Kiryat Tiv'on. It flourished in the Roman and Byzantine periods and became the principal burial place of Jews from across the ancient world after the seat of the Sanhedrin moved there in the 2nd century CE.

History

The town rose to prominence when Rabbi Judah the Prince (Yehuda HaNasi), redactor of the Mishnah, made his home there and was buried in its cemetery around 217 CE. Thereafter Jews from throughout the Land of Israel and the Diaspora — from as far as Palmyra and Yemen — sought burial near him, believing in the sanctity of the site. The town was largely destroyed by fire in the 4th century, possibly during the Jewish revolt against Gallus (351 CE).

The necropolis

The site contains more than thirty rock-cut catacombs with carved sarcophagi, decorated facades, and inscriptions in Hebrew, Aramaic, Greek, and Palmyrene. Motifs include menorahs, lions, eagles, and ships. The largest catacombs belonged to the family of Rabbi Judah the Prince.

Archaeology and recognition

Excavations led by Benjamin Mazar (1936–40) and Nahman Avigad (1953–58) uncovered the necropolis and the remains of a synagogue and public buildings. Beit She'arim National Park is administered by the Israel Nature and Parks Authority, and in 2015 the necropolis was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
