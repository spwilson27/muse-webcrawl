Jewish prayer

Jewish prayer (Hebrew: תְּפִלָּה, tefillah) is the prayer recitation that forms part of the observance of Rabbinic Judaism. Prayers are traditionally recited three times daily — morning (Shacharit), afternoon (Mincha), and evening (Ma'ariv) — with additional services on Sabbaths and festivals. Most prayer is conducted in Hebrew using a prayer book called a siddur, and communal prayer requires a quorum (minyan) of ten adults for certain passages.

Origins

According to the Talmud, daily prayer was instituted by the patriarchs and formalised by the Men of the Great Assembly after the destruction of the Second Temple, when prayer came to substitute for the sacrificial offerings. The Shema and the Amidah, both rooted in biblical and early rabbinic tradition, form the core of the services.

Structure of the services

The weekday morning service includes introductory psalms, the Shema with its blessings, and the Amidah (the "standing prayer" of nineteen blessings), followed by supplications and Torah reading on certain days. The Amidah is also central to Mincha and Ma'ariv. On Shabbat and festivals a Musaf (additional) service is added, and the liturgy includes the Hallel psalms on joyous days.

Modes and customs

Prayer may be sung to traditional chant (nusach), which varies between Ashkenazi, Sephardi, and other rites. Worshippers traditionally face Jerusalem, wear a tallit at morning services, and don tefillin on weekdays. Personal spontaneous prayer also has a long tradition alongside the fixed liturgy.
