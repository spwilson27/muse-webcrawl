Halakha

Halakha (Hebrew: הֲלָכָה, "the way of walking") is the collective body of Jewish religious law derived from the Written and Oral Torah. It encompasses the 613 commandments (mitzvot) of the Torah as interpreted and expanded by rabbinic tradition, and governs nearly every aspect of Jewish life: prayer, diet (kashrut), the Sabbath, festivals, marriage, civil and criminal law, and ethics.

Sources and development

Orthodox Judaism holds that alongside the written Torah, Moses received an oral law, transmitted through generations of sages and eventually codified in the Mishnah (c. 200 CE) and elaborated in the Talmud (c. 500 CE). Later authorities produced commentaries and codes — most influentially Maimonides' Mishneh Torah (12th century) and Joseph Karo's Shulchan Aruch (16th century) — and a living literature of responsa (she'elot u-teshuvot) continues to apply halakha to new questions, from electricity on the Sabbath to medical ethics.

Interpretation

Halakhic reasoning proceeds through established hermeneutical principles and precedent, and its rulings are issued by rabbis and poskim (decisors). Different movements relate to halakha differently: Orthodoxy regards it as binding and divinely authorised; Conservative Judaism treats it as binding but historically evolving; Reform Judaism treats it as guidance rather than obligation.

Scope

Because halakha interweaves ritual, civil, and moral norms, scholars compare it to other comprehensive religious legal systems such as Islamic sharia. In the modern State of Israel, halakha influences personal-status law (marriage and divorce for Jews) while the general legal system is secular.
