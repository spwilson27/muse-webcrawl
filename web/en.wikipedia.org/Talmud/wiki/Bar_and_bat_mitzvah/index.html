Bar and bat mitzvah

A bar mitzvah (for a boy) or bat mitzvah (for a girl) is a Jewish coming-of-age ritual marking the point at which a child becomes responsible for their own actions under Jewish law. Traditionally this occurs at age 13 for boys and 12 for girls. The terms mean "son of the commandment" and "daughter of the commandment" respectively.

Religious significance

Upon reaching this age, a young person becomes obligated to observe the commandments (mitzvot), may be counted in a prayer quorum (minyan), and may be called to the Torah reading in synagogue. Before this age, religious responsibility for a child's actions traditionally rests with the parents.

The ceremony

The celebrant typically chants blessings over the Torah, reads a portion from the Torah and the haftarah, and may lead parts of the service and deliver a speech (d'var Torah). The ceremony is usually followed by a festive meal and celebration. Preparation often takes a year or more of study in Hebrew and cantillation.

History

The age of thirteen as the age of majority appears in the Mishnah (Pirkei Avot 5:21). The synagogue ceremony for boys developed in medieval Europe. The bat mitzvah ceremony for girls is a modern innovation: the first recorded American bat mitzvah was that of Judith Kaplan, daughter of Rabbi Mordecai Kaplan, in 1922; it is now standard in non-Orthodox movements and increasingly marked in Orthodox communities in varying forms.
