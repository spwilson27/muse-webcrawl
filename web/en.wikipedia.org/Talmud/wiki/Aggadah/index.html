Aggadah

Aggadah (Hebrew: אַגָּדָה, "telling" or "narrative"; also spelled Agada or Aggada) refers to the non-legalistic texts of classical rabbinic literature — the stories, parables, legends, folklore, historical anecdotes, moral exhortations, and theological discourses found in the Talmud and Midrash. It stands in contrast to halakha, the legal material.

Content

Aggadic material makes up a large portion of the Babylonian Talmud (roughly a third) and entire midrashic collections such as Genesis Rabbah and Midrash Tanhuma. It includes expansions of biblical narratives, biographies of the sages, parables (meshalim) illustrating ethical teaching, eschatological speculation, medicine and natural lore, and sayings about God, Israel, and the nations.

Interpretation

Rabbinic tradition itself treats aggadah differently from law: while halakha is binding, aggadic statements were often understood as homiletic and not to be taken as dogma — "one does not rely upon aggadah" as a source of law. Medieval authorities such as Maimonides warned against both literalist readings of fantastic tales and dismissing them entirely, arguing that many conceal philosophical meaning in parable form. Modern scholars study aggadah as the imaginative literature through which rabbinic values and worldviews were transmitted.

The Haggadah

The best-known aggadic work is the Passover Haggadah, the text recited at the seder that retells the Exodus from Egypt through biblical quotation, midrashic expansion, songs, and ritual instruction. It is not part of the Talmud proper but a liturgical compilation of largely aggadic material.
