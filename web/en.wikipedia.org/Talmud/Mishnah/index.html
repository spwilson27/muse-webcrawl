Mishnah

Mishnah (Hebrew: מִשְׁנָה, "study by repetition") — the first major written collection of the Jewish oral tradition (the Oral Torah), compiled c. 200 CE by Rabbi Judah ha-Nasi. The foundational text of the Talmud.

The Mishnah or Mishna (/ˈmɪʃnə/; Hebrew: מִשְׁנָה, "study by repetition", from the verb shanah שנה‎, or "to study and review", also "secondary") is the first major written collection of the Jewish oral traditions known as the Oral Torah. It is also the first major work of rabbinic literature.

Compilation

The Mishnah was compiled around 200 CE by Rabbi Judah ha-Nasi (Judah the Prince) after the destruction of the Second Temple (70 CE) and the Bar Kokhba revolt, when the chain of oral transmission appeared at risk. It records the rulings and disputes of the Tannaim, the rabbinic sages of the first two centuries CE, often without settling disagreements.

Structure

The Mishnah is divided into six orders (sedarim): Zeraim (Seeds — agricultural laws and prayers), Moed (Festival — Sabbath and holiday laws), Nashim (Women — marriage and family law), Nezikin (Damages — civil and criminal law, courts, and the ethical tractate Pirkei Avot), Kodashim (Holy things — sacrifices and Temple service), and Tohorot (Purities — ritual purity). The six orders contain 63 tractates in total.

Relationship to the Talmud

Later rabbinic commentary on the Mishnah, the Gemara, combined with the Mishnah to form the two Talmuds: the Jerusalem Talmud (c. 400 CE) and the more extensive Babylonian Talmud (c. 500 CE), which became the central text of Rabbinic Judaism.
