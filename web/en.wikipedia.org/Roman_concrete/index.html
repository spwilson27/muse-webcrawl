Roman concrete

Roman concrete, called opus caementicium in Latin, was a building material used in ancient Rome from the late Roman Republic through the Empire. It combined a hydraulic-setting mortar of lime and volcanic ash (pozzolana) with aggregate (caementa) ranging from rock rubble to ceramic pieces and brick. Unlike modern Portland-cement concrete, it was typically laid by hand in layers and faced with brick or stone.

Properties

Roman concrete set under water and proved extraordinarily durable: marine structures exposed to two thousand years of seawater often outlast modern equivalents. The pozzolanic reaction between lime and volcanic ash formed durable minerals such as aluminium tobermorite, and recent research attributes additional longevity to "lime clasts" that let cracks self-heal as water dissolves and re-precipitates calcium carbonate. Vitruvius and Pliny the Elder described its ingredients and uses.

Architectural revolution

Because it could be moulded and cured into massive monolithic forms, Roman concrete enabled the "architectural revolution" of vaulted and domed spaces: the markets and baths of Rome, harbour moles such as those at Caesarea Maritima, the great vaulted Basilica of Maxentius, and above all the Pantheon (completed c. AD 126), whose 43.3-metre unreinforced concrete dome remains the largest of its kind in the world.

Decline and rediscovery

Concrete technology declined in the West after the fall of Rome, surviving mainly in foundations and fortifications. Interest revived with John Smeaton's hydraulic lime experiments in the eighteenth century and the invention of Portland cement (1824). Modern studies of Roman recipes continue to inform low-carbon and self-healing concrete research.
