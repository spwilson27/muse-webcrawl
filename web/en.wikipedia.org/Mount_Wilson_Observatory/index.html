Mount Wilson Observatory

LocationMount Wilson, San Gabriel Mountains, California, United States
Altitude1,742 m (5,715 ft)
Established1904
FounderGeorge Ellery Hale

The Mount Wilson Observatory (MWO) is an astronomical observatory in Los Angeles County, California, United States. The MWO is located on Mount Wilson, a 1,740-metre (5,700-foot) peak in the San Gabriel Mountains near Pasadena, northeast of Los Angeles. The observatory contains two historically important telescopes: the 100-inch (2.5 m) Hooker telescope, which was the largest aperture telescope in the world from its completion in 1917 to 1949, and the 60-inch telescope, completed in 1908, which was briefly the largest operational telescope.

The observatory was founded by solar astronomer George Ellery Hale under the auspices of the Carnegie Institution of Washington. The site's exceptional atmospheric stability, caused by the temperature inversion layer that traps smog below the summit, made it ideal for astronomical observations.

Discoveries

Mount Wilson was central to the transformation of cosmology in the early 20th century. Working with the Hooker telescope, Edwin Hubble identified Cepheid variable stars in the Andromeda "Nebula" in 1923–24, proving that it was a galaxy far beyond the Milky Way, and in 1929 he and Milton Humason established the expansion of the universe by relating galaxy distances to redshifts. Harlow Shapley used Mount Wilson observations in debates over the scale of the Milky Way, and Walter Baade carried out landmark studies of stellar populations there during the Second World War blackouts.

The observatory was also a pioneer of solar physics, with its solar tower telescopes (1908 and 1913), and of interferometry: Albert A. Michelson measured the diameter of Betelgeuse with a 20-foot interferometer mounted on the Hooker telescope in 1920.

Later history

Light pollution from Los Angeles reduced the site's value for deep-sky research, and the Carnegie Observatories transferred operations to the Mount Wilson Institute in 1989. The site now hosts the CHARA Array interferometers operated by Georgia State University, along with public outreach, educational programs, and the historic telescopes maintained for observing nights and tours.
