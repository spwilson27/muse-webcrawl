Distracted driving

Distracted driving is the act of driving while engaged in other activities which distract the driver's attention away from the road. Distractions are shown to compromise the safety of the driver, passengers, pedestrians, and people in other vehicles.

Types of distraction

Safety researchers classify distractions into three main types:

  • Visual — taking one's eyes off the road, for example to read a text message or look at a navigation screen.
  • Manual — taking one's hands off the wheel, for example to eat, adjust controls or type.
  • Cognitive — taking one's mind off driving, for example during an engrossing conversation, whether on a hand-held or hands-free device.

Texting while driving combines all three types and is considered especially hazardous: studies using naturalistic driving data have found that texting substantially multiplies the risk of a crash or near-crash.

Prevalence and effects

Mobile phone use is the most studied modern distraction. The U.S. National Highway Traffic Safety Administration has attributed several thousand deaths per year to distraction-affected crashes, and distraction is estimated to be a factor in a significant share of all police-reported crashes. Young and inexperienced drivers are disproportionately involved. Research also shows that hands-free phone conversations carry much of the same cognitive risk as hand-held conversations, a finding sometimes called the "hands-free myth".

Laws and countermeasures

Many jurisdictions ban hand-held phone use while driving, and a number ban all phone use by novice drivers. Enforcement ranges from primary offences (police may stop a vehicle solely for the violation) to secondary offences. Countermeasures also include public-awareness campaigns, graduated licensing provisions, in-vehicle technologies that block or limit phone functions, and road designs that reduce the consequences of momentary inattention.

Distraction beyond phones

Distractions also include eating and drinking, grooming, adjusting the radio, interacting with passengers, unrestrained pets, and external events such as billboards and crash scenes ("rubbernecking"). Commercial drivers face additional rules covering dispatching devices and paperwork.
