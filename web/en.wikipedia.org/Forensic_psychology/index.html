Forensic psychology

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Forensic psychology is the application of scientific psychology and its research methods to legal questions and proceedings. Forensic psychologists work within the criminal justice and civil court systems, conducting assessments, providing expert testimony, treating offenders, and consulting with law enforcement, attorneys and the courts.

FieldApplied psychology
Key areasCompetency evaluation, criminal responsibility, risk assessment, custody evaluation
RecognitionSpecialty recognized by the American Psychological Association (2001)

History

An early landmark in the field was Hugo Münsterberg's On the Witness Stand (1908), which argued that psychological science could improve the reliability of eyewitness testimony. The discipline grew through the twentieth century as courts increasingly admitted psychological expertise on questions such as competency to stand trial and the mental state of defendants. Landmark cases, including Dusky v. United States (1960), which set the standard for competency, and Jenkins v. United States (1962), which affirmed psychologists as expert witnesses, helped define the specialty, formally recognized by the American Psychological Association in 2001.

Roles and tasks

Assessment

Forensic psychologists evaluate defendants' competency to stand trial, criminal responsibility (the insanity defense), risk of future violence or sexual reoffending, and, in civil matters, psychological injury and child custody. They use structured professional judgment tools and actuarial instruments alongside clinical interviews and records review.

Consultation and testimony

Practitioners consult on jury selection, witness credibility and police interrogation practices, and they testify as expert witnesses. Unlike treating clinicians, forensic evaluators owe their primary duty to the court or retaining party rather than to the person evaluated, a distinction governed by the American Psychological Association's Specialty Guidelines for Forensic Psychology.

Treatment and research

Many forensic psychologists provide treatment in prisons, forensic hospitals and probation settings, and conduct research on eyewitness memory, deception, offender rehabilitation and the prediction of dangerousness.

Training

Forensic psychologists typically hold a doctorate in clinical, counseling or school psychology, with specialized coursework and supervised experience in forensic settings, and licensure as psychologists. Board certification is available through the American Board of Forensic Psychology.
