The Three Rs (animal research)

PrinciplesReplacement, Reduction, Refinement

The Three Rs are guiding principles for the more ethical use of animals in product testing and scientific research. They were first described by W. M. S. Russell and R. L. Burch in The Principles of Humane Experimental Technique (1959), commissioned by the Universities Federation for Animal Welfare. The principles are now embedded in legislation and research-funding requirements in many countries, including the European Union's Directive 2010/63/EU and policies of the UK, US, and other research systems.

The principles

Replacement means using methods that avoid or replace animal use — for example computer models, cell cultures, or human volunteers — where they can achieve the same scientific purpose.

Reduction means obtaining comparable information from fewer animals, or more information from the same number, through better experimental design and statistics.

Refinement means modifying procedures and husbandry to minimise pain, suffering, distress, or lasting harm and to improve animal welfare — for example through anaesthesia, analgesia, environmental enrichment, and humane endpoints.

Implementation

In the UK, the National Centre for the Replacement, Refinement and Reduction of Animals in Research (NC3Rs), founded in 2004, promotes the principles. Ethics committees and animal care and use committees (IACUCs) in many jurisdictions require researchers to justify animal numbers and demonstrate that replacements and refinements have been considered. Critics debate how far the principles, designed to reform rather than abolish animal research, have reduced overall animal use.
