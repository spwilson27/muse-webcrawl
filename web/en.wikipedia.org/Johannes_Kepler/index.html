Johannes Kepler (1571–1630) was a German astronomer, mathematician, and astrologer. A key figure in the seventeenth-century Scientific Revolution, he is best known for his laws of planetary motion, derived from Tycho Brahe's precise observations, which provided one of the foundations for Newton's theory of universal gravitation.

Life and work

Kepler taught mathematics at Graz, where his Mysterium Cosmographicum (1596) defended Copernican heliocentrism and sought geometric harmony in the planetary system. Forced out by Counter-Reformation persecution, he joined Tycho Brahe in Prague in 1600 and succeeded him as imperial mathematician. Analysing Tycho's data on Mars, Kepler abandoned circular orbits and published his first two laws in Astronomia Nova (1609): planets move in ellipses with the Sun at one focus, and sweep out equal areas in equal times. The third law — the square of the orbital period is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis — appeared in Harmonices Mundi (1619).

Kepler also founded modern optics (Astronomiae Pars Optica, 1604; Dioptrice, 1611), explained the telescope, discovered the supernova of 1604, and produced the Rudolphine Tables (1627), the most accurate astronomical tables of his age. His mother was tried for witchcraft, and he defended her in a long legal campaign. He died in Regensburg in 1630 during the Thirty Years' War.
