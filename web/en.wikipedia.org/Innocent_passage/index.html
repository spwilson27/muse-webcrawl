Innocent passage

Innocent passage is a concept in the law of the sea defined by the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS). It is the right of a vessel to pass through the territorial sea of another state, subject to certain restrictions, provided the passage is continuous and expeditious and not prejudicial to the peace, good order, or security of the coastal state.

Definition

Under UNCLOS Articles 17–26, ships of all states enjoy the right of innocent passage through the territorial sea (up to 12 nautical miles from the baseline). Passage is not innocent if the vessel engages in activities such as the threat or use of force, weapons exercises, espionage, fishing, pollution, or research without consent. Submarines must navigate on the surface and show their flag while in innocent passage. The coastal state may regulate passage for safety and environmental reasons and may temporarily suspend it in specified areas for security, but it may not hamper or discriminate against passage in general.

Debates

States disagree on whether prior notification or authorisation may be required, particularly for warships. Some states require notification by foreign warships; others, notably maritime powers such as the United States, maintain that no permission is needed and conduct freedom of navigation operations to assert the right. The right of innocent passage is distinct from the stronger regime of transit passage through international straits, which cannot be suspended.
