Black swan theory

Black swan event
ProponentNassim Nicholas Taleb
IntroducedFooled by Randomness (2001); The Black Swan (2007)
CharacteristicsRarity, extreme impact, retrospective predictability

The black swan theory is a metaphor developed by Nassim Nicholas Taleb describing events that are extremely rare, have severe impact, and are explained away as predictable in hindsight. The term derives from the ancient Western assumption that all swans were white — overturned when Dutch explorers encountered black swans in Australia in 1697.

The three attributes

Taleb defines a black swan event by three properties: (1) it is an outlier lying outside the realm of regular expectations; (2) it carries extreme impact; and (3) after the fact, human nature makes us concoct explanations that make it appear predictable. Examples frequently cited include the rise of the Internet, the September 11 attacks, and the 2008 financial crisis.

Implications

Taleb argues that history is driven disproportionately by such events, that statistical models based on the normal distribution dangerously underestimate them, and that decision-makers should build robustness — and even benefit ("antifragility") — rather than attempt precise prediction of rare events.
