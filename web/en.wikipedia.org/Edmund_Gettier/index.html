Edmund Gettier

Edmund L. Gettier III (31 October 1927 – 23 March 2021) was an American philosopher best known for a three-page paper published in 1963 that overturned a definition of knowledge accepted for centuries. The counterexamples he described, now called Gettier problems, remain central to epistemology.

Life and career

Gettier was born in Baltimore, Maryland, and earned his doctorate from Cornell University in 1957, where he studied under Norman Malcolm and Max Black. He taught at Wayne State University and then, from 1967, at the University of Massachusetts Amherst, where he remained for the rest of his career. Famously unprolific, he published very little beyond his celebrated paper, "Is Justified True Belief Knowledge?"

The Gettier problem

Philosophers had traditionally defined knowledge as justified true belief: a person knows a proposition if it is true, they believe it, and their belief is justified. Gettier constructed cases in which all three conditions hold, yet the belief is true only by luck — for instance, a man deduces that "the man who will get the job has ten coins in his pocket" from justified but false premises, and the conclusion turns out true, unbeknownst to him, of himself. Intuitively, such a person lacks knowledge, showing the traditional definition is insufficient.

Impact

The paper, published in the journal Analysis in 1963, launched decades of attempts to repair the definition — adding "no false lemmas" conditions, causal and reliabilist theories, and defeasibility conditions — or to argue that knowledge is a primitive state not analysable in more basic terms. Gettier's counterexample is now a standard starting point in the philosophical study of knowledge.
