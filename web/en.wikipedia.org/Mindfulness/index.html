Mindfulness

Mindfulness is the cognitive skill, usually developed through meditation, of sustaining meta-awareness of the contents of one's own mind in the present moment. The term derives from the Pali word sati, a significant element of Buddhist traditions. In recent decades, clinical psychologists and psychiatrists have developed a number of therapeutic applications based on mindfulness for helping people experiencing a variety of psychological conditions.

Jon Kabat-Zinn, who founded the Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR) program at the University of Massachusetts Medical Center in 1979, defined mindfulness as "paying attention in a particular way: on purpose, in the present moment, and nonjudgmentally".

Buddhist origins

Mindfulness (sati) is the first factor of the Noble Eightfold Path in Buddhism and plays a central role in the Satipatthana Sutta, which describes the four foundations of mindfulness: contemplation of the body, of feelings, of mind, and of phenomena. In early Buddhist texts, sati originally meant "recollection" or "memory", and its function was to keep the teachings of the Buddha in mind during practice.

Clinical applications

Mindfulness-based interventions include mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR), mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (MBCT), and elements of dialectical behavior therapy and acceptance and commitment therapy. Studies have shown mindfulness programs help reduce anxiety, depression, and pain, though researchers note that more rigorous trials are needed and that the quality of early evidence was often modest.

Criticism

Critics have raised concerns about the secularization and commercialization of mindfulness, sometimes described as "McMindfulness", arguing that stripped of its ethical and communal Buddhist context, the practice can be used to shift responsibility for stress onto individuals. Others note potential adverse effects of intensive meditation, including anxiety and depersonalization, which are underreported in the literature.
