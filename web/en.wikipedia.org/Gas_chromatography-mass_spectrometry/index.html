Gas chromatography–mass spectrometry

Gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC-MS) is an analytical method that combines the separation power of gas chromatography with the identification capabilities of mass spectrometry. It is widely regarded as a "gold standard" for the identification of substances in complex mixtures, and is used extensively in forensic science, environmental analysis, food safety and drug testing.

How it works

A sample is vaporised and carried by an inert gas (usually helium or hydrogen) through a long capillary column coated with a stationary phase. Different compounds travel through the column at different rates according to their volatility and affinity for the stationary phase, and so leave (elute from) the column at different, characteristic retention times. As each component elutes, it enters the mass spectrometer, where it is ionised — commonly by electron ionisation — and fragmented. The resulting mass spectrum, a pattern of fragment ions by mass-to-charge ratio, acts as a chemical fingerprint that can be matched against reference libraries to identify the compound.

Instrumentation

A GC-MS system consists of a gas chromatograph (injector, oven and column), an interface, and a mass spectrometer comprising an ion source, a mass analyser (frequently a quadrupole, ion trap or time-of-flight analyser) and a detector. Modern instruments are highly automated and sensitive, detecting analytes at picogram levels or below.

Applications

  • Forensics: identification of drugs, explosives, accelerants in arson debris, and poisons.
  • Environmental monitoring: detection of pesticides, pollutants and volatile organic compounds in air, water and soil.
  • Medicine and sport: screening for drugs of abuse and performance-enhancing substances; diagnosis of metabolic disorders.
  • Food and flavour: analysis of aromas, contaminants and authenticity.
  • Space exploration: GC-MS instruments have flown on probes such as Viking, Cassini-Huygens and Rosetta.

Limitations

GC-MS is best suited to volatile, thermally stable compounds; non-volatile or heat-sensitive analytes usually require derivatisation or an alternative technique such as liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC-MS).
