Spin (physics)

Spin
TypeIntrinsic angular momentum
Introduced1925 (Uhlenbeck and Goudsmit)
ValuesInteger or half-integer multiples of ħ
ExamplesElectron, proton, quark: spin-½; photon: spin-1; graviton: spin-2 (hypothetical)

Spin is an intrinsic form of angular momentum carried by elementary particles, composite particles and atomic nuclei. Despite its name, it does not correspond to literal rotation of a body; it is a purely quantum-mechanical property with no classical analogue, though it behaves mathematically like angular momentum.

History

Spin was proposed in 1925 by George Uhlenbeck and Samuel Goudsmit to explain fine structure in atomic spectra and the results of the Stern–Gerlach experiment (1922), in which silver atoms were deflected into two discrete beams. Wolfgang Pauli incorporated spin into quantum mechanics with his spin matrices and the exclusion principle; Paul Dirac's relativistic equation (1928) predicted electron spin naturally.

Properties

Spin is quantised: a particle has a fixed spin quantum number s (0, ½, 1, ³⁄₂, …) with total spin angular momentum √[s(s+1)]ħ, and its measured component along any axis takes values from −sħ to +sħ in steps of ħ. The spin–statistics theorem connects half-integer spin to fermionic behaviour (obeying the Pauli exclusion principle) and integer spin to bosonic behaviour.

Applications

Electron spin underlies magnetism, the structure of the periodic table, and technologies including magnetic resonance imaging (via nuclear spin) and spintronics. Spin states are also used as qubits in quantum computing.
