Lymphatic filariasis

SpecialtyInfectious disease
CausesFilarial nematode worms spread by mosquitoes
PreventionMass drug administration, mosquito control
TreatmentAlbendazole, ivermectin, diethylcarbamazine

Lymphatic filariasis is a parasitic disease caused by microscopic, thread-like filarial nematode worms. The adult worms live in the human lymphatic system and disrupt its function. It is considered a neglected tropical disease and is one of the world's leading causes of long-term disability.

Cause and transmission

Three species of worm cause the disease: Wuchereria bancrofti, responsible for about 90% of cases, and Brugia malayi and Brugia timori. The worms are transmitted to humans through the bites of infected mosquitoes. Larvae migrate to the lymphatic vessels, where they mature into adults that can live for six to eight years, producing millions of microscopic microfilariae that circulate in the blood.

Symptoms

Most infections are asymptomatic but still damage the lymphatic system and kidneys. Repeated episodes of inflammation can lead to lymphoedema — severe swelling of the legs, arms, breasts, or genitals — and, in its most extreme form, elephantiasis, with thickened, roughened skin. In men, hydrocele (swelling of the scrotum) is common. Acute attacks of fever and pain accompany secondary bacterial infections.

Prevention and control

The World Health Organization's Global Programme to Eliminate Lymphatic Filariasis relies on mass drug administration: annual single doses of combinations of albendazole with ivermectin or diethylcarbamazine given to entire at-risk communities to interrupt transmission. Morbidity management — careful hygiene of swollen limbs, exercise, and surgery for hydrocele — alleviates suffering in those already affected.
