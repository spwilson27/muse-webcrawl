Ramrod

A ramrod (or rammer) is a metal or wooden device used with early firearms to push the projectile up against the propellant (mainly gunpowder). Ramrods were used with muzzle-loading weapons such as muskets and pistols, and were also commonly used with cap-and-ball revolvers, where the rod was used to seat the ball firmly on the powder in each chamber.

Description

The ramrod was typically carried in pipes beneath the barrel of the weapon, from which it could be drawn, reversed, and used to drive the charge home. Early ramrods were made of wood; these were liable to break and were later replaced by metal rods, often of iron or steel, sometimes with a tip shaped to fit the bullet or fitted with attachments for cleaning.

Use

To load a musket, the soldier poured a measured charge of powder down the barrel, followed by the ball — often wrapped in a paper patch or the cartridge paper itself — and then used the ramrod to push the ball firmly down onto the powder. A properly seated charge was essential both for accuracy and safety: an air gap between powder and ball could burst the barrel.

Figurative use

By extension, "ramrod" came to describe anything stiff or straight, and in American usage a forceful foreman or trail boss who drives work forward. The word is also used as an adjective meaning rigidly erect, as in "ramrod straight".
