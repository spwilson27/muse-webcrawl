Euclidean distance

In mathematics, the Euclidean distance between two points in Euclidean space is the length of the line segment between them. It can be calculated from the Cartesian coordinates of the points using the Pythagorean theorem, and is occasionally called the Pythagorean distance.

Definition

The Euclidean distance between points p and q is the length of the line segment connecting them. In Cartesian coordinates, if p = (p1, p2, …, pn) and q = (q1, q2, …, qn) are two points in Euclidean n-space, then the distance d from p to q is given by:

d(p, q) = √( (q1 − p1)² + (q2 − p2)² + ⋯ + (qn − pn)² )

In one dimension, the distance between two points on the real line is simply the absolute value of their difference. In two dimensions it reduces to the familiar Pythagorean formula.

Properties

Euclidean distance is a metric: it is non-negative, symmetric, zero only for identical points, and satisfies the triangle inequality. The Euclidean distance is also invariant under rotations and translations of the coordinate system.

Applications

Euclidean distance is used throughout mathematics, physics, statistics, and computer science, including in clustering algorithms, nearest-neighbor search, and machine learning, although in high dimensions other metrics such as Manhattan distance may sometimes be preferred.
