View from the Window at Le Gras

The earliest surviving photograph made in a camera, c. 1826 or 1827.

View from the Window at Le Gras (French: Point de vue du Gras) is a heliographic image and the oldest surviving camera photograph. It was created by French inventor Nicéphore Niépce in 1826 or 1827 at Saint-Loup-de-Varennes, France, and shows parts of the buildings and surrounding countryside of his estate, Le Gras, as seen from a high window.

Niépce captured the scene with a camera obscura focused onto a polished pewter plate coated with bitumen of Judea, a naturally occurring asphalt. The bitumen hardened where exposed to light; the unhardened portions were then washed away with lavender oil, leaving a permanent image. The exposure is thought to have lasted many hours or even days, which is why sunlight appears to illuminate buildings on both sides. The plate measures about 16.2 by 20.2 centimetres.

Rediscovery

The plate was long thought lost. Niépce gave it to the English botanist Francis Bauer in 1827; it passed through collectors and was exhibited occasionally, last publicly in 1898, before vanishing into storage. Photo historian Helmut Gernsheim tracked it down in 1952 in a trunk belonging to the descendants of its last recorded owner, and the image became famous as the world's first photograph. Gernsheim made a retouched reproduction — for decades the most widely published version — before donating the original to the University of Texas at Austin, where it is displayed in the Harry Ransom Center in a sealed, oxygen-free case.

Significance

The image marks the birth of photography: the first successful attempt to fix an image from nature permanently using light-sensitive material and a camera. Niépce's partnership with Louis Daguerre, formed in 1829, led after Niépce's death to the daguerreotype, announced in 1839.
