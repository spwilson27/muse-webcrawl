Linguistic modality

[Image: Diagram of modal expressions across languages]
Modality encodes a speaker's attitude toward a proposition.

In linguistics, modality is the use of language to express a speaker's attitude toward the truth, necessity, possibility, obligation or desirability of a proposition. Modal expressions include auxiliary verbs such as English can, must, may and should, as well as adverbs, particles, affixes and dedicated grammatical moods such as the subjunctive.

Types of modality

Semanticists commonly distinguish several types:

  • Epistemic modality concerns knowledge and belief: "She must be home by now."
  • Deontic modality concerns obligation and permission: "You must wear a seatbelt."
  • Dynamic modality concerns ability and volition: "He can swim."
  • Alethic modality concerns logical necessity and possibility.

Modal logic and formal semantics

The study of modality in language draws on modal logic, in which necessity and possibility are treated as operators over possible worlds. A proposition is necessarily true if it holds in all accessible possible worlds, and possible if it holds in at least one. This framework, developed by philosophers such as Saul Kripke and applied to natural language by linguists including Angelika Kratzer, underlies much contemporary research on modal meaning.

Expression across languages

Languages differ widely in how they encode modality. Some, such as Turkish and Quechua, mark evidentiality and modality with verb suffixes; others use sequences of auxiliary verbs, modal adverbs, or intonation. Many languages lack a distinct class of modal verbs altogether and rely on ordinary verbs of wanting, knowing or being able.
