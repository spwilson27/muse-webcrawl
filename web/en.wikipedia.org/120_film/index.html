120 film

120 is a popular photographic film format for still photography, introduced by Kodak in 1901 for its Brownie No. 2 camera. It is the most common medium format film, surviving into the twenty-first century while most other roll film formats have been discontinued.

Introduced1901, by Kodak
Film width61.5 mm (unperforated)
BackingPaper-backed roll on a spool
Common frame sizes6×4.5, 6×6, 6×7, 6×9 cm
StatusIn production

Characteristics

The film is wound on a spool with a protective backing paper printed with frame numbers, which are read through a red window on early cameras or by automatic counters on later ones. Because the film itself carries no frame markings or perforations, the number of exposures per roll depends on the camera's format: 16 frames at 6×4.5 cm, 12 at 6×6, 10 at 6×7, or 8 at 6×9. A wider version, 220 film, omits most of the backing paper to double the length, but it has been discontinued.

Use

120 film was the standard professional format for much of the twentieth century, used in cameras such as the Rolleiflex twin-lens reflex, the Hasselblad 500 series, and the Mamiya and Pentax medium-format systems. It remains in production by manufacturers including Kodak, Ilford and Fujifilm, and is widely used by fine-art, portrait and landscape photographers as well as in Holga and other toy cameras.
