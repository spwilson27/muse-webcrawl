Ninth Fort

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Ninth Fort (Lithuanian: Devintas Fortas) is a stronghold in the northern part of Kaunas, Lithuania. Built in the late 19th and early 20th centuries as part of the Kaunas Fortress of the Russian Empire, it is notorious as a place of imprisonment and mass murder during the Nazi occupation of Lithuania, when tens of thousands of Jews from Lithuania and elsewhere in Europe were killed there. Today the fort houses a museum and a monumental memorial to the victims.

History

Construction of the fort began in 1902 and was still incomplete at the outbreak of the First World War. It was the most modern of the nine forts ringing Kaunas, built of concrete with armoured casemates. In independent Lithuania it served from 1924 as a hard-labour prison camp of the Kaunas prison. During the first Soviet occupation (1940–41) it was used by the NKVD for holding political prisoners.

The Holocaust

Following the German invasion of the Soviet Union in June 1941, the fort became a site of mass executions carried out by German SS and SD units and Lithuanian auxiliaries. Jews from the Kaunas Ghetto were marched to the fort and shot into prepared pits; the largest single action, the "Great Action" of 29 October 1941, murdered roughly 9,000–10,000 Jews in one day. Transports of Jews deported from Germany, Austria, France, and elsewhere were also killed on arrival in late 1941. Estimates of the total number of victims at the Ninth Fort range from about 30,000 to 50,000. In December 1943 a group of prisoners assigned to exhuming and burning the bodies — the so-called "burning brigade" — escaped and publicised what had happened.

Museum and memorial

A museum was established at the fort in 1958. In 1984 a monumental sculptural group, 32 metres high, designed by Alfonsas Vincentas Ambraziūnas, was erected near the mass graves in memory of the victims of Nazism. The museum's exhibitions document the history of the fortress, the prison, and the Holocaust in Lithuania. Walls of the fort preserve inscriptions left by prisoners, including those of French Jews of Convoy 73 deported in May 1944.
