Takasugi Shinsaku

Takasugi Shinsaku (高杉 晋作, 12 September 1839 – 17 May 1867) was a samurai of Chōshū Domain who played a leading role in the movement to overthrow the Tokugawa shogunate during the Bakumatsu period. He is best remembered as the founder of the Kiheitai, a revolutionary militia that broke with feudal tradition by recruiting men regardless of social class.

Early life

Takasugi was born in Hagi, the castle town of Chōshū, into a middle-ranking samurai family. He studied at the Shōka Sonjuku, the private school of Yoshida Shōin, alongside other future leaders of the Meiji Restoration such as Itō Hirobumi and Yamagata Aritomo. Yoshida's teaching of sonnō jōi ("revere the emperor, expel the barbarians") profoundly influenced him.

Activism

In 1862 Takasugi traveled to Shanghai aboard a shogunal vessel, where he witnessed the devastation of the Taiping Rebellion and the reach of Western imperial power in China — an experience that convinced him Japan had to modernize to avoid colonization. Returning home, he participated in attacks on foreigners and on the shogunate's representatives.

Kiheitai

In 1863, after Western warships bombarded Shimonoseki, Takasugi organized the Kiheitai, an auxiliary rifle militia open to commoners and peasants as well as samurai. During the Chōshū civil conflict of 1864–65 (the "Chōshū expedition" crisis), his forces defeated the conservative faction, setting Chōshū on the path that led, with Satsuma, to the Boshin War and the Meiji Restoration. Takasugi did not live to see the outcome: he died of tuberculosis in 1867 at the age of 27.

Legacy

Takasugi is remembered in Japan as a brilliant, mercurial visionary — a "revolutionary" whose ideas about meritocratic military organization anticipated the modern Meiji state.
