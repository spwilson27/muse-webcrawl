Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease

Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease (CMT) is a group of inherited disorders of the peripheral nervous system characterised by progressive loss of muscle tissue and touch sensation in the extremities. It is one of the most common inherited neurological disorders, affecting roughly 1 in 2,500 people, and is named after the neurologists Jean-Martin Charcot, Pierre Marie and Howard Henry Tooth, who described it in 1886.

Symptoms

Symptoms usually begin in childhood or adolescence and progress slowly: weakness and wasting of the foot and lower leg muscles (producing a "stork leg" or inverted champagne-bottle appearance), foot deformities such as high arches (pes cavus) and hammer toes, frequent ankle sprains, and later weakness of the hands. Sensation is reduced in a stocking-glove distribution, and deep tendon reflexes are diminished.

Causes and types

CMT is genetically heterogeneous, with mutations in dozens of genes. The commonest form, CMT1A, is caused by a duplication of the PMP22 gene on chromosome 17. Types are classified by whether the primary defect is demyelination of the nerve (CMT1, with slowed conduction) or degeneration of the axon itself (CMT2), along with rarer X-linked and recessive forms.

Treatment

There is no cure; management is supportive, involving physiotherapy, occupational therapy, orthoses and orthopaedic surgery for deformity. Certain drugs, notably vincristine, can worsen the neuropathy and must be avoided.
