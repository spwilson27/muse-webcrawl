Louis-Joseph de Montcalm-Gozon, Marquis de Montcalm de Saint Véran (February 28, 1712 – September 14, 1759) was a French soldier best known as the commander of the French forces in North America during the Seven Years' War (whose North American theatre is called the French and Indian War in the United States). He was mortally wounded at the Battle of the Plains of Abraham outside Quebec City, and his death, alongside that of his British opponent James Wolfe at the same battle, became one of the most celebrated episodes of the war.

Early career

Montcalm was born into an ancient noble family of Rouergue, in southern France. He joined the French army at a young age and served with distinction in the War of the Polish Succession and the War of the Austrian Succession, being wounded several times and rising steadily in rank. By 1756 he had reached the rank of maréchal de camp.

Service in New France

In 1756 Montcalm was appointed to command French regular troops in New France. He achieved a string of victories: the capture of Fort Oswego (1756), the capture of Fort William Henry (1757) — an event followed by the killing of British prisoners by France's Indigenous allies, which shadowed his reputation — and a dramatic defensive triumph at the Battle of Carillon (Fort Ticonderoga) in 1758, where his roughly 3,600 men repelled a British force of some 16,000.

Montcalm frequently clashed with the Governor General of New France, the Marquis de Vaudreuil, over strategy: Vaudreuil favored the traditional petite guerre of raids, while Montcalm preferred European-style set-piece tactics. Meanwhile British resources in North America grew overwhelming.

Quebec and death

In 1759 a British expedition under James Wolfe sailed up the St. Lawrence and besieged Quebec. After a summer of bombardment and failed attacks, British troops scaled the cliffs west of the city on the night of September 12–13 and deployed on the Plains of Abraham. Montcalm engaged them on September 13 and was decisively defeated in a battle lasting less than an hour. Mortally wounded, he was carried into the city and died the following morning, reportedly saying he was glad he would not live to see Quebec surrender. The city capitulated days later, and Montreal fell in 1760, ending French rule in Canada.
