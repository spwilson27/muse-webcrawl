West Florida stretched along the northern Gulf Coast from the Mississippi to the Apalachicola.

West Florida was a region on the northern coast of the Gulf of Mexico that underwent several boundary and sovereignty changes during the 18th and early 19th centuries. It was created by Britain in 1763 from lands ceded by France and Spain at the end of the Seven Years' War, with its capital at Pensacola, and initially extended from the Mississippi River east to the Apalachicola River and north to the 31st parallel (later moved to 32°28′).

Spanish rule and American acquisition

Spain regained West Florida during the American Revolutionary War — Bernardo de Gálvez captured Pensacola in 1781 — and it was formally returned by the Treaty of Paris (1783). The western portion, including Baton Rouge and Mobile districts, was claimed by the United States as part of the Louisiana Purchase; American forces seized Baton Rouge in 1810 (after the short-lived Republic of West Florida) and Mobile in 1813. The remainder was ceded with East Florida under the Adams–Onís Treaty of 1819 and transferred in 1821.

Legacy

The former West Florida now forms the Florida Panhandle and the Gulf Coast portions of Alabama, Mississippi, and Louisiana (the Florida Parishes). Its history is commemorated in the flag and name of the Republic of West Florida and in regional heritage festivals.
