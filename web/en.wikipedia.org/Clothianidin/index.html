Clothianidin

Clothianidin
TypeNeonicotinoid insecticide
Mode of actionNicotinic acetylcholine receptor agonist
Main usesSeed treatment, foliar and soil insect control

Clothianidin is an insecticide belonging to the neonicotinoid class, developed by Takeda Chemical Industries and Bayer. Like other neonicotinoids, it acts on the central nervous system of insects by binding to nicotinic acetylcholine receptors, causing paralysis and death. It is systemic: plants take it up from treated seed or soil and distribute it through their tissues, protecting the growing plant from sucking and chewing pests.

Uses

Clothianidin is widely used as a seed treatment on crops such as maize (corn), canola, cereals, and soybeans, and as a foliar or soil treatment on a variety of fruits and vegetables. It is effective against aphids, whiteflies, thrips, beetles, and other pest insects. It is also the active metabolite of another neonicotinoid, thiamethoxam.

Environmental concerns

Clothianidin has been at the center of controversy over its effects on pollinators, especially honey bees. Because it is systemic, residues occur in pollen and nectar, and dust from treated seed can expose bees during planting. Concerns about sublethal effects on bee navigation, learning, and colony health led the European Union to restrict outdoor uses of clothianidin and two related neonicotinoids in 2013 and to ban nearly all outdoor uses in 2018. Regulatory assessments in other countries, including the United States and Canada, have produced a mix of restrictions and label changes.

Properties

Clothianidin is moderately persistent in soil and mobile enough to be taken up by plants and to appear in water. Its toxicity to insects is far greater than to mammals because of the different structure of their nicotinic receptors.
