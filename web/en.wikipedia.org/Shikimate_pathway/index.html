Shikimate pathway


The seven steps of the shikimate pathway produce chorismate.

The shikimate pathway is a seven-step metabolic pathway used by bacteria, archaea, fungi, algae, some protozoans, and plants for the biosynthesis of folates and aromatic amino acids (phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tryptophan). The pathway is absent from animals, which must obtain these amino acids from their diet, making its enzymes attractive targets for herbicides and antimicrobial drugs.

Steps

The pathway begins with the condensation of phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) and erythrose 4-phosphate to form 3-deoxy-D-arabino-heptulosonate 7-phosphate (DAHP). Through a sequence of reactions passing through the intermediates 3-dehydroquinate, 3-dehydroshikimate, and shikimic acid — the compound from which the pathway takes its name, first isolated from the Japanese star anise (shikimi) — the pathway produces shikimate 3-phosphate and then 5-enolpyruvylshikimate 3-phosphate (EPSP), which is converted to chorismate, the branch point for the synthesis of the three aromatic amino acids and many other aromatic compounds.

Glyphosate

The herbicide glyphosate kills plants by inhibiting EPSP synthase, the sixth enzyme of the pathway. Because animals lack the pathway, glyphosate has low direct toxicity to them. Crops engineered to express a glyphosate-insensitive bacterial EPSP synthase (such as Roundup Ready varieties) survive spraying. Shikimic acid itself is industrially valuable as the starting material for the synthesis of the antiviral drug oseltamivir (Tamiflu).

Regulation

The first enzyme, DAHP synthase, exists in bacteria as multiple isoenzymes, each feedback-inhibited by one of the three end-product amino acids, allowing balanced production. In plants the pathway is localised in plastids and is regulated largely at the transcriptional level in response to light and stress.
