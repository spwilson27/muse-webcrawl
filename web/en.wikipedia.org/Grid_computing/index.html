Grid computing is the use of widely distributed computer resources to reach a common goal. A computing grid can be thought of as a distributed system with non-interactive workloads that involve many files. Grid computing is distinguished from conventional high-performance computing systems such as cluster computing in that grid computers have each node set to perform a different task or application; grid nodes also tend to be more loosely coupled, heterogeneous and geographically dispersed. The analogy used to popularise the concept, by Ian Foster and Carl Kesselman in the late 1990s, was the electrical power grid: computing power available on demand from a shared infrastructure.

Concepts and architecture

A grid is built from middleware that coordinates resources across administrative domains while letting each site retain local control. Foster proposed three criteria for a grid: it coordinates resources not subject to centralised control; it uses standard, open, general-purpose protocols and interfaces; and it delivers nontrivial qualities of service. Common architectural layers include the fabric (computers, storage, networks), resource and connectivity layers (security, authentication, resource access), the collective layer (brokering, scheduling, replica management) and applications. Globus Toolkit, descended from the Globus Alliance's work, became the de facto standard software and gave rise to grid service standards such as OGSA and WSRF.

Applications

The largest scientific grid is the Worldwide LHC Computing Grid (WLCG), built to store and analyse the tens of petabytes of data produced annually by CERN's Large Hadron Collider, federating hundreds of sites in over 40 countries in a tiered hierarchy. Other major grids include the Open Science Grid (US), EGI (Europe), and national e-infrastructures. Volunteer computing platforms such as BOINC — which powered SETI@home and Einstein@Home — apply grid-like ideas to donated home computers, and are sometimes called "desktop grids".

Relation to cloud computing

From the late 2000s, commercial cloud computing — centralised, virtualised data centres offered on pay-as-you-go terms, such as Amazon Web Services — absorbed much of the grid vision's "utility computing" promise, and the term "grid" declined in industry use. Scientific grids continue in production, though increasingly layered on cloud and high-throughput computing resources.
