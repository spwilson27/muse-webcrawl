Film stock

Film stock is an analog medium used for recording motion pictures or animation. It is a strip or sheet of transparent plastic film base on which a gelatin emulsion is coated, containing microscopically small light-sensitive silver halide crystals. Exposed and developed film produces a series of still images — frames — which, when projected in rapid succession, create the illusion of motion.

Characteristics

Film stocks are described by their gauge (such as 8 mm, 16 mm, 35 mm or 65/70 mm), their speed or sensitivity to light (measured as an ISO/ASA rating), their colour balance (tungsten or daylight), and whether they are negative, reversal or print stocks. Faster stocks are more sensitive but produce coarser grain.

History

Flexible transparent film base was introduced in the late 1880s, initially made of highly flammable cellulose nitrate, which was replaced for safety by acetate "safety film" in the mid-20th century, and later by polyester bases. Colour processes such as Technicolor and Eastmancolor made colour cinematography standard from the 1950s.

Manufacturers

For most of cinema history the market was dominated by Kodak, with Fujifilm and Agfa as significant rivals. Fujifilm discontinued its motion picture stocks in 2013, leaving Kodak the principal manufacturer, though smaller companies such as ORWO have continued or resumed production.

Digital era

Since the 2000s, digital cinematography has largely replaced film for production and distribution. Nevertheless, some filmmakers continue to shoot on film for its aesthetic qualities, and film remains an important medium for long-term archival storage.
