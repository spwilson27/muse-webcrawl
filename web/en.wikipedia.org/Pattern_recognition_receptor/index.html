Pattern recognition receptor

Pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) are proteins capable of identifying molecules that are broadly shared by pathogens but distinguishable from host molecules, collectively called pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), or endogenous molecules released from damaged cells, called damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs). They are a central component of the innate immune system, encoded in the germline and presented by cells including macrophages, dendritic cells, neutrophils and epithelial cells.

Classes

The major PRR families are the Toll-like receptors (TLRs) on cell surfaces and endosomes, which detect bacterial lipopolysaccharide, flagellin and viral nucleic acids; the cytosolic NOD-like receptors (NLRs), some of which assemble inflammasomes that activate caspase-1 and the cytokine IL-1β; the RIG-I-like receptors (RLRs), cytosolic sensors of viral RNA; and the C-type lectin receptors, which recognise fungal and bacterial carbohydrates. Plants possess analogous cell-surface PRRs that confer pattern-triggered immunity.

Function

On binding their ligands, PRRs initiate signalling cascades — often converging on the transcription factor NF-κB — that induce inflammatory cytokines, chemokines and type I interferons, recruit immune cells, and prime the adaptive immune response. Because PRRs distinguish microbial from self molecules, they provide the innate immune system's primary means of sensing infection.

History

The concept was proposed by Charles Janeway in 1989, who predicted that innate immune cells would recognise conserved microbial patterns through dedicated receptors. The prediction was confirmed when the Drosophila Toll protein was shown to mediate antifungal defence (1996) and mammalian TLR4 was identified as the receptor for lipopolysaccharide (1998), work recognised by the 2011 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.
