Baleen whales (systematic name Mysticeti) are a parvorder of carnivorous marine mammals forming one of the two living groups of cetaceans, the other being the toothed whales (Odontoceti). Instead of teeth, baleen whales have baleen plates in their mouths — fringed plates of keratin hanging from the upper jaw — which they use to filter vast quantities of small prey, such as krill, plankton and small fish, from seawater.

Characteristics

Baleen whales include the largest animals ever known: the blue whale can reach about 30 metres in length and over 170 tonnes in weight. Mysticetes have two blowholes (unlike the single blowhole of toothed whales), a symmetrical skull, and they do not echolocate, instead producing some of the loudest and lowest-frequency sounds of any animal, used for long-distance communication.

Feeding

Feeding strategies vary by family. Rorquals (Balaenopteridae — blue, fin, humpback, minke whales) are lunge feeders with expandable throat grooves that engulf huge mouthfuls of water which are then expelled through the baleen. Right and bowhead whales (Balaenidae) are continuous ram "skim" feeders, while the gray whale sucks up sediment from the sea floor and filters out benthic invertebrates.

Taxonomy and species

Sixteen species are generally recognised in four families, including the blue whale, fin whale, sei whale, Bryde's whale, common and Antarctic minke whales, humpback whale, gray whale, North Atlantic and North Pacific right whales, southern right whale, bowhead whale, and the pygmy right whale.

Whaling and conservation

Baleen whales were hunted intensively for baleen ("whalebone"), oil and meat, driving several species to near extinction before the International Whaling Commission's moratorium on commercial whaling took effect in 1986. Many populations have since recovered in part — humpbacks notably — but the North Atlantic right whale remains critically endangered, with ship strikes and fishing-gear entanglement as the main threats.
