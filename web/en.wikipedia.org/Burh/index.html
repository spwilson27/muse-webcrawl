Periodlate 9th – 10th centuries
RegionWessex and English Mercia

A burh (Old English: burh, pronounced [burx]) was a fortified settlement in Anglo-Saxon England, forming part of a network of defences created to resist Viking attacks. The system was developed systematically under Alfred the Great of Wessex in the late 9th century and extended by his children Edward the Elder and Æthelflæd, Lady of the Mercians.

The Burghal Hidage

The Burghal Hidage, an early 10th-century document, lists more than thirty burhs in Wessex together with the number of hides (units of land) assigned to maintain and garrison each. The formula given allowed historians to calculate the length of wall each community was responsible for: sixteen hides provided enough men to defend an acre's breadth (about 22 yards) of rampart. Sites ranged from reused Roman towns such as Winchester and Bath to newly built fortified towns such as Wallingford and Cricklade.

Function

Burhs were more than fortresses. They served as refuges for the surrounding population, as garrisoned strongpoints that denied Viking armies easy movement or winter bases, and — increasingly — as planned market towns with mints, streets laid out in grids and trading rights. Many burhs grew into enduring English boroughs; the word survives in place names such as Canterbury, Salisbury and Edinburgh (via the cognate burgh).
