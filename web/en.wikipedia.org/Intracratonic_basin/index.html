Intracratonic basin

An intracratonic basin is a large, roughly circular to oval sedimentary basin located in the interior of a stable continental craton, far from active plate boundaries. Such basins subside slowly over hundreds of millions of years, accumulating thick sequences of shallow-marine and continental sedimentary rocks while remaining tectonically quiet.

Characteristics

Intracratonic basins are typically saucer-shaped, with strata dipping gently toward the basin centre and thinning toward the margins. Subsidence rates are low compared with rift or foreland basins, and the sedimentary fill is often dominated by repeated cycles of sandstone, shale and carbonate deposited as shallow seas advanced and retreated across the continent.

Mechanisms

The cause of the long-lived subsidence is debated. Proposed mechanisms include cooling and contraction of anomalously thick or altered lithosphere, phase changes (such as eclogite formation) in the lower crust increasing its density, dynamic subsidence from mantle flow beneath the continent, and the lingering thermal effects of ancient failed rifts beneath the basin.

Examples and economic importance

Examples include the Michigan, Illinois and Williston basins of North America, the Congo Basin in Africa, and the Great Artesian and Eromanga basins of Australia. Because they host mature, slowly buried sedimentary sequences, several intracratonic basins are important sources of petroleum, natural gas, coal and groundwater.
