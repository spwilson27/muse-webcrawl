First-run syndication

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

First-run syndication refers to programming that is broadcast for the first time as a syndicated show. In the United States, a producer or distributor sells a program directly to individual television stations (or station groups) market by market, rather than offering it through a broadcast network. The term contrasts with off-network syndication, in which reruns of programs that originally aired on a network are sold into syndication.

History

First-run syndication dates to the earliest days of American television, when independent producers such as Ziv Television Programs sold series like The Cisco Kid and Sea Hunt directly to stations. Because the Federal Communications Commission's Prime Time Access Rule (1971–1995) barred network affiliates in the largest markets from taking network programming in the first hour of prime time, a large market opened for first-run syndicated series, game shows, and talk shows in the access hour.

Genres and notable examples

First-run syndication became the home of long-running game shows such as Wheel of Fortune and Jeopardy!, talk shows such as The Oprah Winfrey Show, court shows such as Judge Judy, and entertainment newsmagazines such as Entertainment Tonight. In scripted programming, the model produced major successes including Star Trek: The Next Generation and Baywatch, which became global hits without initial network backing.

Economics

Stations typically license first-run syndicated shows for cash plus "barter" time, in which the distributor retains some advertising minutes within the program to sell nationally. Because the distributor bears the production risk but can aggregate audiences across nearly every market, a successful first-run series can be more profitable than a network series, while a failed one can be cancelled market by market without a network decision.

Decline and legacy

Consolidation of station ownership, the rise of cable and streaming, and networks' expansion of their own prime-time schedules reduced the supply of new first-run scripted syndication after the 1990s, though game, talk, court, and magazine shows remain staples of daytime and access schedules.
