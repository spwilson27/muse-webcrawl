Peace of Riga

Signed18 March 1921
LocationRiga, Latvia
PartiesSecond Polish Republic; Russian SFSR and Ukrainian SSR
ContextEnd of the Polish–Soviet War

The Peace of Riga, formally the Treaty of Peace Between Poland, Russia and Ukraine, was signed in Riga, Latvia, on 18 March 1921. It ended the Polish–Soviet War (1919–1920) and fixed the border between Poland and the Soviet republics for the interwar period, dividing the contested lands of Belarus and Ukraine between the two sides.

Background

Following the collapse of the Russian and German empires in 1918, Polish and Bolshevik forces clashed over the borderlands between them. The war swung dramatically: the Polish Kyiv offensive of spring 1920 was followed by a Soviet counteroffensive that reached the gates of Warsaw, where Poland's victory in the Battle of Warsaw (August 1920)—the "Miracle on the Vistula"—and the Battle of Niemen reversed the tide. An armistice and preliminary peace were agreed in October 1920, and negotiations moved to Riga. The Polish delegation, dominated by National Democrats who favoured a compact, ethnically defensible frontier, accepted less territory than Marshal Józef Piłsudski's federalist allies had hoped for.

Terms

The treaty set Poland's eastern border roughly along the Zbruch and a line east of the 1793 (second partition) frontier but well west of the pre-partition border, leaving some 1.5 million ethnic Poles in the USSR and millions of Ukrainians and Belarusians within Poland. Soviet Russia agreed to pay Poland 30 million gold roubles in compensation for Polish property and railway stock, and to return Polish cultural treasures and archives looted since 1772—a repatriation partially carried out. Both sides renounced war indemnities, pledged non-interference, and agreed to exchange prisoners and refugees.

Consequences

The settlement stabilised Eastern Europe for nearly two decades but satisfied neither Ukrainian and Belarusian national movements, whose lands were partitioned without their participation, nor Soviet leaders, who regarded the border as temporary. The frontier drawn at Riga was erased in 1939 by the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact and the Soviet invasion of eastern Poland, and was superseded after the Second World War by Poland's eastward-shifted borders.
