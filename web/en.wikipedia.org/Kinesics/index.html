Kinesics studies communication through body movement and facial expression.

Kinesics is the interpretation of body motion communication such as facial expressions and gestures — that is, nonverbal behaviour related to movement of any part of the body, or of the body as a whole. The term was coined in 1952 by the American anthropologist Ray L. Birdwhistell, who founded the field with his work Introduction to Kinesics.

Birdwhistell's approach

Birdwhistell, influenced by structural linguistics, proposed that body motion could be analysed the way language is: he broke movement down into small units called kines and kinemes (analogous to phonemes) and developed an elaborate notation system for recording posture, gesture, and facial movement on film. He estimated that the average person makes hundreds of thousands of distinct facial expressions and argued that no body movement or expression is without meaning in context.

Categories of movement

Following the work of Birdwhistell and later researchers such as Paul Ekman and Wallace Friesen, kinesic behaviours are commonly classified as:

  • Emblems – gestures with a direct verbal equivalent, such as a thumbs-up.
  • Illustrators – movements that accompany and accent speech.
  • Affect displays – facial and bodily expressions of emotion.
  • Regulators – movements that manage conversational flow, such as nodding.
  • Adaptors – self-directed movements, often unconscious, such as fidgeting.

Criticism

Birdwhistell's highly structuralist system, including his claim that kinesics had a universal structure comparable to grammar, has been criticised as overly rigid and his notation as too cumbersome for general use. Nonetheless, kinesics remains a foundational component of the study of nonverbal communication, alongside proxemics, haptics, and chronemics.
