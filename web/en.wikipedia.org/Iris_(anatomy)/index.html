Iris (anatomy)

In humans and most mammals and birds, the iris (plural irides or irises) is a thin, annular structure in the eye, responsible for controlling the diameter and size of the pupil, and thus the amount of light reaching the retina. The colour of the iris determines eye colour. In optical terms, the pupil is the aperture of the eye and the iris is its diaphragm.

Structure

The iris consists of two layers: the front pigmented fibrovascular stroma and, behind it, the posterior pigmented epithelial cells. Two sets of smooth muscle control pupil size: the sphincter pupillae, arranged in a ring and supplied by parasympathetic fibres, constricts the pupil in bright light, while the radially arranged dilator pupillae, under sympathetic control, enlarges it in darkness. The pupil's diameter ranges from about 2 mm in bright light to 8 mm in darkness in young adults.

Colour

Eye colour depends mainly on the amount of melanin in the stroma and the scattering of light by its fibres. Brown eyes contain abundant melanin; blue eyes have little melanin in the stroma, and their colour results from Tyndall scattering, the same phenomenon that makes the sky blue. Green and hazel eyes are intermediate. Complete absence of pigment produces the pink-red eyes of albinism. Heterochromia — differently coloured irises — may be congenital or acquired.

Clinical significance

Inflammation of the iris is called iritis, a form of anterior uveitis that can cause pain, redness and photophobia. Congenital absence of part or all of the iris (aniridia) is associated with mutations in the PAX6 gene. Iridology — the claim that health can be diagnosed from iris patterns — is not supported by evidence. Because iris texture is unique and stable, iris recognition is widely used as a biometric identifier.
