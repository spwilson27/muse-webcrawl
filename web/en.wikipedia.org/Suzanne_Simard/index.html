Suzanne W. Simard (born 1960) is a Canadian ecologist, professor of forest ecology at the University of British Columbia, known for her research on underground networks of forests characterised by fungi and roots. Her work demonstrated that trees transfer carbon and other resources to one another through common mycorrhizal networks, and she has popularised the concept of hub or "mother trees" — the largest, oldest trees that are most highly connected within the network. Her research has significantly influenced both forest science and public understanding of forest ecology.

Education and career

Simard grew up in the Monashee Mountains of British Columbia in a family of small-scale loggers. She studied forestry at the University of British Columbia and worked for the British Columbia Ministry of Forests before completing a PhD at Oregon State University in 1997. Her doctoral research, using isotope tracers in field experiments with paper birch and Douglas-fir, showed bidirectional transfer of carbon between species through ectomycorrhizal fungi, with net transfer toward shaded, carbon-limited seedlings. The 1997 Nature paper reporting this work drew wide attention and its cover likened the fungal links to a "wood-wide web".

Research

Simard's subsequent research has mapped the architecture of mycorrhizal networks in interior Douglas-fir forests, showing that older trees act as hubs with many connections and that kin recognition may influence resource transfer to related seedlings. She leads the Mother Tree Project, a long-term experiment established in 2015 across nine sites in British Columbia, testing how retention of old trees and different harvesting regimes affect regeneration and carbon storage under climate change.

Public profile and reception

Simard's 2016 TED talk "How trees talk to each other" has been viewed millions of times, and her memoir and synthesis Finding the Mother Tree: Discovering the Wisdom of the Forest (2021) became a bestseller. Her framework influenced depictions of connected forests in popular culture. Some aspects of her popular framing — particularly claims about tree communication, warning signals, and "mother trees" nurturing offspring — have been debated within the scientific community; a 2023 review in Nature Ecology & Evolution argued that evidence for some claims has been overstated in popular media, while acknowledging the reality of resource transfer through fungal networks.
