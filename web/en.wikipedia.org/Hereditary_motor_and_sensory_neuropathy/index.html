Hereditary motor and sensory neuropathy

SpecialtyNeurology, medical genetics
SymptomsDistal muscle weakness and wasting, reduced reflexes, foot deformities, sensory loss
InheritanceAutosomal dominant, recessive, or X-linked

Hereditary motor and sensory neuropathy (HMSN) is a name formerly used for a group of inherited disorders of the peripheral nerves now most commonly called Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease (CMT). The disorders cause progressive damage to peripheral nerves, leading to muscle weakness and wasting that begins in the feet and legs and later affects the hands, together with loss of sensation in the extremities.

Classification

Older classifications divided HMSN into types I through VII based on nerve conduction studies and clinical features. Type I, the demyelinating form, corresponds largely to CMT1, in which the myelin sheath is damaged and nerve conduction velocities are markedly slowed. Type II, the axonal form, corresponds to CMT2. Intermediate and X-linked forms are also recognized. Modern diagnosis relies increasingly on genetic testing, which has identified mutations in dozens of genes, including PMP22, MPZ, GJB1, and MFN2.

Signs and symptoms

Typical features include high-arched feet (pes cavus), hammertoes, "inverted champagne bottle" wasting of the lower legs, absent ankle reflexes, and gradually progressive distal weakness. Onset is usually in the first two decades of life. The condition is slowly progressive and generally does not affect life expectancy.

Management

There is no cure; management is supportive, with physiotherapy, occupational therapy, ankle–foot orthoses, and corrective orthopedic surgery for severe foot deformities. High-dose ascorbic acid and other proposed disease-modifying therapies have not shown consistent benefit in trials.
