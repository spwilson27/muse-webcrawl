Ur (Sumerian: Urim) was an important Sumerian city-state in ancient Mesopotamia, located at the site of modern Tell el-Muqayyar in southern Iraq. Founded during the Ubaid period, Ur became a wealthy port city on the Euphrates near the Persian Gulf and reached its zenith under the Third Dynasty of Ur, when its king Ur-Nammu built the great Ziggurat of Ur. In biblical tradition, "Ur of the Chaldees" is named as the birthplace of the patriarch Abraham.

History

Ur was ruled successively by the First Dynasty (whose royal tombs contained treasures such as the Standard of Ur and the lyres of Queen Puabi), the Akkadian Empire, and the Third Dynasty, founded by Ur-Nammu, author of the earliest surviving law code. His son Shulgi expanded the empire and its bureaucracy. The city declined after the Elamite sack around 2004 BC, revived under the Babylonians — Nebuchadnezzar II rebuilt its temples — and was gradually abandoned as the Euphrates shifted course, by about 450 BC.

Excavations

Sir Leonard Woolley's excavations for the British Museum and the University of Pennsylvania (1922–1934) uncovered the Royal Cemetery with its "death pits", the ziggurat, and residential quarters Woolley associated with Abraham's time. Finds included the Standard of Ur, the Ram in a Thicket figures and elaborate gold jewellery. The ziggurat's facade was partially reconstructed under Saddam Hussein.

Religion

Ur's patron deity was the moon god Nanna (Akkadian Sin), whose temple complex dominated the city; his wife was Ningal. The high priestess Enheduanna, daughter of Sargon of Akkad, is regarded as the earliest named author in history.
