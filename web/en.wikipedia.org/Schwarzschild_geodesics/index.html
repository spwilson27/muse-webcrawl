Schwarzschild geodesics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In general relativity, Schwarzschild geodesics are the paths (geodesics) followed by test particles in the Schwarzschild geometry—the spacetime around a spherically symmetric, non-rotating mass such as a star or a Schwarzschild black hole. Because the metric is static and spherically symmetric, the motion has conserved energy and angular momentum per unit mass, and every orbit lies in a plane through the centre.

Orbit equation and effective potential

Writing u = 1/r, the orbit of a massive particle satisfies an equation resembling the Newtonian Binet equation with an additional relativistic term proportional to u². The motion can be analysed with an effective potential that combines the Newtonian attraction, the centrifugal barrier, and a relativistic correction that becomes significant at small radii. Unlike the Newtonian potential, the relativistic effective potential has a maximum: inside a certain radius no centrifugal barrier prevents infall.

Key phenomena

Perihelion precession

Bound orbits are not closed ellipses but rosettes whose perihelion advances each revolution. For Mercury, this effect accounts for the anomalous 43 arcseconds per century in the precession of its perihelion, one of the classical tests of general relativity predicted by Einstein in 1915.

Innermost stable circular orbit (ISCO)

Circular orbits exist for radii above the photon sphere; stable circular orbits for massive particles require r ≥ 6M (in geometrized units), i.e. three times the Schwarzschild radius. The ISCO sets the inner edge of accretion discs around non-rotating black holes.

Light bending and photon sphere

Null geodesics (light rays) can orbit unstably at the photon sphere, r = 3M. Light passing near the mass is deflected—by about 1.75 arcseconds for a ray grazing the Sun—another classical test of the theory. Particles or light crossing the horizon at r = 2M inevitably fall to the singularity.

History

Karl Schwarzschild obtained his exact solution in 1916, and Einstein used its geodesic motion to explain Mercury's perihelion the same period. Johannes Droste independently derived the particle motion in 1916, giving his name to the Droste coordinates. The precession, light deflection, and gravitational redshift derived from these geodesics constituted the three classical tests of general relativity.
