Samnites

The Samnites were an ancient Italic people inhabiting Samnium, a rugged mountainous region of the south-central Apennines in Italy, corresponding roughly to the modern regions of Molise and parts of Abruzzo, Campania and Apulia. Speaking the Oscan language, they were organised as a confederation of tribes and became the most formidable rivals of the expanding Roman Republic in Italy, fighting three great wars against Rome between 343 and 290 BC.

Origins and society

According to tradition, the Samnites descended from Sabine migrants who, following a ver sacrum ("sacred spring") ritual, were guided to their new homeland by a bull or a wolf — the Hirpini took their name from hirpus, the Oscan word for wolf. Samnite society was pastoral and agricultural, centred on fortified hilltop towns (oppida) and rural sanctuaries rather than large cities. The confederation comprised four principal tribes — the Pentri, Caraceni, Caudini and Hirpini — with the Frentani on the Adriatic coast associated more loosely; affairs of the league were directed by an elected official titled meddix tuticus.

Samnite warriors were renowned throughout Italy. Their equipment typically included a broad belt, a crested or feathered helmet, a large rectangular shield and a javelin; the Roman manipular legion and the gladius-style sword are often thought to have been influenced by Samnite practice, and one class of Roman gladiator was simply called the "Samnite".

The Samnite Wars

Rome and the Samnites first clashed in 343 BC over Campania, beginning the First Samnite War. The Second (or Great) Samnite War (326–304 BC) brought Rome its most humiliating early defeat: in 321 BC a Roman army was trapped in the mountain pass of the Caudine Forks and forced to surrender and pass under the yoke. The Third Samnite War (298–290 BC) saw the Samnites ally with Etruscans, Umbrians and Gauls, but Rome's victory at the Battle of Sentinum in 295 BC broke the coalition, and by 290 BC Samnium had submitted.

Later history

The Samnites rose once more alongside Pyrrhus of Epirus in the 270s BC and supported Hannibal after Cannae in the Second Punic War, for which their territory was ravaged. Their last great stand came in the Social War (91–87 BC), when the Samnites were the most determined of the Italian peoples fighting Rome for citizenship; the Roman general Sulla's brutal campaigns devastated Samnium, and the Samnite cause ended with the massacre at the Battle of the Colline Gate in 82 BC. Thereafter the Samnites were rapidly Romanised, though Oscan inscriptions and local customs lingered into the early Empire.
