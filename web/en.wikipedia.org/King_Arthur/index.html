King Arthur (Welsh: Brenin Arthur; Cornish: Arthur Gernow; Breton: Roue Arzhur) is a legendary king of Britain and a central figure in the medieval literary tradition known as the Matter of Britain. In Welsh sources, Arthur is portrayed as a leader of the post-Roman Britons in battles against Anglo-Saxon invaders of the late 5th and early 6th centuries. He first appears in two early medieval historical sources, the Annales Cambriae and the Historia Brittonum, but these date to some 300 years after the time he is supposed to have lived, and most historians who study the period dismiss Arthur as a historical figure.

The legendary Arthur developed as a figure of international interest largely through Geoffrey of Monmouth's 12th-century Historia Regum Britanniae ("History of the Kings of Britain"), which portrayed him as a great king who defeated the Saxons and established a vast empire. Many elements of the story were later added by French writers: Chrétien de Troyes introduced the knight Lancelot and the Holy Grail, and Robert de Boron and the Vulgate Cycle developed the roles of Merlin, Mordred, and the Round Table fellowship. The legend reached its classic form in Thomas Malory's Le Morte d'Arthur (1485).

Historicity

The historical basis for King Arthur has long been debated by historians. The earliest sources mentioning Arthur — the Historia Brittonum (c. 830), which lists twelve battles culminating at Mount Badon, and the Annales Cambriae, which record the Battle of Badon (c. 516–518) and the Battle of Camlann (c. 537–539) — are centuries later than the events described. Contemporary accounts, notably Gildas's De Excidio et Conquestu Britanniae (6th century), describe the victory at Badon without naming Arthur, and Bede's Ecclesiastical History likewise omits him. Some scholars argue Arthur was a real Romano-British war leader whose deeds were embellished; others regard him as a composite or a figure of folklore elevated into pseudo-history. No conclusive archaeological evidence of a historical Arthur has been found.

The legend

In the developed legend, Arthur is the son of Uther Pendragon and Igraine, conceived with the aid of the wizard Merlin. As a boy he proves his kingship by drawing a sword from a stone (in later versions, the sword Excalibur is given to him by the Lady of the Lake). He marries Guinevere and establishes his court at Camelot, where he gathers the Knights of the Round Table — among them Lancelot, Gawain, Perceval, Galahad, Kay, and Bedivere. Arthur's knights undertake quests, most famously the quest for the Holy Grail, achieved by Galahad, Perceval, and Bors.

The fellowship is destroyed by the adulterous love of Lancelot and Guinevere and by the treachery of Arthur's son (or nephew) Mordred. Arthur and Mordred kill each other at the Battle of Camlann. The mortally wounded Arthur is carried away by boat to the Isle of Avalon to be healed — giving rise to the belief, the rex quondam rexque futurus ("the once and future king"), that he will return in Britain's hour of need.

Literary tradition

Welsh literature preserves early Arthurian material in poems and the prose tales of the Mabinogion, including Culhwch and Olwen (c. 1100), the oldest surviving Arthurian tale. Geoffrey of Monmouth's Historia (c. 1138) made Arthur a European sensation and was adapted by Wace and Layamon. Chrétien de Troyes (fl. 1170s–1180s) founded the Arthurian romance genre with works including Lancelot, the Knight of the Cart and Perceval, the Story of the Grail. German poets such as Wolfram von Eschenbach (Parzival) and Gottfried von Strassburg expanded the material.

Interest revived in the 19th century with Tennyson's Idylls of the King and the Pre-Raphaelites, and again in the 20th century through works such as T. H. White's The Once and Future King (1958), Mary Stewart's Merlin trilogy, Marion Zimmer Bradley's The Mists of Avalon (1983), and numerous films, including Monty Python and the Holy Grail (1975) and Excalibur (1981). Arthur remains one of the most retold figures in Western literature.
