Superfluidity

Superfluidity is the characteristic property of a liquid with zero viscosity which therefore flows without any loss of kinetic energy. When stirred, a superfluid forms vortices that continue to rotate indefinitely. The phenomenon was discovered in liquid helium-4 cooled below the lambda point (about 2.17 K), and later in helium-3 at much lower temperatures; it is understood as a macroscopic quantum phenomenon related to Bose–Einstein condensation.

Helium II

Below the lambda transition, liquid helium becomes helium II, described by the two-fluid model as a mixture of a normal component and a superfluid component with zero entropy. Helium II exhibits remarkable effects: it flows through the finest capillaries without friction, conducts heat hundreds of times better than copper, climbs container walls as a thin Rollin film, and produces the fountain effect, in which heating drives liquid through a porous plug.

Theory

Lev Landau developed a phenomenology of superfluidity based on elementary excitations (phonons and rotons) and a critical velocity below which flow is dissipationless. For helium-4, superfluidity is connected with Bose–Einstein condensation of the bosonic atoms. Helium-3 atoms are fermions, and its superfluidity (discovered in 1972 at about 2.5 mK) arises instead from pairing of atoms analogous to Cooper pairing in superconductors, producing rich phases with anisotropic order parameters.

Quantised vortices

Rotation in a superfluid is carried by quantised vortex lines, each with a fixed circulation. These vortices form regular lattices and underpin applications and analogues ranging from neutron-star interiors, where superfluid neutrons are thought to exist, to precision gyroscopes and studies of quantum turbulence.
