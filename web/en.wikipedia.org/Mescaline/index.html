Mescaline

Mescaline, also known as 3,4,5-trimethoxyphenethylamine, is a naturally occurring psychedelic protoalkaloid of the substituted phenethylamine class, found in the peyote cactus (Lophophora williamsii), the San Pedro cactus, the Peruvian torch, and several other cacti. It produces effects comparable to those of LSD and psilocybin.

History

Peyote has been used for at least 5,700 years by Native Americans in Mexico, and its ceremonial use spread to the Native American Church in the United States, where it enjoys legal protection for religious use. Mescaline was first isolated and identified in 1897 by the German chemist Arthur Heffter, and first synthesized in 1919 by Ernst Späth. Aldous Huxley's 1954 essay The Doors of Perception described his mescaline experience and popularized the drug in Western culture.

Pharmacology

Mescaline binds to and activates the serotonin 5-HT2A receptor, as do other classic psychedelics. Effects typically begin within 1–2 hours of ingestion and last up to 12 hours, with characteristic visual distortions, altered thinking, and euphoria or nausea. It has low toxicity and is not considered addictive, though tolerance develops rapidly with repeated use.

Legal status

Mescaline is a Schedule I controlled substance in the United States and is controlled in most countries under the UN Convention on Psychotropic Substances, with exemptions for religious use of peyote by the Native American Church.
