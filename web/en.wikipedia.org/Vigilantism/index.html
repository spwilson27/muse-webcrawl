Vigilantism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Vigilantism is the act of preventing, investigating and punishing perceived offenses and crimes without legal authority. A person who engages in such acts is a vigilante, from the Spanish vigilante ("watchman"). Vigilantism typically arises where people perceive official law enforcement as absent, inadequate or corrupt, but because vigilantes act outside the law, their actions — ranging from citizen patrols to lynching — are themselves generally illegal and often condemned as a threat to the rule of law.

History

Self-help justice has ancient roots, but organized vigilantism is especially associated with the American frontier, where vigilance committees in mining camps and new settlements — most famously in San Francisco in 1851 and 1856 — tried and hanged suspected criminals where courts were weak or nonexistent. The term "lynching" is linked in popular tradition to Charles Lynch, a Virginia justice of the peace who punished Loyalists extrajudicially during the Revolutionary War. In the post-Reconstruction South, vigilante violence was a tool of racial terror against African Americans.

Vigilante movements have appeared worldwide, from the Whiteboys of 18th-century Ireland to anti-crime groups in Latin America, "people's courts" and moral-policing squads, and modern internet vigilantism, in which online crowds identify and harass alleged offenders ("digilantism").

Legal and social analysis

States claim a monopoly on legitimate violence, so vigilantism is usually prosecuted as assault, kidnapping, murder or related offences; only limited doctrines such as citizen's arrest and self-defense permit private intervention. Scholars note the paradox that vigilantism claims to defend law and order while undermining legal institutions, and that it frequently targets marginal groups and produces grave miscarriages, since accused persons receive no due process. In fiction, by contrast, the vigilante is often romanticized — from Zorro and Batman to the Death Wish films.
