Hybridization probe

In molecular biology, a hybridization probe is a fragment of DNA or RNA, usually 100–1000 bases long, that is labelled so that its binding to a complementary target sequence can be detected. Probes exploit nucleic acid hybridization: under suitable conditions a single-stranded probe anneals specifically to a target with a complementary sequence, revealing the target's presence, location, or quantity.

Labels and detection

Probes have historically been labelled with radioisotopes such as 32P and detected by autoradiography. Modern probes more commonly carry fluorescent dyes, enzymes, or chemiluminescent tags. Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) uses fluorescent probes to locate specific DNA sequences on chromosomes in intact cells and tissues, widely used in cytogenetics and prenatal diagnosis.

Applications

Hybridization probes underpin many core techniques: Southern blotting (DNA targets) and northern blotting (RNA targets), colony and plaque screening of DNA libraries, microarray expression profiling, and clinical diagnostics for infectious agents and genetic mutations. Short synthetic oligonucleotide probes are used in allele-specific assays and in real-time PCR formats such as TaqMan probes.
