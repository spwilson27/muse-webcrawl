Meson

Composition: one quark + one antiquark
Statistics: bosonic (integer spin)
Interaction: strong, weak, electromagnetic, gravity

In particle physics, a meson is a type of hadronic subatomic particle composed of an equal number of quarks and antiquarks, usually one of each, bound together by the strong interaction. Mesons are part of the hadron family, alongside baryons such as protons and neutrons (three quarks). Because they contain an even number of fermions, mesons are bosons with integer spin.

History

Hideki Yukawa predicted the meson in 1935 as the carrier of the nuclear force, estimating its mass at about 200 times the electron's. The pion (pi-meson) matching the prediction was discovered in cosmic rays by Cecil Powell's group in 1947; Yukawa and Powell received Nobel Prizes. The muon, found earlier (1936), was briefly called a "mu-meson" but is actually a lepton.

Types

The lightest mesons are the pions (π⁺, π⁰, π⁻), followed by kaons (strange mesons), and the eta and eta prime. Vector mesons such as the rho, omega, and phi have spin 1; heavier systems include charmonium (J/ψ, discovered 1974) and bottomonium (upsilon, 1977). Mesons are classified by isospin, G-parity, flavor quantum numbers, and their quark-model wavefunctions (²S⁺¹L_J notation).

Properties

All mesons are unstable, decaying with lifetimes from about 10⁻²³ s (resonances) down to the charged pion's 2.6×10⁻⁸ s. Mesons mediate the residual strong force between nucleons in atomic nuclei.
