Bribery Act 2010

Citation: 2010 c. 23
Royal assent: 8 April 2010
Commencement: 1 July 2011
Jurisdiction: United Kingdom

The Bribery Act 2010 (c. 23) is an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom that covers criminal law relating to bribery. Introduced to Parliament after criticism from the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) of Britain's fragmented anti-corruption laws, it received royal assent on 8 April 2010 and came into force on 1 July 2011. It is often described as one of the strictest anti-bribery statutes in the world, in some respects broader than the U.S. Foreign Corrupt Practices Act.

Offences

The Act creates four offences: bribing another person (section 1); being bribed (section 2); bribery of a foreign public official (section 6); and, for commercial organisations, failure to prevent bribery by an associated person (section 7). The general offences cover offering, promising, giving, requesting, agreeing to receive, or accepting a financial or other advantage intended to bring about the improper performance of a relevant function or activity. There is no exception for facilitation payments, and private-sector bribery is covered as well as bribery of officials.

Corporate liability and defence

Section 7 is a strict-liability offence: a company that carries on business in the United Kingdom can be liable when a person associated with it bribes another intending to obtain or retain business or a business advantage for the company, wherever in the world the bribe occurs. The sole defence is that the organisation had "adequate procedures" in place to prevent such conduct; the Ministry of Justice issued statutory guidance built around six principles, including risk assessment, due diligence, and top-level commitment.

Penalties and prosecutions

On conviction on indictment, individuals face up to ten years' imprisonment and an unlimited fine; companies face unlimited fines. Enforcement rests chiefly with the Serious Fraud Office. Significant cases have included deferred prosecution agreements with Standard Bank (2015) and Rolls-Royce (2017), the latter involving payments of over £670 million across several jurisdictions, and the conviction of Sweett Group, the first company convicted under section 7, in 2016.
