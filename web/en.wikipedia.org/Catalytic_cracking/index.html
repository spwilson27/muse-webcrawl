Catalytic cracking

Catalytic cracking is a petroleum refining process in which large hydrocarbon molecules in heavy oil fractions are broken down ("cracked") into smaller, more valuable molecules — principally gasoline-range hydrocarbons and light olefins such as propene — in the presence of a catalyst. The dominant industrial technology is fluid catalytic cracking (FCC), in which a powdered zeolite catalyst is circulated as a fluid between a reactor and a regenerator.

Process

In an FCC unit, preheated gas oil is vaporised and contacted with hot regenerated catalyst in a riser reactor at around 500 °C. Cracking reactions deposit coke on the catalyst, which is separated from the products in cyclones; the spent catalyst flows to a regenerator where the coke is burned off with air, reheating the catalyst and supplying the process heat. Products are fractionated into gases, gasoline, light cycle oil and slurry.

Catalysts

Modern FCC catalysts are based on zeolite Y (faujasite) embedded in a matrix of silica-alumina and clay, with additives such as ZSM-5 to boost octane and light olefin yields, and metal traps and SOx-reduction components to manage feed contaminants and emissions.

History

Thermal cracking was commercialised in the 1910s; the first catalytic cracking process, using fixed beds of clay catalyst, was developed by Eugène Houdry in the 1930s. Fluid catalytic cracking, first operated commercially in 1942 at Baton Rouge, was crucial to Allied aviation-fuel production in the Second World War. The introduction of zeolite catalysts in the 1960s greatly increased gasoline yield and selectivity, and FCC remains one of the principal conversion processes in refineries worldwide.
