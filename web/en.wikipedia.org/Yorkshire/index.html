Yorkshire is a historic county in northern England and the largest in the United Kingdom by area. Because of its size — historically about 3.9 million acres — it was traditionally divided into three ridings (East, North, and West, from the Old Norse þriðjungr, "third part") plus the city of York. Since local government reorganisations, its territory is now covered by the ceremonial counties of North Yorkshire, South Yorkshire, West Yorkshire, and the East Riding of Yorkshire, along with parts of neighbouring counties.

Geography

The county spans the Pennine hills in the west — including the Yorkshire Dales and the peaks of Whernside, Ingleborough, and Pen-y-ghent — the Vale of York in the centre, and the Yorkshire Wolds and North York Moors to the east, meeting the North Sea at resorts such as Whitby and Scarborough. Major rivers include the Ouse, Aire, Calder, Don, and Tees.

History

Yorkshire was inhabited in Roman times, with York (Eboracum) a major military and civil centre where Constantine the Great was proclaimed emperor in 306. The area later fell under Anglian Northumbria, then Scandinavian settlement during the Danelaw, whose Norse influence survives in place-names and dialect. The Norman "Harrying of the North" devastated the county in 1069–70. During the Wars of the Roses, the House of York — symbolised by the white rose — contested the throne. In the industrial era, Yorkshire became a powerhouse of wool textiles, coal mining, and steel (notably Sheffield), and its cricket club and rugby league traditions became central to regional identity.

Culture

Yorkshire people are noted for a strong regional identity, a distinctive dialect, and contributions from Yorkshire pudding to the Brontë sisters, David Hockney, and the Tour de Yorkshire cycle race. Yorkshire Day is celebrated on 1 August.
