Generalized function

In mathematics, generalized functions are objects extending the notion of a function. They are recognisable objects arising in physics and engineering, most famously the Dirac delta function, which is zero everywhere except at a single point yet integrates to one — behaviour no ordinary function can have. Generalized functions smooth out irregularities that would otherwise occur, and on the domains where they are defined they permit differentiation of any order, even of functions that are discontinuous or singular.

Motivation

Physicists in the early twentieth century, notably Paul Dirac, freely differentiated step functions and used "functions" concentrated at a point to model impulses and point charges. Mathematicians sought a rigorous framework. Sergei Sobolev introduced key ideas in the 1930s, and Laurent Schwartz completed the theory of distributions in the 1940s, work for which he received the Fields Medal in 1950.

Distributions

In Schwartz's theory, a distribution is a continuous linear functional on a space of smooth "test functions" of compact support: instead of assigning a value at each point, a generalized function assigns a number to each test function, generalising the idea of averaging a function against a smooth weight. Every locally integrable ordinary function defines a distribution, and every distribution has derivatives of all orders; the delta function is the derivative of the Heaviside step function in this sense. Because arbitrary distributions cannot always be multiplied together, alternative frameworks such as Colombeau algebras embed distributions into algebras of generalized functions where multiplication is defined, at the cost of a more elaborate structure.

Applications

Generalized functions are fundamental to the modern theory of partial differential equations, where fundamental solutions are distributions; to Fourier analysis, where the Fourier transform extends naturally to tempered distributions; and throughout theoretical physics, including quantum field theory and signal processing.
