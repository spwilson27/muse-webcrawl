Structural variation

Structural variation in the human genome is operationally defined as a region of DNA approximately 1 kilobase to 3 megabases in size that has been inverted, translocated or deleted, or in which a sequence has been inserted or duplicated. (The size threshold is conventional; many definitions now use 50 base pairs or more.) Structural variants account for more differing nucleotides between two human genomes than single-nucleotide variants, because each event changes a large stretch of sequence.

Types

The main classes are deletions, insertions, duplications (including copy-number variants, CNVs), inversions and translocations. Balanced rearrangements (inversions and translocations) change the order or location of DNA without changing its total amount, while unbalanced variants change copy number. Mobile element insertions, notably of Alu and LINE-1 retrotransposons, are a common source of new structural variation.

Detection and significance

Structural variants were historically found by cytogenetics and later by array comparative genomic hybridisation and array-based CNV detection; whole-genome sequencing, especially with long reads, now reveals many more, including variants in repetitive regions. Structural variation contributes to normal human diversity, gene expression differences and evolution (for example, duplication of the amylase gene AMY1), and to disease: large deletions and duplications cause disorders such as Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease and DiGeorge syndrome, and somatic rearrangements are a hallmark of cancer genomes.
