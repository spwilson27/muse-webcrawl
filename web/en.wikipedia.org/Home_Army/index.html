Home Army

The Home Army (Polish: Armia Krajowa, abbreviated AK) was the dominant resistance movement in German-occupied Poland during the Second World War. Formed in February 1942 from the Union of Armed Struggle (ZWZ) and loyal to the Polish government-in-exile in London, it grew into one of the largest underground armies in Europe, with some 350,000–400,000 sworn members at its 1944 peak. The Home Army conducted sabotage, intelligence, and guerrilla operations, and in August 1944 launched the Warsaw Uprising. It was officially disbanded on 19 January 1945 as Soviet forces occupied Poland, and many of its members were persecuted by the communist authorities.

Origins and organisation

Polish resistance began within weeks of the September 1939 defeat, with the Service for Poland's Victory and then the ZWZ, subordinated to the government-in-exile first in Paris and then London. On 14 February 1942 General Władysław Sikorski renamed it the Home Army, and most (though not all) independent resistance groups merged into it; the far-right National Armed Forces remained partly outside, and the communist People's Guard/Army answered to Moscow. The AK formed the armed wing of the Polish Underground State — a clandestine polity with courts, education, and a parliament (the Council of National Unity) — and was organised territorially into districts mirroring prewar Poland, with specialist directorates for sabotage (Kedyw), intelligence, propaganda, and production of clandestine weapons such as the Błyskawica submachine gun.

Operations

Home Army intelligence was among the Allies' most valuable assets: it reported on German forces, supplied London with V-1 and V-2 rocket intelligence (including parts of a V-2 flown out in 1944), and ran agents such as Witold Pilecki, who volunteered to be imprisoned in Auschwitz and reported on the Holocaust from inside. Kedyw units assassinated senior SS and police officials (including Franz Kutschera, the "butcher of Warsaw", in 1944), derailed trains, and freed prisoners. Couriers such as Jan Karski carried eyewitness accounts of the Holocaust to Western governments. The AK also fought a growing partisan war in the forests and, in 1944, escalated under Operation Tempest (Burza), seizing territory ahead of the advancing Red Army — whose commanders frequently arrested AK officers once the fighting was over, as at Wilno and Lwów.

The Warsaw Uprising

On 1 August 1944, as Soviet forces approached Warsaw, Bór-Komorowski ordered a general uprising to liberate the capital under Polish authority before the Soviets arrived. Some 40,000–50,000 AK soldiers rose, but they were lightly armed, and the expected Soviet offensive halted across the Vistula. After 63 days of brutal fighting, in which German forces under SS command murdered an estimated 150,000–200,000 civilians (including the Wola massacre) and systematically demolished the city, the AK capitulated on 2 October 1944; its soldiers were granted combatant status and marched into POW camps. The uprising remains the central episode of Polish wartime memory — heroic to most Poles, its wisdom still debated by historians.

Aftermath

With Poland under Soviet domination, Commander Leopold Okulicki disbanded the AK on 19 January 1945. Many soldiers demobilised; others joined anti-communist successor organisations such as Freedom and Independence (WiN). The communist regime arrested, tried, and executed numerous AK veterans — Okulicki died in a Moscow prison, Pilecki was executed in 1948 — and officially vilified the AK for decades. The "cursed soldiers" who fought on into the 1950s were rehabilitated in Polish memory after 1989; the Warsaw Uprising Museum (opened 2004) and annual commemorations mark the AK's central place in modern Polish identity.
