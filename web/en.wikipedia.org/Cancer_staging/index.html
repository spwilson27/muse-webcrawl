Cancer staging

Cancer staging is the process of determining the extent to which a cancer has developed and spread in the body. It is a crucial step in planning treatment and estimating prognosis. Most solid tumours are staged using the TNM system, maintained by the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) and the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC).

The TNM system

ComponentMeaning
T (tumour)Size and extent of the primary tumour (T0–T4)
N (node)Spread to nearby lymph nodes (N0–N3)
M (metastasis)Distant metastasis (M0 = none, M1 = present)

Combinations of T, N, and M are grouped into overall stages, conventionally written with Roman numerals from stage 0 (carcinoma in situ) through stage IV (distant metastasis). In general, lower stages indicate a smaller, localised cancer with a better prognosis; stage IV indicates advanced disease.

How staging is determined

Staging may be clinical (based on physical examination, imaging such as CT, MRI and PET scans, and biopsy) or pathological (based on examination of tissue removed during surgery), the latter generally being more accurate. Some cancers use other systems: leukaemias and most lymphomas are not TNM-staged (lymphomas use the Ann Arbor system), and brain tumours are classified mainly by type and grade rather than stage.

Grading

Staging is distinct from grading, which describes how abnormal the cancer cells look under the microscope and how quickly they are likely to grow. Both stage and grade are considered, along with tumour markers and the patient's overall health, when choosing between surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and other treatments.
