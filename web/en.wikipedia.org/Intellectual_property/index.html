Intellectual property (IP) is a category of property that includes intangible creations of the human intellect. There are many types of intellectual property, and some countries recognise more than others. The best-known types are patents, copyrights, trademarks, and trade secrets. The modern concept of intellectual property developed in England in the 17th and 18th centuries. The term "intellectual property" began to be used in the 19th century, although it was not until the late 20th century that intellectual property became commonplace in legal systems around the world.

History

Early forms of exclusive rights over creations appeared in ancient Greece and Rome, but the modern system traces its origins to statutes such as the English Statute of Monopolies (1624) and the Statute of Anne (1710), the first copyright law. The Paris Convention (1883) and Berne Convention (1886) established international frameworks for industrial property and copyright respectively. In 1967 the World Intellectual Property Organization (WIPO) was founded as a United Nations agency, and the 1994 TRIPS Agreement tied IP protection to the world trading system.

Types

Copyright

Copyright protects original works of authorship — literature, music, art, film and software — giving the creator exclusive rights to reproduce, distribute and adapt the work for a limited term, commonly the author's life plus 70 years.

Patents

A patent grants an inventor the right to exclude others from making, using or selling an invention for a limited period (typically 20 years) in exchange for public disclosure of the invention.

Trademarks

A trademark is a sign capable of distinguishing the goods or services of one enterprise from those of others. Trademark rights can in principle last indefinitely so long as the mark remains in use and renewals are paid.

Trade secrets

Trade secrets are confidential business information that provides a competitive edge, protected for as long as secrecy is maintained, without registration.

Purpose and criticism

The main purpose of intellectual property law is to encourage the creation of a wide variety of intellectual goods by granting limited exclusive rights as an incentive. Critics argue that overly broad or long rights can impede innovation, restrict access to medicines and knowledge, and create monopolies; movements such as free software, open access and the public domain advocate weaker or alternative regimes.
