Secret Intelligence Service


The SIS Building, Vauxhall Cross, London

The Secret Intelligence Service, commonly known as MI6 (Military Intelligence, Section 6), is the foreign intelligence service of the United Kingdom, tasked mainly with the covert overseas collection and analysis of human intelligence in support of the UK's national security. SIS is one of the British intelligence agencies and the Chief of the Secret Intelligence Service ("C") is directly accountable to the Foreign Secretary.

Formed in 1909 as part of the Secret Service Bureau, the service grew dramatically during the Second World War, when it was officially and popularly known as MI6. The agency's existence was not officially acknowledged until 1994, when the Intelligence Services Act placed SIS on a statutory footing.

History

The service was founded in 1909 under Commander (later Captain Sir) Mansfield Smith-Cumming, its first chief, who signed his correspondence with the initial "C" — a tradition maintained by every subsequent chief. During the First World War the service focused on Germany; between the wars it struggled with budget cuts and Communist penetration. In the Second World War it worked alongside the Special Operations Executive and received the signals intelligence of Bletchley Park.

Structure and role

SIS operates under the statutory authority of the Intelligence Services Act 1994. It collects human intelligence abroad through case officers and agents, and works with its domestic counterpart MI5 and with GCHQ. Since 1995 its headquarters has been the SIS Building at Vauxhall Cross in London. The service is accountable to Parliament through the Intelligence and Security Committee.

Intelligence cycle and operations

Requirements are set by the Joint Intelligence Committee. SIS shares intelligence with allied services, notably through the UKUSA ("Five Eyes") arrangements, and its officers operate under official cover in embassies or non-official cover.

In popular culture

MI6 is famously depicted in Ian Fleming's James Bond novels and their film adaptations, in which Bond's employer is the service and its chief is known as "M". Authors such as John le Carré, a former officer, have presented a more bureaucratic portrait.
