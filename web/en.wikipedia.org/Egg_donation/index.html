Egg donation

Egg donation is the process by which a woman donates eggs to enable another woman to conceive as part of an assisted reproduction treatment, or for biomedical research. For assisted reproduction purposes, egg donation normally involves in vitro fertilisation (IVF) technology, with the eggs being fertilised in the laboratory. After the eggs have been obtained, the role of the egg donor is complete. Egg donation is a third-party reproduction process and is a form of assisted reproductive technology (ART).

History

The first child born from egg donation was reported in Australia in 1983 (with the first successful birth using a donated egg and IVF techniques following in 1984 in the United States and elsewhere). The technique was first developed for women with premature ovarian failure or absent ovaries, and its indications later expanded to include advanced maternal age, diminished ovarian reserve, genetic disease avoidance, and use by male couples with a gestational surrogate.

Procedure

A donor undergoes screening and then ovarian stimulation with gonadotropin injections for roughly ten to twelve days to mature multiple follicles. Her cycle is synchronised with the recipient's using hormonal medication. The eggs are retrieved transvaginally under ultrasound guidance and sedation, a procedure taking about twenty to thirty minutes. The eggs are fertilised with the intended father's or donor's sperm, and embryos are transferred to the recipient's uterus or to a gestational carrier, or frozen for later use.

Donors

Donors may be known to the recipients (such as a sister or friend), recruited through agencies or clinics, or drawn from egg-sharing schemes in which a woman undergoing IVF shares some of her eggs in return for reduced treatment costs. Donors are typically women between the ages of 21 and 34 who undergo medical, genetic, and psychological screening. Donation may be anonymous, identity-release, or fully known, depending on the jurisdiction; in the United Kingdom, donor-conceived people have been entitled to identifying information about their donor at age 18 since 2005.

Law and ethics

The law varies widely: some countries prohibit egg donation entirely, others allow it only altruistically, while the United States permits compensated donation with payments commonly in the thousands of dollars per cycle. Ethical debates centre on the risks to donors from ovarian stimulation (including ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome), the commodification of reproduction, the rights of donor-conceived people to know their genetic origins, and limits on the number of families created from one donor.
