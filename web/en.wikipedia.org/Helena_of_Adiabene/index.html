Helena of Adiabene

Helena of Adiabene (died c. 50–56 AD) was queen of Adiabene, a kingdom in northern Mesopotamia (in present-day Iraq), and wife — and sister — of King Monobaz I. She is remembered for converting to Judaism in the 1st century AD, for her generosity to Jerusalem during a famine, and for the monumental tomb complex she built north of the city, later known as the Tombs of the Kings.

Conversion and family

According to the Jewish historian Josephus, Helena and her son Izates were instructed in Judaism by a Jewish merchant named Ananias and another teacher, Eleazar. Izates, who succeeded to the throne of Adiabene around 36 AD as Izates II, adopted Jewish practices including, after deliberation, circumcision. Helena's piety and that of her sons made the Adiabenian royal house the most famous example of converts to Judaism in antiquity, and members of the family later supported the Jewish side in the First Jewish–Roman War.

Jerusalem benefactions

Helena spent extended periods in Jerusalem, where she built palaces in the Lower City and became famed for charitable works. During the famine under Emperor Claudius (c. 46–48 AD) she purchased grain from Egypt and figs from Cyprus at great expense and distributed them to the starving population — a deed praised in the Talmud as well as by Josephus. Rabbinic tradition also credits her with donating a golden lamp and a golden tablet inscribed with a Torah passage to the Temple.

Tomb

Helena had a monumental burial complex hewn into rock north of Jerusalem's Damascus Gate, with pyramidal monuments above it that were landmarks described by Josephus and Pausanias. She and Izates were buried there. The site, excavated by Félicien de Saulcy in 1863, is now known as the Tombs of the Kings; a decorated sarcophagus bearing the Aramaic inscription "Tzaddan the Queen" is thought by many scholars to be hers and is held in the Louvre. Coins and ossuaries found at the site link it to the Adiabenian royal family.
