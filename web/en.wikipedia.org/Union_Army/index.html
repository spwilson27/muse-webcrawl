The Union Army was the land force that fought for the Union of the United States during the American Civil War. It consisted of the small United States Army (the regular army), augmented by massive numbers of volunteer units supplied by the Northern states, including troops contributed by counties, cities, and ethnic groups. It fought and eventually triumphed over the Confederate States Army in the war, which ended with the surrenders of Robert E. Lee's Army of Northern Virginia at Appomattox Court House in April 1865 and the remaining Confederate armies in the following weeks.

Organisation

The Union Army was organised into field armies named (mostly) after rivers, such as the Army of the Potomac, the Army of the Tennessee, and the Army of the Cumberland, each subdivided into corps, divisions, brigades, and regiments. Regiments of about 1,000 men, recruited locally by the states, were the basic tactical units. Of the roughly 2.1 million men who served, about 180,000 were Black soldiers, most of them organised after 1863 in the United States Colored Troops following the Emancipation Proclamation.

Leadership and strategy

Command of the armies changed repeatedly in the east, where early commanders such as Irvin McDowell, George B. McClellan, Ambrose Burnside, and Joseph Hooker failed to destroy Lee's army. In March 1864 President Abraham Lincoln appointed Ulysses S. Grant general-in-chief; Grant directed a coordinated strategy of simultaneous advances, waging attritional campaigns in Virginia while William Tecumseh Sherman manoeuvred through Georgia and the Carolinas. Winfield Scott's early "Anaconda Plan" for blockading and dividing the Confederacy anticipated this strategy.

Casualties and demobilisation

Approximately 360,000 Union soldiers died during the war — about 110,000 in battle or of wounds and the rest chiefly of disease. Following the Grand Review of the Armies in Washington in May 1865, the volunteer force was rapidly mustered out, and the army shrank back to a small regular establishment.
