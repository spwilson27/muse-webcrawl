Flower

A flower, also known as a bloom or blossom, is the reproductive structure found in flowering plants (angiosperms). Flowers consist typically of a perianth of petals and sepals surrounding the reproductive organs — stamens producing pollen, and one or more carpels containing ovules. Their biological function is to mediate reproduction, usually by providing a mechanism for the union of sperm and eggs, often with the involvement of pollinators.

Structure

A typical flower is borne on a stalk (pedicel) ending in a receptacle, and has four whorls of organs: the calyx of green sepals, the corolla of often colourful petals, the androecium of stamens, and the gynoecium of carpels. Each stamen bears an anther producing pollen; each carpel contains an ovary with ovules, topped by a stigma that receives pollen. Flowers may be bisexual or unisexual, radially or bilaterally symmetrical, and solitary or grouped in inflorescences such as spikes, umbels and capitula.

Pollination and fertilisation

In pollination, pollen is transferred to a stigma — by wind, water or animals, above all insects. Bright colours, scent, nectar and ultraviolet patterns evolved as advertisements and rewards for pollinators, while wind-pollinated flowers are usually small and inconspicuous. After a compatible pollen grain germinates, a pollen tube grows to an ovule; angiosperms characteristically undergo double fertilisation, producing both the embryo and its nutritive tissue, the endosperm. The ovary then develops into a fruit enclosing the seeds.

Evolution and human use

Flowering plants appeared and diversified explosively in the Cretaceous — Darwin's "abominable mystery" — and now number roughly 300,000 species, dominating most terrestrial ecosystems. Flowers are central to agriculture (most food crops are angiosperms), horticulture and floristry, and have deep cultural and symbolic roles, from religious offerings to the language of flowers in Victorian courtship.
