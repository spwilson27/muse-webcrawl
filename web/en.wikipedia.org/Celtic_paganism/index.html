Celtic paganism

Celtic paganism, sometimes Celtic polytheism, comprises the religious beliefs and practices adhered to by the Iron Age people of Western Europe now known as the Celts, roughly between 500 BCE and 500 CE, spanning the La Tène period and the Roman era, and in the case of the Insular Celts the era of Christianisation that followed.

Celtic paganism was one of a larger group of Iron Age polytheistic religions across Europe. It was a polytheistic and animistic religion, centred on a large pantheon of deities, many of them local or tribal in character, alongside more widely venerated gods and goddesses associated with nature, warfare, healing, and crafts.

Sources

Because the Celts left few written records of their own religion, knowledge of Celtic paganism comes from archaeology, from Greco-Roman writers such as Julius Caesar and Lucan, and from the later literature of Ireland and Wales recorded after Christianisation. Archaeological evidence includes votive deposits in rivers and bogs, temple sites, carved images, and inscriptions, many of them in Romanised form.

Deities and ritual

Roman writers described gods they equated with their own deities: Caesar identified the chief Gaulish god with Mercury. Insular traditions preserve figures such as the Dagda, Lugh, Brigid, and the Morrígan in Ireland, and Lleu and Rhiannon in Wales. Ritual life included sacrifices, offerings at sacred springs and groves, and seasonal festivals such as Samhain, Imbolc, Beltane, and Lughnasa. Druids served as priests, judges, and keepers of lore.

Modern revival

Since the 20th century, Celtic neopagan movements, including Celtic reconstructionist paganism and Celtic-inspired forms of Wicca, have sought to revive or reconstruct these traditions.
