Analytic geometry

The Cartesian plane, the central tool of analytic geometry.

In mathematics, analytic geometry, also known as coordinate geometry or Cartesian geometry, is the study of geometry using a coordinate system. This contrasts with synthetic geometry. Analytic geometry is used in physics and engineering, and also in aviation, rocketry, space science, and spaceflight. It is the foundation of most modern fields of geometry, including algebraic, differential, discrete, and computational geometry.

Principles

Usually the Cartesian coordinate system is applied to manipulate equations for planes, straight lines, and circles, often in two and sometimes three dimensions. A curve in the plane is the set of points satisfying an equation — a line has an equation of the form ax + by + c = 0, a circle (x − h)2 + (y − k)2 = r2 — and geometric problems become algebraic problems of solving equations. Distance, angle, slope, and intersection can all be computed algebraically.

History

The subject was created independently in the 17th century by René Descartes, whose La Géométrie (1637) introduced the coordinate method, and by Pierre de Fermat. It supplied the conceptual bridge between algebra and geometry that made possible the later development of calculus, and today its vector-based extensions underlie computer graphics, robotics, and machine learning.
