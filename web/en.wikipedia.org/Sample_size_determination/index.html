Sample size determination

Sample size determination is the process of choosing the number of observations or replicates to include in a statistical sample. The sample size is a fundamental design decision in surveys, experiments and clinical trials: it must be large enough for the study's estimates to have acceptable precision and for hypothesis tests to have adequate statistical power, yet not so large as to waste resources or expose unnecessary numbers of subjects to risk.

Key concepts

The required sample size depends on several quantities: the expected variability of the outcome in the population; the size of the effect (or margin of error) the study is designed to detect; the chosen significance level (typically α = 0.05); and the desired statistical power (typically 0.80 or 0.90), the probability of detecting a true effect. Larger variability and smaller effects demand larger samples, whereas greater tolerated error permits smaller ones.

Common formulas

For estimating a population mean with margin of error E at confidence level corresponding to z, the classic formula is n = (z·σ / E)², where σ is the population standard deviation. For comparing two means, sample size per group is approximately n = 2σ²(zα/2 + zβ)² / Δ², with Δ the difference to be detected. For estimating a proportion, n = z²·p(1−p) / E², maximised at p = 0.5, which yields the familiar figure of about 384 respondents for a ±5 percentage-point margin at 95% confidence, before any finite population correction or allowance for non-response.

Practical considerations

Calculated sizes are routinely inflated to allow for dropouts, missing data and non-response, and adjusted for design effects in cluster or stratified sampling, which reduce the effective sample size. In clinical trials, sample size justification is an ethical as well as statistical requirement reviewed by regulators and ethics committees: under-powered trials may fail to answer their question, while over-powered trials can detect trivial effects and waste participant exposure. Pilot studies, published literature and simulation are commonly used to supply the variance and effect-size assumptions the formulas require.
