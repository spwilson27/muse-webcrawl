IP address spoofing

IP address spoofing, or IP spoofing, is the creation of Internet Protocol (IP) packets with a false source IP address, with the intent to conceal the identity of the sender or impersonate another computing system. It exploits the fact that the basic protocol of the Internet does not authenticate the source address field of a packet header. A more ware form is used in some legitimate network testing and address-management applications, but spoofing is most commonly associated with malicious activity.

Uses in attacks

Spoofing is a core technique in denial-of-service attacks: attackers flood a victim with packets bearing forged or reflected source addresses, making filtering and traceback difficult. In reflection/amplification attacks, requests with the victim's spoofed address are sent to third-party servers (such as DNS or NTP servers), whose larger responses overwhelm the victim. Spoofing has also historically been used to defeat address-based trust relationships between machines, as in the 1994 attack by Kevin Mitnick on Tsutomu Shimomura's computers.

Defenses

The primary mitigation is packet filtering: ingress filtering (BCP 38) blocks packets leaving a network with source addresses not belonging to that network, while egress and upstream filtering provide additional layers. Other defences include reverse-path forwarding checks, cryptographic authentication at higher protocol layers (such as IPsec and TLS), and TCP sequence-number randomization, which makes blind spoofing of connections impractical.
