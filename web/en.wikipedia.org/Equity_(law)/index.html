Equity is the body of law developed in the English Court of Chancery and now administered concurrently with the common law in many jurisdictions. It supplements the common law where strict application of legal rules would produce injustice, guided by principles of fairness and conscience. Its characteristic remedies — injunctions, specific performance, and the trust — are discretionary: they are awarded only where the claimant has behaved equitably ("clean hands") and where damages would be inadequate.

History

In medieval England, litigants dissatisfied with the rigid writ system of the common-law courts petitioned the king, who delegated the petitions to his Chancellor — the "keeper of the king's conscience." By the fifteenth century the Chancellor was deciding cases in his own Court of Chancery according to principles of equity rather than strict law. Conflict between the two systems peaked in the Earl of Oxford's Case (1615), after which it was settled that where equity and common law conflict, equity prevails — a rule later preserved in statute. The Judicature Acts of 1873–75 fused the administration of law and equity into a single court system in England, though the two bodies of doctrine remain distinct.

Principles and maxims

Equity is traditionally summarised in maxims, including: "Equity will not suffer a wrong to be without a remedy"; "Equity follows the law"; "He who seeks equity must do equity"; "He who comes into equity must come with clean hands"; "Delay defeats equity" (laches); and "Equity looks to the intent rather than the form." From Chancery practice grew the law of trusts, mortgages, fiduciary obligations, equitable estoppel, and the discretionary remedies of injunction, specific performance, rescission, rectification, and account of profits.

Modern application

Equity remains a living source of law in England and Wales, the United States, Canada, Australia, and other common-law jurisdictions. The United States preserves a distinction between actions at law and in equity for some purposes, including the Seventh Amendment right to jury trial. In Delaware, a separate Court of Chancery still hears corporate and fiduciary disputes.
