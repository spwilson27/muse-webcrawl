In Christian tradition, the Four Evangelists are Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John, the authors ascribed to the four canonical Gospel accounts. ("Evangelist" derives from the Greek for "bringer of good news".)

Three of the four — Matthew, Mark and Luke — are called the Synoptic Gospels because of their similar structure and overlapping content, while John's Gospel is markedly different in style and theology.

The evangelists

  • Matthew — traditionally the tax collector called by Jesus; his Gospel, written for a Jewish-Christian audience, stresses Jesus as fulfilment of prophecy.
  • Mark — companion of Peter and Paul; his Gospel is considered the earliest (c. 65–70 AD) and a source for Matthew and Luke.
  • Luke — a physician and companion of Paul; author also of Acts, emphasising Jesus's ministry to the marginalised.
  • John — traditionally the beloved disciple; his Gospel presents a highly theological portrait with the "I am" sayings.

Symbols

Since the early church, each evangelist has been associated with one of the four living creatures of Ezekiel's vision and Revelation — the tetramorph: Matthew the winged man, Mark the winged lion, Luke the winged ox, and John the eagle. The assignments, popularised by Jerome, appear throughout medieval art.

In art

Evangelist portraits open each Gospel in illuminated manuscripts such as the Lindisfarne Gospels and the Book of Kells. The symbols figure prominently in church decoration, sculpture, and heraldry — most famously St Mark's lion in Venice.

Authorship questions

The Gospels are formally anonymous; the attributions date from the 2nd century. Modern scholarship debates the extent to which the named figures were the actual authors, while churches have traditionally affirmed the attributions.
