Separate but equal

Separate but equal was a legal doctrine in United States constitutional law under which racial segregation did not violate the Fourteenth Amendment's guarantee of equal protection, so long as the facilities provided for each race were purportedly equal in quality. It provided the legal foundation for the Jim Crow laws of the American South.

Origins and Plessy v. Ferguson

The doctrine took its name from an 1849 Massachusetts case and was cemented by the U.S. Supreme Court's decision in Plessy v. Ferguson (1896). Homer Plessy, a man of mixed ancestry, was arrested for sitting in a whites-only railway car under Louisiana's Separate Car Act. The Court upheld the law 7–1, reasoning that enforced separation did not stamp either race with a "badge of inferiority." Justice John Marshall Harlan's lone dissent argued that "our Constitution is color-blind."

Application

In practice, facilities for Black Americans were systematically inferior despite the doctrine's promise of equality. "Separate but equal" was applied to schools, transport, housing, and public accommodations across the segregated South for more than half a century.

Overturning

A series of NAACP-led challenges, including Sweatt v. Painter (1950) and McLaurin v. Oklahoma (1950), eroded the doctrine, and in Brown v. Board of Education (1954) the Supreme Court unanimously held that "separate educational facilities are inherently unequal," overturning Plessy in public education. The Civil Rights Act of 1964 and later decisions dismantled the doctrine across other spheres.
