Francophone literature

Francophone literature is literature written in the French language by authors from countries and regions where French is an official, widely spoken, or historically significant language outside metropolitan France — including Belgium, Switzerland, Quebec and Acadia in Canada, the Caribbean, West and Central Africa, the Maghreb, Lebanon, and Southeast Asia. The term littérature francophone emerged after the Second World War as decolonization produced a body of work distinct from, and often in dialogue or tension with, French literature proper.

Major movements and authors

The Négritude movement of the 1930s, founded by Aimé Césaire (Martinique), Léopold Sédar Senghor (Senegal), and Léon-Gontran Damas (French Guiana), asserted Black cultural identity against colonial assimilation. Mid-twentieth-century Maghrebi writers such as Kateb Yacine and Assia Djebar (Algeria) and Tahar Ben Jelloun (Morocco) explored colonialism, language, and gender. In Quebec, writers including Gabrielle Roy, Anne Hébert, and Michel Tremblay forged a distinct national literature. Caribbean authors such as Édouard Glissant and Maryse Condé (Guadeloupe) and Patrick Chamoiseau (Martinique) developed theories of créolité and relation.

The "world literature" debate

In 2007 a manifesto signed by 44 writers called for a littérature-monde ("world literature") in French, arguing that the centre–periphery hierarchy between Paris and the francophone margins was obsolete — a claim dramatized by the awarding of major French literary prizes to non-metropolitan authors. Major francophone prizes include the Prix des cinq continents de la francophonie.
