Simple living

A simple rural lifestyle centred on self-sufficiency and modest consumption

Simple living, also known as voluntary simplicity, is a lifestyle characterised by minimising one's consumption of material goods and services and reducing dependence on technology, money, and institutional structures. Practitioners may adopt simple living for a wide variety of personal reasons, including spirituality, health, environmental concern, anti-consumerism, and a desire for greater free time and meaningful work.

History

The idea of living simply has ancient roots. Religious traditions such as Buddhism, early Christianity, and Jainism encouraged renunciation of worldly possessions. In the 19th century, the American writer Henry David Thoreau famously chronicled his experiment in simple living in Walden (1854), based on his stay in a cabin at Walden Pond. The term "voluntary simplicity" was popularised by Richard B. Gregg in the 1930s and later by Duane Elgin's 1981 book Voluntary Simplicity.

Practices

Common practices associated with simple living include decluttering and reducing possessions, growing one's own food, using fewer resources, choosing public transport or cycling over car ownership, working fewer hours, and participating in local exchange and barter systems. Some practitioners embrace related movements such as downshifting, the tiny house movement, and homesteading.

Motivations

Motivations vary widely: environmental sustainability and reduced ecological footprint; financial independence and freedom from debt; spiritual fulfilment; resistance to consumer culture and advertising; improved mental and physical health; and a wish to spend more time with family and community. Critics argue that voluntary simplicity is a privilege available mainly to those who already have economic security, and that it should not be confused with involuntary poverty.
