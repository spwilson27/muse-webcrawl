Reporter gene

In molecular biology, a reporter gene is a gene that researchers attach to a regulatory sequence or gene of interest in bacteria, cell culture, animals, or plants. Reporter genes encode products with easily measurable characteristics—such as fluorescence, colour change, or light emission—that allow scientists to track whether a gene has been taken up or expressed, and where, when, and how strongly it is active.

Common reporter genes

  • GFP (green fluorescent protein), from the jellyfish Aequorea victoria, and its colour variants (YFP, CFP, mCherry and other fluorescent proteins), visualised by fluorescence microscopy.
  • lacZ, encoding β-galactosidase, detected by the blue product formed from the substrate X-gal or by quantitative colorimetric assays.
  • Luciferase genes from fireflies or the sea pansy Renilla, which produce bioluminescence measurable with great sensitivity.
  • CAT (chloramphenicol acetyltransferase), a historically important enzymatic reporter.
  • GUS (β-glucuronidase), widely used in plant molecular biology.

Uses

Reporter genes are used to study gene expression and regulation: by placing a reporter under the control of a promoter of interest, its activity reports the promoter's strength and its response to transcription factors, drugs, or signals. They are used in reporter assays for high-throughput drug screening, in enhancer and promoter bashing experiments, to monitor transfection and transduction efficiency, and in transgenic organisms to visualise developmental expression patterns. Reporter systems also underpin two-hybrid assays and biosensors for environmental and clinical samples.

Design considerations

An ideal reporter has little or no endogenous background activity in the host, a sensitive and linear assay, and a stable product. Fusion reporters join the reporter protein to the protein of interest to track its localisation, while transcriptional reporters leave the protein unmodified. Destabilised reporter variants, with short half-lives, are used to follow rapid changes in gene activity over time.
