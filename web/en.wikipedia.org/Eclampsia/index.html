Eclampsia is the onset of seizures (convulsions) in a woman with pre-eclampsia, a hypertensive disorder of pregnancy characterised by high blood pressure and proteinuria. It is a serious complication of pregnancy and one of the leading causes of maternal death worldwide. Eclampsia most often occurs during or shortly after labour, but can also occur before delivery or up to several weeks postpartum.

Signs and symptoms

The defining feature is a tonic–clonic seizure in a woman with pre-eclampsia, not attributable to another cause. Warning features may include severe headache, blurred vision or flashing lights, epigastric pain, brisk reflexes and reduced urine output. Complications include placental abruption, cerebral haemorrhage, aspiration, pulmonary oedema, HELLP syndrome, disseminated intravascular coagulation and death.

Causes and pathophysiology

Pre-eclampsia arises from abnormal development of the placental vasculature, leading to widespread maternal endothelial dysfunction, vasospasm and hypertension. In eclampsia, cerebral oedema and loss of normal cerebral autoregulation are believed to precipitate the seizures.

Treatment

Management prioritises stabilising the mother: protection of the airway, control of blood pressure with agents such as labetalol or hydralazine, and prevention of further seizures with intravenous magnesium sulfate, which large trials (notably the Magpie Trial) showed halves the risk of eclampsia in women with pre-eclampsia. The definitive treatment is delivery of the baby and placenta, usually expedited once the mother is stable. Low-dose aspirin given from early pregnancy reduces the risk in women at high risk of pre-eclampsia.
