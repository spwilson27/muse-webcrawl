Electric charge

Electric charge (symbol q) is a physical property of matter that causes it to experience a force when placed in an electromagnetic field. Charge comes in two types, conventionally called positive and negative; like charges repel and opposite charges attract. Electric charge is an intrinsic property of subatomic particles: the electron carries charge −e and the proton +e, where the elementary charge e = 1.602176634×10−19 coulombs exactly.

Properties

Charge is quantised: it occurs in integer multiples of e (with the exception of quarks, which carry ±e/3 and ±2e/3 but are confined within hadrons). It is conserved: the total charge of an isolated system never changes, a law expressed locally by the continuity equation. The force between two point charges is given by Coulomb's law, falling off with the square of the distance.

History

The Greeks observed that rubbed amber attracts light objects (the word "electric" derives from Greek ēlektron, amber). Benjamin Franklin proposed the single-fluid theory and assigned the positive/negative signs in the 1740s. Charles-Augustin de Coulomb quantified the electrostatic force in 1785; Michael Faraday and James Clerk Maxwell developed field theory; and Robert Millikan's oil-drop experiment (1909) measured the elementary charge.

Charge in circuits and matter

Moving charge constitutes electric current, measured in amperes (coulombs per second). In materials, charge may be carried by electrons, ions or both; charging by friction (the triboelectric effect) transfers electrons between surfaces. In quantum electrodynamics charge is the coupling constant of the electromagnetic interaction, linked by gauge symmetry (U(1)) to charge conservation.
