Alternative dispute resolution

Mediation is one of the principal forms of ADR.

Alternative dispute resolution (ADR) refers to processes and techniques for resolving legal disputes outside the courts, with the help of a neutral third party. The main forms are negotiation, mediation, arbitration and conciliation. ADR is generally faster, cheaper and less adversarial than litigation, and courts in many jurisdictions encourage or require parties to attempt it.

Forms

Mediation uses a neutral mediator who helps the parties reach their own settlement; the mediator has no power to impose an outcome. Arbitration resembles a private trial: an arbitrator hears evidence and renders a binding award, which courts will usually enforce. Conciliation is similar to mediation but the conciliator may propose terms. Other forms include early neutral evaluation, mini-trials and online dispute resolution (ODR).

History and development

Although arbitration and mediation are ancient, the modern ADR movement dates from the 1970s in the United States, driven by concern over court congestion and cost; the 1976 Pound Conference is often taken as its launch point. Since then ADR clauses have become standard in commercial contracts, and international arbitration institutions such as the ICC, LCIA and SIAC handle large numbers of cross-border cases.

Advantages and criticism

Supporters cite flexibility, confidentiality, party control and the preservation of business relationships. Critics argue that mandatory arbitration clauses in consumer and employment contracts can disadvantage weaker parties by waiving access to courts and class actions, and that private proceedings lack transparency and precedent.
