Carl David Anderson (September 3, 1905 – January 11, 1991) was an American physicist. He is best known for his discovery of the positron in 1932, an achievement for which he received the 1936 Nobel Prize in Physics, and of the muon in 1936.

Discovery of the positron

Working with cloud chambers in strong magnetic fields at Caltech under Robert Millikan, Anderson photographed cosmic-ray particle tracks in 1932. One track curved as an electron's would if positively charged and showed ionization consistent with an electron-mass particle moving upward through a lead plate—evidence for the antielectron predicted by Paul Dirac, which Anderson named the positron.

Muon

In 1936 Anderson and his student Seth Neddermeyer identified a particle of intermediate mass in cosmic rays, later named the muon. Initially mistaken for Yukawa's predicted meson, it proved to be a heavier cousin of the electron.

Later life

Anderson spent his entire career at Caltech, contributing to rocket and defense research during the Second World War. He remains among the youngest recipients of the Nobel Prize in Physics.
