Diophantus of Alexandria was a Greek mathematician who lived in the 3rd century AD, probably between AD 200 and 284. He is sometimes called "the father of algebra", though the title is debated, and he is best known for his Arithmetica, a treatise on solving algebraic equations that profoundly influenced the development of number theory.

Arithmetica

The Arithmetica originally comprised 13 books, of which six survive in Greek and four more in an Arabic translation discovered in the 20th century. It presents some 130 problems — many seeking rational solutions to systems of equations in several unknowns — with worked solutions. Diophantus introduced a syncopated algebraic notation, using an abbreviation for the unknown and symbols for its powers, a step between purely rhetorical algebra and modern symbolism.

Legacy

Equations requiring integer solutions are called Diophantine equations in his honour, and the study of such problems is called Diophantine analysis. A famous Latin translation of the Arithmetica, published by Claude Gaspard Bachet de Méziriac in 1621, was read by Pierre de Fermat, whose note in its margin about xⁿ + yⁿ = zⁿ became Fermat's Last Theorem, unsolved until 1994.

Little is known of Diophantus's life. A puzzle-epigram in the Greek Anthology, if taken literally, implies he married at 33, had a son who died at 42, and died himself aged 84.
