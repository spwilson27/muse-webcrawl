Maskirovka

Russian military doctrine of deception

Maskirovka (Russian: маскировка, "masking" or "camouflage") is a military doctrine developed by the Soviet Union and continued in Russian practice, encompassing a broad range of measures — camouflage, concealment, decoys, feints, disinformation and denial — intended to deceive an adversary about the presence, position, strength and intentions of friendly forces. Unlike narrower Western concepts of tactical camouflage, maskirovka was developed as a formal, centrally planned component of operations at every level from tactical to strategic.

Second World War

The Red Army systematised maskirovka during the Second World War. Elaborate deception plans concealed the build-up for major offensives such as Operation Uranus at Stalingrad (1942) and Operation Bagration (1944), in which dummy armies, false radio traffic, strict movement discipline and disinformation led German intelligence to misjudge Soviet strength and axes of attack. The deceptions preceding the Battle of Kursk in 1943 similarly hid the depth of the Soviet defensive preparations.

Cold War and after

Soviet doctrine extended maskirovka to the strategic level, including concealing weapons programmes; the West learned of some only through espionage and satellite reconnaissance. The concept has been widely invoked by analysts to describe Russian information operations and deniable deployments in the 21st century, though scholars debate how far contemporary practice reflects the formal doctrine.
