Chlorination is the most widely used method of water disinfection.

Water chlorination is the process of adding chlorine or chlorine compounds — such as sodium hypochlorite or chloramine — to water in order to kill bacteria, viruses and other microorganisms. It is the most widely used method of disinfecting public drinking-water supplies and swimming pools, and it is credited, together with filtration, with dramatically reducing deaths from waterborne diseases such as cholera and typhoid fever in the developed world during the twentieth century.

History

Chlorine compounds were used for emergency disinfection in the nineteenth century — notably by John Snow's contemporaries and at Maidstone, England, in 1897 during a typhoid epidemic. The first continuous municipal chlorination began at Jersey City, New Jersey, in 1908, using calcium hypochlorite on the Boonton Reservoir supply. The practice spread rapidly through North America and Europe.

Mechanism and practice

Chlorine added to water forms hypochlorous acid, which penetrates microbial cell walls and disrupts enzymes and DNA. A small residual of chlorine is maintained throughout distribution systems to guard against recontamination, which distinguishes chlorine from ozone and ultraviolet disinfection, which leave no residual. Chloramine (chlorine combined with ammonia) provides a longer-lasting residual and is used by many utilities. Chlorination is highly effective against bacteria and most viruses but less effective against some protozoan cysts, notably Cryptosporidium, which is usually controlled by filtration.

By-products and safety

Chlorine reacts with natural organic matter to form disinfection by-products such as trihalomethanes and haloacetic acids, which are regulated because of long-term health concerns at elevated levels. Utilities balance microbial safety — the primary and acute risk — against by-product formation through source protection, removal of organic matter and careful dosing.
