Roland Gérard Barthes (1915–1980) was a French literary theorist, essayist and semiotician whose writings transformed the study of literature and popular culture. His career moved from Marxist cultural criticism through structuralism to a more personal, post-structuralist mode.

Life

Barthes was born in Cherbourg; his father, a naval officer, was killed in the First World War when Roland was an infant. Raised in Bayonne and Paris, he studied classics at the Sorbonne while suffering recurrent tuberculosis that kept him in sanatoria and out of military service. He taught in Bucharest and Alexandria — where he met Algirdas Greimas — before joining the CNRS and then the École pratique des hautes études in Paris. In 1976 he was elected to a chair of literary semiology at the Collège de France. In February 1980 he was struck by a laundry van in Paris and died of his injuries a month later.

Major ideas

Writing Degree Zero (1953) attacked the conventions of classical French prose. Mythologies (1957) decoded everyday phenomena — wrestling, soap advertisements, steak and chips, the Citroën DS — as bourgeois myths, founding modern cultural studies. In S/Z (1970), a line-by-line reading of Balzac's "Sarrasine", Barthes distinguished five codes of narrative and contrasted "readerly" with "writerly" texts.

His 1967 essay "The Death of the Author" argued that meaning is completed by the reader, not dictated by the author's intention — a slogan of post-structuralism. His final book, Camera Lucida (1980), written after his mother's death, distinguished photography's studium from the piercing detail he called the punctum, and remains a classic of photographic theory.
