Twin prime

Twin prime pairs plotted along the number line.

A twin prime is a prime number that is either 2 less or 2 more than another prime number — that is, one member of a twin prime pair, a pair of primes (p, p + 2). The first twin prime pairs are (3, 5), (5, 7), (11, 13), (17, 19), (29, 31), (41, 43), (59, 61) and (71, 73).

The twin prime conjecture

The twin prime conjecture states that there are infinitely many twin primes. It is one of the oldest open problems in number theory, attributed in some form to antiquity, and a special case of Alphonse de Polignac's 1849 conjecture that for every even number k there are infinitely many prime pairs differing by k. Although the sum of the reciprocals of the twin primes converges (Brun's theorem, 1919 — its sum is Brun's constant, about 1.902), a counting argument of this kind cannot settle the conjecture either way.

Progress

Major progress came in 2013, when Yitang Zhang proved that there are infinitely many prime pairs separated by at most 70 million. Collaborative work through the Polymath8 project, and independent results by James Maynard and Terence Tao, reduced this bounded gap to 246 unconditionally. Related milestones include Chen Jingrun's theorem that there are infinitely many primes p for which p + 2 is prime or a product of two primes.

Except for (3, 5), every twin prime pair has the form (6n − 1, 6n + 1), since every prime greater than 3 lies adjacent to a multiple of six. The largest known twin prime pair, found in 2016, consists of numbers with 388,342 decimal digits.
