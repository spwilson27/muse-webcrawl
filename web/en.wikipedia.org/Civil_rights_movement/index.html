Civil rights movement

The civil rights movement was a social movement in the United States during the 1950s and 1960s which aimed to give African Americans equal rights under the law. It sought to end racial segregation and discrimination enforced by the "Jim Crow" laws of the Southern states, and to secure federal protection of the constitutional rights of citizenship — above all the right to vote — promised by the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments but denied in practice for nearly a century.

Background

After Reconstruction, Southern states erected a regime of segregation and disenfranchisement upheld by the Supreme Court's "separate but equal" doctrine in Plessy v. Ferguson (1896). Black Americans responded through the courts (the NAACP's legal campaign), the Great Migration to Northern cities, and activism by veterans returning from the Second World War.

Major campaigns

The movement's modern phase opened with Brown v. Board of Education (1954), which declared school segregation unconstitutional, and the Montgomery bus boycott (1955–56), sparked by Rosa Parks's arrest and led by the young Martin Luther King Jr., which ended with desegregated buses. The Little Rock Nine (1957), the student sit-ins of 1960 (beginning in Greensboro), the Freedom Rides of 1961, and the Albany and Birmingham campaigns of 1962–63 brought nonviolent direct action to national attention — images of fire hoses and police dogs in Birmingham shocked television audiences worldwide. The March on Washington in August 1963 drew some 250,000 people. Freedom Summer (1964) registered Black voters in Mississippi amid violence, including the murders of three civil rights workers. The Selma to Montgomery marches of 1965, attacked on "Bloody Sunday", directly galvanised the Voting Rights Act.

Legislation and later years

The Civil Rights Act of 1964 outlawed segregation in public accommodations and employment discrimination; the Voting Rights Act of 1965 suspended literacy tests and sent federal registrars to the South, transforming the Southern electorate; the Fair Housing Act followed in 1968, days after King's assassination in Memphis on 4 April 1968. In the later 1960s the movement's focus widened to Northern de facto segregation, housing and economic inequality — King's Poor People's Campaign — while the Black Power movement urged pride, self-determination and, for some, a break with integrationist strategy.

Legacy

The movement dismantled legal segregation and restored Black voting rights, provided a template for subsequent movements (women's rights, disability rights, LGBTQ rights), and remains central to American civic memory, commemorated by the federal Martin Luther King Jr. Day holiday and numerous museums and historic sites.
