Banana

A banana is an elongated, edible fruit produced by several kinds of large herbaceous flowering plants in the genus Musa. In some countries, bananas used for cooking may be called "plantains", distinguishing them from dessert bananas. The fruit is variable in size, color, and firmness, but is usually elongated and curved, with soft flesh rich in starch covered with a peel, which may be green, yellow, red, purple, or brown when ripe. The fruits grow upward in clusters near the top of the plant.

Description

Banana plants are the largest herbaceous flowering plants. All the above-ground parts of a banana plant grow from a structure called a corm. Plants are normally tall and fairly sturdy, and are often mistaken for trees, but their main stem is a false stem called a pseudostem, composed of tightly packed leaf sheaths. Each pseudostem can produce a single bunch of bananas.

Cultivation

Bananas are grown in at least 135 countries, primarily for their fruit, and to a lesser extent to make fiber, banana wine, and as ornamental plants. The vast majority of bananas sold commercially are of the Cavendish subgroup, which replaced the Gros Michel variety after the spread of Panama disease. India is the world's largest producer of bananas, followed by China.

Nutrition

Raw bananas (not including the peel) are 75% water, 23% carbohydrates, 1% protein, and contain negligible fat. A 100-gram reference amount supplies 89 calories, and bananas are a rich source of vitamin B6 and a moderate source of vitamin C, manganese, and potassium.

History

Bananas were originally domesticated in Southeast Asia and Papua New Guinea. They spread westward through trade and were known in the Mediterranean region by the early medieval period. Portuguese explorers brought bananas to the Americas in the 16th century, and commercial cultivation expanded greatly in the 19th century with companies such as United Fruit.
