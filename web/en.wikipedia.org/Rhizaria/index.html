The Rhizaria are a diverse and species-rich supergroup of mostly unicellular eukaryotes. Except for the Chlorarachniophytes and three species in the genus Paulinella, they are non-photosynthetic. This supergroup was proposed by Thomas Cavalier-Smith in 2002 on the basis of molecular phylogenies, and its members were formerly lumped among the amoebae. Rhizaria range from naked amoeboid cells to some of the most intricate mineral skeletons known among protists.

The name refers to the root-like, thread-like pseudopodia (reticulopodia, filopodia or axopodia) that most members use for feeding and locomotion. Together with the Stramenopiles and Alveolata, Rhizaria forms the SAR supergroup, one of the major divisions of eukaryotes.

Groups

  • Cercozoa — a large, varied phylum of flagellates and amoebae, abundant in soils and fresh water; includes the plant-parasitic plasmodiophorids and the photosynthetic chlorarachniophytes.
  • Foraminifera — marine (and some freshwater) amoebae with granular reticulopodia and chambered shells (tests) usually of calcium carbonate; their fossil shells are vital in biostratigraphy and palaeoclimate reconstruction, and form chalk and limestone deposits.
  • Radiolaria — marine plankton with stiff axopodia radiating from a central capsule and intricate siliceous skeletons; together with the related Acantharea (with strontium sulfate spicules) they form the Retaria with foraminifera in most classifications.

Ecology and significance

Rhizarians are major components of marine plankton and benthos and of soil microbial food webs. Foraminiferal and radiolarian skeletons raining onto the deep-sea floor form extensive oozes and contribute significantly to the global carbon and silica cycles. Some rhizarians are parasites of plants and animals of economic importance.
