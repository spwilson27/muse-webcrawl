War

War is an intense armed conflict between states, governments, societies, or paramilitary groups such as mercenaries, insurgents, and militias. It is generally characterized by extreme violence, aggression, destruction, and mortality, using regular or irregular military forces. While some scholars regard war as a universal and ancestral aspect of human nature, others argue it is a result of specific sociocultural, economic, or ecological circumstances.

History

The earliest evidence of warfare dates to the Mesolithic era, and organized warfare expanded with the rise of states and empires. Gunpowder, industrialization, and conscription transformed war's scale in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries; the two world wars killed tens of millions. Since 1945, interstate wars have become less common while civil wars, insurgencies, and proxy conflicts have persisted. The deadliest conflict in history was the Second World War, with an estimated 70–85 million deaths.

Laws and conduct

The conduct of war is governed by international humanitarian law, including the Hague and Geneva Conventions, which protect prisoners of war, the wounded, and civilians, and prohibit certain weapons and methods. The United Nations Charter prohibits wars of aggression, reserving the lawful use of force mainly to self-defense and Security Council authorization.

Theory

Carl von Clausewitz famously defined war as "the continuation of policy by other means." Theories of war's causes range across human nature, the structure of the state system, ideology, resources, and miscalculation. Related concepts include just war theory, deterrence, total war, asymmetric warfare, and hybrid warfare.

Effects

Beyond battlefield deaths, war causes famine, disease, displacement, psychological trauma, and destruction of infrastructure and cultural heritage, with consequences lasting generations. Efforts to limit war include diplomacy, international institutions, arms control, and peacekeeping.
