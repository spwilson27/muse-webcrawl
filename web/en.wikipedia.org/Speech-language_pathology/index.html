Speech–language pathology

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Speech–language pathology (also known as speech and language therapy or, in Australian English, speech pathology) is a field of healthcare expertise practiced globally. Speech–language pathology is the study and treatment of speech and language problems, including speech sound disorders, language disorders, social communication disorders, cognitive-communication disorders, voice disorders, fluency disorders such as stuttering, and swallowing disorders (dysphagia).

Scope of practice

Speech-language pathologists (SLPs) assess, diagnose, and treat communication and swallowing disorders in people of all ages, from infants with feeding difficulties to adults recovering from stroke or traumatic brain injury. They work in schools, hospitals, rehabilitation centers, private practice, and research. Interventions may target articulation, receptive and expressive language, augmentative and alternative communication (AAC), voice therapy, and fluency shaping.

Conditions treated

Common caseloads include developmental language disorder, speech sound disorders, childhood apraxia of speech, aphasia, dysarthria, autism-related communication difficulties, hearing-impairment-related speech problems, and dysphagia. SLPs also provide accent modification and communication training on an elective basis.

Training and regulation

In the United States, SLPs typically hold a master's degree in speech-language pathology, complete a clinical fellowship, and obtain state licensure and optional certification from the American Speech-Language-Hearing Association (ASHA). In the United Kingdom, speech and language therapists are regulated by the Health and Care Professions Council.

History

The profession developed in the early twentieth century from elocution teaching and from medical work with soldiers with brain injuries after the First World War; professional associations were founded in the 1920s and 1930s, and the field expanded greatly after the Second World War.
