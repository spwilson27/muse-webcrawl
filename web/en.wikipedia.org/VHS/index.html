The Video Home System (VHS) is a standard for consumer-level analogue video recording on tape cassettes, developed by the Victor Company of Japan (JVC) and introduced in Japan in September 1976 and in North America in 1977. VHS won the "format war" against Sony's Betamax and Video 2000 to become the dominant home video format of the 1980s and 1990s, enabling widespread time-shifting of television and the home video rental industry. It was eventually displaced by DVD and then by streaming media.

History

JVC's engineers, led by Yuma Shiraishi and Shizuo Takano, designed VHS around simplicity, low cost and a two-hour recording time — long enough for a feature film or an American football game — at a moment when Betamax initially offered only one hour. JVC licensed the format widely to other manufacturers, a strategy that built a large installed base and a deep catalogue of prerecorded tapes. The format war is a classic case study in business schools: although Betamax was widely perceived as technically superior in picture quality, VHS's longer recording time, lower prices and wider availability won the market. The 1984 U.S. Supreme Court decision in Sony Corp. of America v. Universal City Studios (the "Betamax case") established that home time-shifting was fair use, clearing a legal cloud over all VCRs.

Technical characteristics

VHS records a composite analogue signal on half-inch tape using helical scan: two video heads on a rotating drum lay down slanted tracks as the tape moves at 33.35 mm/s (NTSC SP mode). Horizontal resolution is about 240 lines. The cassette measures 187 × 103 × 25 mm and contains up to about 430 m of tape. Later refinements included hi-fi stereo audio recorded by additional heads, the higher-resolution S-VHS variant (1987), VHS-C compact cassettes for camcorders, and digital D-VHS. Copy protection on commercial tapes commonly used Macrovision.

Cultural impact and decline

VHS created the video rental store (epitomised by Blockbuster), the sell-through market for films, and new habits of home viewing; the slogan "Be Kind, Rewind" captured the etiquette of the rental era. DVD overtook VHS rentals in the United States in 2003; the last major Hollywood film released on VHS was A History of Violence in 2006, and Funai, the last manufacturer of VCRs, ended production in July 2016. VHS has since acquired a nostalgic afterlife among collectors, horror-film enthusiasts and artists who prize its distinctive image artefacts, tracking noise and degradation.
