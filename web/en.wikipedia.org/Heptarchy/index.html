The Heptarchy (from Greek hepta, "seven", and arkhein, "to rule") is a term for the seven kingdoms of Anglo-Saxon England that flourished between the end of Roman Britain and the unification of England in the 10th century. The traditional seven are East Anglia, Essex, Kent, Mercia, Northumbria, Sussex, and Wessex. The term was popularised by the 12th-century historian Henry of Huntingdon, though modern historians regard the political map as far more fluid than a stable group of seven states.

The kingdoms

  • Kent — the earliest established kingdom, said to have been founded by the Jutish brothers Hengist and Horsa (traditionally 449).
  • Northumbria — formed by the union of Bernicia and Deira, dominant in the 7th century under kings such as Edwin and Oswald.
  • Mercia — the great midland power, supreme in the 8th century under Æthelbald and Offa.
  • Wessex — the West Saxon kingdom that resisted the Vikings under Alfred the Great and produced the kings who united England.
  • East Anglia — kingdom of the East Angles, home of the Sutton Hoo ship burial.
  • Essex and Sussex — smaller kingdoms of the East and South Saxons, usually subordinate to stronger neighbours.

Supremacy and bretwaldas

At any time one king might be recognised as overlord of the others; the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle lists eight kings who held the title bretwalda ("ruler of Britain"), including Ælle of Sussex, Ceawlin of Wessex, Edwin and Oswiu of Northumbria, and Egbert of Wessex. In practice, supremacy passed from Northumbria (7th century) to Mercia (8th century) to Wessex (9th century).

End of the Heptarchy

The Viking invasions from 865 destroyed or subjugated Northumbria, East Anglia, and eastern Mercia, leaving Wessex as the surviving English kingdom. Alfred's successors Edward the Elder and Æthelflæd reconquered the Danelaw, and in 927 Alfred's grandson Æthelstan took York and was recognised as king of all England, ending the age of the separate kingdoms.
