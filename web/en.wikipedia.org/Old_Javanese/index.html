Old Javanese

Old Javanese, also known as Kawi, is the oldest attested stage of the Javanese language, spoken in the central and eastern parts of Java from roughly the 9th to the 15th century AD, during the Hindu-Buddhist period of Javanese history. It was the language of a rich court literature — inscriptions, prose works (parwa) and poems (kakawin) — composed under kingdoms such as Mataram and Majapahit. Its lexicon absorbed a very large number of loanwords from Sanskrit.

Corpus and literature

Old Javanese is known from more than a thousand stone and copper inscriptions and from literary manuscripts preserved especially in Bali, where the tradition continued after Islamisation changed Java's culture. Major works include the Kakawin Ramayana (9th–10th century), the oldest and grandest kakawin; the Arjunawiwaha of Mpu Kanwa (c. 1030); the Bharatayuddha (1157) of Mpu Sedah and Mpu Panuluh; and the Nagarakretagama (1365) of Mpu Prapanca, a eulogy of King Hayam Wuruk of Majapahit that is a prime historical source. Kakawin poetry follows Sanskrit quantitative metres and conventions while telling Indian epic stories in a Javanese setting.

Language features

Old Javanese grammar retains an Austronesian voice system with actor, patient and benefactive verb forms, and uses particles and pronouns quite unlike the later speech-level system of modern Javanese. Sanskrit influence is most visible in vocabulary, literary style and the Kawi script, an early descendant of the Pallava script of southern India.

Legacy

Modern Javanese descends from Old Javanese through a Middle Javanese stage. Old Javanese gave Indonesia key vocabulary: the national motto Bhinneka Tunggal Ika ("Unity in Diversity") is a quotation from the 14th-century kakawin Sutasoma by Mpu Tantular, and the name Nusantara for the archipelago comes from Old Javanese usage. The language is still studied and ceremonially used in Bali.
