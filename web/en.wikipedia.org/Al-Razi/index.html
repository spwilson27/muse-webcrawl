Al-Razi

Born: c. 864 CE, Rayy, Persia (near modern Tehran)

Died: c. 925 CE, Rayy

Fields: Medicine, alchemy, philosophy

Known in Latin as: Rhazes (Rasis)

Abu Bakr Muhammad ibn Zakariya al-Razi (c. 864 – c. 925 CE), known in Latin as Rhazes, was a Persian physician, philosopher, and alchemist of the Islamic Golden Age, widely regarded as one of the greatest physicians of the medieval world.

Medical works

Al-Razi directed hospitals in Rayy and Baghdad and wrote more than 200 works. His encyclopaedic Kitab al-Hawi ("The Comprehensive Book of Medicine") compiled Greek, Indian, and Persian medical knowledge with his own observations. His treatise on smallpox and measles gave the first clear clinical differentiation of the two diseases and was translated into many languages and printed in Europe into the 19th century. He emphasized clinical observation, case histories, diet, and hygiene, and wrote a famous work on medical ethics and one on quackery.

Alchemy and philosophy

In alchemy, al-Razi described laboratory apparatus and procedures systematically and distinguished substances empirically; his Kitab Sirr al-Asrar ("Book of the Secret of Secrets") classified minerals and described distillation and sublimation. Philosophically he was an independent and controversial thinker who criticized revealed religion in works that survive mainly through refutations, and he developed a theory of the "five eternals" — God, soul, matter, time, and place.

Legacy

Al-Razi's medical writings were standard texts in European universities for centuries. The Razi Institute in Iran and numerous hospitals bear his name.
