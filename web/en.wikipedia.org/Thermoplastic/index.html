Thermoplastic

A thermoplastic, or thermosoftening plastic, is a polymer material that becomes pliable or mouldable at a certain elevated temperature and solidifies upon cooling. Most thermoplastics have a high molecular weight, with long chains held together by intermolecular forces that weaken with heat, allowing the material to be repeatedly melted and reshaped. This contrasts with thermosetting polymers, which form irreversible chemical bonds during curing and cannot be remelted.

Properties and examples

Common thermoplastics include polyethylene, polypropylene, polyvinyl chloride (PVC), polystyrene, nylon, polycarbonate, acrylic (PMMA) and polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE). Depending on their structure they are amorphous, softening over a range around their glass transition temperature, or semi-crystalline, with a definite melting point. Thermoplastics are processed by injection moulding, extrusion, blow moulding and thermoforming, and are used in packaging, pipes, textiles, automotive parts, medical devices and 3D-printing filaments such as PLA and ABS.

Recycling

Because they can be remelted, thermoplastics are in principle recyclable, and the most common types carry resin identification codes 1–6 (PET, HDPE, PVC, LDPE, PP and PS). In practice, recycling rates are limited by collection, contamination and the degradation of polymer properties on reprocessing.
