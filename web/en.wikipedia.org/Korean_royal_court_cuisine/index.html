Korean royal court cuisine

Korean royal court cuisine (Joseon wangjo gungjung yori) is the style of cooking developed in the royal palace of the Joseon dynasty (1392–1910). Refined, mild, and highly codified, it represents the pinnacle of traditional Korean food culture and was designated an Important Intangible Cultural Heritage of South Korea in 1971.

The royal table (surasang)

The king and queen received two formal rice meals a day, the sura, served on a low table arranged according to strict Confucian etiquette. The full setting (12-cheop bansang) comprised rice, soups, stews, kimchi, sauces, and twelve side dishes in covered brass bowls, accompanied by specific dishes such as jeon (pan-fried items), steamed and braised meats, and sinseollo, the elaborate hot pot cooked at the table in a vessel with a central chimney. Food was tasted first by attendants, and serving was handled by court ladies (sura sanggung).

Characteristics

Unlike much modern Korean food, court cuisine is mild — restrained in chilli, garlic, and salt — emphasising seasonality, balance of colour and texture, and the finest ingredients drawn as tribute from all eight provinces. Presentation was meticulous; dishes were cut to uniform size and arranged in yin-yang colour harmony.

Preservation

After the fall of Joseon, the tradition was carried on by former court ladies. The cook Han Bok-ryeo, designated a "holder" of the intangible heritage, founded the Institute of Korean Royal Cuisine and helped codify the repertoire, which today is taught and served at specialist restaurants in Seoul.

Famous dishes

  • Sinseollo — royal hot pot of meat, seafood, and vegetables
  • Gujeolpan — nine-section platter of fillings with thin pancakes
  • Japchae — originally a court vegetable dish, later made with glass noodles
  • Galbijjim — braised short ribs
  • Eomandu — fish dumplings served to the king
