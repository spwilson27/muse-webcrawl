Antibiotic resistance

Antibiotic resistance (also antimicrobial resistance, AMR) occurs when microbes evolve mechanisms that protect them from the effects of antibiotics, making standard treatments ineffective and infections harder or impossible to treat. Resistant microbes are commonly called superbugs. Antibiotic resistance is a major global public health threat: bacterial AMR was directly responsible for an estimated 1.27 million deaths worldwide in 2019.

Mechanisms and causes

Bacteria become resistant through genetic mutation and horizontal gene transfer, acquiring genes that produce drug-destroying enzymes (such as beta-lactamases), modify drug targets, pump drugs out of the cell, or block drug entry. Resistance is accelerated by overuse and misuse of antibiotics in human medicine and by their widespread use in agriculture and aquaculture, which select for resistant strains that spread between people, animals and the environment.

Response

Responses include antimicrobial stewardship programs, infection prevention, surveillance, rapid diagnostics, development of new antibiotics and alternatives such as phage therapy, and global action plans coordinated by the World Health Organization. Notorious resistant pathogens include MRSA, extensively drug-resistant tuberculosis, and carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales.
