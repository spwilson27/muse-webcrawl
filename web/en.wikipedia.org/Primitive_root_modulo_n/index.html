Primitive root modulo n

In modular arithmetic, a number g is a primitive root modulo n if every number coprime to n is congruent to a power of g modulo n. That is, the powers of g generate the multiplicative group of integers modulo n, and the multiplicative order of g equals Euler's totient φ(n). For example, 3 is a primitive root modulo 7, since its powers 3, 2, 6, 4, 5, 1 exhaust all residues coprime to 7.

Powers of a primitive root cycle through every unit modulo n.

Existence

Primitive roots exist modulo n exactly when n is 1, 2, 4, pk, or 2pk, where p is an odd prime — that is, exactly when the multiplicative group modulo n is cyclic. When a primitive root exists, there are φ(φ(n)) of them. Gauss developed the theory in his Disquisitiones Arithmeticae (1801), introducing the concept under the name "primitive root."

Finding primitive roots

To test whether g is a primitive root modulo prime p, one factors p − 1 and checks that g(p−1)/q is not congruent to 1 for any prime factor q of p − 1. No simple formula gives the least primitive root, though it is known to be comparatively small; Artin's conjecture, still unproven, predicts that any non-square integer other than −1 is a primitive root for infinitely many primes.

Applications

Primitive roots underlie the Diffie–Hellman key exchange and the ElGamal cryptosystem, whose security rests on the difficulty of the discrete logarithm problem — recovering the exponent k from gk mod p.
