Euclid's Elements

Euclid's Elements (Ancient Greek: Stoikheîa) is a mathematical treatise consisting of 13 books attributed to the ancient Greek mathematician Euclid, written in Alexandria around 300 BC. It is a collection of definitions, postulates, propositions (theorems and constructions), and mathematical proofs of the propositions.

AuthorEuclid
LanguageAncient Greek
SubjectGeometry, number theory
Publishedc. 300 BC

Contents

The books cover plane and solid Euclidean geometry, elementary number theory, and incommensurable lines. Book I begins with 23 definitions, five postulates, and five common notions, and builds up the foundations of geometry, culminating in proofs of the Pythagorean theorem. Books VII through IX treat number theory, including the Euclidean algorithm and the infinitude of prime numbers. Book X classifies incommensurable magnitudes, and Books XI through XIII treat solid geometry, ending with the construction of the five Platonic solids.

Influence

The Elements is the most successful and influential textbook ever written, having been printed in more editions than any book except the Bible. Its logical, axiomatic structure served as the standard model for mathematical proof for over two thousand years, and its content remained a core part of mathematics education into the modern era.

Editions and transmission

The text survived through Greek manuscripts and Arabic translations, and was first printed in Latin in Venice in 1482. Modern scholarship distinguishes the Theonine recension, based on the edition prepared by Theon of Alexandria in the 4th century AD.
