The Moluccas (Indonesian: Kepulauan Maluku) are an archipelago in eastern Indonesia, lying between Sulawesi and New Guinea. Historically known as the Spice Islands, they were the original — and for a time the only — source of nutmeg, mace, and cloves, commodities that drew Portuguese, Spanish, Dutch, and English traders and made the islands a focus of early European colonial expansion in Asia.

Geography

The archipelago comprises over a thousand islands, the largest being Halmahera, Seram, and Buru. It is divided administratively between the provinces of Maluku and North Maluku, with capitals at Ambon and Sofifi (Ternate) respectively. The region is volcanically active and lies near the junction of several tectonic plates.

History

The Portuguese reached the islands in the early 16th century, followed by the Spanish and then the Dutch East India Company, which imposed a brutal monopoly over the spice trade, including the near-extermination of the Bandanese in 1621. The islands became part of independent Indonesia in 1949–50. Between 1999 and 2002 the region experienced severe communal violence between Muslim and Christian communities, ended by the Malino II Accord.
