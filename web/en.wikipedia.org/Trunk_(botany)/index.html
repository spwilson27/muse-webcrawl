Trunk (botany)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In botany, the trunk (or bole) is the stem and main wooden axis of a tree, which is a crucial part of the tree's structure that supports the branches and connects the roots to the leaves. The trunk is covered by bark, which protects the tree from injury, parasites, and weather.

Structure

A cross-section of a trunk reveals concentric zones: the outer bark, the phloem that transports sugars, the vascular cambium that produces new wood and bark, the sapwood (xylem) that conducts water upward, the older heartwood that gives structural strength, and the central pith. Annual growth rings in the xylem of temperate trees record each year's growth and can be counted to determine the tree's age — the science of dendrochronology.

Function and form

The trunk elevates the crown into the light, stores water and carbohydrates, and mechanically withstands wind loads; it tapers from the buttressed base to the crown and may branch at various heights depending on species and competition. When a trunk is felled, the portion left in the ground is the stump. Trunk wood — timber — is humanity's principal structural and fuel material, and abnormal growths such as burls are prized for figured veneer.
