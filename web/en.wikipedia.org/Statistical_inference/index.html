Statistical inference

Statistical inference is the process of using data analysis to infer properties of an underlying distribution of probability. Inferential statistical analysis infers properties of a population from a sample — for example, by testing hypotheses and deriving estimates. It is assumed that the observed data set is sampled from a larger population. Statistical inference contrasts with descriptive statistics, which only summarizes the data at hand.

Approaches

The two principal schools of statistical inference are frequentist inference and Bayesian inference. Frequentist methods treat the parameter as fixed and quantify uncertainty through the long-run behaviour of procedures: confidence intervals, hypothesis tests, and p-values. Bayesian methods treat the parameter as a random variable, combining a prior distribution with the likelihood of the observed data to obtain a posterior distribution, summarized by credible intervals and Bayes factors.

Estimation and hypothesis testing

Point estimation seeks a single best guess of a population parameter (such as the mean), while interval estimation provides a range of plausible values. Statistical hypothesis testing evaluates whether observed data are consistent with a null hypothesis, using a test statistic and its sampling distribution. Key concepts include statistical significance, statistical power, Type I and Type II errors, and the sampling distribution of an estimator, governed in large samples by the central limit theorem.

Assumptions and models

Inference is usually based on a statistical model of the data-generating process. Important assumptions include the random sampling of units, independence of observations, and — for many parametric methods — particular distributional forms, such as normality. Randomization in experiments, pioneered by R. A. Fisher, provides a design-based justification for inference about causal effects.
