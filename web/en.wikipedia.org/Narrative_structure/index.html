Narrative structure
FieldNarratology, literary theory
Key modelsThree-act structure, Freytag's pyramid, hero's journey
Key figuresAristotle, Gustav Freytag, Vladimir Propp, Joseph Campbell

Narrative structure is the structural framework that underlies the order and manner in which a narrative is presented to a reader, listener, or viewer. The study of such structures, narratology, examines how stories organise events into plots, how acts and scenes are arranged, and how devices such as flashbacks, subplots, and framing narratives shape meaning and suspense.

Classical models

Aristotle's Poetics held that a plot should be a unified whole with a beginning, middle, and end. In 1863 the German novelist Gustav Freytag modelled drama as a pyramid: exposition, rising action, climax, falling action, and denouement. The three-act structure popularised in screenwriting divides stories into setup, confrontation, and resolution, typically marked by plot points at the act breaks.

Twentieth-century theories

Vladimir Propp's Morphology of the Folktale (1928) analysed Russian fairy tales into 31 narrative functions performed by a small set of character roles. Joseph Campbell's The Hero with a Thousand Faces (1949) proposed the monomyth or hero's journey — departure, initiation, and return — which strongly influenced Hollywood storytelling through Christopher Vogler's adaptation. Other frameworks include Tzvetan Todorov's equilibrium model and the Japanese four-part kishōtenketsu structure, which can proceed without conflict.

Nonlinear and modern forms

Many works depart from linear structure: in medias res openings, frame stories, parallel timelines, unreliable narrators, and reverse chronology. Digital media have added branching and interactive structures in video games and hypertext fiction, where the audience helps determine the sequence of events.
