Bell's theorem

Bell's theorem is a term encompassing a number of closely related results in physics, all of which determine that quantum mechanics is incompatible with local hidden-variable theories given some basic assumptions about the nature of measurement. "Local" here refers to the principle of locality, the idea that a particle can only be influenced by its immediate surroundings, and that interactions mediated by physical fields cannot propagate faster than the speed of light. "Hidden variables" are hypothetical properties not described by quantum theory but that would determine measurement outcomes.

Background

The theorem grew out of the 1935 Einstein–Podolsky–Rosen paradox, which argued that quantum mechanics must be incomplete because entangled particles appear to affect one another instantaneously. In 1964, John Stewart Bell showed that any theory in which physical systems have definite properties independent of measurement, and in which no influence travels faster than light, must satisfy certain statistical bounds — Bell inequalities — that quantum mechanics violates.

The CHSH inequality

The most widely tested form is the Clauser–Horne–Shimony–Holt (CHSH) inequality (1969), which bounds a correlation measure S by |S| ≤ 2 for local hidden-variable theories, while quantum mechanics allows values up to 2√2.

Experiments

Experiments from the 1970s onward, notably those of Alain Aspect in 1982, consistently violated Bell inequalities in agreement with quantum mechanics. In 2015, three groups performed "loophole-free" Bell tests, closing the major detection and locality loopholes simultaneously. Aspect shared the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics for this work. The residual possibilities for hidden variables are non-local theories (such as Bohmian mechanics), superdeterminism, or retrocausal models.

Significance

Bell's theorem is often described as one of the most profound results in the foundations of physics, and its experimental violations underpin quantum information technologies such as device-independent quantum cryptography and certified randomness generation.
