Sargassum

KingdomChromista
ClassPhaeophyceae (brown algae)
OrderFucales
GenusSargassum C. Agardh

Sargassum is a genus of large brown seaweeds (in the class Phaeophyceae) found in shallow marine waters worldwide, most famously as free-floating mats in the open ocean. The genus contains more than 300 species. Many species have leafy appendages and small spherical air bladders (pneumatocysts) that keep them buoyant. The name comes from the Portuguese word sargaço for the seaweed that Portuguese sailors encountered in the North Atlantic, which reminded them of grapes.

Floating species and the Sargasso Sea

Two species, Sargassum natans and S. fluitans, are holopelagic — they spend their entire life cycle afloat, reproducing by fragmentation. They accumulate in the gyre of the North Atlantic, giving the Sargasso Sea its name. The floating rafts form a unique ecosystem sheltering crabs, shrimp, sea turtles and juvenile fish, including species found nowhere else such as the sargassum fish.

The Great Atlantic Sargassum Belt

Since 2011, unprecedented blooms of pelagic Sargassum have formed a recurrent "Great Atlantic Sargassum Belt" stretching from West Africa to the Gulf of Mexico, in some years exceeding 20 million tonnes. Enormous quantities wash ashore in the Caribbean, Mexico and Florida, where the rotting weed releases hydrogen sulphide, harms tourism and smothers turtle nesting beaches. The blooms have been linked to nutrient inputs from the Amazon and Congo rivers and to changing ocean circulation.

Uses and other species

Attached Sargassum species form underwater forests in temperate and tropical seas. The genus is eaten in several Asian cuisines — in Japan S. fusiforme is the seaweed hijiki — and species are used as fertiliser, animal feed and sources of alginates, though some accumulate arsenic and must be consumed with caution.
