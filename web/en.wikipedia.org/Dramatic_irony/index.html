Dramatic irony

Dramatic irony: the audience knows more than the character

Dramatic irony is a form of irony in which the audience or reader knows more about a situation, the causes of conflict, or a character's true nature than the characters in the work do. This discrepancy between the character's limited understanding and the audience's fuller knowledge creates tension, suspense, humour or pathos. It is a staple of tragedy, melodrama, horror and comedy alike.

Classical examples

The classic case is Sophocles' Oedipus Rex: the audience knows that Oedipus himself is the murderer he seeks and that Jocasta is both his wife and mother, so his every vow to punish the killer acquires a terrible second meaning. In Shakespeare's Romeo and Juliet, the audience knows Juliet is only sleeping when Romeo, believing her dead, kills himself. In Macbeth, Duncan praises the security of Macbeth's castle even as Macbeth plots his murder.

Other uses

Dramatic irony generates suspense in thrillers — Alfred Hitchcock explained it with his "bomb under the table" example: if the audience sees a bomb placed under a table, an innocent conversation becomes gripping. In horror films the audience may know the killer lurks nearby while characters do not. In comedy, mistaken-identity plots in Shakespeare, sitcom misunderstandings, and characters speaking double meanings all rely on the audience knowing more than the people on stage.

Related concepts

Dramatic irony contrasts with verbal irony (saying the opposite of what is meant) and situational irony (an outcome contrary to expectation). In historical irony, readers know the future that historical figures could not foresee. Socratic irony — feigning ignorance to expose an opponent's error — is a related rhetorical mode.
