Megafauna

Megafauna are large animals of any particular region, habitat or geological period. The most common threshold used is a body mass of 46 kilograms (100 lb) or more, though some definitions restrict the term to animals over one tonne. Extant examples include elephants, giraffes, hippopotamuses, rhinoceroses, whales and large bovines. The term is especially associated with the giant animals of the Pleistocene epoch, such as woolly mammoths, giant ground sloths and saber-toothed cats.

Pleistocene megafauna and extinctions

During the late Pleistocene and early Holocene, most large terrestrial animals outside Africa went extinct in the Quaternary extinction event, including mammoths and mastodons in the Americas and Eurasia, giant kangaroos and diprotodons in Australia, and giant birds in New Zealand and Madagascar. The causes remain debated: proposed drivers include rapid climate change at the end of the last Ice Age, hunting by newly arrived humans (the "overkill hypothesis"), disease, and combinations of these factors. Africa and southern Asia retained more megafauna, possibly because their animals co-evolved with humans over a longer period.

Ecological role

Megafauna shape ecosystems by dispersing seeds, trampling vegetation, cycling nutrients and creating habitat for other species. Their loss can trigger trophic cascades, leading to proposals for "rewilding" and even Pleistocene rewilding using surviving relatives as proxies for extinct species.
