Claude Lévi-Strauss

Claude Lévi-Strauss (28 November 1908 – 30 October 2009) was a French anthropologist and ethnologist whose work was key in the development of the theories of structuralism and structural anthropology. He held the chair of Social Anthropology at the Collège de France between 1959 and 1982, was elected to the Académie française in 1973, and was a member of the French Academy's seat 29. He has been called, along with James George Frazer and Franz Boas, one of the three fathers of modern anthropology.

Life

Born in Brussels to French Jewish parents and raised in Paris, Lévi-Strauss studied philosophy at the Sorbonne. In 1935 he took a post at the University of São Paulo and conducted fieldwork among the Caduveo, Bororo, Nambikwara, and Tupi-Kawahib peoples of the Brazilian interior — experiences later recounted in his celebrated memoir-cum-ethnography Tristes Tropiques (1955). Fleeing Vichy France during the Second World War, he worked in New York at the New School for Social Research, where his friendship with linguist Roman Jakobson decisively shaped his structural method; he also served as French cultural attaché. He returned to France in 1948.

Work

In The Elementary Structures of Kinship (1949) he analysed kinship systems as systems of exchange structured by the incest taboo. His structural anthropology, set out in Structural Anthropology (1958) and the four volumes of Mythologiques (1964–1971, beginning with The Raw and the Cooked), treated myths as transformations of underlying structures of binary oppositions in the human mind. The Savage Mind (1962) argued that "primitive" thought is a rigorous intellectual system — a bricolage — rather than a deficient stage of reason.

Legacy

Lévi-Strauss's structuralism profoundly influenced philosophy, literary theory, and the social sciences, shaping figures from Roland Barthes to Jacques Lacan, even as post-structuralists later criticised its universalism. He died in Paris aged 100, a few weeks short of his 101st birthday.
