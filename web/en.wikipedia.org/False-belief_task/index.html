False-belief task

The false-belief task is a widely used test in developmental psychology for assessing a child's theory of mind — the understanding that other people can hold beliefs about the world that are false. Passing the task is generally taken as evidence that the child appreciates that others' behaviour is guided by their beliefs, even when those beliefs differ from reality and from the child's own knowledge.

The Sally-Anne test

The best-known version, introduced by Simon Baron-Cohen, Alan Leslie and Uta Frith in 1985, is the Sally-Anne test. A child is shown two dolls: Sally places a marble in her basket and leaves; Anne moves the marble to a box. The child is then asked where Sally will look for her marble. A child who answers "in the basket" — where Sally falsely believes it to be — passes; younger children typically answer "in the box", where the marble really is. Most typically developing children pass by about age four or five, while the original study found that most children with autism failed, a result that helped launch the "mindblindness" hypothesis of autism.

Development and debate

Earlier versions of the task were devised by Heinz Wimmer and Josef Perner (1983). Later research using non-verbal measures, such as anticipatory looking, suggested that infants in their second year may show sensitivity to others' false beliefs, sparking debate about whether the classic verbal task underestimates young children's competence and whether "implicit" and "explicit" theory of mind are distinct systems. Performance also varies with language ability, executive function and culture.
