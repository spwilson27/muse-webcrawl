Philae (Greek: Φιλαί; Egyptian: Pilak) was an island in the Nile near Aswan, Egypt, famous as the site of a temple complex dedicated to the goddess Isis. One of the last strongholds of ancient Egyptian religion, Philae's temples were dismantled and moved to nearby Agilkia Island during the UNESCO Nubian rescue campaign of the 1970s to save them from flooding caused by the Aswan High Dam.

The temple complex

The principal monument is the Temple of Isis, begun under Ptolemy II in the 3rd century BC and enlarged by later Ptolemies and Roman emperors. It is approached through a colonnaded court between two great pylons; inside are the birth house (mammisi), chapels of Osiris, and reliefs depicting the myth of Isis and Osiris. Nearby stand the elegant Kiosk of Trajan, a small Temple of Hathor, and a Temple of Augustus.

Last outpost of the old religion

Philae was a major pilgrimage centre for Egyptians and Nubians alike. It preserves the last known hieroglyphic inscription, carved in AD 394, and the last demotic graffito, from AD 452. The temple was finally closed under Justinian around AD 535–537 — centuries after the empire had become Christian — and converted into a church. A treaty-protected cult of Isis there served the neighbouring Blemmyes and Nobades into the 5th century.

Relocation

The building of the Aswan Low Dam (1902, heightened later) left the temples submerged for much of each year. After the decision to build the Aswan High Dam, UNESCO's International Campaign to Save the Monuments of Nubia cut the complex into some 40,000 blocks between 1972 and 1980 and reassembled it on Agilkia Island, reshaped to resemble the original Philae. The monuments were inscribed on the World Heritage List in 1979 as part of the Nubian Monuments from Abu Simbel to Philae.
