Sarcopenia

Sarcopenia is a type of muscle loss (muscle atrophy) that occurs with ageing and physical inactivity. It is characterised by the degenerative loss of skeletal muscle mass, quality, and strength, and is associated with an increased risk of falls, fractures, physical disability, and mortality in older people. The term derives from the Greek sarx ("flesh") and penia ("poverty"). Sarcopenia received its own ICD-10 code (M62.84) in 2016, formally recognising it as a disease.

Age-related decline in muscle mass and quality is the hallmark of sarcopenia.

Signs, causes, and diagnosis

Muscle mass declines by roughly 3–8 percent per decade after the age of 30, and the rate accelerates after 60. Contributing factors include hormonal changes, chronic inflammation, reduced protein intake and synthesis, loss of motor neurons, and sedentary lifestyle. Diagnostic frameworks such as that of the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People (EWGSOP2) define probable sarcopenia by low muscle strength, typically measured by grip strength or a chair stand test; confirmation requires demonstration of low muscle quantity or quality, usually by DXA or bioelectrical impedance, and poor physical performance such as slow gait speed indicates severe sarcopenia.

Treatment and prevention

No drug is specifically approved for sarcopenia. The most effective intervention is progressive resistance exercise training, which can increase strength and function even in very old adults. Adequate dietary protein — often recommended at 1.0–1.2 g per kilogram of body weight per day for older adults — and correction of vitamin D deficiency are also recommended. Pharmacological candidates under investigation include selective androgen receptor modulators and myostatin inhibitors.
