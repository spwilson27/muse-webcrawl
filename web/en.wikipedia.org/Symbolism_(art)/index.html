Symbolism (art)

Symbolism was a late 19th-century art movement of French, Russian, and Belgian origin in poetry and the other arts, seeking to express the individual emotional experience rather than to describe the external world. It arose as a reaction against naturalism and realism, favouring spirituality, imagination, mythology, and dreams.

Origins

The movement took its name from Jean Moréas's “Symbolist Manifesto”, published in Le Figaro in 1886. Its literary roots lay in the poetry of Charles Baudelaire, especially Les Fleurs du mal (1857), and it was carried forward by poets such as Paul Verlaine and Stéphane Mallarmé. In painting, its forerunners included the visionary art of William Blake and the Pre-Raphaelites.

Artists and themes

Leading Symbolist painters included Gustave Moreau, Odilon Redon, Pierre Puvis de Chavannes, Arnold Böcklin, Ferdinand Hodler, Gustav Klimt, and the early Edvard Munch. Recurring subjects were femmes fatales, biblical and mythological figures such as Salome and Orpheus, death, and the borderland between dream and waking. Symbolists prized suggestion over statement: as Mallarmé put it, to name an object is to suppress three-quarters of the enjoyment of a poem.

Legacy

Symbolism fed directly into Art Nouveau, the Decadent movement, and early modernism, and its emphasis on inner states paved the way for expressionism and surrealism.
