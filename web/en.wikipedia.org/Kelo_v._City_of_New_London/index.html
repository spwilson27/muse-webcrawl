Kelo v. City of New London

Kelo v. City of New London, 545 U.S. 469 (2005), was a case decided by the Supreme Court of the United States involving the use of eminent domain to transfer land from one private owner to another private owner to further economic development. In a 5–4 decision, the Court held that the general benefits a community enjoyed from economic growth qualified private redevelopment plans as a permissible "public use" under the Takings Clause of the Fifth Amendment.

Background

In 2000, the city of New London, Connecticut, approved a development plan intended to create jobs, increase tax revenue, and revitalize the economically distressed Fort Trumbull neighbourhood, in connection with a nearby Pfizer research facility. Susette Kelo and other homeowners in the area refused to sell their properties, and the city initiated condemnation proceedings. The plaintiffs, represented by the Institute for Justice, argued that economic development was not a "public use".

Decision

Justice John Paul Stevens wrote for the majority, which relied on precedents such as Berman v. Parker (1954) and Hawaii Housing Authority v. Midkiff (1984) and deferred to the legislature's judgment of public purpose. Justice Sandra Day O'Connor's dissent warned that the decision effectively eliminated any distinction between private and public use, while Justice Clarence Thomas's dissent argued that the burden of such takings falls disproportionately on the poor and politically powerless.

Reaction and aftermath

The decision provoked an intense national backlash; more than 40 states eventually reformed their eminent domain laws to restrict takings for private development. Ironically, the New London project itself collapsed: Pfizer closed its facility in 2009, and the development site stood vacant for years.
