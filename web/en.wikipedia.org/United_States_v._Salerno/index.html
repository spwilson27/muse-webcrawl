United States v. Salerno

The Supreme Court upheld the Bail Reform Act of 1984 in this case.

United States v. Salerno, 481 U.S. 739 (1987), was a United States Supreme Court case that upheld the constitutionality of the Bail Reform Act of 1984, which permitted the federal courts to detain an arrestee pending trial if the government could prove the individual posed a danger to the community. The case is a leading precedent on pretrial detention and the limits of the Eighth Amendment's Excessive Bail Clause.

Background

Anthony "Fat Tony" Salerno, an alleged leader of the Genovese crime family, and Vincent Cafaro were indicted on racketeering charges under RICO. The government moved for pretrial detention under the Bail Reform Act, arguing that no conditions of release would reasonably assure the safety of the community. The district court ordered detention, and the Second Circuit reversed, holding that the Act's detention provisions violated substantive due process.

Decision

Chief Justice William Rehnquist, writing for a 6–3 majority, held that the Bail Reform Act did not violate the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment or the Excessive Bail Clause of the Eighth Amendment. The Court reasoned that pretrial detention under the Act was a regulatory measure, not punishment, and that the government's interest in preventing crime by arrestees was a legitimate and compelling objective. The Court also rejected the facial challenge, emphasizing the Act's procedural safeguards, including a prompt detention hearing, counsel, and findings by clear and convincing evidence.

Dissent

Justice Thurgood Marshall, joined by Justice Brennan, dissented, arguing that the decision permitted the imprisonment of persons accused—but not convicted—of crimes, inconsistent with the presumption of innocence. Justice Stevens filed a separate dissent.

Significance

Salerno remains the controlling authority on the constitutionality of preventive detention statutes and is frequently cited in debates over bail reform and the scope of substantive due process.
