Tangihanga

Tangihanga, or tangi, is the traditional Māori funeral rite practised by the indigenous people of New Zealand. Held on a marae, a tangihanga typically lasts for at least three days, during which the body of the deceased (tūpāpaku) lies in state and mourners travel from near and far to pay their respects.

Customs

The body is prepared and brought onto the marae, where it lies in an open coffin (or, traditionally, in a carved waka or wrapped in flax mats) in the meeting house or a sheltered area. Family (whānau pani, the bereaved) remain with the body throughout and traditionally do not leave it unattended. Visitors arrive in groups (manuhiri), are welcomed with karanga (calls) and pōwhiri, deliver speeches (whaikōrero) and songs (waiata tangi) addressing both the dead and the living, and offer koha (gifts).

Meaning

Tangihanga express core Māori values: the continuing presence of ancestors, the importance of collective mourning, and the restoration of balance (utu and ea) between the community of the living and the dead. Speeches explicitly address the deceased, and grief is expressed openly and communally — the word tangi itself means to weep or mourn.

Modern practice

Although New Zealand law generally requires burial or cremation within a set period and many Māori now live in cities, tangihanga remain a central institution of Māori life and have influenced wider New Zealand funeral customs, including the practice of bringing the deceased home before a funeral service. Burial usually takes place in a tribal cemetery (urupā).
