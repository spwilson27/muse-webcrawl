Sieve theory

Sieve theory is a set of general techniques in number theory, designed to count, or more realistically to estimate the size of, sifted sets of integers. The prototypical example of a sifted set is the set of prime numbers up to some prescribed limit N, obtained by deleting from the integers all those divisible by the primes below N1/2 — the sieve of Eratosthenes. Modern sieve theory generalises this idea to extract numbers satisfying arithmetic conditions that cannot be handled by simple inclusion–exclusion.

History and methods

The modern theory began with Viggo Brun's work around 1919, in which he proved that the sum of the reciprocals of the twin primes converges (Brun's theorem) and that every sufficiently large even number is the sum of two numbers each having at most nine prime factors. Later milestones include the Selberg sieve, the large sieve of Linnik and Rényi, and the Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem, which supplies the level-of-distribution estimates that feed many applications.

Applications and limitations

Sieve methods produced Chen's theorem — that every sufficiently large even number is the sum of a prime and a number with at most two prime factors — the closest result to the Goldbach conjecture of its era, and underlie modern bounded-gap results on primes. A fundamental obstacle is the parity problem: classical sieves cannot distinguish numbers with an even number of prime factors from those with an odd number, which blocks direct attacks on problems such as the twin prime conjecture.
