Fermentation

Fermentation
TypeAnaerobic metabolic process

Fermentation is a metabolic process in which an organism converts a carbohydrate, such as sugar or starch, into an alcohol or an acid, without using oxygen. In biochemistry it is defined more strictly as the extraction of energy from carbohydrates in the absence of oxygen: glycolysis produces pyruvate and a net gain of two ATP molecules, and fermentation regenerates the NAD+ needed to keep glycolysis running by reducing pyruvate to products such as ethanol or lactate.

Types

In alcoholic fermentation, yeasts such as Saccharomyces cerevisiae convert glucose to ethanol and carbon dioxide — the basis of beer, wine and bread-making. In lactic acid fermentation, bacteria (and animal muscle cells under oxygen debt) reduce pyruvate to lactate; this sours milk into yoghurt and cheese and preserves vegetables as sauerkraut and kimchi. Acetic acid bacteria oxidise ethanol to vinegar; industrial fermentations also produce acetone, butanol, citric acid, antibiotics and biofuels.

History

Fermented foods and drinks are at least as old as agriculture. Scientifically, fermentation was long a puzzle: Louis Pasteur showed in the 1850s–60s that it is caused by living microorganisms ("life without air"), while Eduard Buchner's 1897 demonstration of cell-free fermentation by yeast extracts founded modern enzymology. The word derives from the Latin fervere, "to boil", describing the frothing of fermenting wine.

Uses

Beyond food and drink — bread, cheese, yoghurt, soy sauce, pickles, chocolate and coffee processing — fermentation is central to biotechnology, producing insulin and other pharmaceuticals, enzymes, bioethanol, and cultured ("precision fermentation") proteins. In the gut, microbial fermentation of fibre yields short-chain fatty acids important to health.
