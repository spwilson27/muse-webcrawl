Acanthus (ornament)

Acanthus is one of the most common plant forms used in architectural ornament and decoration. The stylised leaves of the Mediterranean Acanthus plant — especially Acanthus spinosus and Acanthus mollis — appear in carved capitals, friezes, borders and scrollwork from ancient Greek architecture through to the present day.

Origins

The acanthus motif appeared in Greek architecture in the 5th century BC. It is the defining feature of the Corinthian order, whose capital is carved with two rows of acanthus leaves beneath scroll volutes. According to Vitruvius, the sculptor Callimachus invented the Corinthian capital after seeing acanthus leaves growing through a woven basket placed on a young girl's grave, its tiles pressing the leaves into curls.

Development

Roman architecture adopted the acanthus lavishly, combining it with scrolling vines in the Composite order and in relief panels such as the Ara Pacis. Byzantine, Romanesque and Gothic craftsmen continued the motif in capitals and manuscript illumination; Renaissance architects revived the classical Corinthian form directly. The motif also passed into Islamic art as the arabesque, and into furniture, metalwork and typography — the fleuron and many printer's ornaments derive from acanthus scrolls.

Symbolism

In classical symbolism the acanthus, a thorny and enduring plant, could represent immortality, rebirth and the overcoming of trial. In Victorian floriography it came to symbolise art and artifice.
