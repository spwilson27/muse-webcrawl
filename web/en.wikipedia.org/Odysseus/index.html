Odysseus

Odysseus (Greek: Ὀδυσσεύς; Latin: Ulixes, hence English "Ulysses") is a legendary Greek king of Ithaca and the hero of Homer's epic poem the Odyssey. He also plays a key role in the Iliad and other works of the Epic Cycle. Son of Laertes and Anticlea, husband of Penelope, and father of Telemachus, Odysseus is renowned for his intellectual brilliance, guile, and versatility—his characteristic quality is mētis, cunning intelligence—and he is famed for the ten years it took him to return home after the Trojan War.

In the Iliad and the Trojan War

In the Iliad, Odysseus is one of the foremost Achaean leaders, a persuasive speaker and wily diplomat who undertakes the night raid with Diomedes in Book 10. Later tradition credited him with devising the Trojan Horse that ended the war, and with episodes such as feigning madness to avoid the expedition and unmasking the young Achilles on Skyros.

The Odyssey

The Odyssey recounts his ten-year voyage home: the raid on the Cicones; the Lotus-Eaters; the blinding of the Cyclops Polyphemus, which brings down the wrath of Poseidon; Circe, who turns his men into swine; the descent to the underworld; the Sirens; Scylla and Charybdis; the cattle of the Sun; seven years detained by Calypso; and shipwreck among the Phaeacians, who convey him home. In Ithaca he finds his house beset by suitors courting Penelope; disguised as a beggar, he wins the contest of the bow and, with Telemachus, kills the suitors.

Later tradition

Odysseus remained a compelling and ambiguous figure in later literature: Dante places Ulysses in the eighth circle of Hell for fraudulent counsel and tells of a final fatal voyage beyond the Pillars of Hercules; Tennyson's "Ulysses" and James Joyce's Ulysses (1922), which reworks the epic in a single Dublin day, are landmarks of his afterlife, as is Nikos Kazantzakis's sequel The Odyssey: A Modern Sequel.
