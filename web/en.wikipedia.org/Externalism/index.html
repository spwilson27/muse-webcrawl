Externalism

Externalism is a family of positions in philosophy according to which mental content, meaning, or justification is partly determined by factors outside the individual mind or body. It is opposed to internalism, which holds that everything that determines a subject's mental states is internal to the subject.

Semantic externalism

Hilary Putnam's Twin Earth thought experiment (1975) argued that "meanings just ain't in the head": two molecule-for-molecule identical speakers, one on Earth where water is H2O and one on Twin Earth where the watery liquid is a different compound XYZ, mean different things by "water", so meaning depends on the environment. Tyler Burge extended the argument to the social environment, showing how a speaker's beliefs can depend on the usage of experts in their linguistic community.

Content and epistemic externalism

In the philosophy of mind, content externalism holds that some mental states are individuated by relations to the external world, challenging the idea that thinking is purely an inner process. Vehicle externalism, associated with Andy Clark and David Chalmers's "extended mind" thesis, goes further, arguing that external artefacts such as notebooks can literally form part of cognitive processes. In epistemology, reliabilism is an externalist theory of justification: a belief is justified if produced by a reliable process, whether or not the subject can tell that it is reliable.

Criticisms

Internalists object that externalism makes self-knowledge of one's own thoughts mysterious and severs the connection between content and the subject's point of view. The debate shapes contemporary work on mental content, consciousness and epistemic warrant.
