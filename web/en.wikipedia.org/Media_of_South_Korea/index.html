The media of South Korea comprises a highly developed and competitive landscape of newspapers, terrestrial and cable television, radio and online media. South Korea is consistently ranked among the most wired societies in the world, and most news consumption now occurs through internet portals and smartphones rather than print.

Newspapers

The three conservative broadsheets — The Chosun Ilbo (founded 1920), JoongAng Ilbo and The Dong-A Ilbo — long dominated circulation and political influence, and are often grouped by critics as the Chojoongdong. Progressive papers such as the Hankyoreh (founded by dismissed journalists in 1988) and the Kyunghyang Shinmun provide contrasting editorial lines. English-language dailies include The Korea Herald and The Korea Times.

Broadcasting

Public broadcasters KBS and MBC and private network SBS anchor terrestrial television, joined by educational broadcaster EBS and, after a controversial 2011 licensing round, general-programming cable channels JTBC, TV Chosun, Channel A and MBN. Korean television drama and variety — the core of the Hallyu (Korean Wave) — are exported across Asia and beyond. YTN and Yonhap News TV provide rolling news.

Press freedom and history

Under the authoritarian governments of the 1960s–80s, media were censored, consolidated and intimidated, most severely after the 1980 media merger under Chun Doo-hwan. Democratization from 1987 brought liberalization and a proliferation of outlets, though press-freedom assessments continue to note concentrated ownership, political pressure and defamation suits. South Korea typically ranks in the upper-middle range of world press-freedom indexes.

Internet media

Portals such as Naver and Daum (Kakao) aggregate and rank news, making their editorial algorithms politically contentious. Online-native outlets, citizen journalism (OhmyNews, founded 2000), webtoons and streaming platforms further fragment the audience, alongside the global rise of K-pop and Korean film and drama through services such as Netflix.
