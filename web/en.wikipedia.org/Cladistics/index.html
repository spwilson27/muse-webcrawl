Cladistics is an approach to biological classification in which organisms are categorized into groups called clades based on hypotheses of their most recent common ancestor. It is a method of classifying organisms according to the branching patterns of their evolutionary history, so that a valid group must include an ancestor and all of its descendants — a property called monophyly.

History

The method was founded by the German entomologist Willi Hennig, who published his ideas in German in 1950 in Grundzüge einer Theorie der phylogenetischen Systematik, and in revised English form in 1966 as Phylogenetic Systematics. Hennig argued that classification should reflect genealogy alone, and that shared derived characters, or synapomorphies, are the evidence for grouping organisms into clades.

Principles

Cladistic analysis distinguishes three kinds of character similarity. A synapomorphy is a derived trait shared by two or more taxa and inherited from their common ancestor; synapomorphies define clades. A symplesiomorphy is an ancestral trait shared by taxa but inherited from a more remote ancestor, and does not indicate close relationship. A homoplasy is a trait that evolved independently, by convergence or reversal, and can mislead analysis if mistaken for a synapomorphy.

Cladograms

The results of a cladistic analysis are commonly presented as a branching diagram called a cladogram. The branching points, or nodes, represent hypothetical common ancestors, and the terminal branches represent the taxa being compared. The principle of parsimony is often used to choose among competing cladograms, preferring the tree that requires the fewest evolutionary changes.

Impact

Cladistics transformed systematics in the second half of the twentieth century and became the dominant framework for classification in biology. It displaced older approaches such as phenetics, which grouped organisms by overall similarity, and evolutionary taxonomy, which allowed paraphyletic groups. Modern molecular phylogenetics extends cladistic reasoning to DNA and protein sequence data.
