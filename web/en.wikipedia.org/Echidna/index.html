Echidnas (/ɪ/), sometimes known as spiny anteaters, are quill-covered monotremes (egg-laying mammals) belonging to the family Tachyglossidae in the order Monotremata. The four extant species of echidnas and the platypus are the only living mammals that lay eggs and the only surviving members of the order Monotremata. The diet of some species consists of ants and termites, but they are not closely related to the true anteaters of the Americas, which (along with sloths and armadillos) are part of the order Pilosa. Echidnas live in Australia and New Guinea.

Short-beaked echidna

Echidnas have evolved between 20 and 50 million years ago, descending from a platypus-like monotreme. This ancestor was aquatic, but echidnas adapted to life on land.

Description

Echidnas are medium-sized, solitary mammals covered with coarse hair and spines. Superficially they resemble the anteaters of South America and other spiny mammals such as hedgehogs and porcupines. They usually weigh between 2 and 7 kilograms (4.4 and 15.4 lb) and measure 30 to 45 cm (12 to 18 in) in length. The spines are modified hairs made of keratin and can reach 50 millimetres (2 in) in length. Echidnas have short, strong limbs with large claws, and are powerful diggers. Their claws on their hind limbs are elongated and curved backwards to aid grooming and digging.

Echidnas have a small brain relative to body mass. They have electrosensory receptors in their snouts that help them detect prey underground. They are also among a small group of mammals that can enter torpor and hibernation.

Diet

The short-beaked echidna's diet consists largely of ants and termites, while the long-beaked Zaglossus species typically eat earthworms and insect larvae. The tongues of long-beaked echidnas have sharp, tiny spines that help them catch their prey. They have no teeth, and break up their food by grinding it between the bottom of their mouth and their tongue.

Reproduction

The female lays a single leathery egg in a burrow or into a pouch-like fold of skin about 22 days after mating. The egg hatches after about 10 days of gestation; the young echidna, called a puggle, then suckles milk from patches on the mother's skin. Echidnas are notable among mammals for their low body temperature and slow rate of reproduction.

Species

There are four extant species of echidna: the short-beaked echidna (Tachyglossus aculeatus) and three long-beaked echidnas of the genus Zaglossus: the western long-beaked echidna (Z. bruijnii), Sir David's long-beaked echidna (Z. attenboroughi), and the eastern long-beaked echidna (Z. bartoni). The three Zaglossus species are all threatened, two of them critically endangered.
