Rosemary Nelson

Rosemary Nelson (1958–1999)
Born:September 4, 1958, Lurgan, County Armagh
Died:March 15, 1999 (aged 40), Lurgan
Occupation:Solicitor, human rights lawyer

Rosemary Nelson (née Magee; September 4, 1958 – March 15, 1999) was a Northern Irish solicitor and human rights lawyer from Lurgan, County Armagh. She was assassinated by loyalist paramilitaries in 1999, in a car bomb attack that gave rise to lasting allegations of collusion by members of the security forces.

Career

Nelson trained as a solicitor and built a practice in Lurgan, taking on high-profile and controversial cases in the deeply divided atmosphere of 1990s Northern Ireland. She represented nationalist residents' groups during the Drumcree parade disputes at Portadown, the family of Robert Hamill—a Catholic man beaten to death by a loyalist mob in Portadown in 1997 while police were present—and other clients alleging misconduct by the Royal Ulster Constabulary (RUC).

She reported receiving death threats and intimidation, including threats relayed through clients who said they came from RUC officers. Her concerns were raised internationally; in 1998 she testified before the United States Congress about threats against defence lawyers in Northern Ireland, and the UN Special Rapporteur on the independence of judges and lawyers cited her case.

Assassination

On March 15, 1999, a booby-trap bomb placed under her car exploded as she drove from her home in Lurgan, severely injuring her; she died hours later in hospital. The Red Hand Defenders, a cover name used by loyalist paramilitaries, claimed responsibility. Nelson was 40 years old and the mother of three children.

Inquiry

Suspicions that state agencies had advance knowledge of threats to Nelson led to a long-running investigation and a public inquiry chaired by Sir Michael Morland, which reported in 2011. The inquiry found no evidence that state agencies had directly facilitated or caused her death, but concluded that the RUC had failed to take reasonable steps to protect her, that police officers had publicly abused and assaulted her, and that these failures and the prevailing climate of hostility could not be disentangled from the risk she faced. No one has been convicted of her murder.

Legacy

Nelson's killing, coming a year after the Good Friday Agreement, underscored the vulnerability of lawyers in conflict situations and prompted continued scrutiny of collusion allegations in Northern Ireland. A human rights award for women human rights defenders, the Rosemary Nelson Prize, was established in her memory.
