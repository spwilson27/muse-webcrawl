Adam style

ArchitectsRobert Adam and James Adam
Active periodc. 1760s–1790s
RegionGreat Britain, with international influence
MovementNeoclassicism

The Adam style (also called Adamesque and, in books, the "Style of the Brothers Adam") is an 18th-century neoclassical style of interior design and architecture, as practised by Scottish architect Robert Adam (1728–1792) and his brothers. It is an elaboration of the early phase of neoclassical design which succeeded the palladian and rococo styles in Britain.

Robert Adam trained in Italy, studying the architecture of ancient Rome and the newly excavated ruins of Diocletian's Palace at Split, before returning to Britain in 1758. With his brother James, he developed a light, elegant style characterised by classical Roman motifs — urns, vases, arabesques, swags, griffons and sphinxes — arranged in delicate, often pastel-coloured schemes. Unlike earlier neoclassicism, the Adam style treated the interior as a unified work of art: ceilings, walls, carpets, furniture, mirrors and even door furniture were designed as parts of a single harmonious composition, sometimes called a "movement" through contrasting room shapes (circles, ovals, octagons) and colour palettes.

Principal works

Notable examples include Syon House and Osterley Park in London, Harewood House and Kedleston Hall in the country, and the Register House and Charlotte Square in Edinburgh. The brothers published their designs in The Works in Architecture of Robert and James Adam (1773–79, with a posthumous volume in 1822), which spread the style across Britain, Europe and North America, where it influenced the Federal style.

Influence

The Adam style was widely copied by contemporaries such as Thomas Chippendale in furniture and Josiah Wedgwood in pottery, whose jasperware medallions echoed Adam ornament. In the 19th and early 20th centuries the style was revived, and "Adam revival" interiors became a hallmark of grand hotels and ocean liners.
