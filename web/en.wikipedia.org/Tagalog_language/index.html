Tagalog (tə-GAH-log) is an Austronesian language spoken by the Tagalog people, who form roughly a quarter of the population of the Philippines. It is the first language of most inhabitants of Metro Manila and central and southern Luzon, and together with English it serves as a lingua franca throughout the archipelago. The standardized register of Tagalog, called Filipino, is one of the two official languages of the Philippines, alongside English.

Baybayin, the pre-colonial script once used for writing Tagalog

History

Tagalog descends from the languages brought to the Philippines by Austronesian-speaking peoples. Before Spanish colonization, it was written in the Baybayin syllabary. Under Spanish rule (1565–1898), missionaries produced grammars and dictionaries— beginning with the Doctrina Christiana of 1593, one of the first books printed in the Philippines—and the language absorbed extensive Spanish vocabulary. In 1937 Tagalog was chosen as the basis of the national language, which was renamed Pilipino and later Filipino.

Grammar and features

Tagalog is known for its complex verb morphology: verbs are marked with affixes indicating focus (voice), aspect, and mood, and the "focus" system aligns the verb's marking with the role of the topic of the sentence. Basic word order is verb-initial. Reduplication is common for expressing plurality, incompleteness, or intensity. The language has large sets of loanwords from Spanish, Malay, Chinese (especially Hokkien), Sanskrit, Arabic, and increasingly English; everyday code-switching between Tagalog and English is known as "Taglish".

Usage today

Tagalog/Filipino dominates Philippine broadcast media, cinema, and publishing, and is spoken by large diaspora communities in the United States, Canada, the Middle East, and elsewhere. It is among the most widely spoken languages of the Austronesian family.
