Ring (mathematics)

Algebraic structure → Ring theory

In mathematics, a ring is an algebraic structure consisting of a set together with two binary operations usually called addition and multiplication, generalizing the arithmetic operations of the integers. In a ring, addition forms an abelian group, multiplication is associative, and multiplication distributes over addition.

Definition

A ring is a set R equipped with two binary operations + (addition) and ⋅ (multiplication) satisfying the following axioms, known as the ring axioms:

  • (R, +) is an abelian group: addition is associative and commutative, there is an additive identity 0, and every element has an additive inverse.
  • (R, ⋅) is a monoid: multiplication is associative and there is a multiplicative identity 1 (in the modern convention adopted by most authors, though some older texts allow rings without 1, sometimes called rngs).
  • Multiplication is distributive with respect to addition: a ⋅ (b + c) = (a ⋅ b) + (a ⋅ c) and (b + c) ⋅ a = (b ⋅ a) + (c ⋅ a).

Examples

The prototypical example is the ring of integers ℤ with ordinary addition and multiplication. Other examples include polynomial rings, matrix rings, the ring of integers modulo n, and rings of functions. If multiplication in a ring is also commutative, the ring is called a commutative ring; a commutative ring in which every nonzero element has a multiplicative inverse is a field.

History

Ring theory developed in the late 19th century from work on algebraic number theory (Dedekind's ideals) and polynomial equations. The abstract axiomatic treatment was given by Emmy Noether in her landmark 1921 paper "Idealtheorie in Ringbereichen". The word "ring" (German: Ring) was introduced by David Hilbert, possibly referring to a cyclic group of elements.

Basic concepts

Substructures of rings include subrings and ideals, the latter playing the role that normal subgroups play in group theory because they are exactly the kernels of ring homomorphisms. Quotient rings, localization, and the study of prime and maximal ideals are central themes of commutative algebra.
