Born: 19 March 1821, Torquay, England
Died: 20 October 1890, Trieste, Austria-Hungary
Occupation: Explorer, writer, translator, soldier, diplomat

Richard Francis Burton

Sir Richard Francis Burton (19 March 1821 – 20 October 1890) was a British explorer, writer, orientalist scholar, translator, and soldier. He is famed for his travels in Asia, Africa, and the Americas, his pilgrimage to Mecca in disguise, his search for the source of the Nile, and his translations of One Thousand and One Nights and the Kama Sutra.

Early life and career

Born in Torquay and raised partly in France and Italy, Burton joined the army of the East India Company and served in Sindh, where his linguistic gifts and undercover survey work earned him a reputation. He reportedly spoke some 29 languages and many more dialects.

Explorations

In 1853 Burton performed the Hajj pilgrimage to Mecca disguised as a Muslim, an account published as Personal Narrative of a Pilgrimage to Al-Madinah and Meccah. In 1854–55 he travelled to Harar in present-day Ethiopia. With John Hanning Speke he reached Lake Tanganyika in 1858 while searching for the Nile's source; their subsequent feud over whether Lake Victoria was the source became a famous Victorian controversy. He later explored West Africa, Brazil, Syria, and Iceland, and served as consul in Fernando Po, Santos, Damascus, and Trieste.

Writings and legacy

Burton wrote dozens of books and translated the Kama Sutra (1883) and a celebrated 16-volume The Book of the Thousand Nights and a Night (1885–88). He co-founded the Anthropological Society of London. His widow Isabel burned many of his papers after his death, a loss scholars still lament.
