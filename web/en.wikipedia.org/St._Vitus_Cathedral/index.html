St. Vitus Cathedral, the largest church in the Czech Republic.

The Metropolitan Cathedral of Saints Vitus, Wenceslaus and Adalbert (Czech: Metropolitní katedrála svatého Víta, Václava a Vojtěcha), commonly called St. Vitus Cathedral, is a Catholic cathedral in Prague, and the seat of the Archbishop of Prague. Until 1997, the cathedral was dedicated only to Saint Vitus, and is still commonly named solely as St. Vitus Cathedral. It is the largest and most important temple in the country and an excellent example of Gothic architecture; located within Prague Castle, it contains the tombs of many Bohemian kings and Holy Roman Emperors.

History

The first church on the site, a rotunda built by Wenceslaus I, Duke of Bohemia, around 930, was replaced by a Romanesque basilica in 1060. The present Gothic cathedral was founded on 21 November 1344 by John of Bohemia and his son Charles IV, who intended it as a coronation church, family crypt, and treasury for the kingdom's most precious relics. Its first architects were Matthias of Arras and, after his death, Peter Parler, whose innovative net vaults and the Chapel of St. Wenceslaus defined the building. Construction halted during the Hussite Wars and the cathedral remained half-finished for centuries; it was finally completed in the Neo-Gothic campaign of the 19th and early 20th centuries and consecrated in 1929.

Features

Notable features include the Great South Tower with the largest Czech church bell, Zikmund; the mosaic of the Last Judgment over the Golden Gate; the Chapel of St. Wenceslaus, encrusted with semi-precious stones; the silver tomb of St. John of Nepomuk; and the Bohemian Crown Jewels, kept in a chamber secured by seven locks. The 20th-century stained glass includes a celebrated window designed by Alphonse Mucha. The cathedral is owned by the Czech state following a long legal dispute resolved in 2010.
