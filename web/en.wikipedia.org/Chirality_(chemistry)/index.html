Chirality (chemistry)

In chemistry, a molecule or ion is called chiral if it cannot be superposed on its mirror image by any combination of rotations, translations, and conformational changes. This geometric property is called chirality. The word is derived from the Greek χείρ (kheir), "hand", a familiar chiral object. A pair of non-superposable mirror-image molecules are called enantiomers.

Chiral centres

The most common cause of molecular chirality is the presence of a stereogenic centre, typically a carbon atom bonded to four different substituents (a chiral centre). Chirality can also arise from axial or planar arrangements, as in allenes, biphenyls and helicenes. Absolute configurations are described with the R/S (Cahn–Ingold–Prelog) system, and enantiomers rotate plane-polarised light in opposite directions (optical activity), denoted (+)/(−) or, for sugars and amino acids, D/L.

Properties of enantiomers

Enantiomers have identical physical properties in an achiral environment — melting point, boiling point, and ordinary spectra — but interact differently with other chiral substances. This is crucial in biology, where receptors and enzymes are themselves chiral: the two enantiomers of a drug may differ dramatically in activity, the classic example being thalidomide, one enantiomer being therapeutic and the other teratogenic.

Occurrence and significance

Living organisms overwhelmingly use one handedness: L-amino acids in proteins and D-sugars in nucleic acids, a phenomenon called homochirality whose origin is a major open question in the study of the origin of life. Equal mixtures of enantiomers are called racemic mixtures. Chiral synthesis, separation of enantiomers (chiral resolution), and asymmetric catalysis are central to the pharmaceutical industry.
