Ozone depletion consists of two related events observed since the late 1970s: a steady decline of about four percent in the total amount of ozone in Earth's atmosphere (the ozone layer), and a much larger springtime decrease in stratospheric ozone over Earth's polar regions. The latter phenomenon is referred to as the ozone hole. There are also springtime polar tropospheric ozone depletion events in addition to these stratospheric events.

The main causes of ozone depletion and the ozone hole are manufactured chemicals, especially manufactured halocarbon refrigerants, solvents, propellants, and foam-blowing agents (chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs), HCFCs, halons), referred to as ozone-depleting substances (ODS). These compounds are transported into the stratosphere by turbulent mixing after being emitted at the surface, mixing much faster than the molecules can settle. Once in the stratosphere, they release atoms from the halogen group through photodissociation, which catalyze the breakdown of ozone (O3) into oxygen (O2). Both types of ozone depletion have been observed to increase as emissions of halocarbons have increased.

History of observations

In 1974, chemists Mario Molina and F. Sherwood Rowland at the University of California, Irvine, published a paper suggesting that CFCs could destroy ozone in the stratosphere. In 1985, scientists Joseph Farman, Brian Gardiner and Jonathan Shanklin of the British Antarctic Survey published results showing that ozone levels over Antarctica had fallen by more than 35 percent during the austral spring compared with 1960s levels, a finding confirmed by NASA satellite data from the Total Ozone Mapping Spectrometer (TOMS). Molina and Rowland shared the 1995 Nobel Prize in Chemistry with Paul Crutzen for their work on ozone formation and decomposition.

Ozone hole

The Antarctic ozone hole forms each year during the Antarctic spring (September–November), when returning sunlight triggers catalytic ozone destruction cycles involving chlorine and bromine compounds activated on polar stratospheric clouds. In severe years, ozone concentrations over Antarctica have fallen by as much as 70 percent locally. A smaller Arctic ozone hole occurs in some cold winters. Because the ozone layer absorbs most of the Sun's ultraviolet (UV) radiation, depletion increases UV reaching the surface, raising risks of skin cancer, cataracts, and harm to phytoplankton and ecosystems.

Policy response

The discovery prompted international action. The Vienna Convention (1985) established a framework, and the Montreal Protocol on Substances that Deplete the Ozone Layer (1987) phased out production and consumption of CFCs and other ODS. The protocol has been ratified by every United Nations member state and is widely regarded as the most successful international environmental agreement. CFC concentrations peaked in the late 1990s and are declining; scientists project recovery of the ozone layer to 1980 levels around the middle of the 21st century, with the Antarctic hole closing somewhat later (around the 2060s). The 2016 Kigali Amendment added a phase-down of hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs), potent greenhouse gases used as CFC substitutes.
