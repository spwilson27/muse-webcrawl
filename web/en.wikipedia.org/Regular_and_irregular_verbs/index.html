Regular and irregular verbs

Image placeholder: verbs.jpg — Diagram of verb inflection classes

A regular verb is a verb whose conjugation follows the typical pattern, or one of the typical patterns, of the language to which it belongs. A verb whose conjugation follows a different pattern is called an irregular verb. The classification is often language-specific and may apply chiefly to the formation of the past tense and past participle.

In English

In English, regular verbs form their past tense and past participle by adding the suffix -ed (with minor spelling adjustments such as doubling a final consonant or changing final y to i). Irregular verbs form these parts in other ways, inherited largely from older Germanic strong-verb patterns or from suppletion.

English has roughly 200 irregular verbs in common use. The most frequent verbs in the language — be, have, do, say, get, make, go, know, take, see — are overwhelmingly irregular, a pattern found in many languages: frequent use preserves archaic forms against the levelling pressure of regularisation. Over time, some irregular verbs become regular (in English, help once had the past form holp), and very occasionally a regular verb becomes irregular, as with American English dove for dived or snuck for sneaked.

Strong and weak verbs

In Germanic linguistics, regular verbs correspond largely to the historical "weak" verbs, which used a dental suffix (the ancestor of -ed), while many irregular verbs descend from the "strong" verbs, which marked tense by ablaut (vowel gradation), as in sing–sang–sung. German, Dutch, and the Scandinavian languages retain productive strong/weak distinctions.

In other languages

In French and Spanish, verbs fall into conjugation classes (-er, -ir, -re in French; -ar, -er, -ir in Spanish), and irregular verbs deviate from their class patterns, sometimes in only a few forms. Latin had four conjugations, with irregular verbs such as esse (to be) and ferre (to carry). In Japanese, nearly all verbs are regular, belonging to two classes, with only a handful of exceptions such as suru (to do) and kuru (to come). Highly inflecting languages such as Arabic organise verbs into derived stems with largely predictable patterns but pervasive "weak" consonant alternations.

Acquisition

The distinction is a classic topic in psycholinguistics: children learning English typically pass through a stage of overregularisation, producing forms such as goed and breaked after having earlier used the correct irregular forms, evidence that they have extracted a general rule. The "words and rules" model of Steven Pinker and related dual-route theories use this pattern to argue for separate lexical memory and grammatical computation.
