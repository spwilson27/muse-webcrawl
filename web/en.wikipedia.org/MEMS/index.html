MEMS (microelectromechanical systems) is the technology of microscopic devices incorporating both electronic and moving parts. MEMS are made up of components between 1 and 100 micrometres in size (i.e., 0.001 to 0.1 mm), and MEMS devices generally range in size from 20 micrometres to a millimetre (i.e., 0.02 to 1.0 mm), although components arranged in arrays (e.g., digital micromirror devices) can be more than 1000 mm2.

They usually consist of a central unit that processes data (an integrated circuit chip such as a microprocessor) and several components that interact with the surroundings, such as microsensors. Because of the large surface area to volume ratio of MEMS, forces produced by ambient electromagnetism (e.g., electrostatic charges and magnetic moments) and fluid dynamics (e.g., surface tension and viscosity) are more important design considerations than in larger-scale mechanical devices.

History

The term MEMS was coined in the 1980s, although the underlying technology has older roots in the silicon revolution and in devices such as the resonant gate transistor described in the 1960s. The commercial breakthrough came in the early 1990s with MEMS accelerometers used to trigger automotive airbags, which displaced conventional bulky accelerometers and dramatically reduced cost. Inkjet printer heads and, later, smartphone sensors drove further mass adoption.

Fabrication

MEMS are fabricated using modified semiconductor device fabrication technologies: deposition of material layers, patterning by photolithography, and etching to produce the required shapes. Two principal machining approaches are used: bulk micromachining, in which structures are etched into the silicon substrate itself, and surface micromachining, in which structures are built up in deposited thin films and released by etching a sacrificial layer. Silicon is the dominant material because of its well-understood mechanical and electronic properties, but polymers, metals, and ceramics are also used.

Applications

  • Accelerometers and gyroscopes for airbag deployment, vehicle stability control, smartphones, game controllers, and navigation.
  • Pressure sensors for automotive, medical, and industrial monitoring.
  • Microphones in mobile phones and hearing aids.
  • Digital micromirror devices (DMD) in video projectors.
  • Microfluidic devices and lab-on-a-chip systems for chemical and biological analysis.
  • RF MEMS switches and resonators for communications.

MEMS and NEMS

Nanoelectromechanical systems (NEMS) are a related class of devices integrating electrical and mechanical functionality on the nanoscale, extending MEMS concepts to components smaller than a micrometre.
