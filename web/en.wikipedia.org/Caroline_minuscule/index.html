Caroline minuscule

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Caroline minuscule or Carolingian minuscule is a script developed as a calligraphic standard in the medieval European period so that the Latin alphabet of Jerome's Vulgate Bible would be easily recognised by literate readers across the Carolingian empire. It was developed in the late 8th century, during the reign of Charlemagne, at scriptoria such as those of Corbie Abbey and Tours, and was used in the Holy Roman Empire between approximately 800 and 1200.

Origins

The script grew out of earlier Merovingian and Insular minuscules as part of the programme of educational and liturgical reform known as the Carolingian Renaissance. Scholars such as Alcuin of York, abbot of St Martin's at Tours from 796, supervised the production of manuscripts in a clear, regular hand. Charlemagne's Admonitio generalis (789) mandated consistent, corrected texts, creating demand for a uniform and legible book hand.

Characteristics

Caroline minuscule is characterised by round, well-separated letterforms of even height, generous word spacing, consistent use of the four-line scheme (with ascenders and descenders), and a limited set of abbreviations and ligatures. Distinctive forms include the open "a" resembling "cc", the tall "s" used initially and medially, and the ampersand for "et". Punctuation, including the punctus and an early form of question mark, became more systematic.

Spread and decline

The script spread throughout the Frankish world and to Italy, Spain and England (where it was reintroduced in the 10th-century Benedictine reform). Its uniformity enabled the copying and preservation of most surviving classical Latin literature: the great majority of ancient Roman texts survive only through Carolingian copies. From the 12th century, Caroline minuscule was gradually compressed and angularised into the Gothic textualis hands, though it persisted regionally for longer.

Legacy

Renaissance humanists such as Poggio Bracciolini encountered Caroline manuscripts and, mistaking them for authentic Roman writing, imitated their letterforms in the humanistic minuscule — the direct ancestor of the lowercase typefaces used in printing today.
