In numerical analysis, Newton's method, also known as the Newton–Raphson method, is a root-finding algorithm which produces successively better approximations to the roots (or zeroes) of a real-valued function. It is named after Isaac Newton and Joseph Raphson.

The method

Starting from an initial guess x0, the method iterates the rule xn+1 = xn − f(xn)/f′(xn), which corresponds geometrically to extending the tangent line at the current point down to the x-axis. Under suitable conditions the guesses converge to a root, and near a simple root the convergence is quadratic — the number of correct digits roughly doubles with each step.

History

Newton described a version of the method for polynomials in work of 1669 (published posthumously), and Raphson published a simplified presentation in 1690. The fully general analytic form was published by Thomas Simpson in 1740.

Limitations

The method can fail if the derivative is zero or near zero at a guess, if the starting point is far from the root, or if the iterates fall into a cycle. In the complex plane, the basins of attraction of different roots form Newton fractals. Generalisations to systems of equations use the Jacobian matrix and underlie much of modern numerical optimisation.
