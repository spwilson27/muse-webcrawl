Conversation analysis

Conversation analysis (CA) is an approach to the study of social interaction that investigates the structure and process of interaction in natural settings, especially everyday talk. It emerged in the 1960s and 1970s from the work of sociologist Harvey Sacks and his collaborators Emanuel Schegloff and Gail Jefferson, drawing on Harold Garfinkel's ethnomethodology and Erving Goffman's interaction order.

Core concepts

CA works from detailed transcripts of recorded interactions, using a notation system developed by Jefferson that captures pauses, overlaps, intonation and other features of delivery. Central findings include the turn-taking model (Sacks, Schegloff and Jefferson, 1974), which describes how speakers are selected and turns are allocated at transition-relevance places; adjacency pairs, such as question–answer and greeting–greeting, which organize sequences of actions; preference organization, the asymmetry between preferred and dispreferred responses; and repair, the practices by which speakers fix problems of speaking, hearing and understanding.

Applications

Because talk is the primordial site of social organization, CA has been applied to interaction in institutions — medicine, law, education, emergency calls, news interviews — as well as to ordinary conversation, and increasingly to video-recorded embodied interaction, gesture and gaze. It has influenced linguistics, anthropology, psychology, communication studies and human–computer interaction design.
