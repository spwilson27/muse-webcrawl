Podzol

A podzol profile with its distinctive ash-grey eluvial horizon.

Podzols are soils characteristic of coniferous (taiga) forests and heathlands in cool, humid climates, most extensive in Russia, Scandinavia, Canada, and northern Europe. The name derives from the Russian pod ("under") and zola ("ash"), referring to the bleached, ash-coloured layer that develops beneath the surface organic horizon. In the World Reference Base for Soil Resources, Podzols are defined by a subsurface horizon enriched in iron, aluminium, and/or humus compounds leached from above; in USDA soil taxonomy they correspond broadly to the order Spodosols.

Formation

Podzolisation occurs on acidic, coarse-textured, freely draining parent materials under vegetation — typically conifers or heather — whose litter decomposes into strongly acidic organic acids. Rainwater percolating through the litter mobilises iron and aluminium as organic complexes and strips them, along with clay and nutrients, from the upper mineral horizon (eluviation), leaving a pale, silica-rich E horizon. At depth the transported compounds precipitate in a reddish-brown or dark spodic B horizon; an accumulation of iron may cement into an iron pan that impedes drainage and root penetration.

Properties and land use

Podzols are infertile: they are acidic, deficient in most plant nutrients, and prone to waterlogging above pans or droughtiness in sandy profiles. Unimproved, they support forestry, rough grazing, and heathland. For agriculture they require liming, fertilisation, and sometimes deep ploughing to break the iron pan. Large areas of former heathland podzols in Denmark, the Netherlands, and northern Germany were reclaimed for farming in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, while the greatest expanse of natural podzols underlies the boreal forests of Siberia and Canada.

Distribution

Podzols cover roughly 4–5 per cent of the world's ice-free land, forming a near-continuous belt across the northern hemisphere's boreal zone, with outliers on humid temperate heaths and on quartz-rich sands in the tropics, where giant podzols occur in Amazonia and Borneo.
