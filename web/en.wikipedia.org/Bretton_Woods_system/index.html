Bretton Woods system

The Bretton Woods system of monetary management established the rules for commercial and financial relations among the United States, Canada, Western European countries, Australia, and Japan after the Bretton Woods Agreement of 1944. Delegates from 44 Allied nations met at the Mount Washington Hotel in Bretton Woods, New Hampshire, in July 1944 and designed a system in which national currencies were pegged to the U.S. dollar, which was itself convertible into gold at a fixed rate of $35 per ounce.

The conference and institutions

The negotiations were shaped above all by the American plan of Harry Dexter White and the British plan of John Maynard Keynes, who proposed an international clearing union and a world currency, the bancor. The final compromise leaned toward the American design. The agreement created the International Monetary Fund (IMF) and the International Bank for Reconstruction and Development, now part of the World Bank, to police exchange rates and finance postwar reconstruction.

Operation

Under the system, member states fixed their currencies within a band of one percent against the dollar and could draw on the IMF to meet balance-of-payments shortfalls. The arrangement underpinned the rapid growth of world trade in the postwar decades, but it contained a structural tension identified by economist Robert Triffin: supplying the world with dollar liquidity required persistent U.S. deficits, which steadily undermined confidence in the dollar's gold convertibility.

Collapse

By the late 1960s, U.S. spending on the Vietnam War and domestic programs, combined with mounting foreign dollar holdings, made the $35 gold price untenable. On August 15, 1971, President Richard Nixon suspended dollar convertibility into gold — the "Nixon shock." Efforts to rescue fixed rates through the Smithsonian Agreement failed, and by 1973 the major currencies had moved to floating exchange rates, ending the Bretton Woods system, although the IMF and World Bank it created endure.
