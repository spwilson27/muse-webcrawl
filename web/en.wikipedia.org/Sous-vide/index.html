Sous-vide

Sous-vide (French for "under vacuum") is a method of cooking in which food is placed in a vacuum-sealed plastic pouch and cooked in a water bath maintained at a precisely controlled, relatively low temperature for an extended time. The technique yields evenly cooked food that retains moisture and can be held safely at serving temperature.

History

Low-temperature water-bath cooking was described as early as 1799 by Benjamin Thompson, Count Rumford. The modern method was developed in the 1970s by the French chef Georges Pralus for the restaurant Troisgros, who found that foie gras cooked this way retained its fat and texture, and by Bruno Goussault, who systematised the time–temperature parameters. It spread through fine dining in the 2000s and reached home cooks through affordable immersion circulators in the 2010s.

Method

Food is seasoned, vacuum-sealed, and immersed in water circulated at the target core temperature — for example around 54–60 °C for steak cooked rare to medium-rare over one to four hours. Because the whole piece reaches a uniform temperature, edge-to-edge doneness is consistent; meat is often seared briefly afterward for a browned crust. Long cooking at low temperatures can also tenderise tough cuts by converting collagen to gelatin.

Safety

Cooking below 55 °C for long periods risks growth of pathogens such as Clostridium botulinum if time–temperature guidelines (pasteurisation tables) are not followed; food safety authorities publish precise schedules for safe use.
