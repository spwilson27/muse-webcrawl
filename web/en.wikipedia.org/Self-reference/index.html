Self-reference

Self-reference is the phenomenon of a sentence, idea, or formula referring to itself. It arises in language, logic, mathematics, computation, and philosophy, and is at the heart of several famous paradoxes and limitative theorems.

In logic and mathematics

The liar paradox — "this sentence is false" — is the classic example of problematic self-reference. Kurt Gödel's incompleteness theorems (1931) used a rigorous form of self-reference (Gödel numbering) to construct a true arithmetical statement that asserts its own unprovability within a given formal system. Related constructions appear in Turing's proof of the undecidability of the halting problem and in Tarski's undefinability theorem.

In language and literature

Natural languages permit self-referential expressions such as "this sentence has five words." Writers and artists exploit self-reference for humour and metafictional effect; examples include "this page intentionally left blank," the works of Douglas Hofstadter, and self-referential acronyms such as GNU ("GNU's Not Unix").

In computer science

  • A quine is a program that produces its own source code as output.
  • Recursion allows functions and data structures to be defined in terms of themselves.
  • Self-reproducing programs and self-modifying code build on the same idea.
