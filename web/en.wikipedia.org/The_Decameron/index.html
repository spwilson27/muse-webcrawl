The Decameron

The Decameron (Italian: Decameron, from Greek for "ten days") is a collection of novellas by the Italian author Giovanni Boccaccio, written between 1348 and 1353. It is one of the masterpieces of Italian prose and a landmark of European literature.

Frame story

Fleeing the Black Death in Florence, seven young women and three young men — the brigata — take refuge in a villa outside the city. To pass the time, each tells one story on each of ten days, yielding one hundred tales in all. Each day has a king or queen and often a set theme, such as fortune, love that ends happily or unhappily, or clever stratagems.

The tales

The stories range from the erotic and comic to the tragic and exemplary, and satirise clergy, merchants and lovers alike. Famous tales include the patient Griselda (tenth day), Federigo's falcon, the pot of basil (Lisabetta), and the ring parable told by Melchizedek. Boccaccio drew on folk tales, fabliaux, classical sources and contemporary Italian life.

Influence

The frame-tale structure influenced Chaucer's Canterbury Tales and much later storytelling. The book was long censored for its anti-clerical and bawdy content, and expurgated editions circulated for centuries. Films include Pier Paolo Pasolini's Il Decameron (1971).
