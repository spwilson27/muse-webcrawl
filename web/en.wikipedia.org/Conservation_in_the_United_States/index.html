Conservation in the United States

Conservation in the United States is the movement and body of policy concerned with the protection and wise management of the nation's natural resources — its forests, waters, soils, wildlife, and public lands. It arose in the late 19th century in response to the rapid depletion of forests, the near-extermination of species such as the bison and the passenger pigeon, and the wasteful exploitation of the western public domain.

Origins

Early voices included George Perkins Marsh, whose Man and Nature (1864) warned that human action could despoil landscapes. The federal government began setting land aside with Yellowstone National Park in 1872 and the Yosemite grant of 1864. The Forest Reserve Act of 1891 allowed presidents to reserve forest land, and under President Theodore Roosevelt some 230 million acres were placed under federal protection.

The Roosevelt–Pinchot era

Gifford Pinchot, first chief of the US Forest Service (established 1905), defined conservation as "the greatest good of the greatest number for the longest time" — the managed, scientific use of resources. This utilitarian philosophy sometimes clashed with the preservationist ethic of John Muir, who argued for protecting wilderness for its own sake; their conflict came to a head over the flooding of Hetch Hetchy Valley. Roosevelt created the US Fish and Wildlife predecessors, the first national wildlife refuges, five national parks, and 18 national monuments under the Antiquities Act of 1906.

Later developments

The New Deal added the Civilian Conservation Corps, the Soil Conservation Service, and the Tennessee Valley Authority in response to the Dust Bowl. After 1945 the conservation movement broadened into modern environmentalism, producing the Wilderness Act (1964), the Endangered Species Act (1973), and land-management frameworks for the Bureau of Land Management and Forest Service. Conservation today encompasses national parks and refuges, state and private land trusts, habitat restoration, and debates over energy development and climate change on public lands.
