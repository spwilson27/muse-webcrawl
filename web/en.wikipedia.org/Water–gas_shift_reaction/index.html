Water–gas shift reaction

Water–gas shift reaction
EquationCO + H2O ⇌ CO2 + H2
EnthalpyΔH° = −41.1 kJ/mol (exothermic)
CatalystsIron oxide/chromia (high-temperature); copper/zinc oxide (low-temperature)
UsesHydrogen production, ammonia synthesis gas, Fischer–Tropsch adjustment, fuel cells

Shift converters are a core step in industrial hydrogen manufacture.

The water–gas shift reaction (WGSR) describes the reaction of carbon monoxide and water vapour to form carbon dioxide and hydrogen: CO + H2O ⇌ CO2 + H2. The reaction is moderately exothermic and reversible. It is a crucial industrial step in the production of hydrogen and in adjusting the composition of synthesis gas, discovered in essence by Felice Fontana in 1780 and industrialised with the rise of the chemical industry; the related production of "water gas" from coke and steam gave the reaction its name.

Industrial practice

In steam reforming and gasification plants, the raw syngas contains substantial carbon monoxide, which would also poison downstream catalysts such as those in ammonia synthesis. The shift reaction converts CO to additional hydrogen while producing easily removable CO2. Because the equilibrium favours products at low temperature but reaction rates require heat, industry uses two stages: a high-temperature shift (about 350–450 °C) over iron oxide promoted with chromium oxide, followed by a low-temperature shift (about 190–250 °C) over a copper–zinc oxide catalyst, which reduces residual CO to a fraction of a percent. Sulphur-tolerant cobalt–molybdenum "sour shift" catalysts are used with coal-derived syngas.

Applications

Beyond bulk hydrogen for refineries and ammonia plants, the shift reaction adjusts the H2:CO ratio for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis and methanol production, and it is used in fuel processors for fuel cells to remove CO that would poison platinum electrodes. The reverse reaction — the reverse water–gas shift — is studied for converting captured CO2 back into syngas using hydrogen. Biological versions of the reaction are performed by some microorganisms, such as Rhodospirillum rubrum, which use carbon monoxide as an energy source.
