Christology

Christology is the branch of Christian theology concerned with the nature, person and work of Jesus Christ. It studies how Jesus relates to God the Father, how his divine and human natures coexist, and how his life, death and resurrection accomplish salvation.

Early debates

The first centuries of the Church produced competing accounts of Christ's nature. Docetists held that Christ only appeared to be human; adoptionists taught that Jesus was a man adopted as God's son; Arians maintained that the Son was a created being subordinate to the Father. The Council of Nicaea (325) condemned Arianism, affirming that the Son is "of one substance" (homoousios) with the Father, a formula embodied in the Nicene Creed.

Chalcedonian definition

The Council of Chalcedon (451) produced the classic settlement: Christ is one person in two natures, divine and human, "without confusion, change, division or separation". Chalcedon's definition is accepted by Roman Catholic, Eastern Orthodox and most Protestant churches, while Oriental Orthodox churches rejected it in favour of miaphysitism, holding to one united nature of God the Word incarnate.

Modern approaches

Modern Christology distinguishes "Christology from above", beginning with Christ's divinity, from "Christology from below", which starts from the historical Jesus and his human life. Theological study also examines Christ's titles in the New Testament — Messiah, Son of God, Son of Man, Logos, Lamb of God — and doctrines such as the hypostatic union, kenosis (self-emptying) and the communication of attributes.
