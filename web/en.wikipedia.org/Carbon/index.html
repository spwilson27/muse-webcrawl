Carbon

Carbon (from Latin carbo 'coal') is a chemical element with the symbol C and atomic number 6. It is nonmetallic and tetravalent — its atom making four electrons available to form covalent chemical bonds. It belongs to group 14 of the periodic table. Carbon makes up about 0.025% of Earth's crust, yet it is the fourth most abundant element in the universe by mass and the second most abundant in the human body. Three isotopes occur naturally: 12C and 13C are stable, while 14C is a radionuclide decaying with a half-life of about 5,730 years, the basis of radiocarbon dating.

Characteristics and allotropes

Carbon's ability to catenate — to form long chains and rings of C–C bonds — underlies the immense diversity of organic chemistry and all known life. Its crystalline allotropes contrast dramatically: graphite, layers of sp²-bonded sheets, is soft, black and electrically conducting, used in pencils, lubricants and electrodes; diamond, a three-dimensional sp³ network, is the hardest known natural material and a superb thermal conductor, prized as a gemstone and abrasive. Discovered from 1985 onward, molecular and nanoscale forms — fullerenes (buckminsterfullerene, C₆₀), carbon nanotubes and single-atom-thick graphene (isolated in 2004, Nobel Prize 2010) — have become major fields of materials science.

Occurrence and the carbon cycle

Carbon is produced in stars by the triple-alpha process. On Earth it cycles among the atmosphere (as CO₂ and methane), the biosphere, the oceans and the lithosphere (carbonate rocks, coal, oil and gas). Photosynthesis fixes roughly 120 billion tonnes of carbon per year. Human combustion of fossil fuels and land-use change have raised atmospheric CO₂ from about 280 ppm pre-industrially to over 420 ppm, driving modern climate change.

Applications

Beyond fuels, carbon appears as carbon black (tyres, inks), activated carbon (filtration), graphite electrodes for steelmaking, carbon fibre composites for aircraft and sports goods, coke for smelting, and diamonds for cutting tools. The organic chemicals industry — plastics, pharmaceuticals, solvents — is built on carbon chemistry.

History

Charcoal and soot were known in prehistory; diamond and graphite were recognised as forms of the same element in the late 18th century (Lavoisier; Smithson Tennant proved diamond is pure carbon in 1797). The name derives from the Latin for charcoal.
