Death Valley is a desert valley in Eastern California, in the northern Mojave Desert, bordering the Great Basin Desert. It is thought to be the hottest place on Earth during summer. Death Valley is home to Badwater Basin, the point of lowest elevation in North America at 282 feet (86 m) below sea level — lying just 84.6 miles (136.2 km) east-southeast of Mount Whitney, the highest point in the contiguous United States.

On the afternoon of 10 July 1913, the United States Weather Bureau recorded a high temperature of 134 °F (56.7 °C) at Furnace Creek in Death Valley, which stands as the highest ambient air temperature ever recorded at the surface of the Earth. This reading, and several others taken in that period, are disputed by some modern experts, but remain officially recognised.

Geology

Death Valley is a graben — a long, down-dropped block of crust between the Amargosa Range to the east and the Panamint Range to the west — formed by the extensional tectonics of the Basin and Range Province. Beneath a thin layer of sediment the valley floor continues to subside. During the Pleistocene, the valley held a deep lake, Lake Manly, which evaporated some 10,000 years ago, leaving the salt flats and mineral deposits visible today. Notable formations include the colourful Artist's Palette, the moving stones of Racetrack Playa, the sand dunes at Mesquite Flat, and Zabriskie Point.

Climate

Death Valley is the driest place in North America, averaging about 2.2 inches (56 mm) of rain per year. Its extreme heat results from a combination of low elevation, clear skies, dry soil and the rain shadow of four mountain ranges; air descending into the deep valley is compressed and heated further. Despite the harshness, the valley supports surprisingly diverse life, from bighorn sheep and coyotes to spring wildflower blooms after wet winters, and the endemic Death Valley pupfish surviving in isolated salt pools.

History

The Timbisha Shoshone have inhabited the valley for at least a thousand years. Its English name dates to the California Gold Rush: in the winter of 1849–50 a party of pioneers became lost in the valley for weeks, and one of them is said to have looked back and said "Goodbye, Death Valley" on finally escaping. Borax mining in the 1880s — popularised by the "20-mule team" wagons — and later tourism followed. Death Valley was proclaimed a national monument in 1933 and redesignated a national park in 1994; at over 3.4 million acres it is the largest national park in the contiguous United States.
