Paternalism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Paternalism models authority on a father's care for a child

Paternalism is action that limits a person's or group's liberty or autonomy for their own good, on the model of a father's authority over his children. Paternalistic policies — such as compulsory seat-belt laws, bans on harmful drugs, mandatory pension contributions and restrictions on smoking — interfere with people's choices in the name of their welfare, sometimes against their stated wishes.

Philosophical debate

The classical objection is John Stuart Mill's harm principle in On Liberty (1859): "the only purpose for which power can be rightfully exercised over any member of a civilized community, against his will, is to prevent harm to others. His own good, either physical or moral, is not a sufficient warrant." Defenders of paternalism reply that choices may be uninformed, impulsive or distorted by addiction and bias, so that some interference can protect a person's deeper autonomy rather than violate it. Philosophers distinguish hard paternalism, which overrides the choices of fully informed adults, from soft paternalism, which intervenes only where a choice is substantially non-voluntary or ill-informed. Gerald Dworkin's influential 1972 essay systematised these distinctions.

Libertarian paternalism

In policy, the "libertarian paternalism" of Richard Thaler and Cass Sunstein proposes nudges — choice architectures such as automatic enrolment in pension schemes with an opt-out — that steer people toward better outcomes while formally preserving freedom of choice. Critics on one side call this manipulation; critics on the other regard it as too weak to address serious harms.

Other applications

The term is also used historically for the self-justifying ideology of slaveholders who claimed to care for enslaved people as children, for colonial "trusteeship" over subject peoples, and for employer welfare schemes in company towns. In medicine, paternalism describes the older model in which doctors made decisions for patients, largely displaced in modern practice by informed consent and shared decision-making.
