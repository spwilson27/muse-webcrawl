The Dutch Golden Age (Dutch: de Gouden Eeuw) was a period in the history of the Netherlands roughly spanning the seventeenth century, in which Dutch trade, science, military power and art were among the most acclaimed in the world. The first half of the period was framed by the Eighty Years' War against Spain, ending with the Peace of Westphalia in 1648; the era is conventionally said to end with the Disaster Year (Rampjaar) of 1672.

Trade and finance

The Dutch Republic became Europe's entrepôt: Amsterdam's commodity market, stock exchange (1602) and Bank of Amsterdam (1609) pioneered modern finance, while the Dutch East India Company and West India Company built a global trading empire. Cheap shipping (the fluyt), Baltic grain trade, herring fisheries and textile and shipbuilding industries made the republic, per capita, the richest society in Europe — though the wealth rested in part on colonial exploitation and the slave trade.

Science and thought

Relative religious tolerance attracted thinkers from across Europe. Christiaan Huygens advanced astronomy, optics and probability; Antonie van Leeuwenhoek's microscopes revealed microorganisms; Hugo Grotius founded international law; Baruch Spinoza wrote his philosophy in the republic; and Descartes spent much of his productive life there. Dutch cartographers such as Blaeu set the standard for world atlases.

Art

A prosperous middle class bought paintings on an unprecedented scale — perhaps millions were produced — in genres from landscape and still life to portraiture and scenes of daily life. Masters include Rembrandt van Rijn, Johannes Vermeer, Frans Hals, Jacob van Ruisdael and Jan Steen.

Decline

Competition from England and France, three Anglo-Dutch Wars, and the invasion of 1672 by France, England, Münster and Cologne ended the Republic's primacy, though Dutch prosperity and culture remained formidable for decades.
