Fafnir

In Norse mythology, Fafnir (Old Norse: Fáfnir) was a son of the dwarf king Hreidmar who, seized by greed for a cursed hoard of gold, murdered his father and transformed into a mighty dragon to guard the treasure. He was slain by the hero Sigurd (Siegfried), who, after bathing in or tasting the dragon's blood, gained the ability to understand the speech of birds.

The myth

As told in the Poetic Edda poems Reginsmál and Fáfnismál, the Prose Edda, and the Völsunga saga, the gods Odin, Loki and Hœnir were forced to pay a ransom for killing the shapeshifter Ótr, filling his otter skin with gold — including the dwarf Andvari's cursed ring and treasure. Fafnir killed his father Hreidmar to seize the hoard, drove away his brother Regin, and turned into a serpent or dragon out of greed, poisoning the land around his lair on Gnitaheid.

Regin, Fafnir's brother, fostered the young Sigurd and reforged his father's sword, Gram, urging him to kill the dragon. Sigurd dug a pit on the path Fafnir crawled to drink, stabbed upward into the dragon's belly as it passed over him, and spoke with the dying Fafnir, who warned that the gold would be the death of whoever possessed it. Following the advice of birds, Sigurd killed Regin as well, when he learned Regin planned to murder him, and loaded the treasure onto his horse Grani.

Legacy

The Fafnir story influenced Richard Wagner's Ring cycle, in which Fafner appears first as a giant and then as a dragon, and it helped shape J. R. R. Tolkien's dragons, notably Smaug. The figure remains a popular archetype of the treasure-guarding dragon in fantasy literature and games.
