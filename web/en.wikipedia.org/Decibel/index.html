The decibel (symbol: dB) is a relative unit of measurement equal to one tenth of a bel (B). It expresses the ratio of two values of a power or root-power quantity on a logarithmic scale, and is widely used in acoustics, electronics, telecommunications, and control theory.

Definition

For power quantities, the level in decibels is
LdB = 10 log10(P1/P0).
For root-power (field) quantities such as voltage, current, or sound pressure, whose power is proportional to the square of the amplitude, the definition becomes
LdB = 20 log10(A1/A0).
Thus a doubling of power is approximately +3 dB, a tenfold increase in power is +10 dB, and a tenfold increase in amplitude is +20 dB.

History

The unit originated in the 1920s in telephone engineering as a convenient measure of signal loss in cables; the "transmission unit" was renamed the bel in honour of Alexander Graham Bell, and the decibel, being one tenth of a bel, proved the more practical size.

Sound levels

In acoustics, sound pressure level is usually referenced to 20 micropascals, the nominal threshold of human hearing, written dB SPL. Typical values:

Level (dB SPL)Example
0Threshold of hearing
30Whisper at 1 m
60Normal conversation
85Level at which prolonged exposure risks hearing damage
120Threshold of pain (approx.)

Suffixes and references

Decibel values are often given with a suffix denoting the reference quantity: dBm (1 milliwatt), dBW (1 watt), dBV and dBu (voltage references), dBA (A-weighted sound level), and dBi (antenna gain relative to an isotropic radiator).
