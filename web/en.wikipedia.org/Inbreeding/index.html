Inbreeding

Inbreeding is the production of offspring from the mating or breeding of individuals or organisms that are closely related genetically. By analogy, the term is sometimes used in reference to human reproduction, where it denotes marriages or unions between close relatives. Inbreeding results in increased homozygosity, which can increase the chances of offspring being affected by deleterious recessive traits — a phenomenon called inbreeding depression.

Genetic effects

The measure of inbreeding of an individual is the coefficient of inbreeding (F), the probability that both alleles at any locus are identical by descent. Offspring of first cousins have F = 1/16. Inbreeding does not change allele frequencies by itself, but it redistributes them: heterozygotes decrease and homozygotes increase, exposing recessive deleterious alleles to selection. In small populations, inbreeding also accompanies genetic drift, reducing overall genetic diversity and increasing extinction risk through the accumulation of deleterious mutations.

Inbreeding depression and avoidance

Inbreeding depression — reduced survival, fertility, and vigor — has been documented in wild and captive populations, from wolves and lions to laboratory animals and plants. Many species have evolved inbreeding-avoidance mechanisms such as sex-biased dispersal, kin recognition, and delayed reproduction in the presence of kin. Conversely, habitual self-fertilization in many plants and some animals purges recessive load and can be stable.

In humans and domestic animals

Marriage between close relatives is restricted by law and custom in many societies, though cousin marriage is legal and common in parts of the world; children of first cousins face a modestly elevated risk of congenital disorders. In animal breeding, controlled inbreeding (line breeding) is used to fix desirable traits in livestock and purebred dogs, at the cost of elevated rates of inherited disease. Famous historical examples of dynastic inbreeding include the Spanish Habsburgs, whose line ended with the severely disabled Charles II in 1700.
