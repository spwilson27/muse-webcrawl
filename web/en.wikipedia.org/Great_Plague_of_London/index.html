Great Plague of London

The Great Plague of London, lasting from 1665 to 1666, was the last major epidemic of the bubonic plague to occur in England. It happened within the centuries-long Second Pandemic, a period of intermittent bubonic plague epidemics that originated in Central Asia. The Great Plague killed an estimated 100,000 people — almost a quarter of London's population — in 18 months. The plague was caused by the Yersinia pestis bacterium, transmitted through the bites of infected fleas living on rats.

Background

Plague had visited England repeatedly since the Black Death of 1348–49. The epidemic of 1665 probably arrived from the Netherlands, where plague had raged in Amsterdam in 1663–64; trading ships carrying cotton bales are thought to have brought infected fleas and rats into the London docks. The first recorded cases appeared in the parish of St Giles-in-the-Fields in the winter of 1664–65.

Course of the epidemic

As deaths mounted in the spring and summer of 1665, King Charles II and his court fled London in July, eventually settling in Oxford. Many wealthy residents, merchants and clergy who could leave did so. Those who remained faced strict public-health measures: infected households were shut up and marked with a red cross and the words "Lord have mercy upon us"; watchmen guarded the doors. "Searchers" — usually elderly women — determined causes of death, recorded weekly in the Bills of Mortality. At the September peak, more than 7,000 Londoners died in a single week by the official count, which certainly understated the true figure.

Mass burial pits were dug as churchyards overflowed. Carts passed through the streets collecting corpses to the cry of "Bring out your dead!" The diarist Samuel Pepys left a vivid eyewitness account of the plague year, and Daniel Defoe's A Journal of the Plague Year (1722) remains a famous, semi-fictionalised narrative. The epidemic declined through the winter of 1665–66 and into 1666, though plague never again returned to London on such a scale.

Aftermath

The Great Fire of London of September 1666 was once popularly credited with ending the plague by destroying the rats' harbourages, but the epidemic had already waned before the fire. Rebuilding after the fire, with wider streets and brick construction, nonetheless improved the city's sanitation.
