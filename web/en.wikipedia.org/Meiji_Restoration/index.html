Meiji Restoration

The Meiji Restoration (Japanese: 明治維新, Meiji Ishin), referred to at the time as the Honorable Restoration, was a political revolution in Japan in 1868 that ended the Tokugawa shogunate and restored practical imperial rule under Emperor Meiji. It inaugurated the Meiji era (1868–1912), during which Japan rapidly industrialised and adopted Western institutions, transforming from a feudal society into a modern great power.

Background

The arrival of Commodore Matthew Perry's American "Black Ships" in 1853 and the unequal treaties that followed exposed the shogunate's weakness. Loyalist samurai from the Satsuma and Chōshū domains, rallying under the slogan sonnō jōi ("revere the emperor, expel the barbarians"), formed an alliance in 1866. In November 1867 the last shogun, Tokugawa Yoshinobu, returned governing authority to the emperor; on 3 January 1868 the restoration of imperial rule was proclaimed. Loyalist forces defeated shogunate loyalists in the Boshin War (1868–1869).

Reforms

The Charter Oath of April 1868 promised deliberative assemblies and the pursuit of knowledge worldwide. The new government abolished the domains and replaced them with prefectures (1871), dissolved the samurai class privileges, introduced conscription (1873) and a land-tax reform, and built railways, telegraph lines, lighthouses and a modern navy and army. The Iwakura Mission (1871–1873) studied Western institutions first-hand. Education was made compulsory, and thousands of foreign advisers (oyatoi gaikokujin) were hired.

Consolidation

Discontent among former samurai culminated in the Satsuma Rebellion of 1877, led ironically by restoration hero Saigō Takamori; its defeat confirmed the conscript army. The Meiji Constitution (1889) created a constitutional monarchy with an elected Diet (first convened 1890). Victories in the First Sino-Japanese War (1894–95) and Russo-Japanese War (1904–05) announced Japan's arrival as a great power.
