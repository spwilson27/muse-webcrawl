Rayleigh scattering

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Rayleigh scattering is the elastic scattering of light or other electromagnetic radiation by particles much smaller than the wavelength of the radiation — for example, the molecules of a gas. Named after the British physicist Lord Rayleigh (John William Strutt), who derived its law in the 1870s and explained it fully in 1899, the effect is responsible for the blue colour of the daytime sky and the red colours of sunsets. The intensity of the scattered light varies inversely with the fourth power of the wavelength:

I ∝ 1/λ⁴

so short (blue) wavelengths are scattered far more strongly — about five times more than red light across the visible spectrum, and more still in practice because of the solar spectrum and the eye's sensitivity.

Explanation

When light passes through a gas, its oscillating electric field induces oscillating dipoles in the molecules, which re-radiate light in all directions without changing its wavelength (elastic scattering). Because molecules are much smaller than visible wavelengths, the scattering follows the λ⁻⁴ law. In the Earth's atmosphere, nitrogen and oxygen molecules scatter blue light toward the observer from every part of the sky, making the sky appear blue; the scattered light is also strongly polarised at 90° from the Sun. At sunrise and sunset, sunlight traverses a much longer path through the air, most blue light is scattered away entirely, and the remaining direct light appears yellow, orange or red; the glow of the whole sky is likewise reddened.

Scope and related effects

Rayleigh's treatment applies when particles are less than roughly a tenth of the wavelength; for larger particles (cloud droplets, dust, aerosols) the more general Mie scattering applies, which is nearly wavelength-independent and makes clouds white. Rayleigh scattering by density fluctuations also occurs in liquids and solids. In optical fibres, Rayleigh scattering from microscopic variations in glass is the dominant source of signal loss and sets the attenuation floor of telecommunications fibres; it is exploited in its own right in lidar and Raman-based distributed sensing.
