The Harrier, informally referred to as the Harrier jump jet, is a family of jet-powered attack aircraft capable of vertical/short takeoff and landing operations (V/STOL). Named after the bird of prey, it was originally developed by British manufacturer Hawker Siddeley in the 1960s. The Harrier emerged as the only truly successful V/STOL design created during that era.

Development

The Harrier grew out of the experimental Hawker P.1127 and the Kestrel evaluation aircraft of the early 1960s, which proved the concept of directing the thrust of a single turbofan engine — the Rolls-Royce Pegasus — downward through four rotating nozzles. The Harrier GR.1 entered service with the Royal Air Force in 1969, the world's first operational vertical takeoff and landing fixed-wing aircraft. Its ability to operate from small clearings, roads, and ships without catapults or long runways made it uniquely flexible, though it carried a limited payload and was subsonic.

Variants and service

The Sea Harrier variant served with the Royal Navy from 1978 and achieved fame during the 1982 Falklands War, when Sea Harriers from HMS Hermes and HMS Invincible shot down 20 Argentine aircraft without air-to-air loss. The United States Marine Corps operated the AV-8A and later the much redesigned AV-8B Harrier II, developed jointly by McDonnell Douglas and British Aerospace with a larger composite wing. The RAF and Royal Navy operated the joint-force Harrier GR.7/GR.9 until British Harriers were retired in 2010. Spain and Italy operated Sea Harrier derivatives from light carriers, and India flew Sea Harriers until 2016.

The Harrier family is being succeeded in carrier and Marine service by the STOVL variant of the Lockheed Martin F-35 Lightning II, the F-35B, which uses a shaft-driven lift fan rather than direct vectored thrust. The AV-8B remains in service with the U.S. Marine Corps, Spain, and Italy into the 2020s.

Characteristics

The Harrier's vectored-thrust Pegasus engine allows it to hover, take off vertically, and land vertically, at the cost of high fuel consumption in the hover and demanding pilot workload — small "puffer" reaction-control valves in the nose, tail and wingtips provide control at low speed. The type has suffered a comparatively high accident rate during its career, largely attributable to the difficulty of V/STOL operations.
