Motor speech disorders

Field: Speech-language pathology, neurology
Main types: Dysarthria, apraxia of speech
Causes: Stroke, traumatic brain injury, Parkinson's disease, ALS, cerebral palsy

Motor speech disorders are a group of neurological conditions that impair the planning, programming, control, or execution of speech movements. They are conventionally divided into two main categories: dysarthria, caused by weakness, paralysis, slowness, or incoordination of the speech musculature, and apraxia of speech, a disorder of motor planning and programming in which the muscles themselves are not weak.

Dysarthria

Dysarthria results from damage to the central or peripheral nervous system affecting respiration, phonation, resonance, articulation, and prosody. The Mayo Clinic classification, developed by Darley, Aronson, and Brown, distinguishes flaccid, spastic, ataxic, hypokinetic, hyperkinetic, and mixed dysarthrias, each associated with particular sites of lesion and diseases—for example, the hypokinetic dysarthria of Parkinson's disease and the mixed dysarthria of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS).

Apraxia of speech

Apraxia of speech (AOS) is characterised by inconsistent sound errors, groping articulatory movements, and disrupted prosody, with relatively preserved automatic speech. It typically follows damage to the left frontal lobe, including the insula and premotor areas, often from stroke. Childhood apraxia of speech is a developmental form not attributable to muscle weakness.

Assessment and treatment

Speech-language pathologists assess motor speech through perceptual analysis, oral mechanism examination, and instrumental measures. Treatment may target intelligibility, rate control, breath support, and compensatory strategies, including augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) in severe cases.
