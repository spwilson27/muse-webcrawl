In biology, a hybrid is the offspring resulting from combining the qualities of two organisms of different varieties, species, or genera through sexual reproduction. Hybrids are not always intermediates between their parents, but may show hybrid vigour (heterosis), growing larger or more robustly than either parent. Conversely, hybrids may suffer outbreeding depression or be sterile.

Types of hybrids

Hybridization can occur between different species (interspecific), between subspecies (intraspecific intersubspecific), or between genera (intergeneric). Crosses may be designated by portmanteau names for animal hybrids—such as the liger (lion × tiger) or zonkey (zebra × donkey)—or by a multiplication sign in botanical nomenclature, as in × Cupressocyparis. The sex of the parents matters in some crosses: a mule is the offspring of a female horse and a male donkey, whereas the reciprocal cross is called a hinny.

Fertility and speciation

Many hybrids are sterile because mismatched chromosome numbers disrupt meiosis; the mule, with 63 chromosomes, is a classic example. In plants, chromosome doubling (allopolyploidy) can restore fertility and instantly create new species, a route taken by bread wheat and many other crops. In animals, hybridization can also introduce genes between species (introgression), and modern genomics has shown that early modern humans interbred with Neanderthals and Denisovans.

Hybrids in agriculture and conservation

Hybrid seed, especially hybrid maize developed in the 20th century, underlies much of modern agriculture because first-generation (F1) hybrids yield substantially more than their inbred parents. In conservation biology, hybridization is double-edged: it can rescue inbred populations through genetic rescue, but it can also threaten rare species with genetic swamping, as when domestic or introduced forms interbreed with wild relatives.
