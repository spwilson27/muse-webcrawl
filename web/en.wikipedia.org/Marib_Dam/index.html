The Marib Dam (Arabic: سد مأرب) is a dam blocking the valley of the Dhana river in the Balaq Hills of modern-day Yemen, near the ancient city of Marib. The ancient dam was one of the engineering wonders of the ancient world, sustaining the irrigation agriculture that made Marib, capital of the Sabaean kingdom, prosperous on the incense trade route.

Ancient dam

Construction of a dam at the site began around the mid-eighth century BC. The structure was an earth dam about 580 metres long and 4 metres high, later rebuilt in stone and raised to roughly 14 metres. It slowed the seasonal floodwaters (sayl) of the wadi and diverted them through sluice gates into a network of canals that irrigated fields on both banks, supporting an estimated tens of thousands of inhabitants in the oasis.

The dam was breached and repaired several times over its long life. Its final collapse, traditionally dated to about AD 570, is described in South Arabian inscriptions and is associated in Islamic tradition with the flood mentioned in the Qur'an (the "Flood of the Dams", sayl al-ʿarim). After the breach the irrigation system failed, the oasis was largely abandoned, and many inhabitants migrated north.

Modern dam

A new dam was constructed near the ancient site with funding from the United Arab Emirates and opened in 1986. The modern structure stands about 38 metres high and impounds a reservoir used to replenish groundwater and support agriculture in the Marib region. The area around the dam and the ruins of ancient Marib suffered during the Yemeni civil war, and the archaeological remains are protected as part of the UNESCO World Heritage listing for the landmarks of the ancient Sabaean kingdom.
