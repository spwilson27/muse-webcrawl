Cannon–Bard theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Cannon–Bard theory of emotion is a physiological theory of emotion developed by Walter Cannon and Philip Bard in the 1920s. It states that an emotion-arousing stimulus simultaneously triggers both physiological responses and the subjective experience of emotion.

History

Walter Cannon first proposed the theory in 1927 as a critique of the earlier James–Lange theory, which held that physiological arousal precedes and causes emotional experience. Cannon argued that visceral changes are too slow and too similar across different emotions to account for the speed and specificity of emotional feelings. His student Philip Bard expanded the theory, emphasizing the role of the thalamus in coordinating emotional responses.

Comparison with other theories

Unlike the James–Lange theory, in which arousal causes emotion, and the later two-factor theory of Stanley Schachter and Jerome Singer, in which arousal is interpreted through cognitive appraisal, the Cannon–Bard theory holds that emotion and arousal occur at the same time and independently.

Criticism

Critics have noted that the theory overstates the role of the thalamus and that patients with thalamic damage can still experience emotions, suggesting that other brain structures are also involved.
