Edward Williams Morley (January 29, 1838 – February 24, 1923) was an American scientist known for his precise measurements of the atomic weight of oxygen and for the Michelson–Morley experiment, performed with Albert A. Michelson in 1887, which failed to detect the luminiferous aether and became a cornerstone of the evidence for special relativity.

Life and work

Born in Newark, New Jersey, Morley graduated from Williams College in 1857 and initially studied theology, ordained as a Congregationalist minister, before turning to science. From 1869 to 1906 he was professor of chemistry at Western Reserve College (now Case Western Reserve University) in Cleveland, Ohio.

Morley was famed for meticulous quantitative work. His determination of the relative atomic weights of hydrogen and oxygen — published in 1895 after more than a decade of work — was among the most accurate of its era and earned international honours. His collaboration with Michelson applied interferometry to look for the Earth's motion through the aether; the celebrated null result, repeated by Morley with Dayton Miller, resisted later attempts to explain it away and is often called the most famous "failed" experiment in physics.
