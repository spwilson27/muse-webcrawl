The polymerase chain reaction (PCR) is a method widely used to make millions to billions of copies of a specific DNA sample rapidly, allowing scientists to amplify a very small sample of DNA to a large enough amount to be studied in detail. It was invented in 1983 by American biochemist Kary Mullis, who received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1993 for the work.

Procedure

PCR relies on thermal cycling — repeated heating and cooling of the reaction in a thermal cycler. Each cycle has three steps:

  • Denaturation (~94–98 °C): the double-stranded DNA melts into single strands.
  • Annealing (~50–65 °C): short DNA primers bind to their complementary sequences flanking the target region.
  • Extension (~72 °C): a heat-stable DNA polymerase — originally Taq polymerase from Thermus aquaticus — synthesises new strands.

Because each new strand can serve as a template in the next cycle, the target sequence accumulates exponentially, roughly doubling each cycle.

Applications

PCR underpins modern molecular biology: infectious-disease diagnostics (including RT-PCR tests for viruses such as SARS‑CoV‑2), DNA fingerprinting in forensics, paternity testing, cloning, mutagenesis, and preparation of samples for sequencing. Quantitative variants (qPCR) measure the amount of starting material in real time.
