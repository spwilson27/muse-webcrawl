Gospel (liturgy)

In Christian liturgy, the Gospel reading is the proclamation of a passage from one of the four canonical Gospels — Matthew, Mark, Luke, or John — forming the climax of the Liturgy of the Word in the Mass and the corresponding services of other traditions. The word derives through Old English gōdspel, "good news", translating Greek euangelion.

[ Image placeholder: An illuminated Gospel book carried in procession ]

In the Roman Rite, the Gospel is read or chanted by a deacon or priest after the other readings and the Alleluia (or, in Lent, another acclamation). The congregation stands; the book may be carried in procession with candles and incense, and the reader signs the book and his forehead, lips, and breast with a small cross, praying that the word be in his mind, on his lips, and in his heart. The reading is introduced with "A reading from the holy Gospel according to N." and concluded with "The Gospel of the Lord."

Traditions

In Eastern Orthodox practice, the Gospel is read by the deacon from the ambon or before the iconostasis, and the ornate Gospel book rests on the altar. In many Protestant traditions the Gospel reading holds an honoured place in lectionary-based worship, and in Anglican practice it is often read from the centre of the congregation. The three-year Roman lectionary and the Revised Common Lectionary assign Gospel passages for every Sunday of the liturgical year, working through Matthew, Mark, and Luke in successive years, with John used in all three.

The Last Gospel

In the Tridentine Mass, the beginning of John's Gospel (John 1:1–14) was read at the very end of Mass as the "Last Gospel", a practice suppressed in the 1960s reforms of the Roman Rite.
