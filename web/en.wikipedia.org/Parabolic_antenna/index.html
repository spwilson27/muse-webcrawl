Parabolic antenna

A parabolic antenna is an antenna that uses a parabolic reflector, a curved surface with the cross-sectional shape of a parabola, to direct radio waves. Its most common form is shaped like a dish and is known as a dish antenna or parabolic dish. The main advantage of a parabolic antenna is that it has high directivity: it functions similarly to a searchlight or flashlight reflector to direct radio waves in a narrow beam, or to receive waves from one particular direction only.

Parabolic antennas are used as high-gain antennas for point-to-point communications, satellite television reception, microwave relay links, radar, and radio astronomy. The incoming waves are reflected by the dish and concentrated at its focal point, where a feed antenna (feed horn) is mounted. Conversely, when transmitting, waves from the feed are reflected off the dish into a collimated beam.

History

The idea of using parabolic reflectors for radio antennas was taken from optics, where the power of a parabolic mirror to focus light into a beam has been known since classical antiquity. German physicist Heinrich Hertz constructed the first parabolic reflector antennas in 1888 during his experiments proving the existence of radio waves. Large parabolic dish antennas became widespread during the Second World War with the development of radar, and in 1946 Grote Reber built a parabolic radio telescope dish 9.4 metres in diameter.

Design

The reflector is typically made of metal or metallised fibreglass. The diameter of the dish relative to the wavelength determines the gain and beamwidth: larger dishes produce narrower, more powerful beams. Feed designs include prime-focus feeds, in which the feed sits at the focus in front of the dish, and offset and Cassegrain designs, which use secondary reflectors to place the feed behind or beside the main dish and reduce blockage.
