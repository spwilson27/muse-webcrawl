Cult

Cult is a term, in its broadest sense, for a system of religious worship, especially with reference to its rites and ceremonies. In the 20th century the word acquired a strongly negative connotation in popular usage, describing groups alleged to exercise excessive or manipulative control over members, often under a charismatic leader. Sociologists of religion generally prefer the neutral term "new religious movement", arguing that "cult" functions more as a label of social disapproval than as an analytical category.

Etymology and classical usage

The word derives from Latin cultus, "care, cultivation, worship", from the verb colere. In classical and scholarly usage it remains neutral: historians speak of the imperial cult of Rome, the cult of saints in medieval Christianity, or the cult of Isis, meaning simply the organised veneration directed at a deity or figure.

Modern usage and controversy

The pejorative sense crystallised in the United States in the 1970s amid the "cult wars": the rise of new religions, the anti-cult movement, and atrocities such as the Jonestown mass murder-suicide (1978), in which over 900 members of the Peoples Temple died, followed by the Branch Davidian siege (1993) and the Heaven's Gate (1997) and Aum Shinrikyo (1995) attacks. Anti-cult activists promoted theories of "brainwashing" that most academic specialists reject as scientifically unsupported, while warning signs they identify — authoritarian leadership, isolation, financial exploitation — remain reference points in journalism and safeguarding practice. Some governments, notably France and China, maintain official lists or laws targeting groups deemed cultic, measures criticised by religious-freedom advocates.

Other uses

In popular culture, "cult" is positive: a cult film or cult following denotes a small, devoted fanbase. Scholars also use "cult" for devotional movements within mainstream religions without pejorative intent.
