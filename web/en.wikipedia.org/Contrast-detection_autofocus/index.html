Contrast-detection autofocus

Contrast-detection autofocus (CDAF) is a method of autofocus used in many digital cameras, particularly mirrorless cameras, compact cameras, and smartphones. Unlike phase-detection autofocus, which splits the incoming light and compares separate images, contrast detection works directly with the image sensor: the camera adjusts the lens focus position while measuring the contrast of the resulting image, continuing until the contrast reaches a maximum, which corresponds to the sharpest focus.

Operation

Because the system must test multiple focus positions, it works by "hunting" — moving the lens through its focus range and sampling contrast at each point. In general, image contrast is highest when adjacent pixels have clearly different brightness values, which occurs at the point of best focus. Microcontrast at fine detail is typically used as the measured parameter. The process can be slow, particularly with large-aperture lenses and in low light, and it cannot predict where a moving subject will be when the shutter fires, which is why tracking moving subjects is more difficult with CDAF than with phase detection.

Advantages and disadvantages

Contrast detection requires no separate autofocus sensor or calibration, so it avoids the front-focus and back-focus errors that can occur in phase-detection systems, and it can focus anywhere within the frame, not only at dedicated sensor points. Its performance has improved greatly with faster processors and focus algorithms: modern mirrorless cameras combine contrast detection with on-sensor phase detection in hybrid systems, and computational techniques such as depth-from-defocus are used in some smartphones to accelerate the search.
