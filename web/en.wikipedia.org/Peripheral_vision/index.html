Peripheral vision

Peripheral vision, or indirect vision, is vision as it occurs outside the point of fixation, i.e. away from the centre of gaze. The far peripheral visual field extends to roughly 200 degrees horizontally in humans, encompassing most of what the eyes sense.

Characteristics

Outside the fovea, the retina is dominated by rod photoreceptors and larger ganglion cells, so peripheral vision has poor visual acuity and colour discrimination but high sensitivity to motion and flicker and better function in dim light. Visual crowding also makes objects hard to identify when surrounded by clutter in the periphery.

Function

Peripheral vision detects motion and large-scale structure, guiding eye movements (saccades) that bring objects of interest onto the fovea. It is essential for spatial orientation, balance and locomotion.

Clinical aspects

Loss of peripheral vision produces tunnel vision, as in advanced glaucoma and retinitis pigmentosa. Conversely, loss of central vision occurs in macular degeneration. Perimetry measures the extent and sensitivity of the peripheral field.
