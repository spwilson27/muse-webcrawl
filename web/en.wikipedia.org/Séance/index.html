Séance

A 19th-century séance

A séance (from French séance, "session", from seoir, "to sit") is an attempt to communicate with spirits of the dead. Typically a group of people, led by a medium, sit around a table in a dimly lit room and try to receive messages from the deceased — through trance speech, automatic writing, table-turning, rapping sounds, or devices such as the Ouija (talking) board.

History

Séances became widespread with the rise of Spiritualism, conventionally dated to 1848 and the "Rochester knockings" heard by the Fox sisters in Hydesville, New York. The practice spread rapidly through the United States and Europe, attracting grieving families — especially after the American Civil War and the First World War — as well as eminent sitters such as Arthur Conan Doyle. Élisabeth d'Espérance, Eusapia Palladino and other physical mediums claimed to produce ectoplasm and materialisations.

Criticism and investigation

Investigators including the Society for Psychical Research documented both unexplained cases and extensive fraud: mediums were caught using hidden wires, phosphorescent paint, cheesecloth "ectoplasm" and confederates. The magician Harry Houdini devoted his later career to exposing fraudulent mediums, and Margaret Fox publicly confessed in 1888 that the Hydesville raps had been produced by cracking toe joints (she later retracted the confession). Scientists generally attribute séance phenomena to trickery, suggestion and the ideomotor effect.

In popular culture

The séance is a staple of horror fiction and film, and the word has become a general term for any session or sitting in some contexts.
