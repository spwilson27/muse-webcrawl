Soviet dissidents

Soviet dissidents were people in the Soviet Union who publicly disagreed with the policies and actions of their government and actively sought to expose abuses, promote human rights, or advocate political and national freedoms. The dissident movement emerged after the death of Joseph Stalin, especially following the Khrushchev Thaw, and continued until the dissolution of the USSR in 1991.

Emergence

The movement took shape in the 1960s. The trial of writers Andrei Sinyavsky and Yuli Daniel in 1966 provoked public protests and the beginnings of organised human-rights activism. Dissidents circulated uncensored literature in samizdat — typed and retyped manuscripts passed hand to hand — including the Chronicle of Current Events (1968–1982), which documented arrests, trials and psychiatric abuses.

Groups and figures

Prominent dissidents included the physicist Andrei Sakharov, who helped develop the Soviet hydrogen bomb before becoming the country's best-known human-rights campaigner; the writer Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn, whose The Gulag Archipelago exposed the Soviet camp system; and Natan Sharansky, a leading "refusenik" campaigner for Jewish emigration. Other strands of the movement included national movements in Ukraine, Lithuania, Georgia and the Baltic states, Crimean Tatar activists seeking return from exile, and religious believers resisting state atheism. The Moscow Helsinki Group, founded in 1976, monitored Soviet compliance with the Helsinki Accords.

Repression

The authorities responded with arrests, show trials, imprisonment in labour camps and special psychiatric hospitals (where dissent was treated as mental illness), internal exile — Sakharov was exiled to Gorky in 1980 — and forced emigration or expulsion, as in Solzhenitsyn's deportation in 1974. Under Mikhail Gorbachev's glasnost, political prisoners were released from 1987 and the movement's goals were largely absorbed into open politics.
