Natural transformation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In category theory, a branch of mathematics, a natural transformation provides a way of transforming one functor into another while respecting the internal structure (i.e., the composition of morphisms) of the involved categories. Indeed, Samuel Eilenberg and Saunders Mac Lane introduced categories and functors largely in order to give a precise setting for the informal notion of "natural" constructions, and natural transformations were the original motivation for the subject.

Definition

If F and G are functors between categories C and D, then a natural transformation η from F to G assigns to every object X in C a morphism ηX : F(X) → G(X) in D such that for every morphism f : X → Y in C, the square commutes: G(f) ∘ ηX = ηY ∘ F(f). The morphisms ηX are called the components of the transformation.

Natural isomorphism

If every component ηX is an isomorphism, the transformation is called a natural isomorphism, and the functors are said to be naturally isomorphic. Natural isomorphism is a stricter and more meaningful notion of sameness for functors than mere equivalence of the functors' values.

Examples

A standard example is the determinant map for vector spaces, which gives a natural transformation from the functor taking a vector space to its endomorphism algebra (via matrix representations) toward the multiplicative monoid of scalars; another is the canonical isomorphism between a finite-dimensional vector space and its double dual, which is natural, whereas isomorphism with the single dual is not.
