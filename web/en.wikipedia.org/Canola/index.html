Canola is a group of rapeseed cultivars bred in Canada in the 1970s to have low levels of erucic acid in the oil and low glucosinolates in the meal. The name is a contraction of "Canadian oil, low acid". Canola oil is now one of the most widely consumed vegetable oils in the world, and Canada is the largest producer and exporter of canola seed.

History

Traditional rapeseed oil contained high levels of erucic acid (up to 50%), which was associated with health concerns, and the seed meal was bitter and of limited feed value because of glucosinolates. Canadian plant breeders, notably Baldur Stefansson and Keith Downey, used conventional breeding to develop varieties meeting the canola standard: less than 2% erucic acid in the oil and less than 30 micromoles of glucosinolates per gram of meal. The name "canola" was trademarked by the Rapeseed Association of Canada in 1978.

Production and uses

Canola plants grow 1–1.8 metres tall with characteristic bright yellow flowers; the seed pods contain small black seeds with about 44% oil. Pressed oil is low in saturated fat (about 7%) and rich in monounsaturated fat, making it popular for cooking and food manufacturing. The protein-rich meal left after crushing is widely used as livestock feed. Canola is also grown for biodiesel and as a honey plant. Many modern cultivars are genetically modified for herbicide tolerance.
