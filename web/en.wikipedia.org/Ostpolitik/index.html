Ostpolitik

Ostpolitik (German for "eastern policy") was the foreign policy pursued by the West German government of Chancellor Willy Brandt from 1969, which normalized relations between the Federal Republic of Germany and the countries of Eastern Europe, including formal recognition of the existence of the German Democratic Republic (East Germany) and of the post-war borders of Poland.

Background

Under Konrad Adenauer, West Germany had followed the Hallstein Doctrine, refusing diplomatic relations with any state that recognized East Germany, and had not recognized the Oder–Neisse line as Poland's western border. Brandt, foreign minister from 1966 and chancellor from 1969, together with his adviser Egon Bahr (who coined the formula "change through rapprochement," Wandel durch Annäherung), sought to reduce Cold War tensions and increase practical contacts across the Iron Curtain.

Treaties and achievements

The policy produced the Treaty of Moscow (1970) with the Soviet Union, the Treaty of Warsaw (1970) with Poland — during the signing visit Brandt famously knelt at the memorial to the Warsaw Ghetto uprising, the Kniefall von Warschau — the Four Power Agreement on Berlin (1971), and the Basic Treaty (1972) between the two German states, which led to both joining the United Nations in 1973. Brandt received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1971. Critics, especially German expellee organizations, accused the policy of surrendering claims to lost territories, but Ostpolitik is widely credited with easing the division of Europe and laying groundwork that later made German reunification possible in 1990.
