A topological space is the most general type of mathematical space, one that allows the definitions of limit, continuity and connectedness to be made precise without presupposing a notion of distance. A topological space is a set X equipped with a topology — a collection of subsets of X, called open sets, satisfying three axioms. Topological spaces are the central object of study in topology and underlie most of modern analysis and geometry.

Definition

A topology on a set X is a collection τ of subsets of X such that: (1) the empty set and X itself belong to τ; (2) any union (finite or infinite) of members of τ belongs to τ; and (3) any finite intersection of members of τ belongs to τ. Members of τ are called open sets; their complements are closed sets. A function between topological spaces is continuous if the preimage of every open set is open.

Examples

The real line with its usual topology — generated by open intervals — is the archetype. Every metric space carries a natural topology whose open sets are unions of open balls; every manifold is a topological space. At the extremes, the indiscrete topology contains only the empty set and X, while the discrete topology contains every subset. Finite topological spaces, the Zariski topology of algebraic geometry, and product and quotient topologies show the breadth of the concept.

History

The axioms were distilled from the properties of open sets in Euclidean space in the early twentieth century, notably by Felix Hausdorff in his 1914 Grundzüge der Mengenlehre, with related formulations by Fréchet, Riesz and Kuratowski. Hausdorff's separation axiom (T2) singles out the especially well-behaved spaces that bear his name.
