Hohenberg–Kohn theorems


Electron density of a molecule — the central quantity of DFT

The Hohenberg–Kohn theorems are two foundational results of density functional theory (DFT), proved by Pierre Hohenberg and Walter Kohn in their 1964 paper "Inhomogeneous Electron Gas". They establish that the ground-state properties of a many-electron system are uniquely determined by its electron density, a function of only three spatial variables, rather than by the many-body wavefunction of 3N variables. Together with the Kohn–Sham equations (1965), they underpin one of the most widely used computational methods in physics, chemistry and materials science; Kohn received the 1998 Nobel Prize in Chemistry in part for this work.

First theorem

The first theorem states that the external potential (for electrons, the potential due to the nuclei) is determined, up to an additive constant, by the ground-state electron density n(r). The proof proceeds by contradiction using the variational principle: two different potentials cannot share the same ground-state density, since each would give a strictly lower energy for the other's ground state. Consequently every observable of the ground state — in particular the total energy — is a functional of the density.

Second theorem

The second theorem provides a variational principle for the energy functional E[n]: the ground-state energy is obtained by minimising E[n] over all densities that integrate to the correct number of electrons N, and the minimising density is the exact ground-state density. This parallels the variational principle for wavefunctions and justifies practical minimisation schemes. The original formulation was later generalised (Levy's constrained-search formulation, 1979) to make the universal functional rigorous for all N-representable densities.

Legacy

The theorems do not themselves give the form of the energy functional; the Kohn–Sham scheme reformulates the problem in terms of a fictitious non-interacting system with the same density, relegating ignorance into the exchange–correlation functional, which must be approximated (LDA, GGA, hybrids and beyond). Despite this, DFT based on the Hohenberg–Kohn foundations is the workhorse of computational materials design, catalysis, and electronic structure research.
