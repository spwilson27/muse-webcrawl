Foliot

The foliot is a type of regulator used in early mechanical clocks, part of the verge escapement mechanism that appeared in Europe in the late thirteenth century. It consists of a horizontal bar with two adjustable weights (called foliots) mounted on a vertical rod — the verge — which engages with a toothed crown wheel. As the crown wheel turns, it pushes the pallets of the verge first one way and then the other, causing the bar to oscillate back and forth.

Operation and adjustment

The period of oscillation of the foliot depends on the weights' distribution: sliding the two weights outward increases the moment of inertia and slows the clock; sliding them inward speeds it up. Because the foliot is not a true harmonic oscillator, its rate is sensitive to the driving force and to friction, and early verge-and-foliot clocks typically gained or lost as much as fifteen minutes a day.

Replacement

The foliot regulated clocks for nearly four centuries, appearing in tower clocks and early domestic clocks. It was replaced in the mid-seventeenth century by the much more accurate pendulum, applied to clocks by Christiaan Huygens in 1656, and by the balance wheel and hairspring in portable timepieces.
