Overhead power line

An overhead power line is a structure used in electric power transmission and distribution to transmit electrical energy across large distances. It consists of one or more uninsulated electrical cables (usually multiples of three for three-phase power) suspended by towers or poles.

Construction

Conductors are usually aluminium — often steel-reinforced (ACSR) — chosen for its conductivity and light weight. They hang from insulators made of glass, porcelain, or polymer, which are fixed to lattice steel towers, concrete pylons, or wooden utility poles. Transmission lines operate at high voltages (from tens of kilovolts up to more than 1,000 kV) to reduce resistive losses over distance, while local distribution lines use lower voltages.

Advantages and disadvantages

Overhead lines are far cheaper to build and easier to repair than underground cables, and air provides free insulation and cooling. However, they are vulnerable to wind, ice, lightning, and falling trees; they are visually intrusive; and contact with conductors is a major cause of electrocution and of wildfires in some regions. Sag between spans, governed by conductor weight, tension, and temperature, is a key engineering consideration.
