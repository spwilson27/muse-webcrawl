Bézout's identity

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In elementary number theory, Bézout's identity states that for integers a and b, with greatest common divisor d, there exist integers x and y such that ax + by = d. Moreover, the integers of the form ax + by are exactly the multiples of d. The x and y are called Bézout coefficients, and they can be computed with the extended Euclidean algorithm.

History

Although named for the French mathematician Étienne Bézout, who stated a polynomial generalization in 1779, the integer version was known earlier: it appears in the work of Claude Gaspard Bachet de Méziriac in the 17th century, and equivalent results occur in Indian mathematics, notably in Āryabhaṭa's method for solving linear indeterminate equations.

Consequences

Bézout's identity underlies Euclid's lemma and hence the fundamental theorem of arithmetic. It implies that a has a multiplicative inverse modulo n exactly when a and n are coprime, a fact used throughout cryptography; the coefficients are also used to find partial fraction decompositions and solutions of linear Diophantine equations. The identity generalizes to any principal ideal domain: in a polynomial ring over a field, two polynomials' gcd is a combination of them.

Example

For a = 12 and b = 42, gcd(12, 42) = 6, and one pair of coefficients is (−3, 1), since (−3)·12 + 1·42 = 6. Coefficients are not unique: adding multiples of b/d to x and subtracting the corresponding multiples of a/d from y yields further solutions.
