Structural equation modeling

Structural equation modeling (SEM) is a family of statistical techniques for testing and estimating models that relate observed variables to unobserved (latent) constructs and specify causal or associational relationships among them. It combines factor analysis and multiple regression / path analysis into a single framework, and is widely used in psychology, sociology, education, marketing, epidemiology and other social and behavioural sciences.

Components

A typical SEM has a measurement model, in which latent variables (such as "verbal ability" or "socioeconomic status") are inferred from multiple observed indicators, explicitly modelling measurement error; and a structural model, which specifies regressions among the latent (and observed) variables. Models are drawn as path diagrams — rectangles for observed variables, ovals for latent ones, arrows for hypothesised effects — and estimated, commonly by maximum likelihood, so that the covariance matrix implied by the model matches the sample covariance matrix as closely as possible.

Evaluation and software

Fit is assessed with indices such as the chi-square test, CFI, TLI, RMSEA and SRMR. SEM's roots run from Sewall Wright's path analysis in the 1920s through the LISREL program of Karl Jöreskog in the 1970s, which gave the method its name and popular form. Modern software includes lavaan (R), Mplus, AMOS, and OpenMx. Extensions cover multilevel models, growth curves, categorical indicators and Bayesian estimation. Critics caution that good fit does not prove a causal structure, since statistically equivalent models can imply different causal stories.
