In combinatorics, stars and bars is a graphical aid for deriving certain combinatorial theorems. It was popularised by William Feller in his classic book on probability. The technique can be used to solve counting problems such as how many ways there are to put n indistinguishable balls into k distinguishable bins.

Theorem

The number of ways to distribute n identical objects among k distinct groups — equivalently, the number of solutions in non-negative integers to x₁ + x₂ + ... + xk = n — is the binomial coefficient C(n + k − 1, k − 1).

Proof sketch

Represent any distribution by a row of n stars separated into k groups by k − 1 bars. Each arrangement of the n + k − 1 symbols corresponds to exactly one distribution: the stars before the first bar go to the first group, and so on. Counting the arrangements amounts to choosing the positions of the k − 1 bars among the total positions, giving C(n + k − 1, k − 1).

Variant

If every group must receive at least one object, the count of solutions in positive integers is C(n − 1, k − 1): place one star in each group first, then distribute the remainder freely. The method also yields the multiset coefficient, the number of ways to choose n items from k types with repetition.
