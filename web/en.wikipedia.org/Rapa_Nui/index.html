Rapa Nui

Rapa Nui (Spanish: Isla de Pascua; English: Easter Island) is a Chilean island in the southeastern Pacific Ocean, at the southeasternmost point of the Polynesian Triangle. It is famous for its nearly 1,000 monumental statues, called moai, created by the early Rapa Nui people between roughly 1250 and 1500 CE.

History

Polynesian voyagers settled the island sometime between 700 and 1200 CE. Dutch admiral Jacob Roggeveen reached it on Easter Sunday, 5 April 1722, giving it its European name. Chile annexed the island in 1888. The island's population collapsed in the 19th century through disease, slave raids, and emigration, falling to little more than a hundred people by 1877.

Geography

Rapa Nui is one of the most remote inhabited islands in the world, lying about 3,512 km west of mainland Chile and 2,075 km from the nearest inhabited island, Pitcairn. It is roughly triangular, formed by three extinct volcanoes, and covers about 163.6 km². The capital and only town is Hanga Roa.

Culture

The island is home to the rongorongo script, the only known indigenous writing system of Oceania, whose glyphs remain undeciphered. The annual Tapati festival celebrates Rapa Nui culture. Much of the island is protected within Rapa Nui National Park, a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
