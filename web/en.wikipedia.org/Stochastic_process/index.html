Stochastic process

In probability theory, a stochastic process (or random process) is a collection of random variables indexed by a set, often interpreted as time. Formally, given a probability space (Ω, ℱ, P) and a measurable state space S, a stochastic process is a family {Xt : t ∈ T} of S-valued random variables, where the index set T is typically the non-negative integers (discrete time) or the non-negative reals (continuous time).

Sample paths

For each fixed outcome ω, the function t ↦ Xt(ω) is called a sample path or realisation of the process. A stochastic process can thus be viewed as a random function. Two processes are modifications or versions of one another if they agree at each fixed time with probability one.

Important examples

  • Markov chains — processes whose future depends on the past only through the present state.
  • Poisson process — counts events occurring at a constant average rate with independent increments.
  • Wiener process (Brownian motion) — continuous-time process with independent, normally distributed increments; central to stochastic calculus and mathematical finance.
  • Martingales — processes whose expected future value, given the past, equals the current value.
  • Random walks — cumulative sums of independent random steps.
  • Gaussian processes — processes whose finite-dimensional distributions are multivariate normal.

Applications

Stochastic processes model phenomena that evolve randomly over time: stock prices and interest rates in finance, population dynamics in biology, queues and traffic in operations research, signal processing, and the thermal motion of particles in physics. The Kolmogorov extension theorem guarantees the existence of processes with prescribed finite-dimensional distributions.
