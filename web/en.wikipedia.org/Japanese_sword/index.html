Japanese sword

The Japanese sword (日本刀, nihontō) is a traditionally made bladed weapon of Japan, forged from tamahagane steel by folding and differential hardening. Types include the long katana and tachi, the short wakizashi, and the dagger tantō. Worn as a pair (daishō), the katana and wakizashi were the symbol of the samurai class.

Types: Katana, tachi, wakizashi, tantō, ōdachi

Material: Tamahagane steel

Features: Curved single-edged blade, hamon temper line

Construction

Swordsmiths smelt tamahagane in a tatara furnace, then repeatedly fold and forge-weld the steel to remove impurities and distribute carbon, creating a blade with a hard edge and a softer, resilient spine. Applying clay of varying thickness before quenching produces differential hardening and the visible wavy temper line, the hamon. The blade is polished through successive stones, signed on the tang (nakago) with the maker's name (mei), and fitted with mountings (koshirae) including a lacquered scabbard, silk-wrapped hilt and guard (tsuba).

History

Curved swords emerged in the Heian period; the Kamakura period is considered the golden age of the art, exemplified by Masamune. After the Meiji Restoration's 1876 ban on wearing swords, production declined until revived as a traditional craft. Swords are classified by period as kotō (old), shintō (new), and gendaitō (modern), and the finest are designated National Treasures of Japan.
