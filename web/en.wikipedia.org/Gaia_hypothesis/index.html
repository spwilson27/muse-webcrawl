Gaia hypothesis
Proposed byJames Lovelock (1970s), developed with Lynn Margulis
FieldEarth system science, geophysiology
Key workGaia: A New Look at Life on Earth (1979)
Core claimLiving organisms interact with their inorganic surroundings to form a self-regulating system that maintains conditions for life

The Gaia hypothesis, also known as the Gaia theory or Gaia principle, proposes that living organisms interact with their inorganic surroundings on Earth to form a synergistic and self-regulating complex system that helps to maintain and perpetuate the conditions for life on the planet. The hypothesis was formulated by the chemist James Lovelock in the 1970s, initially while he worked for NASA on methods of detecting life on Mars, and was developed with the microbiologist Lynn Margulis. It is named after Gaia, the primordial Greek goddess of the Earth, at the suggestion of novelist William Golding.

Overview

Lovelock observed that Earth's atmosphere — a far-from-equilibrium mixture of oxygen and methane — and its long-stable temperature and ocean salinity appeared to be regulated in ways that suggested biological feedback. In the "weak" form of the hypothesis, life simply influences the abiotic environment; in stronger forms, the biosphere actively maintains homeostasis favourable to itself. The computer model Daisyworld (1983), in which black and white daisies regulate a planet's temperature through their albedo, showed that planetary regulation could emerge from natural selection without foresight or purpose.

Evidence and mechanisms

Proposed Gaian mechanisms include the biological enhancement of rock weathering, which draws down carbon dioxide and stabilises climate over geological time; the production of cloud-condensation nuclei from marine dimethyl sulfide emitted by plankton (the CLAW hypothesis); and the regulation of atmospheric oxygen within bounds tolerable to life. The hypothesis helped stimulate the modern discipline of Earth system science and biogeochemistry.

Criticism and reception

Evolutionary biologists such as Richard Dawkins and Ford Doolittle objected that planetary self-regulation cannot arise by natural selection, since planets do not reproduce. Lovelock replied that selection "by persistence" and feedback processes suffice, and later restated the idea in terms of self-regulation rather than organismic teleology. The 2001 Amsterdam Declaration of the Earth-system science community endorsed a version of the concept, treating Earth as a single interacting system. Lovelock's later books, including The Revenge of Gaia (2006), warned that the same system could shift abruptly against human interests under climate change.
