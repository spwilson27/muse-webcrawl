Petal

Petals are modified leaves that surround the reproductive parts of flowers. They are often brightly coloured or unusually shaped to attract pollinators. Together, all of the petals of a flower are called the corolla, which together with the calyx (the sepals) forms the perianth.

Function

The principal function of petals is to attract animals — chiefly insects, but also birds and bats — that effect pollination. Petals advertise with colour (including ultraviolet patterns invisible to humans, such as nectar guides), scent, and rewards of nectar. In wind-pollinated plants such as grasses, petals are usually reduced or absent.

Structure and variation

Petals are typically thin and soft, with pigments such as anthocyanins (reds, blues, purples) and carotenoids (yellows, oranges). Their number is often characteristic of a group: monocots commonly have petals in multiples of three, eudicots in multiples of four or five. Petals may be separate (polypetalous) or fused into a tube or bell (sympetalous), as in petunias and foxgloves. In some flowers, such as magnolias, petals and sepals are indistinguishable and are called tepals.

Genetics

The identity of petals is specified by the ABC model of flower development: in the classic model, petals form where A-class and B-class gene activities overlap in the second whorl of the floral meristem.
