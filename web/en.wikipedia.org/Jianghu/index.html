Jianghu (Chinese: 江湖; pinyin: jiānghú, literally "rivers and lakes") is a concept in Chinese culture denoting a social world parallel to and apart from mainstream society — historically the milieu of wandering swordsmen, martial-arts sects, beggars' guilds, travelling performers, fortune-tellers and outlaws, and in modern usage any self-contained professional underworld or subculture with its own codes, including the criminal triads.

Origins

The phrase originally described the wild places beyond government reach where hermits and exiles withdrew; the Daoist philosopher Zhuangzi used "rivers and lakes" for the realm of freedom beyond court life. Over the Song and Ming dynasties it came to name the itinerant world of those who lived by their skills — martial artists, doctors, performers and peddlers — organised in guilds (bang) and martial sects (menpai) such as Shaolin and Wudang.

In wuxia fiction

In wuxia literature and film the jianghu is the stage on which xia (knights-errant) act according to the code of yi (righteousness): keeping one's word, avenging wrongs, and settling scores through duels. Novels by Jin Yong and Gu Long depict its shifting alliances, secret manuals and blood-feud cycles in rich detail. Retirement from this world is called "washing one's hands in the golden basin."

Modern usage

Today jianghu refers metaphorically to any circle with its own unwritten rules — show business, the martial-arts world, street life or organised crime. In Hong Kong crime films and in video games the term remains common, and the triad underworld is often called the jianghu directly.
