Positron emission tomography

Positron emission tomography (PET) is a functional imaging technique in nuclear medicine used to observe metabolic processes in the body as three-dimensional images. A radiotracer — a biologically active molecule labelled with a positron-emitting radionuclide — is introduced into the body, and the scanner detects pairs of gamma rays emitted indirectly when positrons annihilate with electrons.

Principle

The most common tracer is fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG), a glucose analogue labelled with fluorine-18 (half-life about 110 minutes, usually produced in a nearby cyclotron). Cells with high glucose uptake — notably most cancers — accumulate FDG. When F-18 decays, the emitted positron travels a short distance, annihilates with an electron, and produces two 511 keV photons travelling in nearly opposite directions. Coincidence detection of photon pairs localises the event along a line of response, from which tomographic images are reconstructed.

Applications

PET is most heavily used in oncology for detecting tumours, staging cancer, and monitoring treatment response. It is also used in neurology (for example, dementia work-up and epilepsy surgery planning, and amyloid imaging in Alzheimer's disease), cardiology (myocardial viability and perfusion), and research with tracers targeting specific receptors. Modern scanners almost always combine PET with CT (PET-CT) or MRI (PET-MRI) so functional images are registered to anatomy.

Limitations

PET has lower spatial resolution than CT or MRI (a few millimetres), involves ionising radiation, and is costly because of tracer production logistics.
