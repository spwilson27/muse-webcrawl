Begriffsschrift

Begriffsschrift (German for, roughly, "concept-writing" or "concept-script") is a book on logic by Gottlob Frege, published in 1879, and the formal system set out in that book. The full subtitle reads "a formula language, modeled on that of arithmetic, for pure thought" (eine der arithmetischen nachgebildete Formelsprache des reinen Denkens). It is widely regarded as one of the most important works in the history of logic, marking the birth of modern mathematical logic and predicate calculus.

Content

Frege's goal was to reduce arithmetic to logic (the program later called logicism) and to exhibit the inferential structure of proofs completely, eliminating gaps that appeal to intuition. To do so he invented a two-dimensional notation in which propositions are analysed not into subject and predicate, as in Aristotelian logic, but into function and argument.

The book introduces, among other things, the distinction between the content stroke and the judgment stroke; a notation for generality — the quantifier — using a concavity in the content stroke together with a Gothic (fraktur) letter; and a fully rigorous treatment of propositional connectives. Frege also defined the notion of a sequence following in a series (the ancestral relation), which allowed him to define mathematical induction within logic itself.

Notation

Unlike the linear notation of modern logic, Frege's symbols are arranged two-dimensionally: conditionals branch downward, and the negation is a small vertical tick beneath the content stroke. The notation, being typographically cumbersome, was little adopted, and Frege's work was initially overshadowed by the more convenient notation of Peano and later of Russell and Whitehead's Principia Mathematica. Nevertheless, essentially all of the conceptual apparatus of first-order logic appears in the Begriffsschrift.

Reception and influence

The work received a mixed reception at first; some reviewers, such as Ernst Schröder, criticized its notation. Its significance was recognized more fully in the twentieth century through the writings of Bertrand Russell, Ludwig Wittgenstein, and later scholars such as Michael Dummett and Jean van Heijenoort, who called 1879 "the most important single date in the history of logic" up to that point. The book founded the modern discipline of mathematical logic and, through the analytic tradition, much of twentieth-century philosophy of language and mathematics.
