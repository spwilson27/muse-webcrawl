Quince

KingdomPlantae
FamilyRosaceae
GenusCydonia
SpeciesC. oblonga

The quince (Cydonia oblonga) is the sole member of the genus Cydonia in the rose family, Rosaceae. It is a deciduous tree bearing a hard, aromatic, bright golden-yellow pome fruit, similar in appearance to a pear. The fruit is mostly too hard and astringent to eat raw and is usually cooked.

History

Quince is native to western Asia, including the Caucasus region. It was cultivated in ancient Mesopotamia and the Mediterranean world; in Greek mythology it was associated with Aphrodite, and some scholars believe the "golden apples" of myth and the fruit in the Song of Songs were quinces rather than apples. It was grown in medieval gardens across Europe and carried to the Americas by colonists.

Culinary uses

Cooking transforms the flesh to a soft, pink, fragrant state. Quince is made into jams and jellies (its high pectin content helps setting), the Spanish membrillo paste served with cheese, Middle Eastern stews, and liqueurs such as ratafia. The English word "marmalade" derives from the Portuguese marmelo, meaning quince.

Cultivation

Quince trees are hardy, self-fertile, and valued as ornamentals for their pale pink blossoms. Quince rootstocks are widely used to dwarf pear trees in orchards.
