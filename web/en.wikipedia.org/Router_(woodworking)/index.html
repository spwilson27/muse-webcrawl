Router (woodworking)


A modern electric plunge router

A router is a power tool used in woodworking to rout out (hollow out) an area in a workpiece, most commonly wood or plastic. Routers consist of a motor driving a collet that grips a rotating cutting bit, mounted in a base that controls the depth of cut. They are among the most versatile tools in a workshop, used for shaping edges, cutting joinery, carving, and making mouldings.

Types

Handheld routers come in two principal forms. A fixed-base router has the motor locked at a set depth in its base and is well suited to edge work. A plunge router mounts the motor on spring-loaded posts so the bit can be lowered into the work from above, which is essential for stopped cuts and mortises. Many modern kits combine one motor with interchangeable fixed and plunge bases. Routers are also mounted upside-down in a router table, turning the tool into a small spindle moulder, and large industrial versions form the basis of CNC routers.

Smaller, lighter versions called laminate trimmers or palm routers were developed for trimming plastic laminates but are now popular for light joinery and edge profiling.

Bits and operation

Router bits are made with steel bodies and carbide cutting edges, in shank diameters of 6 mm, 8 mm, 1⁄4 inch and 1⁄2 inch. Profiles range from simple round-overs, chamfers and rabbets to elaborate ogees, raised-panel cutters and spiral flush-trim bits. Many edge-forming bits carry a ball-bearing pilot that rides against the workpiece or a template to control the cut.

Typical spindle speeds are 8,000 to 24,000 revolutions per minute, often adjustable electronically to suit large-diameter cutters. Because the bit rotates at high speed, work is usually fed against the direction of rotation, and guides such as fences, template bushings and bearings keep cuts accurate.

History

Before power routers, the same work was done with a hand tool called a router plane. The electric router was patented in the early twentieth century and was popularised after the Second World War, when manufacturers such as Elu introduced the plunge router in Europe. The tool transformed cabinetmaking by making decorative mouldings and precise joinery fast and repeatable.

Uses

  • Profiling edges of boards and tabletops
  • Cutting grooves, dadoes, rabbets and dovetail joints
  • Template and pattern routing
  • Cutting circles, inlays and signs
  • Hollowing mortises for locks and hinges
