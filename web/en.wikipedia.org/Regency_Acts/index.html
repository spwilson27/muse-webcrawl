Regency Acts

The Regency Acts are a series of Acts of the Parliament of the United Kingdom that provide for a regent to exercise the royal functions when the monarch is under the age of eighteen or is incapacitated. The principal legislation currently in force is the Regency Act 1937, amended in 1943 and 1953.

Provisions

Under the 1937 Act, the regent is the next person in the line of succession who is over twenty-one (later eighteen), a British subject, and domiciled in the United Kingdom. Incapacity is declared by specified officials, including the sovereign's spouse and the Lord Chancellor, supported by medical evidence. The Act also established the office of Counsellors of State, who may carry out limited royal functions during the sovereign's absence or illness.

History

Earlier regency legislation was usually passed for specific contingencies, such as the minority of Edward VI or the illness of George III, which produced the Regency of 1811–1820 under the Prince Regent. The 1937 Act was the first general, standing provision.
