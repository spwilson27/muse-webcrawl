Doublethink

Doublethink is a process of indoctrination in which subjects are expected to simultaneously accept two conflicting beliefs as truth, often at the expense of their own memories or sense of reality. The term was coined by George Orwell in his 1949 dystopian novel Nineteen Eighty-Four, where it is a central mechanism of the ruling Party's control in the superstate of Oceania.

In Nineteen Eighty-Four

In the novel's appendix on Newspeak, doublethink is described as being able "to hold simultaneously two opinions which cancelled out, knowing them to be contradictory and believing in both of them". The Party's slogans embody it: War is Peace, Freedom is Slavery, Ignorance is Strength. Total loyalty requires the ability to accept that the Party's account of history has always been correct even when it changes from day to day — so that, when Oceania switches its alliance mid-speech during Hate Week, the crowd instantly accepts that the new enemy has always been the enemy.

Through doublethink, Party members exercise control over their own memories ("reality control"), accept whatever the Party says, and become incapable of independent or critical thought. The protagonist Winston Smith's job at the Ministry of Truth — rewriting historical records — both requires doublethink and demonstrates its operation.

Related concepts

Psychologists have compared doublethink with cognitive dissonance, although doublethink differs in that the subject deliberately holds the contradictory beliefs without experiencing discomfort or resolution. It is also related to Orwellian concepts of Newspeak, thoughtcrime, and crimestop — the faculty of stopping short of dangerous thoughts.

Modern usage

The word has entered ordinary English usage to describe the acceptance of contradictory claims from governments, media, or institutions, or the mental habits by which people endorse incompatible positions for political convenience.
