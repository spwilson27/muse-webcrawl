Emma Goldman (June 27, 1869 – May 14, 1940) was a Russian-born anarchist revolutionary, political activist, and writer. She played a pivotal role in the development of anarchist political philosophy in North America and Europe in the first half of the 20th century.

Life

Born in Kovno in the Russian Empire (now Kaunas, Lithuania), to a Lithuanian Jewish family, Goldman emigrated to the United States in 1885. Attracted to anarchism after the Haymarket affair, she became a writer and a renowned lecturer on anarchist philosophy, women's rights, and social issues, attracting crowds of thousands. She and anarchist writer Alexander Berkman, her lover and lifelong friend, planned to assassinate industrialist and plant manager Henry Clay Frick as an act of propaganda of the deed during the Homestead strike; Frick survived the attempt, and Berkman was sentenced to 22 years in prison.

Activism and exile

Goldman was imprisoned several times in the years that followed, for "inciting to riot" and illegally distributing information about birth control. She founded the anarchist journal Mother Earth in 1906. In 1917, she and Berkman were sentenced to two years in jail for conspiring to "induce persons not to register" for the newly instated draft. After their release, they were arrested—along with 248 others—in the Palmer Raids and deported to Russia.

Initially supportive of that country's October Revolution, Goldman's views changed after the Kronstadt rebellion; she denounced the Soviet Union for its violent repression of independent voices. Her book My Disillusionment in Russia (1923) described her experiences. She later lived in England, Canada, and France, wrote her autobiography Living My Life (1931), and participated in the Spanish Civil War on the side of the anarchists.
