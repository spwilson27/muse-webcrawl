The Cape Colony (Dutch: Kaapkolonie) was a European colony at the southern tip of Africa, centred on Cape Town. Founded by the Dutch East India Company in 1652 as a refreshment station for ships sailing to the East Indies, it was conquered by Britain during the Napoleonic Wars and confirmed as a British colony in 1814. In 1910 it joined the Union of South Africa as the Cape Province.

Dutch period

Jan van Riebeeck established the settlement in 1652. Free burgher farmers spread inland, clashing with the indigenous Khoisan peoples, while slaves were imported from Indonesia, Madagascar, India, and Mozambique. Dutch settlers' descendants, the Boers (later Afrikaners), trekked ever further from Cape Town's authority; frontier wars with the Xhosa began in the east in the late 18th century.

British period

Britain seized the colony in 1795, returned it to the Netherlands in 1803, and retook it in 1806. British rule brought the abolition of slavery (1834), which, together with grievances over land and administration, prompted the Great Trek of Boer emigrants into the interior from 1836. The discovery of diamonds at Kimberley (1867) and gold on the Witwatersrand (1886, in the Transvaal) transformed the region's economy and politics. Under Cecil Rhodes, prime minister from 1890 to 1896, the colony expanded northward. After the Second Boer War (1899–1902), the Cape Colony joined the Orange River Colony, Transvaal, and Natal in forming the Union of South Africa in 1910.
