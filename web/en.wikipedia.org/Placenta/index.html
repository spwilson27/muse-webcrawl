Placenta

The placenta is a temporary embryonic and later fetal organ that begins developing from the blastocyst shortly after implantation. It plays critical roles in facilitating nutrient, gas and waste exchange between the physically separate maternal and fetal circulations, and is an important endocrine organ, producing hormones that regulate both maternal and fetal physiology during pregnancy. In humans the placenta is about 22 cm in diameter and weighs approximately 500 grams at term; it connects to the fetus by an umbilical cord of approximately 55–60 cm in length.

Structure and development

The placenta develops from the trophoblast of the implanting blastocyst together with maternal endometrial tissue (the decidua). Chorionic villi extend into maternal blood-filled lacunae, giving a large surface area for exchange while the maternal and fetal bloodstreams remain separate. The placental barrier thins as pregnancy advances.

Functions

The placenta transfers oxygen and nutrients (glucose, amino acids, lipids, vitamins, ions) to the fetus and removes carbon dioxide and waste products. It secretes hCG, progesterone, estrogens, and human placental lactogen, and modulates the maternal immune system so the genetically distinct fetus is not rejected.

Disorders

Placental disorders include placenta previa, placental abruption, placenta accreta spectrum, and preeclampsia, which is linked to abnormal placental development. The placenta is expelled in the third stage of labour (the "afterbirth"). Placentophagy — consumption of the placenta — is practised by most placental mammals and by some humans.
