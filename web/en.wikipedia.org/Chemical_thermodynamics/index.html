Chemical thermodynamics

Chemical thermodynamics
FieldPhysical chemistry
Key quantitiesEnthalpy, entropy, Gibbs energy
FoundersJ. Willard Gibbs, Hermann von Helmholtz

Chemical thermodynamics is the study of the interrelation of heat and work with chemical reactions and physical changes of state, applying the laws of thermodynamics to chemical systems. It predicts whether a reaction can occur spontaneously, how far it will proceed at equilibrium, and how much energy it will release or absorb.

Core concepts

The first law states that energy is conserved; the change in internal energy equals heat added minus work done. Enthalpy (H) measures heat exchanged at constant pressure. The second law introduces entropy (S), a measure of energy dispersal, which increases in the universe for any spontaneous process. J. Willard Gibbs combined these into Gibbs free energy, G = H − TS: at constant temperature and pressure a process is spontaneous when ΔG is negative, and ΔG determines the maximum non-expansion work obtainable. Chemical potential extends the analysis to mixtures and phase equilibrium.

Applications

Chemical thermodynamics underpins the van 't Hoff equation for temperature dependence of equilibrium constants, Hess's law for reaction enthalpies, Born–Haber cycles for lattice energies, and the design of industrial processes such as ammonia synthesis. Bridgman's and later formulations link thermodynamics with statistical mechanics, explaining macroscopic potentials through molecular partition functions.
