Mahmud II (Ottoman Turkish: محمود ثانى; 20 July 1785 – 1 July 1839) was the 30th Sultan of the Ottoman Empire, reigning from 1808 until his death in 1839. Often described as "Peter the Great of Turkey", he carried out sweeping administrative, military and fiscal reforms that laid the groundwork for the Tanzimat era and the modernisation of the empire.

Mahmud II after his reforms, depicted in European-style uniform.

Accession and early reign

Mahmud came to the throne during the upheavals of 1808, escaping assassination when his brother Mustafa IV ordered the princes killed; he was installed after Mustafa's deposition and the brief rule of the reformist grand vizier Alemdar Mustafa Pasha. He survived by patience and gradual consolidation of power.

Reforms

In 1826 Mahmud abolished the Janissary corps in the "Auspicious Incident", having the corps destroyed when it mutinied, and replaced it with a modern army, the Asakir-i Mansure-i Muhammediye. He centralised government in ministries on the European model, reformed provincial administration, founded new schools and a military medical academy, introduced a postal service and censuses, and in 1831 launched the official gazette Takvim-i Vekayi. His clothing reforms required civil servants to wear the fez and European-style dress.

Foreign crises

His reign saw the Greek War of Independence (1821–29), the Russo-Turkish War of 1828–29, and two wars with his rebellious Egyptian vassal Muhammad Ali, whose forces twice threatened Anatolia and forced the sultan to seek Russian protection under the Treaty of Hünkâr İskelesi (1833). Mahmud died of tuberculosis in 1839, on the eve of the second Egyptian crisis's resolution, and was succeeded by his son Abdulmejid I.
