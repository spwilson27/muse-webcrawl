Worse is better

Worse is better is a term coined by Richard P. Gabriel in a 1989 essay (later published as part of "Lisp: Good News, Bad News, How to Win Big") to describe the hypothesis that software quality does not necessarily increase with functionality: that there is a point where less functionality ("worse") is a preferable option ("better") in terms of practicality and usability.

The New Jersey style

Gabriel contrasted two design philosophies. The "MIT approach", or "the right thing", prioritises correctness, consistency, and completeness of the interface. The "New Jersey style" (also called "worse is better"), exemplified by Unix and the C programming language, prioritises simplicity of implementation, even at the cost of correctness and consistency. Gabriel argued that worse-is-better systems spread more successfully because they are simpler to implement, port, and learn, and can afterwards be improved.

Characteristics

Gabriel listed four characteristics of worse-is-better design: simplicity (above all), correctness (where it can be achieved without sacrificing simplicity), consistency (where it does not compromise simplicity), and completeness (only where it does not compromise the other three).

Influence

The essay became a widely cited text in software engineering culture and is frequently invoked in discussions of Unix philosophy, programming language adoption, and minimum viable products.
