Observer effect (psychology)

In psychology, the observer effect refers to changes in a subject's behaviour that occur because the subject is aware of being observed. It is closely related to the Hawthorne effect, in which individuals modify an aspect of their behaviour in response to their awareness of being watched, and to reactivity in research methodology generally.

Forms

The effect may appear as increased effort or socially desirable behaviour when participants know they are being studied, or as self-consciousness that degrades performance on tasks requiring automatic processing. In clinical settings, patients may report or display symptoms differently during direct observation than in ordinary life.

Relation to other effects

The observer effect overlaps with the experimenter effect, where a researcher's expectations influence outcomes, and with demand characteristics, cues that reveal the purpose of a study and lead participants to behave accordingly. It is distinct from the observer effect in physics, in which the act of measurement physically disturbs the system measured.

Mitigation

Researchers reduce observer effects through unobtrusive observation, habituation periods, naturalistic settings, and concealing the timing or fact of observation where ethically permissible. Archival records and behavioural traces that were not produced for an audience are also used to circumvent reactivity.
