Jurisdiction

[Image: A courtroom, the seat of a court's jurisdiction]

Jurisdiction (from Latin juris, "law" + dictio, "declaration") is the practical authority granted to a legal body to administer justice, as defined by the kind of case and the location of the issue. In federations like the United States, areas of jurisdiction apply to local, state, and federal levels.

Types

Subject-matter jurisdiction is the authority of a court to hear cases of a particular type, such as criminal, civil, family or tax matters. Territorial jurisdiction (or personal jurisdiction over events) concerns the geographical area within which a court's authority applies. Personal jurisdiction (in personam) is power over the parties to a lawsuit, while in rem jurisdiction concerns property within the territory.

Original jurisdiction is the power to hear a case for the first time, as opposed to appellate jurisdiction, the power to review decisions of lower courts. Courts may hold exclusive jurisdiction over certain matters, or concurrent jurisdiction shared with other courts.

International law

In public international law, states exercise jurisdiction on the bases of territoriality, nationality of the offender or victim, the protective principle, and universality for certain crimes such as piracy and genocide. Extraterritorial jurisdiction, and conflicts between states' claims, are governed by treaties and principles of comity.

Jurisdiction as a territory

The term is also used loosely for the geographical area itself — for example, a country, state, county or municipality may be referred to as a "jurisdiction".
