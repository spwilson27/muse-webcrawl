Monument

A monument is a type of structure that was explicitly created to commemorate a person or event, or which has become relevant to a social group as a part of their remembrance of historic times or cultural heritage, due to its artistic, historical, political, technical or architectural importance. The word derives from the Latin monumentum, "a monument, memorial," from monere, "to remind."

Examples of monuments include statues, war memorials, historical buildings, archaeological sites, cultural assets, and entire structures built for symbolic reasons, such as pyramids, triumphal arches, and obelisks. Many monuments are protected as heritage: in many countries the term is also a legal designation for protected historic structures ("historic monuments").

Functions

Monuments serve to preserve memory, to mark triumphs or grief, to legitimate rulers and states, and to create focal points for public ritual. War memorials, from the Arc de Triomphe in Paris to local village cenotaphs, commemorate the dead of conflicts. Monuments to individuals — equestrian statues of kings, the Lincoln Memorial in Washington — celebrate exemplary lives. Monuments can also mark achievements of science and exploration, or simply give identity to a place, as the Statue of Liberty does for New York.

History

Some of the oldest surviving human structures are monuments: the megaliths of the Neolithic, the pyramids of Egypt, and the ziggurats of Mesopotamia served funerary and religious purposes that remain partly debated. Greek and Roman practice added honorary columns, triumphal arches, and public statuary. Medieval monuments were predominantly religious. From the Renaissance onward, and especially with the rise of nation-states in the 19th century, monumental building proliferated as a vehicle of national identity.

Protection and controversy

International instruments such as the 1972 UNESCO World Heritage Convention and national heritage laws protect monuments of outstanding value. At the same time, monuments are contested: statues celebrating colonialists or Confederate leaders have been removed or debated worldwide in recent decades, reflecting changing judgements about whom public memory should honour. Some monuments become protected natural or architectural heritage and are maintained by conservation bodies even when their original ideology is rejected.
