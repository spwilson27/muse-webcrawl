Consort of instruments

A consort of instruments is a phrase used to indicate a musical ensemble, a group of musical instruments, playing together. It is used in the context of the music of the Renaissance and early Baroque periods, particularly in England. A musical composition played by such an ensemble is also described as a piece "for consort of instruments".

Whole and broken consorts

A whole consort is made up of instruments of the same family — for example a set (or "chest") of viols in treble, tenor and bass sizes, or a consort of recorders. A broken consort mixes instruments from different families; the term is usually associated with the English combination of viols, flutes or recorders, lute, cittern, and bandora exemplified by Thomas Morley's First Booke of Consort Lessons (1599).

History

Consorts were a fixture of courtly and civic music-making in the 16th and early 17th centuries. Composers such as William Byrd, John Dowland, Anthony Holborne, and Orlando Gibbons wrote fantasias, pavans, galliards, and In Nomines for viol consort. The consort tradition was gradually displaced by the Baroque orchestra and the string quartet, but has been revived by the modern early-music movement.

Consort songs and dances

The consort song — a solo voice accompanied by a consort of viols — was a distinctive English genre associated with the choirboy theatre companies. Broken consorts also accompanied dances and masques at court.
