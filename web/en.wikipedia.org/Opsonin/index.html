Opsonin

An opsonin is any molecule that enhances phagocytosis by marking an antigen for an immune response, in a process called opsonization. Opsonins coat the surface of pathogens such as bacteria, making them far more readily recognized and ingested by phagocytic cells including neutrophils and macrophages. The word derives from the Greek opsōnein, meaning "to prepare for eating".


Opsonization of a bacterium

Types

The principal opsonins are antibodies of the IgG class and components of the complement system, notably C3b and its fragment iC3b. Mannose-binding lectin and other collectins, C-reactive protein, and fibronectin can also act as opsonins. Each bridges the pathogen surface to specific receptors on the phagocyte: IgG binds Fc receptors, while C3b binds complement receptors such as CR1 and CR3.

Mechanism

When an antibody binds an antigen on a microbial surface, its exposed Fc region is recognized by Fc receptors on phagocytes, triggering engulfment. Complement deposited through the classical, lectin, or alternative pathways leaves C3b covalently attached to the target, similarly tagging it for uptake. Opsonization can increase the efficiency of phagocytosis by orders of magnitude and is essential for defense against encapsulated bacteria, whose capsules otherwise resist ingestion.

Clinical significance

Defects in opsonization — from antibody deficiency, complement deficiency, or splenectomy (the spleen being a major site of opsonin-mediated clearance) — predispose patients to severe infections with encapsulated organisms such as Streptococcus pneumoniae and Haemophilus influenzae. The concept of opsonins grew out of the work of Almroth Wright and Stewart Douglas in 1903, who showed that blood serum contains factors that prepare bacteria for phagocytosis.
