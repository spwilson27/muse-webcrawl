Tanzimat

The reading of the Hatt-ı Şerif of Gülhane in 1839, inaugurating the Tanzimat reforms.

The Tanzimat (Ottoman Turkish: تنظيمات, "reorganisation") was a period of reform in the Ottoman Empire that began with the Edict of Gülhane in 1839 and concluded with the First Constitutional Era in 1876. Driven by reforming statesmen such as Mustafa Reşid Pasha, Mehmed Emin Âli Pasha and Fuad Pasha under sultans Abdülmecid I and Abdülaziz, the Tanzimat sought to modernise the empire, centralise administration, and bind its diverse subjects together through a new civic identity — Ottomanism — so as to halt decline and check European interference and separatist nationalism.

Main reforms

The Gülhane Edict (1839) promised all subjects security of life, honour and property, regular taxation (ending tax farming), and regular conscription. The Reform Edict of 1856 (Islahat Fermanı), issued under European pressure during the Crimean War, proclaimed the legal equality of Muslim and non-Muslim subjects, abolishing the jizya (replaced by a military exemption tax) and opening civil service and schools to all. Subsequent measures included:

  • A reorganised provincial administration under the Vilayet Law of 1864, pioneered by Midhat Pasha in the Danube Vilayet.
  • New secular law codes modelled partly on French codes: a commercial code (1850), penal codes, and the Mecelle (1869–1876), a codification of Hanafi civil law.
  • Mixed commercial courts and later the secular Nizamiye court system.
  • A modernised, conscript army; new schools (the rüşdiye middle schools and Galatasaray lycée, 1868); a central bank (Bank-ı Osmanî, 1856/1863); telegraph lines, railways and an official gazette.
  • The Ottoman nationality law of 1869 and, in 1876, the empire's first constitution (Kanun-ı Esasi) and parliament.

Assessment

The Tanzimat transformed the Ottoman state from a patrimonial empire into a recognisably modern bureaucracy and created the educated cadre that later led the Young Turks and the Turkish Republic. Its promises of equality fell short in practice: non-Muslim elites resented conscription-era changes, Muslim opinion often viewed equality edicts as foreign impositions, and capitulations and mounting foreign debt (culminating in the 1875 default and the Ottoman Public Debt Administration) deepened European penetration. Nonetheless, the era's legal, educational and administrative frameworks reshaped every successor state in the Balkans and the Middle East.
