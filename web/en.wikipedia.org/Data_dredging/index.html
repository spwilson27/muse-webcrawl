Data dredging

Testing enough hypotheses guarantees spurious "significant" results

Data dredging (also known as data fishing, data snooping, or p-hacking) is the misuse of data analysis to find patterns in data that can be presented as statistically significant, thus greatly increasing the risk of false positives. It typically involves performing many statistical tests on a dataset and reporting only those that come back significant, without a prior hypothesis and without correcting for the number of tests performed.

The problem

If a researcher tests 20 independent hypotheses at a significance level of 0.05, on average one will appear significant by chance alone. Data dredging exploits this multiple-comparisons problem: by searching a large dataset for any correlation — between foods and diseases, genes and traits, or jelly bean colours and acne, in one famous demonstration — spurious findings are inevitable. When the exploratory search is not disclosed, the reported result masquerades as a confirmed hypothesis, a practice sometimes called HARKing (hypothesising after the results are known).

Relation to the replication crisis

P-hacking and related questionable research practices are considered major contributors to the replication crisis in psychology, medicine and other fields. Closely related practices include selective reporting of outcome measures, optional stopping of data collection, and excluding inconvenient data points.

Remedies

Proposed safeguards include statistical corrections for multiple comparisons (such as the Bonferroni correction and false discovery rate control), preregistration of hypotheses and analysis plans, registered reports, disclosure of all measures and conditions, and validation of exploratory findings on fresh data.
