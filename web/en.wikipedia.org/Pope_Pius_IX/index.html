Pius IX, the longest-reigning elected pope

Pope Pius IX (Italian: Pio IX; born Giovanni Maria Mastai Ferretti, 13 May 1792 – 7 February 1878) was head of the Catholic Church from 1846 to 1878 — the longest verified reign of any pope at over 31 years. His pontificate saw the definition of the dogma of the Immaculate Conception, the Syllabus of Errors, the First Vatican Council's definition of papal infallibility, and the loss of the Papal States to the Kingdom of Italy.

From liberal hope to conservative pope

Elected as a relative liberal, Pius began with amnesties and reforms that made him briefly a hero of Italian nationalists. The revolutions of 1848 changed everything: his prime minister was assassinated, Rome proclaimed a republic, and the pope fled to Gaeta. Restored by French troops in 1850, he turned decisively against liberalism and nationalism, later condemning modern errors in the encyclical Quanta cura and its attached Syllabus (1864).

Doctrine and Vatican I

In 1854 Pius proclaimed the dogma of the Immaculate Conception of Mary in Ineffabilis Deus. He convened the First Vatican Council in 1869, which in Pastor aeternus (1870) defined papal infallibility when speaking ex cathedra. The council was suspended when Italian troops captured Rome in September 1870, ending the thousand-year Papal States.

"Prisoner of the Vatican"

Pius refused to recognise the Italian annexation of Rome, rejected the Law of Guarantees, and styled himself a "prisoner of the Vatican", inaugurating the Roman Question that lasted until the Lateran Treaty of 1929. Despite the loss of temporal power, his reign strengthened the centralised, devotional character of modern Catholicism: Marian devotion grew, religious congregations expanded, and missions spread worldwide. He was beatified by John Paul II in 2000.
