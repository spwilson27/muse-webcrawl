Turtles all the way down

The phrase evokes a world supported by a flat earth resting on a tower of turtles, each standing on another, infinitely.

"Turtles all the way down" is an expression of the problem of infinite regress. The saying reflects the idea of a mythical cosmology in which the flat Earth is supported on the back of a giant World Turtle, which itself stands on a still larger turtle — and so on, in an endless tower. The phrase is used as a humorous or critical illustration of attempts to explain or justify something by appeal to a foundation that itself needs the same explanation, forever.

Origins and retellings

The image draws loosely on world-turtle myths found in Hindu, Chinese and some Native American traditions, though the exact infinite-tower anecdote is Western. Versions of the story have been attributed to many figures: the philosopher William James used a version involving an old woman and a lecturer in the 19th century; Bertrand Russell was cast as the lecturer in a popular variant. Stephen Hawking opened his 1988 book A Brief History of Time with the best-known modern retelling: after a lecture on astronomy, an elderly lady tells the scientist that the world is really a flat plate supported on the back of a giant tortoise; asked what the tortoise stands on, she replies, "You're very clever, young man, very clever. But it's turtles all the way down!"

Use

The expression is invoked in discussions of epistemology and metaphysics — about foundationalism and the Münchhausen trilemma — in cosmology, and in computer science, where "all the way down" describes layers of abstraction. It is also the title of John Green's 2017 novel Turtles All the Way Down, about a teenager living with obsessive–compulsive disorder, whose spiralling thoughts the phrase is used to evoke.
