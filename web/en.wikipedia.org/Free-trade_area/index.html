Free-trade area

International trade within a free-trade area

A free-trade area is a region encompassing a group of countries that have signed a free-trade agreement (FTA) eliminating tariffs, import quotas, and preferences on most (if not all) goods and services traded between them. Unlike a customs union, members of a free-trade area retain their own independent trade policies toward non-member countries.

Characteristics

Because members keep separate external tariffs, goods from outside could in principle enter through the member with the lowest tariff — a problem known as trade deflection. Free-trade agreements therefore include rules of origin, which specify how much of a product must be made within the area to qualify for duty-free treatment, and require certificates of origin at internal borders.

Examples

  • The United States–Mexico–Canada Agreement (USMCA), formerly NAFTA
  • The European Free Trade Association (EFTA)
  • The ASEAN Free Trade Area (AFTA)
  • The Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership (CPTPP)
  • The African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA)

Stages of integration

In Béla Balassa's classic typology, a free-trade area is the second stage of economic integration, after a preferential trade area and before a customs union, common market, and economic union. Free-trade agreements are permitted under GATT Article XXIV of the World Trade Organization, provided they cover substantially all trade and do not raise barriers against outsiders.
