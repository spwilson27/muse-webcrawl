Adam de la Halle (c. 1240/50 – c. 1287), also known as Adam le Bossu ("Adam the Hunchback"), was a French trouvère, poet and musician from Arras. He was among the first composers of secular polyphonic music in the vernacular and is best known for the Jeu de Robin et Marion (c. 1282–83), often described as one of the earliest secular musical plays with a fully notated score.

Life

Adam was educated in Arras, a thriving centre of urban culture, and may have studied at the Cistercian abbey of Vaucelles and in Paris. He served the counts of Artois and followed Charles of Anjou to Naples, where his works were performed at the Angevin court and where he probably died. His father, Henri, was a well-known citizen of Arras; Adam's nickname "le Bossu" was a family name rather than a description.

Works

Adam's output survives almost complete in a single manuscript (the so-called La Vallière chansonnier). It includes 36 chansons, 46 rondets de carole, 18 jeux-partis, motets, and two theatrical works: the satirical Jeu de la feuillée ("Play of the Bower", c. 1276), set in Arras, and the pastoral Jeu de Robin et Marion, built around the popular story of the shepherdess Marion and the knight's attempted seduction, interspersed with lively songs and dances.

Significance

Adam was one of the last and most versatile trouvères, uniting the monophonic courtly chanson with learned polyphony and drama. The completeness of his surviving oeuvre makes him a central figure in the study of 13th-century French music.
