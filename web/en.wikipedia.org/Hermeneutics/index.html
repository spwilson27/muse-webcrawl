Hermeneutics

Hermeneutics is the theory and methodology of interpretation, especially the interpretation of biblical texts, wisdom literature, and philosophical texts. Hermeneutics is more than interpretative principles or methods used when immediate comprehension fails and includes the art of understanding and communication. Modern hermeneutics includes both verbal and non-verbal communication, as well as semiotics, presuppositions, and pre-understandings.

Etymology

The term derives from the Greek hermēneutikḗ, "interpretive", traditionally linked to Hermes, the messenger god who made the messages of the gods intelligible to humans. The related art of exegesis focuses on the careful, objective interpretation of a text, while hermeneutics concerns the broader theory that makes such interpretation possible.

The hermeneutic circle

A central idea is the hermeneutic circle: understanding a text as a whole depends on understanding its parts, and understanding each part depends on some grasp of the whole. Interpretation therefore proceeds in a circular movement of revision, in which the reader's preliminary understanding is corrected by closer attention to the text. Martin Heidegger reinterpreted the circle not as a logical flaw but as a positive structure of understanding itself, rooted in the interpreter's prior involvement ("fore-understanding").

History

Classical and medieval

Allegorical interpretation of Homer and of Scripture developed in antiquity, and medieval Christian exegesis elaborated a fourfold sense of Scripture: literal, allegorical, tropological (moral), and anagogical. Rabbinic Judaism likewise developed rich interpretive traditions for the Torah.

Modern hermeneutics

Friedrich Schleiermacher universalized hermeneutics into a general art of understanding any utterance, emphasizing both grammatical and psychological interpretation. Wilhelm Dilthey made it the methodological foundation of the human sciences (Geisteswissenschaften), distinguishing understanding (Verstehen) from the causal explanation used in the natural sciences.

Philosophical hermeneutics

Hans-Georg Gadamer's Truth and Method (1960) argued that interpretation is always shaped by the interpreter's historically effected consciousness; understanding a text involves a "fusion of horizons" between past and present. Paul Ricoeur extended hermeneutics to symbols, narratives, and action, while legal and literary hermeneutics continue to apply these ideas to statutes and literature.

Applications

Beyond theology and philosophy, hermeneutics informs jurisprudence (interpretation of laws), literary criticism, historiography, archaeology, and qualitative research in the social sciences, wherever meaning must be recovered from texts, artifacts, or actions produced in contexts different from the interpreter's own.
