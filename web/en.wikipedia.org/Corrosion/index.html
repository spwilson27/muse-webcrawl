Corrosion is the gradual destruction of materials, usually metals, by chemical or electrochemical reaction with their environment. In the commonest example, iron exposed to water and oxygen forms hydrated iron(III) oxide — rust. Corrosion degrades the useful properties of materials and structures, including strength, appearance and permeability; worldwide, its cost has been estimated at several percent of GDP annually.

Mechanism

Most corrosion is electrochemical: at anodic sites metal atoms lose electrons and enter solution as ions, while at cathodic sites a reduction reaction (commonly of oxygen) consumes the electrons, the circuit being completed through the metal and an electrolyte such as water. Differences in potential that drive such cells can arise from dissimilar metals in contact (galvanic corrosion), variations in oxygen concentration, or local stresses and impurities.

Forms

Besides uniform attack, engineers distinguish galvanic corrosion, pitting (localised pinholes, especially in stainless steels exposed to chlorides), crevice corrosion, intergranular corrosion, dealloying (selective leaching), erosion corrosion and stress corrosion cracking, in which a tensile stress and a corrosive environment together produce sudden brittle failure. Some metals resist corrosion by passivation: aluminium and stainless steel form thin, adherent oxide films that protect the underlying metal.

Protection

Methods of corrosion control include protective coatings (paint, enamel, powder coating), metallic coatings such as galvanising (zinc on steel), cathodic protection using sacrificial anodes or impressed current, corrosion inhibitors, environmental control, and materials selection — for example weathering steel or corrosion-resistant alloys. Structures from bridges and ships to pipelines and the Statue of Liberty (whose iron armature suffered galvanic corrosion against its copper skin) require ongoing corrosion management.
