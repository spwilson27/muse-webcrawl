Russian avant-garde

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Russian avant-garde was a large, influential wave of modern art that flourished in the Russian Empire and the Soviet Union approximately from 1890 to 1930. It produced some of the most radical innovations in painting, sculpture, architecture, theatre, film and design, merging European modernism with Russian traditions and, after 1917, with the revolutionary ambitions of the new state.

Movements

Its major movements included Neo-primitivism; Rayonism, launched by Mikhail Larionov and Natalia Goncharova; Cubo-Futurism; Kazimir Malevich's Suprematism, announced by the Black Square of 1915; Vladimir Tatlin's Constructivism, exemplified by the Monument to the Third International (1919–20); and the analytical art of Pavel Filonov. The Vkhutemas art and technical school in Moscow paralleled the Bauhaus, and figures such as El Lissitzky carried the new visual language into typography, exhibition design and photography.

Decline

Initially welcomed by the Bolshevik government, the avant-garde lost official support as Stalin consolidated power. By the early 1930s socialist realism became mandatory and independent movements were dissolved; many artists emigrated, were marginalised, or died in the purges. The works of the Russian avant-garde, long hidden in museum storage, regained international prominence from the 1960s onward and now command record prices and major exhibitions.
