Archimedes' screw

The Archimedes' screw, also known as the Archimedean screw, hydrodynamic screw, water screw or Egyptian screw, is one of the earliest hydraulic machines, using a helical surface surrounding a central cylindrical shaft set inside a hollow pipe. By turning the shaft, water is pushed up the inclined pipe. It is traditionally credited to the Greek mathematician Archimedes of Syracuse (c. 287–212 BC), although screw pumps may have been used earlier in Mesopotamia and Egypt.

An Archimedes' screw lifting water

Design and operation

The device consists of a screw (a helical blade wrapping a cylinder) inside a closely fitting casing, set at an angle with its lower end immersed in water. Rotating the screw traps pockets of water between the blades; paradoxically, the water itself stays at the same angular position while the helix turns, so each pocket travels up the tube until it pours out at the top. The screw can be turned by hand, by animals, by windmill, or by motor.

History

Ancient writers credit Archimedes with the invention, said to have been devised to remove bilge water from the giant ship Syracusia. Screws drained mines in Roman Spain, irrigated fields in Egypt for centuries, and — combined with windmills — pumped the Dutch polders. Leonardo da Vinci sketched improvements, and the principle later inspired screw conveyors for grain and other bulk materials.

Modern uses

Today Archimedes' screws pump water and sewage (handling solids and variable flows gently, even fish-safely), move grain and powders as augers, and run in reverse as Archimedes screw turbines generating hydroelectric power on low-head rivers. The geometry also underlies screw compressors and the design of some ship propellers.
