Racial segregation
Definition: Systematic separation of people by race in daily life
Notable systems: Jim Crow (U.S.); apartheid (South Africa)

Racial segregation is the systematic separation of people into racial or ethnic groups in daily life, in facilities such as schools, housing, transport, restaurants, and workplaces. Segregation may be mandated by law (de jure) or arise through social and economic practices (de facto).

Historical examples

In the United States, "Jim Crow" laws enforced segregation in the South from the late 19th century until the civil rights movement; the Supreme Court upheld "separate but equal" in Plessy v. Ferguson (1896) and repudiated it for public schools in Brown v. Board of Education (1954). In South Africa, apartheid (1948–1994) imposed an elaborate legal system of racial classification and separation. Nazi Germany's Nuremberg Laws and colonial regimes worldwide also imposed segregation.

Persistence

Even after legal desegregation, residential and school segregation persist in many countries through housing patterns, zoning, and private choices, with consequences for wealth, health, and education that remain active subjects of policy and research.
