Divine right of kings

The divine right of kings, or divine-right theory of kingship, is a political and religious doctrine of royal and political legitimacy. It asserts that a monarch is not accountable to any earthly authority — such as a parliament — because the monarch's right to rule is derived directly from the will of God. The king is thus not subject to the will of the people, of the aristocracy, or of any other estate of the realm. It implies that only God can judge an unjust king and that any attempt to depose, dethrone, resist, or restrict the monarch is contrary to God's will and may be defined as a sacrilegious act.

History

The doctrine in its mature form emerged in early modern Europe. King James VI and I of Scotland and England expounded it in works such as The True Law of Free Monarchies (1598) and Basilikon Doron. In France, the bishop Jacques-Bénigne Bossuet defended absolute monarchy as divinely ordained in his Politics Drawn from the Very Words of Holy Scripture. Concepts of divinely sanctioned kingship were far older, however, with antecedents in ancient Egypt, China (the Mandate of Heaven), and medieval theories of the king's two bodies.

Opposition and decline

The doctrine was challenged by resistance theories during the Reformation, by the English Civil War, and philosophically by John Locke's Two Treatises of Government, written in rebuttal of Robert Filmer's Patriarcha. The Glorious Revolution of 1688, the American Revolution, and the French Revolution displaced divine right with theories of popular sovereignty and constitutional government, though the doctrine lingered in legitimist circles into the 19th century.
