Roundup Ready

Roundup Ready soybeans growing weed-free after spraying

Roundup Ready is a trademark of Bayer (formerly Monsanto) for patented lines of genetically modified crop seeds that are tolerant to the herbicide glyphosate, the active ingredient in the herbicide brand Roundup. Farmers can spray glyphosate over Roundup Ready fields to kill weeds while leaving the crop unharmed, simplifying weed control and facilitating no-till farming.

Technology

Glyphosate kills plants by inhibiting the enzyme EPSP synthase (EPSPS), which plants need to make aromatic amino acids. Roundup Ready crops carry a gene (CP4 EPSPS), derived from an Agrobacterium strain found to be naturally glyphosate-insensitive, that produces an alternative EPSPS enzyme so the plant continues growing when sprayed. The first commercial Roundup Ready crop, soybean, was introduced in 1996; glyphosate-tolerant cotton, maize (corn), canola, sugar beet and alfalfa followed. Second-generation traits (such as Roundup Ready 2 Yield soybeans) and stacked combinations with insect-resistance (Bt) traits became widespread, and by the 2010s the large majority of soybeans, corn and cotton grown in the United States carried herbicide-tolerance traits.

Legal and regulatory aspects

Monsanto's patents, and its licensing agreements forbidding farmers from saving and replanting seed, produced landmark litigation, including Monsanto Co. v. McFarling and Bowman v. Monsanto Co. (2013), in which the U.S. Supreme Court held that patent exhaustion did not permit a farmer to reproduce patented seeds. The original Roundup Ready soybean patent expired in 2014–2015, making it the first major GM trait to become "generic," with public and private breeders able to use it royalty-free subject to remaining regulatory approvals. Monsanto was acquired by Bayer in 2018.

Issues

Heavy reliance on glyphosate over large areas has selected for glyphosate-resistant weeds—"superweeds" such as Palmer amaranth—which have undermined the system's effectiveness and driven development of crops tolerant to additional herbicides such as dicamba and 2,4-D, with attendant drift-damage controversies. Roundup Ready crops have also been part of the wider public debate over genetically modified food, labelling and herbicide residues.
