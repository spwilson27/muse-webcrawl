New York Times Co. v. United States, 403 U.S. 713 (1971), often called the Pentagon Papers case, is a landmark decision of the U.S. Supreme Court establishing that the First Amendment creates an exceptionally heavy presumption against prior restraint of the press. The Court refused the Nixon administration's request to stop The New York Times and The Washington Post from publishing a classified government study of U.S. involvement in Vietnam.

Background

The "Pentagon Papers" were a secret 7,000-page Defense Department history of American involvement in Indochina, leaked by analyst Daniel Ellsberg. The Times began publishing excerpts on June 13, 1971. The government obtained a temporary restraining order — the first time in American history that a federal court had halted a newspaper's publication on national-security grounds — and the Washington Post then began publishing its own copy. Conflicting lower-court rulings sent the cases to the Supreme Court within days.

Decision

On June 30, 1971, just four days after argument, the Court issued a brief per curiam opinion: any system of prior restraints bears "a heavy presumption against its constitutional validity," and the government had not carried its "heavy burden" of justification. Six justices concurred, each writing separately; Justices Black and Douglas took the absolutist position that the First Amendment permits no prior restraint at all, while others, including Stewart and White, left theoretical room for restraints against genuinely catastrophic harms. Chief Justice Burger and Justices Harlan and Blackmun dissented, criticizing the haste of the proceedings.

Significance

The decision remains the leading American authority on prior restraint. It allowed the Pentagon Papers to be published in full, deepening public skepticism about the Vietnam War, and is routinely invoked in disputes over leaks, classified information and national-security reporting.
