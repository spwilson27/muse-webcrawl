Lattice-based cryptography

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A mathematical lattice in two dimensions]

Lattice-based cryptography is the construction of cryptographic primitives whose security rests on the presumed difficulty of mathematical problems involving lattices — regular grids of points in high-dimensional space. It is a leading approach to post-quantum cryptography, because no efficient quantum algorithms are known for the underlying problems, in contrast to the factoring and discrete logarithm problems broken by Shor's algorithm.

Hard problems

Foundations of the field include the Shortest Vector Problem (SVP), the Closest Vector Problem (CVP), Miklós Ajtai's Short Integer Solution (SIS) problem, and the Learning With Errors (LWE) problem introduced by Oded Regev in 2005, whose security can be reduced to worst-case lattice problems. Structured variants such as Ring-LWE and Module-LWE offer efficiency by working over polynomial rings.

Constructions

Lattice methods have produced public-key encryption, digital signatures, key encapsulation mechanisms, and — uniquely — fully homomorphic encryption, first constructed by Craig Gentry in 2009 on lattice principles. Earlier lattice systems include the Ajtai–Dwork cryptosystem, GGH, and NTRU, some of which were broken or required repair, informing modern parameter choices.

Standardization

In the United States National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) post-quantum standardization process, the principal algorithms selected were lattice-based: ML-KEM (derived from CRYSTALS-Kyber) for key establishment and ML-DSA (derived from CRYSTALS-Dilithium) for signatures, standardized in 2024, with the signature scheme FALCON (FN-DSA) also selected. Lattice-based cryptography is thus expected to underpin much of the coming migration to quantum-resistant public-key infrastructure.
