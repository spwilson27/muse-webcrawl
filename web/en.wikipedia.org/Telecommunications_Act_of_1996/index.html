Telecommunications Act of 1996

The Telecommunications Act of 1996 is the first major overhaul of telecommunications law in almost 62 years in the United States, amending the Communications Act of 1934. It was signed into law by President Bill Clinton on February 8, 1996. The act's stated goals were to let any communications business compete in any market against any other, reduce regulation, and promote competition in telephone, broadcasting, cable, and emerging services.

Provisions

The act removed barriers between local and long-distance telephone markets, requiring incumbent carriers to interconnect with competitors and offer network elements at regulated rates. It deregulated cable rates, relaxed limits on broadcast station ownership nationally and locally, extended broadcast license terms, and introduced the V-chip and television ratings system. Title V of the act, the Communications Decency Act, sought to regulate indecency and obscenity on the internet; its central provisions were struck down by the Supreme Court in Reno v. American Civil Liberties Union (1997), although Section 230 of that title survived and became foundational to internet law.

Effects and criticism

Following the act, a wave of mergers consolidated the American media and telecommunications industries, including in radio, where companies such as Clear Channel grew from dozens of stations to more than a thousand. Critics argue the act failed to produce the promised competition in local telephone service and contributed to media concentration, while supporters credit it with facilitating investment and the growth of new communications services.
