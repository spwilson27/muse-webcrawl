Alcohol use disorder (AUD) is a medical condition characterised by an impaired ability to stop or control alcohol use despite adverse social, occupational or health consequences. It encompasses the conditions historically called alcohol abuse and alcohol dependence (alcoholism). The DSM-5 classifies AUD as mild, moderate or severe according to how many of eleven diagnostic criteria are met.

Signs and symptoms

Features include drinking more or longer than intended, unsuccessful efforts to cut down, craving, tolerance (needing more alcohol for the same effect), and withdrawal symptoms such as tremor, sweating, anxiety and — in severe cases — seizures and delirium tremens. Continued drinking despite recurrent problems is a hallmark of the disorder.

Causes and epidemiology

Roughly half the risk of AUD is genetic; environmental influences include childhood trauma, availability of alcohol, cultural norms and co-occurring mental disorders such as depression and anxiety. The World Health Organization estimates that harmful use of alcohol causes about 3 million deaths per year worldwide, and that some 283 million people aged 15 and older live with AUD.

Complications

Long-term heavy drinking damages the liver (alcoholic hepatitis, cirrhosis), the heart, the pancreas and the nervous system (including Wernicke–Korsakoff syndrome), and increases the risk of several cancers, accidents and suicide. Drinking in pregnancy can cause fetal alcohol spectrum disorders.

Treatment

Withdrawal is managed medically, often with benzodiazepines, because abrupt cessation can be fatal. Longer-term treatment combines behavioural therapy, mutual-support groups such as Alcoholics Anonymous, and medications — naltrexone and acamprosate reduce relapse, while disulfiram deters drinking by producing an unpleasant reaction. Recovery is often a long-term process with periods of remission and relapse.
