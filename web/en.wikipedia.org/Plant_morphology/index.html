Plant morphology

Plant morphology is the study of the physical form and external structure of plants. It is considered a branch of botany distinct from plant anatomy, which concerns internal structure, especially at the microscopic level, though in practice the two fields overlap.

Scope

Morphology examines the organisation of the plant body: roots, stems, leaves, flowers, fruits, and seeds, and the ways these structures develop and vary across species. It underpins the description and classification of plants, since the diagnostic characters used in taxonomy are overwhelmingly morphological.

History

The systematic study of plant form began with early herbalists and was placed on a scientific footing by botanists such as Goethe, whose essay The Metamorphosis of Plants (1790) proposed that all aerial plant organs are modifications of a fundamental leaf type. Later workers, including Agnes Arber and Wilhelm Troll, developed comparative and typological approaches to plant form.

Modern approaches

Modern plant morphology combines classical comparative description with developmental genetics, which has revealed the gene networks controlling organ identity — for example, the ABC model of flower development — and with evolutionary developmental biology ("evo-devo"), which studies how changes in development produce the diversity of plant forms.
