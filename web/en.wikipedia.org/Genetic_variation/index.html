Genetic variation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Genetic variation is the difference in DNA sequences between individuals within a population or species. It is the raw material on which natural selection and genetic drift act, and it underlies the diversity of traits such as eye colour, blood type, disease resistance and height.

Sources of variation

New variation is created by mutation — point mutations that substitute one base for another, insertions and deletions, duplications of genes or whole genomes, and chromosome rearrangements. Sexual reproduction then shuffles existing variants into new combinations through crossing over during meiosis and the independent assortment of chromosomes, while gene flow between populations introduces further diversity.

Measurement

Biologists measure variation as nucleotide diversity, heterozygosity, or the number of polymorphic sites in a sample. Any two human genomes differ at roughly one base in a thousand (about three million single-nucleotide differences), yet humans are genetically uniform compared with many species, reflecting our recent evolutionary history and past population bottlenecks.

Importance

Populations with low genetic variation are vulnerable: they adapt poorly to environmental change and disease, and inbreeding exposes harmful recessive alleles. Conservation biology therefore aims to preserve variation in endangered species, and plant breeders maintain it in seed banks as insurance for food security. Variation within pathogens and pests likewise shapes epidemics and the evolution of drug and pesticide resistance.
