Ordinary differential equation

In mathematics, an ordinary differential equation (ODE) is a differential equation dependent on only a single independent variable. It contains one or more functions of that variable and their derivatives. Ordinary differential equations are distinguished from partial differential equations, whose solutions involve functions of several variables.

Definitions

The order of an ODE is the order of the highest derivative present. An equation of the form F(x, y, y′, …, y⁽ⁿ⁾) = 0 is an n-th order ODE. It is linear if F is linear in y and its derivatives, and nonlinear otherwise. A solution is a function that satisfies the equation on some interval. The general solution of an n-th order ODE contains n arbitrary constants; particular solutions follow from initial conditions, giving an initial value problem.

Examples

Newton's second law gives the second-order equation m·x″(t) = F, governing a particle under a force. The equation y′ = ky describes exponential growth and decay, with solution y = Ce^(kx). The harmonic oscillator, x″ + ω²x = 0, models springs and pendulums. First-order linear equations can be solved with an integrating factor; separable equations allow the variables to be split and integrated independently. Many ODEs, such as the equations of chaotic systems like the Lorenz equations, have no closed-form solution and are studied numerically and qualitatively.

Applications

ODEs model countless phenomena: population dynamics in ecology, compartmental models of epidemics, electrical circuits, chemical kinetics, orbital mechanics, and heat and fluid problems reduced to a single variable. Numerical methods—including Euler's method and the Runge–Kutta methods—are used when analytic solutions are unavailable.
