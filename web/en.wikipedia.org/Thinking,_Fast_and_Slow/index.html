Thinking, Fast and Slow is a 2011 popular science book by the psychologist Daniel Kahneman, winner of the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences. The book summarises decades of research conducted by Kahneman, much of it with his collaborator Amos Tversky, on the heuristics and biases that shape human judgment and decision-making.

Two systems

The book's central framework distinguishes between two modes of thought: "System 1", which operates automatically, quickly and intuitively, with little or no effort; and "System 2", which allocates attention to effortful mental activities such as deliberate reasoning, computation and choice. System 1 relies on mental shortcuts that are usually serviceable but produce systematic errors — biases such as anchoring, availability, framing effects, overconfidence and loss aversion.

The two selves

The later chapters contrast the "experiencing self", which lives in the present, with the "remembering self", which keeps score of an experience largely according to its peak intensity and its ending (the peak–end rule), often neglecting duration. Kahneman discusses the implications for policy, welfare measurement and personal decisions.

Reception

The book became an international bestseller and is widely credited with popularising behavioural economics. Some findings it popularised, particularly in the chapter on priming, later faced scrutiny during psychology's replication crisis, and Kahneman himself publicly acknowledged concerns about that chapter's evidence base.
