Dál Riata (also spelled Dalriada or Dalriata) was a Gaelic kingdom that encompassed the western seaboard of Scotland and north-eastern Ireland, on both sides of the North Channel, during the Early Middle Ages. At its height in the late 6th and early 7th centuries, it encompassed what is now Argyll in Scotland and part of County Antrim in Northern Ireland.

Origins and structure

Later tradition held that the kingdom was founded by settlers from Ireland — the Scotti — led by Fergus Mór mac Eirc around the end of the 5th century, though archaeologists debate how much actual migration occurred. Scottish Dál Riata was divided into kin-groups (cenéla), including the Cenél nGabráin around Kintyre, the Cenél Loairn in Lorn, and the Cenél nÓengusa on Islay. The fortress of Dunadd, near modern Kilmartin, is strongly associated with its kings and contains carvings including a footprint used in inauguration rites.

History

Dál Riata was a maritime kingdom whose wealth and power rested on sea trade and its formidable fleet. Saint Columba founded the monastery of Iona within its territory in 563, making the kingdom a centre of Gaelic Christianity and learning. The kingdom suffered defeat by the Picts at Mag Rath in 637 (in its Irish part) and in Scotland in the 7th–8th centuries, and was devastated by Viking raids from the 790s. In the 9th century, tradition associates Kenneth MacAlpin (Cináed mac Ailpín) with the union of the Scots of Dál Riata and the Picts, forming the basis of the kingdom of Alba, the predecessor of the Kingdom of Scotland.
