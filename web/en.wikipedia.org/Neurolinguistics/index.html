Neurolinguistics

Neurolinguistics is the study of the neural mechanisms in the human brain that control the comprehension, production, and acquisition of language. As an interdisciplinary field, neurolinguistics draws methods and theories from fields such as neuroscience, linguistics, cognitive science, communication disorders, neurobiology and computer science.

History

The field's roots lie in 19th-century studies of aphasia: Paul Broca's and Carl Wernicke's descriptions of language deficits following localised brain lesions gave rise to the classical model linking Broca's area with speech production and Wernicke's area with comprehension. The term "neurolinguistics" became established in the mid-20th century, associated with researchers such as Edith Crowell Trager and later Harry Whitaker.

Research questions

Neurolinguistics asks where and how language is implemented in the brain: how phonology, morphology, syntax and semantics are processed; how language is acquired by children; and what happens when language breaks down in aphasia, dyslexia and other disorders.

Methods

Early work relied on lesion studies of patients. Modern research uses neuroimaging and electrophysiological techniques including fMRI, PET, EEG and MEG — event-related potentials such as the N400 (semantic processing) and P600 (syntactic processing) are classic neurolinguistic measures — as well as transcranial magnetic stimulation and computational modelling.
