Thomism is the philosophical and theological school which arose as a legacy of the work and thought of Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274), the Dominican friar, philosopher, and Doctor of the Church. In philosophy, Aquinas's disputed questions and commentaries on Aristotle are perhaps his best-known works. In theology, his Summa Theologiae is one of the most influential documents in medieval theology and remains the central point of reference for the philosophy and theology of the Catholic Church.

Overview

Thomism synthesizes Aristotelian philosophy with Christian doctrine, holding that reason and faith are complementary paths to truth. Central principles include the distinction between essence and existence, the doctrine of analogy, hylomorphism, and the Five Ways — arguments for the existence of God presented in the Summa Theologiae.

History

After Aquinas's death, his ideas were initially controversial and were condemned in part by the condemnations of 1277, but they were gradually adopted by the Dominican order and, following his canonization in 1323, became central to Catholic intellectual life. In 1879 Pope Leo XIII's encyclical Aeterni Patris promoted Thomism as the official philosophy of the Church, sparking a revival known as Neo-Thomism, with figures such as Étienne Gilson and Jacques Maritain.
