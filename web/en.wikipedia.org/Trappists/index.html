Trappists

The Trappists, officially the Order of Cistercians of the Strict Observance (OCSO), are a Catholic religious order of cloistered monks and nuns that branched off from the Cistercians. Known for a life of silence, prayer, and manual labor, the order traces its origin to the reforms of Armand Jean le Bouthillier de Rancé, abbot of La Trappe Abbey in Normandy, who in 1664 introduced a strict observance of the Rule of Saint Benedict — from La Trappe the order took its popular name.

Life and observance

Trappists follow the Benedictine motto Ora et Labora ("pray and work"). Their day is structured around the Liturgy of the Hours, lectio divina, and work supporting the community. Though often described as taking a vow of silence, Trappists more precisely practice a discipline of silence, historically using a developed sign language for necessary communication. They abstain from meat and traditionally fast frequently.

Products and monasteries

To support themselves, Trappist monasteries produce foodstuffs and goods; most famous are the Trappist beers brewed in Belgium and the Netherlands (such as Chimay, Orval, Rochefort, and Westvleteren), which carry an "Authentic Trappist Product" label. Notable houses include La Trappe, Gethsemani in Kentucky (home of Thomas Merton), and the Algerian Abbey of Our Lady of Atlas, whose seven monks were martyred in 1996 and beatified in 2018.
