Cross-examination

Cross-examination is the interrogation of a witness by a party other than the party who called the witness to testify. In common-law adversarial trials it follows the direct examination of the witness by the calling party. Cross-examination is confined in most jurisdictions to matters raised in direct examination and to the witness's credibility, though the court may allow wider questioning. Leading questions — questions that suggest the answer — are generally permitted on cross-examination, unlike on direct examination.

Purpose

The purposes of cross-examination are to elicit favourable testimony supporting the cross-examiner's case, to test the reliability of the witness's evidence, and to expose inconsistencies, bias, faulty memory or exaggeration, thereby undermining credibility. The right to cross-examine witnesses is regarded as fundamental to a fair trial: in the United States it is protected by the Confrontation Clause of the Sixth Amendment.

Technique

Advocates are traditionally advised to ask only questions whose answers they can predict, to use a witness's prior statements against them, and to stop once a concession has been secured. After cross-examination, the calling party may conduct re-direct examination to repair damage, which may in turn be followed by re-cross.
