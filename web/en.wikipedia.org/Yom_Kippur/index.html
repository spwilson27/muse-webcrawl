Yom Kippur (Hebrew: יוֹם כִּפּוּר, "Day of Atonement") is the holiest day in Judaism and Samaritanism. It occurs annually on the 10th day of Tishrei, the seventh month of the Hebrew calendar. According to Jewish tradition, on Yom Kippur God inscribes each person's fate for the coming year into the Book of Life, sealing the judgment begun on Rosh Hashanah ten days earlier.

The central themes of the day are atonement and repentance. Jews traditionally observe this holy day with a day-long fast, confession, and intensive prayer, often spending most of the day in synagogue services. It is the climax of the period known as the Ten Days of Repentance (or Days of Awe) and the final day of the High Holy Days.

History

The biblical origins of Yom Kippur are described in the Book of Leviticus, which commands the Israelites to "afflict their souls" on the tenth day of the seventh month. In the Temple in Jerusalem, the High Priest performed an elaborate ritual of atonement, entering the Holy of Holies — the only day of the year on which this was permitted — and offering sacrifices on behalf of the people. Two goats were chosen: one was sacrificed, and the other, the "scapegoat", was sent into the wilderness bearing the sins of the community, a ritual associated with Azazel.

After the destruction of the Second Temple in 70 CE, the sacrificial rites ceased, and prayer, repentance, and charity took their place as the means of atonement, following the prophetic teaching that God desires mercy and a contrite heart.

Observances

Yom Kippur is observed as a day of fasting lasting approximately 25 hours, beginning just before sunset and ending after nightfall the following day. Five prohibitions are traditionally observed: eating and drinking, washing, applying lotions or perfumes, wearing leather shoes, and marital relations. The day is spent largely in prayer. The synagogue services include the Kol Nidre prayer on the eve of the fast, the confession of sins (Vidui), the Yizkor memorial service, and the concluding Ne'ila service, which ends with a single long blast of the shofar.

Before the fast, it is customary to eat a festive meal (the Seudah HaMafseket) and to seek forgiveness from friends and family. Many worshippers wear white, symbolizing purity and the hope of a clean slate.

In the calendar

Because the Hebrew calendar is lunisolar, Yom Kippur falls in September or October in the Gregorian calendar. In Israel it is a legal holiday: no public transport operates, businesses are closed, and broadcast media are silent.
