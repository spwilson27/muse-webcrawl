The Tonlé Sap (Khmer: ទន្លេសាប, "Great Lake") is a seasonally inundated freshwater lake in northwestern Cambodia and one of the most productive inland fisheries in the world. It is joined to the Mekong River by the 120 km Tonlé Sap River.

The reversing river

The lake's hydrology is unique: during the dry season it drains into the Mekong, but when monsoon rains swell the Mekong in June, the flow of the Tonlé Sap River reverses, pushing water back into the lake. The lake's area expands roughly sixfold and its depth rises from about 1 metre to as much as 9–10 metres, flooding surrounding forests and fields. The annual flood pulse drives an immensely productive fishery that supplies much of Cambodia's protein, celebrated in the Water Festival (Bon Om Touk) marking the flow's reversal back in autumn.

Ecology and people

Over a million people live around the lake, many in floating villages and stilt houses, including ethnic Khmer, Vietnamese and Cham communities. The flooded forest shelters endangered species such as the Mekong giant catfish and colonies of large waterbirds. The ecosystem is threatened by upstream Mekong dams, which alter the flood pulse, as well as overfishing and climate change.
