The Treaty of Canandaigua (also known as the Pickering Treaty) is a treaty signed after the American Revolutionary War between the Grand Council of the Six Nations of the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) Confederacy and the United States, represented by Colonel Timothy Pickering, Indian agent for President George Washington. It was signed at Canandaigua, New York, on 11 November 1794 by sachems of the Six Nations and by Pickering.

Provisions

The treaty established peace and friendship between the United States and the Six Nations, and affirmed the reserved lands of the Haudenosaunee in New York State. The United States acknowledged the lands of the Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga and Seneca nations and promised that they would not be disturbed; the Seneca land boundaries were spelled out, recognising the large Seneca reservation in western New York before some of it was sold in the later Treaty of Big Tree (1797). The Six Nations in turn ceded claims to lands further west and agreed to free passage along specified routes, including the road between Lake Ontario and Lake Erie through their territory.

Significance

The treaty remains in force and is still commemorated each year on 11 November in Canandaigua, in a ceremony attended by Haudenosaunee leaders, local citizens and representatives of the U.S. government, at which the treaty cloth — an annual payment of goods promised in the treaty — is symbolically presented. The United States continues to deliver the annuity cloth to the Six Nations. The treaty has been cited in modern land-claim litigation, and the polishing of the "covenant chain" of friendship is central to Haudenosaunee diplomacy with the United States to this day.
