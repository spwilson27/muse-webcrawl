Membrane protein

Membrane proteins are common proteins that are part of, or interact with, biological membranes. They are grouped into integral membrane proteins, which are permanently anchored or span the membrane, and peripheral membrane proteins, which are only temporarily attached to the lipid bilayer or to other membrane proteins. Membrane proteins represent about 20–30% of all proteins in many genomes and are targets of over half of modern medicinal drugs.

Function

Membrane proteins perform a variety of functions vital to the survival of organisms, including receptor proteins that relay signals between the cell's internal and external environments; transport proteins that move molecules and ions across the membrane (channels, pumps, and carriers); cell adhesion molecules; and enzymes embedded in membranes.

Structure

Most integral membrane proteins cross the bilayer with alpha-helices, though beta-barrels are common in outer membranes of bacteria, mitochondria, and chloroplasts. Their hydrophobic surfaces interact with membrane lipids while polar groups face the aqueous phases. Determining membrane protein structures is challenging because the proteins are difficult to crystallize outside their lipid environment; cryo-electron microscopy has transformed the field.

Disease

Defects in membrane proteins underlie many diseases, from cystic fibrosis (a chloride channel defect) to forms of diabetes insipidus and retinitis pigmentosa.
