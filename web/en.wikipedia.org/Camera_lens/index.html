Camera lens

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A camera lens (also known as a photographic lens or photographic objective) is an optical lens or assembly of lenses used in conjunction with a camera body to capture images on photographic film or digital image sensors. Camera lenses range from simple single-element meniscus designs to extremely complex assemblies of twenty or more elements with specialised glass and coatings.

Construction

A photographic lens consists of multiple lens elements mounted in a barrel, together with an aperture diaphragm that controls the amount of light reaching the sensor, a focusing mechanism, and — in modern systems — electronic contacts and motors for autofocus and image stabilisation. Elements are made from optical glass types with different refractive indices and dispersion properties, combined to correct aberrations such as spherical aberration, coma, astigmatism, and chromatic aberration. Multi-coating of element surfaces reduces flare and ghosting.

Types of lenses

Lenses are commonly classified by focal length relative to the format. A normal lens has a focal length roughly equal to the diagonal of the sensor (about 50 mm for full-frame cameras). Wide-angle lenses are shorter and capture a broader field of view, while telephoto lenses are longer and magnify distant subjects. Zoom lenses vary their focal length over a range, whereas prime lenses have a fixed focal length and are often faster and sharper. Specialist designs include macro lenses for close-up work, fisheye lenses, tilt-shift lenses, and anamorphic lenses for cinema.

Focal length and aperture

The two defining specifications of a lens are its focal length, which determines angle of view and magnification, and its maximum aperture, expressed as an f-number (for example f/1.4 or f/2.8), which governs light gathering and depth of field. Lens speed, sharpness, and rendering characteristics strongly influence photographic style, making lens choice central to the craft of photography.
