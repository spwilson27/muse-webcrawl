Galaxy cluster

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A galaxy cluster, or a cluster of galaxies, is a structure that consists of anywhere from hundreds to thousands of galaxies that are bound together by gravity, with typical masses ranging from 1014 to 1015 solar masses. They are the second-largest known gravitationally bound structures in the universe, after some superclusters. Clusters were once believed to be the largest such structures before the discovery of superclusters in the 1980s.

Composition

Only a small fraction of a cluster's mass resides in its visible galaxies. Most of the ordinary (baryonic) matter is in the intracluster medium: very hot, tenuous plasma between the galaxies, with temperatures of tens of millions of kelvin, which shines brightly in X-rays. The dominant component is dark matter, which typically makes up about 80 percent or more of the total mass and is inferred from galaxy motions, X-ray gas temperatures, and gravitational lensing.

Notable clusters

The nearest rich cluster is the Virgo Cluster, about 54 million light-years away, which contains well over a thousand galaxies and forms the heart of the Local Supercluster. Other well-studied examples include the Coma Cluster, the Perseus Cluster, and the Bullet Cluster, whose colliding subclusters provide striking evidence for dark matter because the lensing mass is offset from the X-ray-emitting gas.

Formation and observation

Clusters grow hierarchically over cosmic time as smaller groups merge along filaments of the cosmic web. Because hot intracluster gas emits X-rays and distorts the cosmic microwave background through the Sunyaev–Zel'dovich effect, clusters are used as cosmological probes to measure the matter density of the universe and the growth of structure.
