Elvis taxon

In palaeontology, an Elvis taxon is a taxon that has been misidentified and looks like a member of an extinct group that disappeared much earlier in the fossil record, but is actually a later, unrelated organism that evolved similar features by convergence. The term was coined in 1993 by Dale H. Erwin and Mark L. Droser, by analogy with Elvis impersonators — and with the many supposed sightings of Elvis Presley after his death.

Distinguishing features

An Elvis taxon is neither a true survivor of the older lineage (a Lazarus taxon) nor a descendant that reappears after a gap. Rather, detailed study shows the younger fossil belongs to a different lineage that independently evolved a similar morphology. Correct identification often depends on microstructure or anatomical characters not visible in superficial resemblance.

Examples

A frequently cited example is the brachiopod Cryptoporella, once thought to extend a Palaeozoic order into the Jurassic but later assigned to a different group. Elvis taxa matter in biostratigraphy and extinction studies because a misidentified "survivor" can falsely extend the range of a group and distort estimates of extinction selectivity and post-extinction recovery.
