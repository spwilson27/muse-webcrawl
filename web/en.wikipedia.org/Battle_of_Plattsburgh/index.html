Battle of Plattsburgh

Macdonough's squadron defeating the British fleet in Plattsburgh Bay, 11 September 1814.

Date6–11 September 1814
LocationPlattsburgh, New York, and Lake Champlain
ResultDecisive American victory
CommandersThomas Macdonough and Alexander Macomb vs. George Prévost and George Downie

The Battle of Plattsburgh, also known as the Battle of Lake Champlain, was fought in September 1814 during the War of 1812. An American naval squadron under Master Commandant Thomas Macdonough destroyed the British squadron on Lake Champlain, compelling Sir George Prévost's invading army of some 11,000 men to abandon its attack on Plattsburgh and retreat to Canada.

Background

With Napoleon's defeat in 1814, Britain shipped thousands of veteran troops to Canada and planned a major invasion down the Lake Champlain–Hudson corridor, echoing Burgoyne's campaign of 1777. Prévost's army vastly outnumbered Brigadier General Alexander Macomb's defenders at Plattsburgh, but the advance depended on control of the lake for supply and support.

The naval battle

On 11 September 1814, Captain George Downie's British squadron rounded Cumberland Head to attack Macdonough's anchored line in Plattsburgh Bay. Macdonough had prepared his flagship Saratoga with spring lines and kedge anchors, allowing him, after a murderous two-hour exchange in which his starboard guns were disabled, to wind the ship around and bring a fresh broadside to bear. Downie was killed early in the action; the British flagship Confiance struck her colours, and the whole British squadron was captured or destroyed.

Aftermath

Prévost, despite his huge numerical advantage, immediately broke off the land attack and withdrew to Canada, to the fury of his officers. News of Plattsburgh — together with the repulse at Baltimore — reached the peace negotiators at Ghent and demolished British demands for territorial concessions in the north-west. The battle is regarded as one of the decisive American victories of the war, and Macdonough was hailed as a national hero.
