Music of Scotland

The music of Scotland is a rich and varied tradition that spans folk, classical, and popular forms. Its best-known instrument is the great Highland bagpipe (pìob mhòr), and its dance forms — reels, jigs, and strathspeys — have influenced music across the world, carried abroad by the Scottish diaspora.

Traditional music

Scottish folk music draws on a deep well of ballads, many in the Scots and Scottish Gaelic languages. The fiddle became central to dance music from the eighteenth century, with figures such as Niel Gow celebrated as composers of strathspeys and reels. Gaelic song traditions include puirt à beul (mouth music), waulking songs sung to accompany cloth-working, and the psalm singing of the Highlands and Islands.

The bagpipe tradition divides into ceòl mòr ("great music"), the formal pibroch repertoire of variations on a theme, and ceòl beag ("little music"), the lighter marches, reels, and jigs played for dancing and military occasions. Pipe bands remain a fixture of Scottish public life.

Classical music

Scotland's art-music tradition includes the Renaissance composer Robert Carver, whose masses survive in the Carver Choirbook. In the modern era, composers such as Hamish MacCunn, Peter Maxwell Davies, James MacMillan, and Thea Musgrave have achieved international standing, and institutions including the Royal Scottish National Orchestra and Scottish Opera anchor professional performance.

Popular music

Scottish popular musicians have had global success across genres: rock acts such as Simple Minds, Texas, and Franz Ferdinand; folk-rock groups including Runrig; singer-songwriters such as Donovan, Annie Lennox, and KT Tunstall; and electronic acts such as Calvin Harris. The céilidh — a social gathering with traditional music and set dancing — remains a living part of Scottish culture, and Scotland hosts major festivals including Celtic Connections in Glasgow and the Hebridean Celtic Festival.
