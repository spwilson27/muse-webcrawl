Powdery mildew

Powdery mildew is a fungal disease that affects a wide range of plants. Powdery mildew diseases are caused by many different species of ascomycete fungi in the order Erysiphales. It is one of the easier plant diseases to identify, as its symptoms are quite distinctive: infected plants display white powdery spots on the leaves and stems.

Symptoms and life cycle

The disease typically begins as raised blister-like areas that soon become coated with a white, powdery mass of fungal mycelium and spores (conidia). The lower leaves are the most affected, but the mildew can appear on any above-ground part of the plant. As the disease progresses, leaves may yellow, curl and drop prematurely, and fruit may be stunted or distorted. Unlike many fungal pathogens, powdery mildew fungi do not require free water on the leaf surface to infect; they thrive in warm, humid conditions with poor air circulation.

Hosts and impact

Important hosts include cereals such as wheat and barley, grapevines, cucurbits (squash, melons, cucumbers), apples, strawberries, roses and many ornamental plants. Most powdery mildew species are highly host-specific: the species that infects wheat, for example, does not infect cucumbers. Severe infections reduce photosynthesis, weaken plants and can cause serious yield losses in agriculture.

Control

Management combines cultural practices (good air circulation, resistant varieties, removal of infected material) with fungicides such as sulfur, neem oil, potassium bicarbonate and synthetic fungicides. Sulfur has been used against powdery mildew since ancient times and remains common in organic production, though it can damage plants if applied in hot weather.
