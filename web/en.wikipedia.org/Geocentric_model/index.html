In astronomy, the geocentric model (also known as geocentrism, often exemplified specifically by the Ptolemaic system) is a superseded description of the Universe with Earth at the center. Under most geocentric models, the Sun, Moon, stars and planets all orbit Earth. The geocentric model was the predominant description of the cosmos in many European ancient societies, as well as in China and in the Muslim world.

The Ptolemaic system: Earth at the centre, with the Sun, Moon and planets carried on deferents and epicycles.

Two observations supported the idea that Earth was the center of the Universe. First, from anywhere on Earth, the Sun appears to revolve around Earth once per day. Second, ancient observers felt Earth was solid and stable and not moving.

The Ptolemaic system

The most fully developed geocentric system was that of Claudius Ptolemy of Alexandria (2nd century AD), set out in the Almagest. Each planet moves on a small circle called an epicycle, whose centre moves along a larger circle called a deferent; Ptolemy added the eccentric and the equant — a point about which the epicycle's centre moves with uniform angular speed — to match observations, including retrograde motion, with remarkable accuracy.

Alternatives and decline

Aristarchus of Samos had proposed a heliocentric arrangement in the 3rd century BC, but it failed to gain acceptance. The geocentric model, embedded in Aristotelian physics and Christian theology, dominated for some 1,400 years. Nicolaus Copernicus's De revolutionibus (1543) revived heliocentrism; Galileo's telescopic observations (such as the phases of Venus) and Kepler's elliptical orbits undermined the Ptolemaic system, and Newton's gravitation finally explained why the Sun-centred picture works. Tycho Brahe's geo-heliocentric compromise, in which the planets orbit the Sun but the Sun orbits a fixed Earth, was a transitional model favoured by many astronomers in the late 16th and 17th centuries.

Religious and modern views

The Catholic Church's 1616 assessment of heliocentrism and the 1633 trial of Galileo made geocentrism a matter of doctrine for a time; the Church gradually accepted heliocentrism in the 18th and 19th centuries. Small modern geocentrist movements persist, generally on literalist religious grounds, but are regarded as pseudoscientific.
