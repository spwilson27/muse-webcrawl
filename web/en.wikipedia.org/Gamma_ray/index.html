Gamma ray

A gamma ray, also known as gamma radiation (symbol γ), is a penetrating form of electromagnetic radiation arising from the radioactive decay of atomic nuclei. It consists of the shortest wavelength electromagnetic waves, typically shorter than those of X-rays. With frequencies above 30 exahertz and photon energies above 100 keV, gamma rays are strongly ionizing and are powerful health hazards.

Discovery and properties

Gamma radiation was discovered in 1900 by Paul Villard while studying radium; Ernest Rutherford named it in 1903 as the third form of nuclear radiation after alpha and beta. Gamma rays are emitted when an excited nucleus transitions to a lower energy state. They are highly penetrating — attenuated but not stopped by dense materials such as lead — and travel at the speed of light.

Sources and uses

Natural sources include radioactive decay, cosmic ray interactions in the atmosphere, lightning (terrestrial gamma-ray flashes) and astrophysical events such as supernovae and gamma-ray bursts, the most energetic explosions known. Applications include radiation therapy for cancer, sterilization of medical equipment and food, industrial radiography, and gamma spectroscopy for identifying radioactive materials.

Health effects

Gamma radiation causes ionization in living tissue and can damage DNA, causing burns, sickness and cancer; acute high doses are lethal. Shielding with dense materials and distance are the standard means of protection.
