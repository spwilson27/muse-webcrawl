Stochastic optimization

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Illustration of a stochastic search converging on an optimum.

Stochastic optimization (SO) refers to a family of optimization methods that employ randomness in the search for an optimum, either because the objective function itself is uncertain or noisy, or because random moves are used to explore the solution space.

Motivation

Many real problems — training machine-learning models on finite data samples, simulating complex systems, or optimizing under uncertain demand — provide only noisy estimates of the quantity to be optimized. Stochastic methods are designed to make progress despite this noise, typically converging in probability to an optimum under suitable conditions.

Methods

Prominent examples include stochastic gradient descent (SGD), which updates parameters using gradients computed on random mini-batches of data and is the workhorse of deep learning; simulated annealing, which accepts worsening moves with a probability that decreases over time; stochastic approximation algorithms such as Robbins–Monro and Kiefer–Wolfowitz; and evolutionary and swarm methods such as genetic algorithms and particle swarm optimization.

Theory

Convergence analysis typically requires step sizes that decrease so that their sum diverges while the sum of their squares converges, balancing continued progress against the averaging-out of noise.
