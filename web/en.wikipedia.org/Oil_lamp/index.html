Oil lamp
FuelVegetable oil, animal fat, later kerosene
MechanismCapillary action draws fuel up a wick to the flame
Earliest evidenceStone lamps of the Upper Palaeolithic (c. 40,000 years ago)
SuccessorsCandle, gas light, kerosene lamp, electric light

An oil lamp is a lamp that burns liquid fuel — historically olive oil, other vegetable oils or animal fat — drawn up through a wick by capillary action. Oil lamps were the principal artificial light source of the ancient and pre-industrial world and remained in common use until displaced by kerosene lamps, gaslight and electricity in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.

History

The oldest known stone lamps, hollowed rocks burning animal fat with moss wicks, date to the Upper Palaeolithic and were found in caves such as Lascaux. Pottery lamps became standard in the Bronze and Iron Ages; Greek and Roman terracotta lamps, mould-made and often richly decorated with relief scenes, are among the most common archaeological finds of the classical world. Bronze and glass lamps, hanging multi-wick lamps in Byzantine churches, and the mosque lamps of the Islamic world continued the form. In India the earthen diya remains central to festivals such as Diwali.

Types and improvements

Designs range from simple open saucer lamps to closed reservoirs with pinched nozzles, multiple wicks and handles. Major improvements included the Argand lamp (1780s), with a circular wick and glass chimney giving a much brighter flame, and nineteenth-century kerosene lamps with adjustable flat or round wicks. Ritual and decorative oil lamps are still used worldwide, and simple lamps remain essential lighting in regions without reliable electricity.
