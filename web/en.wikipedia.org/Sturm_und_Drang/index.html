Sturm und Drang

Johann Wolfgang von Goethe, whose early works defined the Sturm und Drang movement.

Sturm und Drang (German for "storm and stress") was a proto-Romantic movement in German literature and music that occurred in the late 1760s and early 1780s. Within the movement, individual subjectivity and, in particular, extremes of emotion were given free expression in reaction to the perceived constraints of rationalism imposed by the Enlightenment and associated aesthetic movements.

Characteristics

The movement took its name from Friedrich Maximilian Klinger's play Sturm und Drang (1776). Its literature exalted nature, feeling and individual genius over classical rules; heroes were passionate, rebellious figures who defied social convention and authority. Key works include Goethe's drama Götz von Berlichingen (1773) and his epistolary novel The Sorrows of Young Werther (1774), as well as the early plays of Friedrich Schiller, notably The Robbers (1781). The philosopher Johann Georg Hamann and the writer and critic Johann Gottfried Herder were central theoretical influences.

Music

In music, the Sturm und Drang style is associated with a group of works, especially Haydn's minor-key symphonies of the late 1760s and early 1770s, marked by dramatic contrasts, syncopation, tremolandi and sudden dynamic extremes.

Legacy

Sturm und Drang is generally regarded as a precursor of Romanticism, into which it merged as its leading figures, above all Goethe and Schiller, turned toward Weimar Classicism.
