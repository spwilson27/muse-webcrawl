Occupation of Latvia

Image placeholder: Red Army entering Riga, 1940
Soviet forces in Riga during the 1940 occupation

The occupation of Latvia refers to the military occupation of the Republic of Latvia by the Soviet Union in 1940, its subsequent annexation as the Latvian Soviet Socialist Republic, the occupation by Nazi Germany from 1941 to 1944–45, and the renewed Soviet occupation that lasted until Latvia restored its independence in 1991.

Soviet occupation of 1940

Under the secret protocols of the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact (1939), Latvia fell within the Soviet sphere of influence. After imposed "mutual assistance" treaties brought Soviet garrisons into the country, the USSR issued an ultimatum in June 1940 and occupied Latvia in full. Stage-managed elections produced a "People's Saeima" that proclaimed a Soviet republic and requested admission to the USSR, which followed in August 1940. The annexation was not recognized de jure by most Western states, including the United States (per the Stimson Doctrine).

The first Soviet year brought nationalization, arrests, and the June 1941 deportations, in which some 15,000 people were sent to Siberia.

German occupation, 1941–1945

Following Germany's invasion of the USSR in June 1941, Latvia was incorporated into the Reichskommissariat Ostland. During the German occupation, the Holocaust in Latvia destroyed the great majority of the country's Jewish community — around 70,000 Latvian Jews were murdered, including in the Rumbula massacre — and Latvian units were raised for German service, including the Latvian Legion of the Waffen-SS, while other Latvians resisted or joined Soviet partisans.

Second Soviet occupation, 1944–1991

The Red Army retook most of Latvia in 1944 (the Courland Pocket held out until May 1945). Soviet rule brought renewed deportations — most massively in March 1949, when more than 42,000 people were deported during collectivization — Russification, industrialization, and demographic change. The guerrilla resistance of the "Forest Brothers" continued into the 1950s.

Restoration of independence

During the Singing Revolution, Latvia's Supreme Council declared the restoration of independence on 4 May 1990; full independence was recognized after the failed August 1991 coup in Moscow, and the last Russian troops withdrew in 1994. Latvia's official position, shared by the other Baltic states and supported by rulings such as those of the European Court of Human Rights, is that the state was illegally occupied and annexed and that the restored republic is legally continuous with the pre-1940 republic.
