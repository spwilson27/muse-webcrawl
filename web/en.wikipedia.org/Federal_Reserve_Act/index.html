Federal Reserve Act

Federal Reserve Act
Enacted: 23 December 1913
Signed by: President Woodrow Wilson
Citation: 38 Stat. 251; 12 U.S.C. ch. 3
Effect: Created the Federal Reserve System
The Federal Reserve Act of 1913 established the United States' central banking system.

The Federal Reserve Act is an Act of Congress that created the Federal Reserve System, the central banking system of the United States. It was signed into law by President Woodrow Wilson on 23 December 1913. The Act authorised the creation of a central board and up to a dozen regional Federal Reserve Banks, empowered to issue a new national currency — Federal Reserve notes — and to rediscount commercial paper, so that the money supply could expand and contract with the needs of commerce, ending the era of inelastic currency that had aggravated financial panics.

Background

The United States had been without a central bank since the charter of the Second Bank of the United States lapsed in 1836. Recurring banking panics, culminating in the Panic of 1907 — stopped largely by the private intervention of banker J. P. Morgan — convinced political leaders that reform was necessary. A 1910 secret meeting of bankers and Senator Nelson Aldrich on Jekyll Island, Georgia, produced a plan for a centralised reserve association, but Democrats distrusted Wall Street control; the eventual Act, shaped by Representative Carter Glass and Senator Robert Owen, split the difference: regional Reserve Banks balanced by a federal board in Washington.

Provisions

The Act established twelve Federal Reserve districts, a Federal Reserve Board (now the Board of Governors), a Federal Advisory Council, and requirements that national banks join the system and hold reserves at their Reserve Bank. Federal Reserve notes were made obligations of the United States, backed by collateral. Later amendments transformed the system: the Banking Acts of 1933 and 1935 centralised monetary policy in the Federal Open Market Committee, the 1977 reform act added the dual mandate of maximum employment and stable prices, and the Dodd-Frank Act of 2010 amended its emergency lending powers.
