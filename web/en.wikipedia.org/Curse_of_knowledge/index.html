Curse of knowledge

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The curse of knowledge is a cognitive bias that occurs when an individual, who is communicating with others, assumes that others have information that is only available to themselves, simply because they themselves are aware of it. In other words, it is the difficulty of imagining what it is like for someone else not to know something that you know.

History

The term was coined by economists Colin Camerer, George Loewenstein, and Martin Weber in 1989. They demonstrated that better-informed agents in economic settings often fail to ignore their privileged information when predicting the behaviour of less-informed agents.

A widely cited demonstration is Elizabeth Newton's 1990 Stanford dissertation experiment, in which "tappers" tapped out the rhythm of well-known songs while "listeners" tried to guess them. Tappers predicted listeners would succeed about half the time; listeners actually identified only about 2.5% of the songs.

Effects

The curse of knowledge affects teaching, writing, product design, and management: experts systematically underestimate how difficult a task or text will be for novices. It is sometimes called the "curse of expertise" and is related to hindsight bias and the false consensus effect. Awareness of the bias only partly mitigates it; feedback from genuine novices is a more reliable remedy.
