Sherry

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: a copita glass of sherry]

Sherry (Spanish: Jerez) is a fortified wine made from white grapes that are grown near the city of Jerez de la Frontera in Andalusia, Spain. Sherry is produced in a variety of styles, ranging from light versions similar to white table wines, such as Manzanilla and Fino, to darker and heavier versions that have been allowed to oxidise as they age in barrel, such as Amontillado and Oloroso. Sweet dessert wines are also made from Pedro Ximénez or Moscatel grapes.

The word "sherry" is an anglicisation of Jerez. The wine's production area, the so-called "Sherry Triangle", is bounded by Jerez de la Frontera, Sanlúcar de Barrameda and El Puerto de Santa María. The chalky albariza soils of the region are ideal for growing the Palomino grape, which accounts for about 95 per cent of plantings.

Production

After fermentation, the base wines are fortified with grape spirit. Wines destined to become Finos are fortified only to about 15.5 per cent alcohol, which permits the growth of flor, a layer of yeast that protects the wine from oxidation. Olorosos are fortified to at least 17 per cent, killing the flor and allowing oxidative ageing. Sherries are then aged in the traditional solera system of fractional blending, in which younger wine is gradually mixed into older barrels.

Styles

  • Manzanilla — a Fino produced around Sanlúcar de Barrameda, light and tangy.
  • Fino — dry, pale, aged under flor.
  • Amontillado — begins under flor, then oxidises; nutty and darker.
  • Oloroso — fully oxidised, rich and dark.
  • Palo Cortado — a rare intermediate style.
  • Cream — sweetened Oloroso blends.
