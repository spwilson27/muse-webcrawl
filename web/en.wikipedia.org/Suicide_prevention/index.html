Suicide prevention

Suicide prevention is a collection of efforts to reduce the risk of suicide. These efforts may occur at the individual, relationship, community, and societal levels. Suicide is often preventable: beyond direct interventions, prevention may include the treatment of the symptoms of mental disorders, improving coping strategies of people who would otherwise seriously consider suicide, reducing the prevalence of conditions believed to constitute risk factors, and giving people hope for a better life after current problems are resolved.

Strategies

General efforts include a range of suicide prevention strategies. Because protective factors such as social support and social engagement can buffer risk, suicide can be a difficult topic to bring up. Prevention strategies may be classified as universal (aimed at whole populations), selective (aimed at at-risk groups), and indicated (aimed at individuals showing warning signs).

Means restriction

Reducing access to lethal means — such as firearms, toxic substances, and high places — is among the best-supported prevention strategies. Examples include barriers on bridges, safer packaging of analgesics, pesticide regulation, and the detoxification of domestic coal gas in the United Kingdom, which was followed by a substantial fall in suicide rates.

Crisis intervention

Crisis hotlines, such as the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline in the United States and the Samaritans in the United Kingdom and Ireland, provide immediate support to people in distress. Crisis intervention may also be delivered by text line, chat services, and trained gatekeepers in schools, workplaces, and communities.

Risk and protective factors

Risk factors include mental disorders (especially depression, bipolar disorder, and substance use disorders), previous suicide attempts, chronic pain, and social isolation. Protective factors include strong social connections, problem-solving skills, access to care, and cultural or religious beliefs that discourage suicide.

Media reporting

Responsible media reporting of suicide — avoiding sensationalism and details of methods, and including information about help resources — can reduce imitative ("copycat") suicides, an effect known as the Werther effect; reporting on people who coped with suicidal crises may have a protective Papageno effect.
