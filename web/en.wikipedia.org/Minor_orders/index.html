Minor orders

Minor orders are ranks of Christian ministry below the major orders — in the Latin Church traditionally the subdiaconate, diaconate and priesthood. In the historic practice of the Roman Catholic Church, the minor orders were four: porter (ostiarius), lector, exorcist and acolyte. Candidates for the priesthood received them in sequence, along with first tonsure, as steps toward ordination.

Each order had a symbolic function at the liturgy: the porter guarded the church doors, the lector read the lessons, the exorcist was associated with prayers over catechumens and the possessed, and the acolyte assisted at the altar, carrying candles and preparing the wine and water. By the later Middle Ages these functions were often performed by others, and the orders had become largely preparatory stages of formation.

Reform after Vatican II

In 1972, Pope Paul VI's motu proprio Ministeria quaedam suppressed the minor orders and the subdiaconate in the Latin Church, replacing lector and acolyte with instituted ministries open to lay men (and, by a later amendment of canon law in 2021, to women). The Eastern Catholic and Orthodox churches retain comparable lower ranks — reader, subdeacon, cantor and acolyte among them — and traditionalist Catholic communities continue to confer the historic minor orders.
