Metallic bonding

Metallic bonding is a type of chemical bonding that arises from the electrostatic attractive force between conduction electrons (in the form of an electron cloud of delocalised electrons) and positively charged metal ions. It may be described as the sharing of free electrons among a structure of positively charged ions (cations).

The electron sea model

In the simplest picture, metal atoms contribute their valence electrons to a common "sea" that pervades the whole crystal. The metal atoms become positive ions arranged in a regular lattice, held together not by directed bonds between pairs of atoms but by their attraction to the surrounding electron cloud. More advanced treatments use band theory, in which the overlapping atomic orbitals of the whole solid merge into energy bands that the electrons partly fill.

Properties explained

Metallic bonding accounts for many characteristic properties of metals. Electrical and thermal conductivity follow from the mobility of the delocalised electrons. Malleability and ductility arise because the bonding is non-directional: layers of ions can slide past one another without breaking the bond, unlike in ionic or covalent crystals. Metallic lustre results from the interaction of light with the free electrons, and the strength of the bonding — increasing with the number of delocalised electrons and the charge on the ions — helps explain the high melting points of metals such as tungsten, as well as the low melting points of the alkali metals, where bonding is weak.

Comparison

Metallic bonding is one of the three main types of strong chemical bonding, alongside ionic bonding (electron transfer between atoms) and covalent bonding (electron sharing between specific atom pairs). Alloys combine metallic bonding with mixtures of different metal atoms in the lattice.
