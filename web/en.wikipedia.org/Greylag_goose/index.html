Greylag goose

The greylag goose (Anser anser) is the ancestor of most domestic geese.

The greylag goose or graylag goose (Anser anser) is a large species of goose in the waterfowl family Anatidae. It is the type species of the genus Anser and the principal wild ancestor of the domestic goose. The name derives from the fact that the bird historically "lagged" behind others in migration, or from dialect words for "grey".

Description

The greylag is the largest member of the genus Anser, measuring 74–91 cm in length with a wingspan of 147–180 cm and weighing 2.3–4.1 kg. Plumage is grey-brown overall with a paler belly, pinkish-orange bill and pink legs. Two subspecies are recognised: the western A. a. anser and the eastern A. a. rubrirostris, the latter also ancestral to some Asian domestic breeds (though most Chinese domestic geese descend from the swan goose).

Distribution and behaviour

Greylags breed across northern and central Europe and Asia, around lakes, marshes and coastal wetlands, and northern populations migrate south in winter; large feral, resident flocks have become established in Britain and elsewhere. They are herbivorous, grazing grasses and roots and feeding on grain and crops. Greylags form long-term monogamous pairs, and their complex social behaviour was made famous by Konrad Lorenz, whose classic ethological studies used imprinted greylag goslings.

Conservation

With a global population estimated at around one million birds, the species is listed as Least Concern. Domesticated for thousands of years — goose imagery appears in ancient Egyptian art — greylags were sacred to the Roman goddess Juno; the geese of the Capitoline Hill famously alerted Rome to a Gallic attack in 390 BC.
