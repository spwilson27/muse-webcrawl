Reverb effect

A reverb effect is an audio effect that simulates reverberation — the persistence of sound after it is produced, caused by reflections of sound waves off surfaces in an enclosed space. Reverb units and plug-ins are used extensively in music production and live sound to give recordings a sense of space and depth.

Types

Spring reverb, common in guitar amplifiers, sends the signal through metal springs. Plate reverb uses a large suspended metal plate, famously the EMT 140. Chamber reverb uses an actual echo chamber with loudspeaker and microphones. Digital reverb algorithms, pioneered by units such as the EMT 250 and Lexicon 224, model reflections computationally, while convolution reverb uses recorded impulse responses of real spaces.

Parameters

Typical controls include decay time (reverb time), pre-delay, diffusion, damping of high frequencies, and the wet/dry mix. Longer decays simulate larger spaces such as cathedrals; short decays suggest small rooms.

Use in music

Reverb has been a defining effect in genres from surf rock and dub to shoegaze and ambient music, and artificial reverb is added to most commercial recordings to blend instruments recorded in dry studio environments.
