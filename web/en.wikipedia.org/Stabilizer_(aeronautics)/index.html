Stabilizer (aeronautics)

In aeronautics, a stabilizer is an aerodynamic surface intended to provide stability and control. Most aircraft feature an empennage (tail) incorporating vertical and horizontal stabilising surfaces, which stabilise the flight dynamics of yaw and pitch respectively, as well as housing control surfaces.

In spite of effective control surfaces, it was found that a large degree of stability could not be achieved with a wing alone, because there is an inevitable tendency for the plane to turn upside-down or sideways. A stabilizer was therefore essential for controlled flight.

Horizontal stabilizer

A horizontal stabilizer is used to maintain the aircraft in longitudinal balance, or trim: it exerts a vertical force at a distance so the summation of pitch moments about the center of gravity is zero. The vertical force exerted by the stabilizer varies with flight conditions, in particular according to the aircraft lift coefficient and wing flaps deflection which both affect the position of the center of pressure, and with the speed and movement of the aircraft elevator control.

On many modern airliners the horizontal stabilizer can be pivoted to change its angle of incidence in flight (an all-moving or trimmable stabilizer, sometimes called a stabilator when combined with the elevator function). This allows the aircraft to be trimmed over a wide range of center-of-gravity positions and speeds.

Vertical stabilizer

The vertical stabilizer, or fin, is the static part of the vertical tail of an aircraft. It provides weathercock stability in yaw, preventing the aircraft from swinging its nose from side to side. The rudder is usually hinged to the rear of the vertical stabilizer and is used by the pilot to control yaw.

Aircraft designs such as the Northrop B-2 Spirit flying wing omit a conventional tail altogether and rely on other means, including computerized flight controls, for stability. See also tailplane and empennage.
