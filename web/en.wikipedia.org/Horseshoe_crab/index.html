Horseshoe crab

Horseshoe crabs are marine and brackish-water arthropods of the family Limulidae, the only living members of the order Xiphosura. Despite their name, they are not true crabs or crustaceans but chelicerates, more closely related to arachnids such as spiders and scorpions. Four species survive today, including the Atlantic horseshoe crab (Limulus polyphemus) of the American Atlantic coast and three species in Asia.

Biology

Horseshoe crabs have a hard exoskeleton divided into a large rounded front section (prosoma), a hinged rear section (opisthosoma), and a long pointed tail spine (telson), which is used for righting themselves and is not a weapon. They have ten eyes, including lateral compound eyes and simple median eyes that can detect ultraviolet light. They feed on worms, molluscs, and other small bottom-dwelling animals. Females are larger than males and come ashore on sandy beaches in spring to lay tens of thousands of eggs, which are an important food for migrating shorebirds such as the red knot.

"Living fossils" and medical use

Horseshoe crabs have changed little over hundreds of millions of years and are often called living fossils; their fossil record extends back some 480 million years. Their blue, copper-based blood contains amebocytes that clot in the presence of bacterial endotoxins. An extract, Limulus amebocyte lysate (LAL), is widely used to test medical equipment and injectable drugs for bacterial contamination, and hundreds of thousands of crabs are collected and bled annually, most being returned to the sea alive, though with some mortality. Conservation concerns over declining populations have encouraged the adoption of synthetic alternatives.
