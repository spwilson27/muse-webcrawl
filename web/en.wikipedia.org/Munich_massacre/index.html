Munich massacre

Date: 5–6 September 1972
Location: Munich, West Germany
Perpetrators: Black September Organization
Deaths: 17 (11 Israeli team members, 1 police officer, 5 attackers)

The Munich massacre was a terrorist attack during the 1972 Summer Olympics in Munich, West Germany, in which the Palestinian militant group Black September took eleven members of the Israeli Olympic team hostage and ultimately killed them, along with a West German police officer. Five of the eight gunmen were killed during a failed rescue attempt at Fürstenfeldbruck airbase.

The attack

In the early hours of 5 September 1972, eight Black September members scaled the fence of the Olympic Village and entered the Israeli apartments at 31 Connollystraße. Wrestling coach Moshe Weinberg and weightlifter Yossef Romano were killed resisting; nine Israelis were taken hostage. The attackers demanded the release of more than 200 prisoners held in Israel, a demand Israel refused. German negotiators, hampered by poor preparation, inadequate police training and constitutional restrictions on using the army, offered money and substitutions, which were refused.

Failed rescue

Under a ruse promising transport to Cairo, the gunmen and hostages were flown by helicopter to Fürstenfeldbruck airbase, where German snipers — without radios, night-vision equipment or adequate numbers — opened fire. In the ensuing gun battle the attackers killed all nine remaining hostages, throwing a grenade into one helicopter and shooting the occupants of the other. A German police officer and five gunmen also died. Three surviving attackers were captured but released weeks later after the hijacking of Lufthansa Flight 615.

Aftermath

The Games were suspended for 34 hours; a memorial service was held in the Olympic Stadium, but IOC president Avery Brundage declared that "the Games must go on." Israel responded with air strikes and Operation Wrath of God, a years-long campaign to assassinate those involved in the attack. The massacre transformed security at international sporting events, led Germany to create the GSG 9 counter-terrorism unit, and remains one of the defining tragedies of modern Olympic history. At the Tokyo 2020 Games, held in 2021, the victims were officially commemorated in an Olympic opening ceremony for the first time.
