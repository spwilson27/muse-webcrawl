Forensic pathology

Forensic pathology is pathology that focuses on determining the cause of death by examining a corpse. A post mortem examination is performed by a medical examiner or forensic pathologist, usually during the investigation of criminal law cases and civil law cases in some jurisdictions. Coroners and medical examiners are also frequently asked to confirm the identity of remains.

Duties

The forensic pathologist performs autopsies to determine the cause, mechanism and manner of death. The manner of death is classified as natural, accident, suicide, homicide, or undetermined. Forensic pathologists document injuries, collect trace evidence and tissue samples for toxicology and histology, estimate the time since death, and provide expert testimony in court. They also investigate sudden unexpected deaths, deaths in custody, and deaths occurring during medical procedures.

History

The roots of forensic pathology lie in medieval European legal medicine; systematic medicolegal autopsies were performed in China as described in the Collected Cases of Injustice Rectified (1248). In the modern era, figures such as Rudolf Virchow established cellular pathology, and in the twentieth century forensic pathology became a recognized subspecialty of anatomic pathology, with board certification in the United States beginning in 1959.

Training

In the United States, a forensic pathologist must complete medical school, a residency in anatomic pathology, and a fellowship in forensic pathology — typically more than a decade of postgraduate training — and pass certification examinations administered by the American Board of Pathology.

Methods

Beyond the external and internal examination, forensic pathologists use postmortem imaging ("virtopsy" with CT and MRI), toxicological analysis of blood and tissues, histology, and increasingly molecular autopsy — genetic testing to identify inherited cardiac or metabolic conditions responsible for sudden death.
