Dark fantasy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Dark fantasy is a subgenre of fantasy literary, artistic, and cinematic works that incorporates disturbing and frightening themes. The term is often used to describe works that combine fantasy with elements of horror, or that have a particularly dark, gloomy tone and a sense of dread. Depending on the definition, dark fantasy may emphasise supernatural horrors within a fantasy world, or the bleakness and moral ambiguity of the setting and characters rather than monsters.

Overview

Early roots of the subgenre lie in Gothic fiction and in the "weird fiction" of the pulp era, such as the stories of H. P. Lovecraft and Clark Ashton Smith, whose work blended fantasy settings with cosmic horror. Gertrude Barrows Bennett, writing as Francis Stevens, has been called the inventor of dark fantasy for novels such as The Citadel of Fear (1918). Karl Edward Wagner's anti-hero Kane stories in the 1970s are frequently cited as landmarks, and Charles L. Grant helped popularise the label in the 1980s.

Notable works

Modern dark fantasy includes Stephen King's The Dark Tower series, Michael Moorcock's Elric saga, Glen Cook's The Black Company, and George R. R. Martin's A Song of Ice and Fire, whose violence and political realism overlap with the related "grimdark" mode. Kentaro Miura's manga Berserk is a widely influential example in comics, and FromSoftware's video games Dark Souls and Bloodborne epitomise the subgenre in gaming, as do the gothic worlds of Warhammer and the Diablo series in popular culture.

Relation to horror and grimdark

The boundary between dark fantasy and horror is porous: publishers and critics tend to call a work dark fantasy when the fantastic elements (magic, invented worlds, quests) predominate over the aim of frightening the reader, and horror when fear predominates. "Grimdark", a term derived from the Warhammer 40,000 tagline "In the grim darkness of the far future there is only war", denotes a still bleaker register of dystopian violence and cynicism; "noblebright" and "hopepunk" have been proposed as its opposites.
