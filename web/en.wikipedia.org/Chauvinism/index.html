Chauvinism
Named afterNicolas Chauvin (legendary soldier)
Related conceptsNationalism, jingoism, sexism

Chauvinism is the unreasonable belief in the superiority or dominance of one's own group or people, who are seen as strong and virtuous, while others are weak, unworthy or inferior. It can describe excessive, belligerent patriotism and glorification of one's country, and by extension biased devotion to any group or cause.

Etymology

The word derives from Nicolas Chauvin, a possibly apocryphal soldier who served under Napoleon Bonaparte and was popularised by the Cogniard brothers' 1831 vaudeville La Cocarde tricolore, in which his blind devotion to the fallen emperor was played for comedy. In French, chauvinisme entered dictionaries by the 1840s.

Uses

In its original sense chauvinism is aggressive nationalism; British English developed the near-synonym "jingoism". In the 20th century the term was extended: Marxist writers spoke of "social chauvinism" among parties supporting their own governments in the First World War, and from the 1960s feminists popularised "male chauvinism" for beliefs in male superiority, giving rise to the phrase "male chauvinist pig". The word is now used for uncritical group loyalty of many kinds, including ethnic, religious and even brand or sports chauvinism.
