Blue wildebeest

The blue wildebeest (Connochaetes taurinus), also called the common wildebeest, white-bearded wildebeest, white-bearded gnu or brindled gnu, is a large antelope and one of the two species of wildebeest. It is placed in the genus Connochaetes and family Bovidae, and has a close taxonomic relationship with the black wildebeest. The blue wildebeest is known to have five subspecies. This broad-shouldered antelope has a muscular, front-heavy appearance, with a distinctive, robust muzzle.

Blue wildebeest herds undertake one of the largest terrestrial migrations on Earth.

Description

Adult blue wildebeest can range from 1.70 to 2.43 metres in head-and-body length and stand 1.15 to 1.45 metres at the shoulder. Males weigh up to about 290 kg and females up to 260 kg. The coat is slaty grey to brown with darker vertical stripes on the neck and flanks—the "brindled" pattern that gives the animal its bluish sheen. Both sexes have large, curved horns. The head is long with a broad muzzle adapted for grazing short grasses, and a dark beard runs along the throat.

Distribution and habitat

The blue wildebeest is native to Angola, Botswana, Eswatini, Mozambique, Namibia, South Africa, Kenya, Tanzania, Zambia, and Zimbabwe. It inhabits short-grass plains and acacia savannas, and depends on reliable access to water, generally needing to drink every day or two in the dry season.

Migration

The species is famed for the great circular migration of the Serengeti–Mara ecosystem, in which roughly 1.5 million wildebeest, accompanied by hundreds of thousands of zebras and gazelles, move continuously in search of fresh grazing and water, calving en masse in the southern Serengeti at the start of the year. The river crossings, where animals brave crocodile-infested waters, are among nature's most dramatic spectacles. Some populations, such as those in the Maasai Mara and Etosha, undertake shorter seasonal movements, while others are largely resident.

Ecology and reproduction

Blue wildebeest are grazers, feeding almost entirely on grasses. Rutting takes place at the end of the rainy season, when territorial bulls defend small temporary territories. Females give birth to a single calf after a gestation of about eight months; remarkably, the great majority of calves in migratory populations are born within a two-to-three-week window, swamping predators. Newborn calves can stand within minutes and run with the herd within days. Major predators include lions, spotted hyenas, leopards, cheetahs, and Nile crocodiles.
