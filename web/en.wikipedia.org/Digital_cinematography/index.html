Digital cinematography is the process of capturing motion pictures as digital images rather than on photochemical film stock. Cinematographers use digital cinema cameras with electronic sensors, recording to solid-state media in raw or lightly compressed formats; the footage is then edited, graded and finished through a fully digital post-production pipeline (the "digital intermediate"), and distributed as Digital Cinema Packages to digital projectors. By the early 2010s digital acquisition had overtaken 35 mm film on major productions worldwide.

History

Early experiments included the video-originated features of the 1980s and the Dogme 95 movement's consumer DV films such as The Celebration (1998). The turning point came with Sony's CineAlta HDW-F900, used by George Lucas for Star Wars: Episode II (2002), the first major studio feature shot entirely on 24p HD video. The Dalsa Origin and Thomson Viper pioneered 4K and raw recording; the Red One (2007) dramatically lowered the price of 4K raw capture, and the Arri Alexa (2010) won over leading cinematographers — Roger Deakins among them — with its highlight roll-off and dynamic range. Since Slumdog Millionaire (2008, partly digital) won the Academy Award for Best Cinematography and The Social Network and Skyfall followed, digitally shot films have won the majority of cinematography Oscars; by 2016 over 90% of major releases were captured digitally.

Technology and workflow

Digital cinema cameras record at resolutions from 2K to 8K and beyond, at high dynamic range, onto media such as CFast, CFexpress and proprietary SSD magazines, with data managed on set by a DIT (digital imaging technician). Shooting in "log" or raw preserves maximum information for colour grading. Advantages over film include immediate playback, longer recording times without magazine changes, high sensitivity allowing shooting in low light, compact camera bodies, and seamless integration with visual effects. Drawbacks raised by advocates of film include highlight clipping, file-based archival fragility, and the loss of film's texture and discipline on set; directors such as Christopher Nolan and Quentin Tarantino continue to shoot on film, and Kodak still manufactures stocks.

Reception

The digital transition reshaped exhibition as well: cinemas converted to DCP projection, saving the industry the cost of thousands of release prints, and enabled 3D, high-frame-rate and streaming-era workflows. Critics debated the aesthetic consequences — documentary realism in handheld work, the "clean" digital look, and the persistence of film grain emulations in grading — while festivals and archives grapple with the long-term preservation of born-digital works.
