The Requiem in D minor, K. 626, is a requiem mass by Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart (1756–1791). Mozart composed part of the work in Vienna in late 1791, but it was unfinished at his death on 5 December that year. A completed version dated 1792, by Franz Xaver Süssmayr, was delivered to Count Franz von Walsegg, who had commissioned it anonymously.

Commission and completion

In July 1791 a messenger, later identified as an agent of Count Walsegg, commissioned a requiem from Mozart; Walsegg, an amateur musician, intended to pass the work off as his own composition in memory of his late wife. Mozart, already ill, worked on the Requiem alongside The Magic Flute and La clemenza di Tito and reportedly came to believe he was writing it for his own funeral. On his death, only the Introitus was fully orchestrated, and several sections existed as sketches with vocal parts, bass line, and indications of instrumentation. To secure the final payment, Mozart's widow Constanze had the work completed, first by Joseph Eybler and then by Süssmayr, Mozart's pupil and copyist, whose completion remains the standard performing version despite later scholarly alternatives.

Structure

The work follows the Latin requiem mass: Introitus (Requiem aeternam), Kyrie, the Sequence (Dies irae, Tuba mirum, Rex tremendae, Recordare, Confutatis, Lacrimosa), Offertorium (Domine Jesu, Hostias), Sanctus, Benedictus, Agnus Dei, and Communio (Lux aeterna). The Lacrimosa breaks off in the autograph after eight bars, the point traditionally identified as among the last music Mozart wrote. The sombre scoring — no flutes, oboes, or trumpets in bright registers, but basset horns, bassoons, trombones, and strings — gives the work its distinctive dark colour.

Reception

The Requiem is among Mozart's most performed and recorded works, central to his posthumous reputation and surrounded by legends about his death. Modern editions by scholars including H. C. Robbins Landon, Richard Maunder, and Robert D. Levin have sought to complete the work using more of Mozart's surviving sketches.
