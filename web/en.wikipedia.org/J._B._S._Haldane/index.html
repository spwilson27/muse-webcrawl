John Burdon Sanderson Haldane (5 November 1892 – 1 December 1964), known as J. B. S. Haldane, was a British-born scientist who later became an Indian citizen. He is known for his work in physiology, genetics, evolutionary biology, and mathematics — particularly as one of the three principal founders of population genetics, alongside Ronald Fisher and Sewall Wright. He also made important contributions to enzyme kinetics and was a prominent populariser of science.

Scientific work

In a celebrated series of papers, A Mathematical Theory of Natural and Artificial Selection (1924–1934), Haldane showed mathematically how natural selection could account for the spread of advantageous genes, helping to reconcile Mendelian genetics with Darwinian evolution in what became the modern synthesis. His 1932 book The Causes of Evolution summarised the new field. With George Edward Briggs he derived the Briggs–Haldane equation of enzyme kinetics (1925), a refinement of Michaelis–Menten kinetics. Independently of Aleksandr Oparin, he proposed in 1929 that life originated from a "primordial soup" of organic chemicals energised by ultraviolet light — the Oparin–Haldane hypothesis. "Haldane's rule" observes that in hybrid offspring, the absent, rare, or sterile sex is the heterogametic sex.

Life

Haldane was the son of the physiologist John Scott Haldane, with whom he famously experimented on himself, including in decompression research for divers. He served with distinction in the First World War. He held chairs at Cambridge and University College London, and his students included Helen Spurway and John Maynard Smith. A committed Marxist for much of his life, he grew disillusioned over Lysenkoism, and in 1956 he emigrated to India, where he worked at the Indian Statistical Institute in Calcutta and later in Bhubaneswar, becoming an Indian citizen in 1961. He was celebrated for his wit — remarking that the Creator, if He exists, must have "an inordinate fondness for beetles", and for his essay On Being the Right Size. His popular science books include Daedalus (1924), Possible Worlds (1927), and The Inequality of Man (1932). Asked what his studies of evolution had taught him of God, he quipped about beetles; asked what he would risk his life for, he answered: "two brothers or eight cousins" — an anticipation of kin selection theory.
