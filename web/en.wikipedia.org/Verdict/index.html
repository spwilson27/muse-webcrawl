Verdict

In law, a verdict is the formal decision or finding made by a jury, or in a bench trial by a judge, on the matters or questions submitted to it in a trial. The word comes from the Latin veredictum, "truly said". In common law systems the verdict in a criminal case is typically "guilty" or "not guilty"; Scots law historically also allowed the verdict of "not proven".

Types of verdict

A general verdict states simply which party prevails and, in civil cases, the amount of damages. A special verdict instead answers specific questions of fact posed by the judge, who then applies the law to those answers. A directed verdict is ordered by the judge when no reasonable jury could reach a different conclusion on the evidence. In some jurisdictions juries may also deliver verdicts on separate counts individually, producing partial verdicts, and a jury that cannot agree produces a "hung jury", often leading to a retrial.

Process

After closing arguments and the judge's instructions on the law, the jury retires to deliberate in private. In many jurisdictions criminal verdicts must be unanimous, though some allow majority verdicts after extended deliberation. Once delivered in open court, the verdict is usually polled at the request of either party. The court then enters judgment on the verdict, and in criminal cases proceeds to sentencing. Verdicts may be challenged on appeal, and a judge may set aside a verdict that is against the weight of the evidence or enter a judgment notwithstanding the verdict in civil cases.
