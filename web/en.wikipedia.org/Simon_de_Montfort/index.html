Simon de Montfort, 6th Earl of Leicester

Bornc. 1208, Montfort-l'Amaury, France
Died4 August 1265, Evesham, England
Known forLeading the baronial revolt against Henry III; summoning the 1265 parliament

Simon de Montfort, 6th Earl of Leicester (c. 1208 – 4 August 1265), was a French-born English nobleman who inherited the earldom of Leicester and became a leading opponent of King Henry III. He is remembered as the effective ruler of England for just over a year in 1264–65 and for summoning the first parliament in England to include representatives of the towns — an assembly of January 1265 often cited as a forerunner of the modern Parliament.

Life and revolt

The younger son of Simon de Montfort, 5th Earl of Leicester, leader of the Albigensian Crusade, Simon came to England in 1229 to claim his father's English earldom. He married the king's sister Eleanor in 1238, secretly and controversially, and later served as Henry's lieutenant in Gascony, where his harsh government made enemies. Relations with the king deteriorated, and by 1258 Montfort had become a leader of the baronial reform movement that imposed the Provisions of Oxford on Henry.

When Henry repudiated the Provisions, civil war followed — the Second Barons' War. At the Battle of Lewes on 14 May 1264 Montfort defeated and captured the king and his son, the Lord Edward. As de facto ruler he summoned the parliament of 1265, extending representation to burgesses from the towns. His regime soon fractured: allies such as the Earl of Gloucester defected, and Edward escaped. Montfort was defeated and killed at the Battle of Evesham on 4 August 1265, and his body was mutilated by royalist troops.

Legacy

Despite his death in rebellion, Montfort was venerated as a saint and martyr by many contemporaries, and miracles were reported at his tomb. Historians have long debated whether his constitutional experiments sprang from principle or ambition; either way, the parliaments he convened left a lasting mark on English government.
