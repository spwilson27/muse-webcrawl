A fungus (plural: fungi) is any member of the group of eukaryotic organisms that includes yeasts, molds and mushrooms. Fungi constitute their own kingdom, distinct from plants, animals and bacteria: unlike plants they do not photosynthesise, instead absorbing dissolved organic matter from their environment, and their cell walls are made of chitin, as in insect exoskeletons.

Structure and reproduction

Most fungi grow as networks of thread-like hyphae, collectively called a mycelium, which may spread over enormous areas — a honey fungus (Armillaria) in Oregon is among the largest and oldest organisms on Earth. Fungi reproduce by spores, both sexually and asexually; the familiar mushroom is merely the spore-producing fruiting body of a much larger underground organism.

Ecology and uses

Fungi are the principal decomposers of dead organic matter in most ecosystems, recycling nutrients from wood and leaf litter. They form symbioses such as mycorrhizae with plant roots and lichens with algae. Humans use fungi to produce bread, beer and wine (yeasts), cheeses, and medicines — the antibiotic penicillin came from a Penicillium mold. Other fungi are serious pathogens of crops and, increasingly, of humans, or produce potent toxins such as aflatoxin.

Diversity

About 150,000 species have been described, grouped into phyla including Ascomycota (sac fungi, the largest group), Basidiomycota (club fungi, including most mushrooms), and Chytridiomycota. Estimates of the true total range into the millions, most of them microscopic and undescribed. The study of fungi is mycology.
