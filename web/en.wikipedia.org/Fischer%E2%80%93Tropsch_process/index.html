Fischer–Tropsch process

TypeChemical process
InventorsFranz Fischer and Hans Tropsch
Year1925 (patented)
InputsCarbon monoxide and hydrogen (syngas)
OutputsLiquid hydrocarbons, waxes

The Fischer–Tropsch process is a collection of chemical reactions that converts a mixture of carbon monoxide and hydrogen, known as synthesis gas or syngas, into liquid hydrocarbons. Developed by Franz Fischer and Hans Tropsch in Germany in the 1920s, it is a key component of gas-to-liquids and coal-to-liquids technology.

Chemistry

The overall reaction can be summarised as (2n + 1) H2 + n CO → CnH2n+2 + n H2O. The reactions occur over metal catalysts, typically iron or cobalt, at temperatures of roughly 150–300 °C and elevated pressures. The syngas feedstock can be derived from coal, natural gas, or biomass.

History and use

Germany used Fischer–Tropsch synthesis extensively during the Second World War to produce fuel from coal. After the war, the process was developed at large scale in South Africa by Sasol, where coal- and gas-derived synthetic fuels remain important. More recent plants in Qatar and elsewhere convert natural gas into low-sulphur diesel and other products, and the process is of renewed interest for producing sustainable aviation fuel from biomass or captured carbon.
