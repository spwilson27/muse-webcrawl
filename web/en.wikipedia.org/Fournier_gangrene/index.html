Fournier gangrene

Fournier gangrene is a type of necrotizing fasciitis (a rapidly spreading bacterial infection of the fascia and soft tissues) affecting the perineum, genitalia, and perianal region. It is a urological emergency with a high mortality rate, requiring immediate surgical debridement and broad-spectrum antibiotics. The condition is named after Jean Alfred Fournier, a French venereologist who described five cases in 1883.

Causes and risk factors

The infection is usually polymicrobial, involving both aerobic and anaerobic bacteria — including streptococci, staphylococci, Escherichia coli, and Clostridium species — acting synergistically. Bacteria enter through colorectal sources (such as perianal abscesses), urinary tract infections or instrumentation, or minor skin trauma. Risk factors include diabetes mellitus (the most common association), alcoholism, immunosuppression, obesity, chronic liver or kidney disease, and malignancy. In 2019 the US Food and Drug Administration warned that SGLT2 inhibitor diabetes drugs are associated with rare cases of the condition. Men are affected about ten times as often as women, most commonly between the ages of 50 and 60, though women and children can also develop it.

Signs, diagnosis and treatment

Early signs include severe perineal pain out of proportion to physical findings, swelling, redness, and fever; as the infection advances, the skin may blister, discolour, and develop crepitus (gas in the tissues) and frank gangrene, accompanied by sepsis. Diagnosis is primarily clinical, supported by imaging such as CT, which can show gas along fascial planes; the LRINEC score may assist in distinguishing necrotizing infection from cellulitis. Treatment consists of urgent resuscitation, broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics, and immediate, often repeated, surgical removal of all necrotic tissue; hyperbaric oxygen and negative-pressure wound therapy are adjuncts, and reconstructive surgery is often needed later. Even with treatment, reported mortality ranges from roughly 20% to over 40%, rising steeply when surgery is delayed.
