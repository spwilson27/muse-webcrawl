Stephen Cook formalised the concept of NP-completeness

Stephen Arthur Cook (born 14 December 1939) is an American-Canadian computer scientist and mathematician who has made foundational contributions to computational complexity theory and proof complexity. A professor at the University of Toronto since 1970, he received the Turing Award in 1982 for his paper "The complexity of theorem-proving procedures" (1971), which introduced the concept of NP-completeness and posed the framework behind the P versus NP problem.

Work

In the 1971 paper, Cook showed that the Boolean satisfiability problem (SAT) is NP-complete: every problem in NP can be reduced to it in polynomial time, so an efficient algorithm for SAT would yield efficient algorithms for all NP problems. Leonid Levin independently reached an equivalent result in the Soviet Union, and the result is known as the Cook–Levin theorem. Karp's subsequent list of 21 NP-complete problems made the concept central to computer science. Cook's 1979 paper with Robert Reckhow founded proof complexity, the study of the lengths of proofs in propositional proof systems, connected to his work on the complexity class NP and coNP.

Career

Cook earned his PhD from Harvard in 1966 and taught at UC Berkeley before being denied tenure, a decision later widely regretted; he moved to Toronto, where he spent the rest of his career. He supervised numerous students and helped build Toronto into a leading centre for theoretical computer science. His son, the economist and writer, is among a family of achievers; Cook has been described by colleagues as the originator of complexity theory's central questions.
