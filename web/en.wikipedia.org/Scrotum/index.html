Scrotum

In most terrestrial mammals, the scrotum (pl.: scrotums or scrota) is a sac of skin and muscle that contains the testicles. It is located behind the penis and below the pubic symphysis. The scrotum keeps the testes at a temperature slightly below that of the rest of the body, which is necessary for normal sperm production.

Structure

The scrotum is divided internally into two compartments by the scrotal septum, each containing one testicle, epididymis, and the lower part of the spermatic cord. Its wall contains the dartos muscle, whose contraction wrinkles the skin and draws the testes closer to the body in cold conditions; the cremaster muscle similarly raises the testes. These muscles, together with the pampiniform plexus of veins, regulate testicular temperature.

Development

The scrotum develops from the labioscrotal swellings of the embryo, which are homologous to the labia majora in females. The testes descend from the abdomen through the inguinal canal into the scrotum, normally before birth; failure of descent is known as cryptorchidism.

Clinical significance

Conditions affecting the scrotum include hydrocele (a collection of fluid), varicocele (enlarged veins), epididymitis, testicular torsion — a surgical emergency in which the spermatic cord twists — and scrotal masses that may indicate testicular cancer.
