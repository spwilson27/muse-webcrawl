Habeas corpus

Habeas corpus
Latin"You shall have the body [in court]"
TypeLegal writ / prerogative writ
PurposeChallenge unlawful detention before a court
Key statutesHabeas Corpus Act 1679 (England); U.S. Constitution, Art. I §9
Related conceptsDue process, amparo, rule of law

Habeas corpus is a legal writ requiring that a person under arrest be brought before a judge or court, so that the lawfulness of their detention can be examined and, if no sufficient cause is shown, their release ordered. A cornerstone of civil liberty in common-law systems, it protects against arbitrary imprisonment by the executive.

History

The writ developed in medieval England and was famously strengthened by the Habeas Corpus Act 1679, passed after governments evaded the remedy by procedural delay or by sending prisoners overseas. The right appears in the United States Constitution, which permits suspension only "when in Cases of Rebellion or Invasion the public Safety may require it". Abraham Lincoln's suspension during the Civil War and detentions after the September 11 attacks — tested in cases such as Rasul v. Bush (2004) and Boumediene v. Bush (2008), which extended habeas to Guantánamo detainees — remain landmark controversies.

Modern use

Today habeas petitions are also the principal vehicle by which convicted prisoners in the United States challenge their convictions in federal court after direct appeals, and analogous remedies exist in many legal systems, including the Latin American amparo.
