The occupation of Denmark by Nazi Germany during the Second World War lasted from Operation Weserübung on 9 April 1940 until the German surrender on 5 May 1945. Denmark capitulated after only a few hours of fighting, and for the first years the Danish government and King Christian X remained in place under a policy of negotiation, making Denmark an unusual case among occupied countries.

Cooperation and its collapse

Germany treated Denmark as a "model protectorate," leaving Danish institutions intact in exchange for agricultural supplies and quiet. As resistance grew, tensions escalated; in August 1943, after strikes and sabotage, the Germans dissolved the Danish government, scuttled much of the Danish navy (whose sailors sank their own ships), and imposed direct rule. In October 1943, Danish civil society famously helped ferry most of Denmark's roughly 7,800 Jews to safety in neutral Sweden, one of the most successful rescue efforts of the Holocaust.

Liberation

Denmark was liberated in May 1945, though the island of Bornholm was bombed and occupied by Soviet forces until 1946. The wartime experience reshaped Danish foreign policy, ending its traditional neutrality and leading it into NATO in 1949.
