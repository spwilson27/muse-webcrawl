Material conditional

Material conditional
Symbol: → (or ⊃)
Form: "if p then q"
False only when p is true and q is false
Equivalent: ¬p ∨ q

The material conditional (also known as material implication) is a binary operation used in logic and mathematics, typically denoted p → q and read "if p then q". It is defined purely by truth values: the conditional is false only when the antecedent p is true and the consequent q is false; in all other cases it is true. Equivalently, p → q has the same truth value as ¬p ∨ q ("not p, or q"). The material conditional is the standard interpretation of the conditional in classical logic and Boolean algebra.

Paradoxes of material implication

Because a conditional with a false antecedent is automatically true, the material conditional validates inferences that seem odd for natural-language "if…then": "If the Moon is made of cheese, then Paris is in France" counts as true, since the antecedent is false. These so-called paradoxes of material implication motivated the development of alternatives such as strict implication, relevance logic, and the analysis of conditionals in terms of possible worlds (as in the work of Robert Stalnaker and David Lewis). Defenders reply that the truth-functional conditional simply captures less than everyday conditionals, which often carry causal or evidential suggestions not part of their literal content.

Use

In mathematics, the material conditional underlies ordinary proof practice: a universally quantified conditional such as "if n is divisible by 4, then n is even" is true precisely because there is no case making the antecedent true and the consequent false. In computing it corresponds to implication operations in logic design and to the semantics of conditional expressions in formal verification.
