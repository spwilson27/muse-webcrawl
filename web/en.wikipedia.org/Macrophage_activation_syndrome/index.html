Macrophage activation syndrome

Macrophage activation syndrome (MAS) is a severe, potentially life-threatening complication of chronic rheumatic diseases of childhood and adulthood, most often systemic juvenile idiopathic arthritis. It is considered a form of secondary haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH), in which uncontrolled activation of macrophages and T lymphocytes produces a hyperinflammatory "cytokine storm" with fever, cytopenias, liver dysfunction, coagulopathy, and multi-organ failure.

Haemophagocytosis — an activated macrophage engulfing blood cells — is a characteristic, though not required, finding.

Signs and symptoms

MAS typically begins with unremitting high fever, a rash, enlargement of the liver and spleen, and lymphadenopathy, and can progress within days to liver failure, disseminated intravascular coagulation, seizures, coma, and shock. Triggers include infections (especially viruses such as EBV), disease flares, and occasionally medication changes.

Diagnosis

Paradoxically, markers of the underlying arthritis may fall as MAS develops: platelet counts and the erythrocyte sedimentation rate drop while ferritin rises to extreme levels (often above 10,000 µg/L). Other features include raised triglycerides and liver enzymes, falling fibrinogen, and haemophagocytosis on bone-marrow examination. The 2016 classification criteria for MAS in systemic juvenile idiopathic arthritis require a ferritin above 684 ng/mL plus two of: platelets ≤181 × 10⁹/L, AST above 48 U/L, triglycerides above 156 mg/dL, and fibrinogen ≤360 mg/dL. The HScore is used to estimate HLH probability in adults.

Pathophysiology

Defective cytotoxic killing by NK cells and CD8 T cells may prevent normal clearance of activated antigen-presenting cells, sustaining interferon-γ and interleukin-1/6/18 signalling. Extremely high IL-18 levels are characteristic of Still's disease-associated MAS, and heterozygous variants in familial HLH genes (such as PRF1 and UNC13D) are over-represented in patients who develop it.

Treatment

Early recognition is crucial; reported mortality is around 8–20% or higher in delayed cases. First-line therapy is high-dose intravenous methylprednisolone, often with ciclosporin A. The interleukin-1 receptor antagonist anakinra is widely used and well tolerated, and etoposide-based HLH protocols or emapalumab (anti-interferon-γ) are options in refractory disease. Supportive care and treatment of any triggering infection are essential.
