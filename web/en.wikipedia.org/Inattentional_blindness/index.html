Inattentional blindness

Inattentional blindness
Field: Cognitive psychology
Definition: Failure to notice a fully visible but unexpected object
Famous study: "Invisible gorilla" experiment (1999)

Inattentional blindness is the failure to notice a fully visible object when attention is devoted to something else. It is a psychological lack of attention that is not associated with any vision defects or deficits.

The invisible gorilla

The phenomenon became famous through an experiment by Daniel Simons and Christopher Chabris (1999). Participants counting basketball passes between players in white shirts often failed to notice a person in a gorilla suit walking through the scene, thumping her chest, and leaving—although the gorilla was on screen for several seconds. Roughly half of observers missed it.

Factors

The likelihood of missing an unexpected object depends on the difficulty of the primary task, the similarity of the object to attended items, expectations, and mental workload. Expertise does not eliminate the effect: in one study, most radiologists searching lung scans for nodules missed a gorilla image embedded in the scan.

Implications

Inattentional blindness matters for driving (including the danger of phone use), aviation, security screening, and eyewitness reliability. It underlies the common but mistaken belief that we would notice anything important that happens in front of us. See also Change blindness and Attention.
