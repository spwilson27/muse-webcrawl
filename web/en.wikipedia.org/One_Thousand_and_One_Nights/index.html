One Thousand and One Nights (Arabic: Alf Layla wa-Layla), known in English also as the Arabian Nights, is a collection of Middle Eastern and South Asian folk tales compiled in Arabic over many centuries by numerous authors and translators. Its famous frame story tells of the vizier's daughter Scheherazade, who saves her life by telling King Shahryar a new tale each night, breaking off at dawn at a cliffhanger so that he spares her to hear the ending — for a thousand and one nights.

Contents and origins

The collection grew from a Persian book, the lost Hazār Afsān ("A Thousand Tales"), itself influenced by Indian story collections, and was reshaped in Baghdad and later Cairo with tales of caliphs (including Harun al-Rashid), merchants, jinn, sailors and tricksters. Famous stories include "Aladdin and the Magic Lamp", "Ali Baba and the Forty Thieves" and "The Seven Voyages of Sinbad the Sailor" — though Aladdin and Ali Baba appear in no early Arabic manuscript: they were added by the first European translator, Antoine Galland, who heard them from the Syrian storyteller Hanna Diyab.

Translations and influence

Galland's French version (1704–1717) introduced the Nights to Europe and shaped Western images of the Middle East for centuries. Later English translations by Edward Lane and Richard Burton (the latter's unexpurgated 16-volume edition of 1885–88) spread the tales further. The Nights have influenced literature from Chaucer and Boccaccio to Borges and Rushdie, and countless films, pantomimes and adaptations; their nested frame-tale structure remains a landmark of world literature.
