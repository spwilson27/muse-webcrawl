Great Library of Alexandria

An artist's impression of the ancient Great Library

The Great Library of Alexandria, in Alexandria, Egypt, was one of the largest and most significant libraries of the ancient world. The library was part of a larger research institution known as the Mouseion, which was dedicated to the Muses, the nine goddesses of the arts. The idea of a universal library in Alexandria may have been proposed by Demetrius of Phalerum, an exiled Athenian statesman, to Ptolemy I Soter, who may have established plans for the library, but the library itself was probably not built until the reign of his son, Ptolemy II Philadelphus (285–246 BC).

Collections and scholarship

The Ptolemies aggressively acquired texts: ships docking at Alexandria were searched, books found aboard were copied (and sometimes the originals kept), and agents bought up collections abroad. Estimates of the holdings range widely, from tens of thousands to several hundred thousand scrolls. The library attracted the leading scholars of the Hellenistic world: Zenodotus, its first librarian, edited Homer; Callimachus compiled the Pinakes, a 120-book catalogue considered the foundation of literary bibliography; Eratosthenes used the library's resources to measure the Earth's circumference; Aristarchus of Samothrace established critical editions of the poets; and Euclid and Herophilus are also associated with Alexandrian scholarship.

Decline and destruction

Contrary to the popular image of a single catastrophic fire, the library declined over several centuries. Ptolemy VIII expelled many scholars in 145 BC; part of the collection may have burned accidentally when Julius Caesar set fire to the Alexandrian fleet in 48 BC; and the Mouseion suffered as Roman patronage waned. A "daughter library" in the Serapeum temple survived longer but was likely destroyed when the Serapeum was demolished by a Christian mob in 391 AD. Later stories blaming Caliph Omar for burning the library after the Muslim conquest of 642 are regarded by historians as legends.

Legacy

The Great Library became the archetype of the universal library and a symbol of lost knowledge. Its modern namesake, the Bibliotheca Alexandrina, opened in Alexandria in 2002 near the presumed site of the ancient institution.
