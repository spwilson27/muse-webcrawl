Saladin

Ṣalāḥ al-Dīn Yūsuf ibn Ayyūb (c. 1137 – 4 March 1193), commonly known in the West as Saladin, was a Kurdish Muslim leader who founded the Ayyubid dynasty. He united Egypt, Syria and much of the Jazira under his rule, defeated the Crusader Kingdom of Jerusalem and recaptured Jerusalem in 1187, becoming the pre-eminent Muslim figure of the Crusades era.

Early career

Saladin was born in Tikrit (in modern Iraq) to a Kurdish family; his father, Najm al-Dīn Ayyūb, governed Baalbek and later served the Zengid ruler Nūr al-Dīn. Saladin accompanied his uncle Shīrkūh on campaigns to Egypt, and on Shīrkūh's death in 1169 became vizier to the Fatimid caliph. In 1171 he abolished the Shi'a Fatimid caliphate and proclaimed Sunni rule in the name of the Abbasid caliph of Baghdad.

Unification and jihad

On Nūr al-Dīn's death in 1174, Saladin moved into Syria, taking Damascus and, after years of negotiation and conflict with Zengid successors and fellow Muslim rivals, Aleppo (1183) and Mosul's submission (1186). Presenting himself as champion of jihad against the Franks, he provoked war with Jerusalem after Raynald of Châtillon's attacks on caravans, destroyed the Crusader field army at the Battle of Hattin on 4 July 1187, and accepted Jerusalem's surrender on 2 October — sparing its inhabitants in deliberate contrast to the Crusader massacre of 1099.

The Third Crusade and death

The Third Crusade (1189–1192) under Richard I of England recovered Acre and the Palestinian coast, but Richard twice declined to besiege Jerusalem. The Treaty of Jaffa (September 1192) ended the war; the two rulers exchanged courtesies and Richard even proposed a marriage alliance between his sister Joan and Saladin's brother al-ʿĀdil. Saladin died in Damascus months later, on 4 March 1193, having given away so much of his personal fortune in charity that little remained to pay for his funeral. He was buried in a mausoleum beside the Umayyad Mosque.

Legacy

Saladin is remembered in the Islamic world as a hero of unity and in the West, unusually for a Crusades-era Muslim leader, as a model of chivalry — Dante placed him among the virtuous pagans in Limbo, and Walter Scott made him a hero of The Talisman. His empire fragmented among his heirs, the Ayyubids, after his death.
