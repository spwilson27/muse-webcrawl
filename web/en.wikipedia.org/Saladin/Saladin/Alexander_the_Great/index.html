Alexander the Great

Alexander at the Battle of Issus (Alexander Mosaic)

Alexander III of Macedon (356 – 323 BC), commonly known as Alexander the Great, was a king of the ancient Greek kingdom of Macedon. He succeeded his father Philip II to the throne in 336 BC at the age of 20 and spent most of his ruling years conducting a lengthy military campaign throughout Western Asia, Central Asia, parts of South Asia, and Egypt. By the age of 30, he had created one of the largest empires in history, stretching from Greece to northwestern India. He was undefeated in battle and is widely considered to be one of history's greatest and most successful military commanders.

Early life and accession

Alexander was born in Pella, the capital of Macedon, and was tutored by Aristotle until the age of 16. After Philip II was assassinated in 336 BC, Alexander inherited a strong kingdom and an experienced army, and swiftly secured the Greek city-states, destroying Thebes as a warning to potential rebels. He was awarded the generalship of Greece and used this authority to launch his father's project of a pan-Hellenic invasion of the Achaemenid Persian Empire.

Conquest of the Persian Empire

In 334 BC Alexander invaded Asia Minor and defeated the Persian satraps at the Granicus River. He then marched down the Phoenician coast, won the decisive Battle of Issus (333 BC) against King Darius III, and besieged Tyre and Gaza before being welcomed into Egypt, where he founded the city of Alexandria in 331 BC and was hailed as a liberator and son of the god Amun. At Gaugamela (331 BC) he destroyed Darius's last great army, then took Babylon, Susa, and Persepolis, whose palace he burned. By 330 BC the Persian Empire was his.

Final campaigns and death

Alexander campaigned in Bactria and Sogdia, married the Bactrian noblewoman Roxana, and in 326 BC invaded India, defeating King Porus at the Hydaspes. His exhausted troops mutinied at the Hyphasis River and forced him to turn back; he led part of the army through the Gedrosian Desert with heavy losses. Planning further conquests, he died in Babylon in 323 BC, aged 32, of a fever whose cause remains debated. Having named no clear successor, his empire was partitioned among his generals, the Diadochi, whose Hellenistic kingdoms spread Greek language and culture across the Near East for centuries.
