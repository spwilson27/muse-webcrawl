Saladin

Saladin (Arabic: صلاح الدين يوسف بن أيوب, Ṣalāḥ ad-Dīn Yūsuf ibn Ayyūb; Kurdish: Selahedînê Eyûbî; 1137 – 4 March 1193) was a Kurdish Muslim leader who founded the Ayyubid dynasty. He united Egypt, Syria, and much of Mesopotamia under his rule and led Muslim resistance to the Crusader states of the Levant, recapturing Jerusalem in 1187 and prompting the Third Crusade. Even among his opponents he became legendary for chivalry and magnanimity.

Early life and rise in Egypt

Saladin (Salah al-Din, "Righteousness of the Faith") was born in Tikrit to a Kurdish family in the service of the Zengid rulers of Mosul. He accompanied his uncle Shirkuh on Nur al-Din's expeditions to Fatimid Egypt; after Shirkuh's death in 1169, Saladin became vizier of Egypt. In 1171 he abolished the Shia Fatimid caliphate and restored Sunni rule, and after Nur al-Din's death in 1174 he moved to take control of Syria in his own name.

Unification of Egypt and Syria

Over the following decade Saladin fought fellow Muslim rulers as much as Crusaders, bringing Damascus, Aleppo (1183), and Mosul's allegiance into a single Ayyubid confederation controlling Egypt, Syria, the Hejaz, Yemen, and parts of Mesopotamia. He built the Cairo Citadel and patronized Sunni madrasas, consolidating his legitimacy as champion of orthodoxy and jihad.

War with the Crusader states

Truces alternated with warfare throughout the 1180s. In July 1187 Saladin destroyed the army of the Kingdom of Jerusalem at the Battle of Hattin, capturing King Guy of Lusignan and the relic of the True Cross. City after city fell; on 2 October 1187, after a short siege, Jerusalem surrendered on terms: citizens could ransom themselves, and the holy places of all faiths were respected — in deliberate contrast to the Crusader massacre of 1099.

The Third Crusade

The fall of Jerusalem provoked the Third Crusade (1189–1192), led by Richard I of England, Philip II of France, and Frederick Barbarossa (who drowned en route). The Crusaders retook Acre (1191) and won at Arsuf, but Richard could not retake Jerusalem. The Treaty of Jaffa (1192) left the coast from Tyre to Jaffa in Crusader hands, guaranteed Christian pilgrim access to Jerusalem, and left the city itself under Muslim control.

Death and legacy

Saladin died of a fever in Damascus on 4 March 1193, so generous with his treasury that barely enough remained for his funeral. He is buried in a mausoleum beside the Umayyad Mosque. His reputation for honour and generosity flourished in European literature — Dante placed him among the virtuous in Limbo, and Walter Scott's The Talisman cast him as Richard's noble equal — while in the modern Middle East he is celebrated as the liberator of Jerusalem and a symbol of Arab and Muslim unity.
