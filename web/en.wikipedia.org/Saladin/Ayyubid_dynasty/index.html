Ayyubid dynasty

The Ayyubid dynasty was a Sunni Muslim dynasty of Kurdish origin that ruled much of the Middle East during the 12th and 13th centuries. Founded by Saladin (Ṣalāḥ al-Dīn Yūsuf ibn Ayyūb, from whom the dynasty takes its name — "sons of Ayyub"), it governed Egypt, Syria, Upper Mesopotamia, the Hejaz, Yemen and parts of North Africa.

Origins

Saladin's father, Najm al-Dīn Ayyūb, and uncle Shīrkūh served the Zengid ruler Nūr al-Dīn of Aleppo. Shīrkūh's expeditions to Egypt (1164–1169) ended with him becoming vizier of the Fatimid caliphate; on his death in 1169 Saladin succeeded him, abolished the Shi'a Fatimid caliphate in 1171 and restored Sunni allegiance to Baghdad.

Height of power

After Nūr al-Dīn's death in 1174, Saladin progressively absorbed Syria, defeating the Crusader Kingdom of Jerusalem at the Battle of Hattin (1187) and retaking Jerusalem. The Third Crusade under Richard I of England recovered the coast but failed to retake the city, ending with the Treaty of Jaffa (1192).

Later rule and decline

Saladin's brother al-ʿĀdil consolidated the empire, and Sultan al-Kāmil negotiated with Frederick II, ceding Jerusalem by treaty in 1229. The dynasty patronised Sunni madrasas, hospitals and fortifications, including the Cairo Citadel. In Egypt the Ayyubids were supplanted in 1250 by their Mamluk slave-soldiers after the Seventh Crusade; Ayyubid branches in Syria fell to the Mongols and Mamluks by 1260, though a line survived in Ḥamāh until 1341 and in Ḥiṣn Kayfā much longer.
