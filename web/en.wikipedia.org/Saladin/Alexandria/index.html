Alexandria (Arabic: الإسكندرية, al-Iskandariyya) is the second-largest city in Egypt and a major Mediterranean port. Founded in 331 BC by Alexander the Great, it became the capital of Hellenistic Egypt under the Ptolemaic dynasty and one of the most famous centres of learning in the ancient world, home to the Great Library of Alexandria and the Pharos lighthouse, one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World.

History

Alexander founded the city on the site of the small settlement of Rhacotis, laying it out on a grid designed by Dinocrates of Rhodes between the sea and Lake Mareotis. Under Ptolemy I and his successors it grew into a cosmopolitan metropolis of Greeks, Egyptians and Jews, renowned for the Mouseion research institute and its library, which attracted scholars such as Euclid and Eratosthenes. The city passed to Rome in 30 BC after the defeat of Mark Antony and Cleopatra, and later to Byzantine and then Arab rule following its capture in AD 642.

Alexandria declined in the medieval period as Cairo rose, but revived in the 19th century under Muhammad Ali as Egypt's principal port, linked to the Nile by the Mahmoudiyah Canal and to world trade by the cotton boom. In the era of Saladin (Ṣalāḥ al-Dīn, r. 1171–1193), the city's defences were strengthened and it served as an important naval and commercial harbour of the Ayyubid sultanate, guarding the coast against Crusader fleets.

Landmarks

Surviving and modern landmarks include the Citadel of Qaitbay, built in 1477 on the site of the Pharos; the Roman amphitheatre at Kom el-Dikka; Pompey's Pillar; the catacombs of Kom el-Shoqafa; and the modern Bibliotheca Alexandrina, inaugurated in 2002 near the site of the ancient library. The long Corniche road sweeps around the Eastern Harbour, the heart of the ancient city.
