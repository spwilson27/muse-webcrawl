Richard I of England

Richard I (8 September 1157 – 6 April 1199), known as Richard the Lionheart, was King of England from 1189 until his death. The third son of Henry II and Eleanor of Aquitaine, he spent most of his reign abroad — on the Third Crusade, in captivity, and defending his French possessions — spending perhaps six months in England as king.

Early life and accession

Raised largely in Aquitaine, Richard became Duke of Aquitaine and a noted troubadour poet and jouster. He rebelled repeatedly against his father alongside his brothers; he was reconciled with Henry shortly before the old king's death in 1189 and was crowned at Westminster Abbey.

The Third Crusade

Richard took the cross and, with Philip II of France, sailed for the Holy Land in 1190. En route he conquered Cyprus from its Byzantine ruler Isaac Komnenos. In the Levant he recaptured Acre (1191) — where he ordered the execution of some 2,700 Muslim prisoners — and defeated Saladin at Arsuf and Jaffa, twice advancing within sight of Jerusalem. Judging the city indefensible if taken, he settled for the Treaty of Jaffa (1192), which kept the coast in Christian hands and allowed unarmed pilgrims access to Jerusalem. Richard and Saladin developed a famous mutual respect, exchanging gifts and courtesies.

Captivity and death

Returning home in disguise, Richard was captured near Vienna by Duke Leopold of Austria and handed to Emperor Henry VI, who released him only for an enormous ransom of 150,000 marks — raised by heavy taxation in England. His final years were spent at war with Philip II in France. In 1199 he was struck by a crossbow bolt while besieging the castle of Châlus-Chabrol in the Limousin and died of the wound, aged 41. He was buried at Fontevraud Abbey beside his father; his heart went to Rouen and his entrails to Châlus.

Legacy

Richard's reputation as the archetypal warrior-king was cemented by romance and later literature, including the Robin Hood legends. Historians have debated whether his absentee kingship neglected England, though his administration under Hubert Walter functioned effectively.
