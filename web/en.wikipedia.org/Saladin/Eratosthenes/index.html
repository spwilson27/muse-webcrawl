Eratosthenes

Eratosthenes of Cyrene (c. 276 – c. 194 BC) was a Greek mathematician, geographer, astronomer and poet. As chief librarian of the Library of Alexandria he is best known for calculating the circumference of the Earth with remarkable accuracy and for devising the sieve that bears his name for finding prime numbers.

Measuring the Earth

Eratosthenes learned that at noon on the summer solstice the Sun stood directly overhead at Syene (modern Aswan), shining down a well, while at Alexandria on the same day a vertical rod cast a shadow showing the Sun 7.2 degrees — one fiftieth of a circle — from the zenith. Knowing the distance between the cities to be about 5,000 stadia, he computed the Earth's circumference as 250,000 stadia, a value within a few percent of the true figure depending on the length of the stadion used.

Other work

His Sieve of Eratosthenes systematically eliminates multiples to isolate prime numbers. He wrote a three-volume Geographica, coining the word "geography," drew a map of the known world with parallels and meridians, estimated the obliquity of the ecliptic, and compiled a chronology of Greek history from the fall of Troy. His versatility earned him the nickname "Beta" — second-best at everything, his detractors said, though first in none.

Death

According to tradition, Eratosthenes went blind in old age and, unable to read, starved himself to death in Alexandria around 194 BC.
