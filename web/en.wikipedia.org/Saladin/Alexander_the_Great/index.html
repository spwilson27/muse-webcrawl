Alexander the Great

Alexander III of Macedon (356 – 10/11 June 323 BC), commonly known as Alexander the Great, was a king of the ancient Greek kingdom of Macedon. He succeeded his father Philip II at the age of twenty and spent most of his ruling years on an unprecedented military campaign through Western Asia and Egypt. By the age of thirty he had created one of the largest empires in history, stretching from Greece to northwestern India.

Early life

Alexander was born at Pella to Philip II and Olympias. He was tutored by Aristotle until the age of sixteen and distinguished himself commanding the cavalry at the Battle of Chaeronea (338 BC). After Philip's assassination in 336 BC, Alexander secured the throne and the hegemony of Greece.

Conquest of Persia

In 334 BC Alexander invaded the Achaemenid Persian Empire, winning at the Granicus River, Issus (333 BC, against Darius III), and Gaugamela (331 BC). He captured Babylon, Susa, and Persepolis, effectively ending the empire founded by Cyrus the Great.

Egypt and the east

Welcomed in Egypt as a liberator, Alexander founded the city of Alexandria in 331 BC and was proclaimed son of the god Amun at the Siwa Oasis. He then campaigned through Central Asia, founding cities such as Alexandria Eschate and marrying the Bactrian princess Roxana.

India and return

In 326 BC Alexander defeated King Porus at the Hydaspes in the Punjab. When his exhausted army refused to march further east, he turned back; the return journey through the Gedrosian desert caused heavy losses.

Death and legacy

Alexander died at Babylon in June 323 BC, aged 32, of a fever whose cause remains debated. His empire was divided among his generals, the Diadochi, founding the Hellenistic kingdoms. His conquests spread Greek culture across the Near East — the Hellenistic Age — and he became a legendary figure in both European and Middle Eastern traditions.
