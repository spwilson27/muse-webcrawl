Crusades

The Crusades were a series of religious wars initiated, supported and sometimes directed by the Latin Church between 1095 and the late Middle Ages. The best known are the expeditions to the eastern Mediterranean aimed at recovering the Holy Land from Muslim rule, beginning with the First Crusade (1096–1099), which captured Jerusalem and established the Crusader states.

Origins

In 1095 Pope Urban II, responding to a Byzantine appeal for help against the Seljuk Turks, preached at the Council of Clermont for an armed pilgrimage to liberate Jerusalem, offering a plenary indulgence to participants. The response far exceeded expectations: a disorderly People's Crusade was destroyed in Anatolia, but the main princely armies fought their way through Syria and stormed Jerusalem on 15 July 1099, massacring its inhabitants.

The Crusader states

The victors founded the Kingdom of Jerusalem, the County of Tripoli, the Principality of Antioch and the County of Edessa — collectively the Outremer. Military orders such as the Knights Templar and Knights Hospitaller defended them. Edessa's fall in 1144 prompted the unsuccessful Second Crusade (1147–1149). Saladin reunified Egypt and Syria, destroyed the kingdom's army at Hattin (1187) and retook Jerusalem.

Later crusades

The Third Crusade (1189–1192), led by Frederick Barbarossa, Philip II of France and Richard I of England, restored the coastal strip but not Jerusalem. The Fourth Crusade (1202–1204) was diverted to sack Christian Constantinople, founding the Latin Empire. Frederick II recovered Jerusalem by treaty in 1229, but the city was finally lost in 1244; Acre, the last mainland stronghold, fell to the Mamluks in 1291.

Other crusades and legacy

Crusading extended to Iberia (the Reconquista), the Baltic (Northern Crusades against pagan peoples) and against heretics such as the Cathars (Albigensian Crusade). The Crusades stimulated east–west trade, left deep marks on European finance, literature and military architecture, and remain a potent — and contested — symbol in both Western and Middle Eastern memory.
