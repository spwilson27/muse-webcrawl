Great Library of Alexandria

The Great Library of Alexandria, in Egypt, was one of the largest and most significant libraries of the ancient world. Part of the larger research institution known as the Mouseion (the shrine of the Muses), it was founded under the Ptolemaic dynasty in the third century BC and flourished for centuries as the leading centre of Hellenistic scholarship, attracting scholars such as Euclid, Eratosthenes, and Aristarchus of Samos. The library's decline and partial destruction over several centuries became a byword for the fragility of knowledge.

Foundation and organisation

The library is usually credited to Ptolemy I Soter and his son Ptolemy II Philadelphus, with Demetrius of Phalerum among its early organizers. Royal patronage offered resident scholars salaries, tax exemption, and communal dining in the Mouseion. The chief librarian was a scholar of the first rank who often served as tutor to the crown prince.

Collection and scholarship

The Ptolemies acquired texts aggressively — buying the libraries of Aristotle, confiscating and copying books from ships in the harbour, and commissioning translations, most famously the Septuagint, the Greek translation of the Hebrew scriptures. Estimates of the collection vary wildly in ancient sources, from about 200,000 to 700,000 scrolls. Callimachus compiled the Pinakes, a 120-book catalogue often considered the first library catalogue of the Western world. Alexandrian scholarship produced critical editions of Homer, advances in geometry, astronomy, anatomy, and geography.

Decline and destruction

There was no single catastrophic destruction. A fire during Julius Caesar's Alexandrine War in 48 BC burned warehouses of books near the harbour and possibly part of the library. Political purges under Ptolemy VIII in 145 BC expelled scholars and weakened the Mouseion. Under Roman rule funding declined; the Brucheion quarter was devastated in the third century AD, and the daughter library at the Serapeum was destroyed when the temple was demolished in 391 AD amid anti-pagan violence. The later story that the Arab conquerors burned the library in 642 AD on Caliph Umar's orders is regarded by modern scholars as a legend.

Legacy

The library stands in cultural memory as the emblem of universal learning lost. Its modern namesake, the Bibliotheca Alexandrina (opened 2002), stands near its presumed site.
