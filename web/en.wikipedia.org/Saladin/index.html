Saladin

Saladin (1137/38 – 4 March 1193), known in Arabic as Ṣalāḥ ad-Dīn Yūsuf ibn Ayyūb, was a Kurdish Muslim ruler who founded the Ayyubid dynasty. He united Egypt and Syria under his rule and led Muslim resistance to the Crusader states, recapturing Jerusalem in 1187 after his victory at the Battle of Hattin. His chivalrous conduct toward opponents, including Richard I of England during the Third Crusade, earned him an enduring reputation in both Muslim and Christian traditions.

Early life

Saladin was born in Tikrit, in present-day Iraq, into a Kurdish family. His father, Najm al-Din Ayyub, served the Zengid rulers of Mosul and Aleppo, and Saladin grew up in the courts of Baalbek and Damascus, receiving an education in religion and administration as well as military training. He accompanied his uncle, Shirkuh, on Zengid expeditions to Egypt in the 1160s, where the declining Fatimid Caliphate was contested by the Crusader Kingdom of Jerusalem.

Rise in Egypt

When Shirkuh died in 1169, Saladin succeeded him as vizier of Fatimid Egypt at about 31 years of age. He consolidated power, abolished the Shia Fatimid Caliphate in 1171, and restored Sunni allegiance to the Abbasid Caliph in Baghdad, becoming effectively ruler of Egypt. Following the death of his Zengid overlord Nur al-Din in 1174, Saladin moved into Syria, taking Damascus and, after conflict with Zengid successors, Aleppo in 1183, uniting Egypt and Syria into a single Ayyubid realm.

Sultan of Egypt and Syria

As sultan, Saladin built a state sustained by iqta land grants to his military following, founded madrasas and hospitals, and refortified cities, commissioning the Citadel of Cairo. His administration balanced Kurdish, Turkish and Arab troops and drew legitimacy from the cause of jihad against the Crusader states, which he pursued while also campaigning against Muslim rivals when necessary.

Conquest of Jerusalem

In July 1187 Saladin destroyed the field army of the Kingdom of Jerusalem at the Battle of Hattin, capturing King Guy of Lusignan and the relic of the True Cross. City after city submitted, and on 2 October 1187 Jerusalem itself surrendered on negotiated terms: unlike the massacre that followed the Crusader capture of 1099, Saladin permitted ransoms and protected churches and inhabitants, an act widely noted by contemporaries.

The Third Crusade

Saladin's capture of Jerusalem triggered the Third Crusade (1189–1192), led by Frederick Barbarossa, Philip II of France, and Richard I of England. Richard retook Acre and defeated Saladin at Arsuf (1191) and Jaffa (1192), but could not retake Jerusalem. The Treaty of Jaffa (1192) left the coast from Tyre to Jaffa in Crusader hands while Jerusalem remained under Muslim control with access guaranteed for Christian pilgrims. The two kings never met, but exchanged courtesies that fed the legend of Saladin's chivalry in European literature.

Death and legacy

Saladin died of a fever in Damascus on 4 March 1193, having given much of his wealth to charity. His empire was divided among his sons and brother al-Adil, though the Ayyubid dynasty endured for decades. He is revered in the Muslim world as a unifier and liberator of Jerusalem, and his mausoleum stands beside the Umayyad Mosque in Damascus. In the West, he became a byword for chivalry, appearing in works from Dante's Divine Comedy (where he is placed honourably among the virtuous pagans) to Walter Scott's The Talisman. The modern Saladin Governorate in Iraq and the figure of Salahuddin in Arab political symbolism both draw on his name.
