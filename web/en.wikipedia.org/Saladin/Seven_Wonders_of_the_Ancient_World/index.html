Seven Wonders of the Ancient World

The Seven Wonders of the Ancient World, also known as the Seven Wonders of the World, is a list of remarkable constructions of classical antiquity compiled by Hellenic travellers as a guidebook to the marvels of the Mediterranean world. The best-known version of the list dates to the 2nd century BC and is associated with Antipater of Sidon.

The list

  1. Great Pyramid of Giza (Egypt, c. 2580–2560 BC) — the oldest wonder and the only one still largely intact.
  2. Hanging Gardens of Babylon (Iraq) — a legendary tiered garden whose very existence in Babylon is disputed; some scholars place it at Nineveh.
  3. Statue of Zeus at Olympia (Greece, 435 BC) — a colossal gold-and-ivory figure by Phidias, later destroyed.
  4. Temple of Artemis at Ephesus (Turkey) — repeatedly rebuilt; famously burned by the arsonist Herostratus in 356 BC.
  5. Mausoleum at Halicarnassus (Turkey, c. 351 BC) — tomb of Mausolus, satrap of Caria; its name became the generic word for grand tombs.
  6. Colossus of Rhodes (Greece, 280 BC) — a giant bronze statue of Helios, toppled by an earthquake after only 56 years.
  7. Lighthouse of Alexandria (Pharos) (Egypt, c. 280 BC) — a tower over 100 metres tall, ruined by earthquakes in the Middle Ages.

History and influence

Earlier Greek lists varied in contents; Callimachus of Cyrene compiled an early collection of wonders in the 3rd century BC. The canonical seven reflect what a Hellenistic traveller could plausibly visit around the Mediterranean. Only the Great Pyramid survives today; the sites of the others are known through archaeology and ancient descriptions. The idea inspired many later lists, including medieval wonders and the modern New7Wonders of the World chosen in 2007.
