Cured-in-place pipe

Cured-in-place pipe (CIPP) is a trenchless rehabilitation method used to repair existing pipelines without excavation. A resin-saturated felt or fibreglass liner is inserted into a damaged pipe and cured in place, forming a tight-fitting, jointless new pipe within the old one. It is widely used for sewer, water, gas, and industrial pipelines.

Cured-in-place pipe
Abbreviation: CIPP
Type: Trenchless pipe rehabilitation
Invented: 1971, London (Eric Wood / Insituform)
Materials: Polyester, vinyl ester, or epoxy resin

Process

The host pipe is cleaned and inspected by camera. A flexible tube impregnated with thermosetting resin is inverted or pulled into the pipe, inflated against the walls with water or air pressure, and then cured with hot water, steam, or ultraviolet light. Service connections are reopened robotically, and the liner is designed to last around 50 years.

Advantages and concerns

CIPP avoids digging up streets and can be completed quickly at lower social cost than open-cut replacement. Concerns include chemical emissions during steam curing — notably styrene from polyester resins — and ensuring full cure to prevent resin washout into waterways.
