Argumentation theory

Argumentation theory studies how conclusions can be reached from premises.

Argumentation theory, or argumentation, is the interdisciplinary study of how conclusions can be supported or undermined by premises through logical reasoning. With historical origins in logic, dialectic, and rhetoric, argumentation theory includes the arts and sciences of civil debate, dialogue, conversation, and persuasion. It studies rules of inference, logic, and procedural rules in both artificial and real-world settings.

Argumentation includes the forms of dialogue, such as deliberation and negotiation, which are concerned with collaborative decision-making procedures. It also encompasses eristic dialogue, the branch of social debate in which victory over an opponent is the primary goal, and didactic dialogue used for teaching. This discipline also studies the methods by which people can express and rationally resolve or merely address their disagreements.

Key concepts

Among the key concepts of argumentation theory are argument — a set of statements, one of which (the conclusion) follows from or is supported by the others (the premises) — and the distinction between deductive and inductive reasoning. Informal logic, developed in the twentieth century, sought to analyse arguments as they occur in natural language, complementing the study of formal deductive systems.

Stephen Toulmin's model of argument, set out in The Uses of Argument (1958), identifies six components: claim, grounds (data), warrant, backing, qualifier, and rebuttal. The pragma-dialectical theory of Frans H. van Eemeren and Rob Grootendorst treats argumentation as part of a critical discussion aimed at resolving a difference of opinion, governed by rules for rational discussion.

History

The study of argumentation reaches back to Aristotle's Topics, Sophistical Refutations, and Rhetoric. In the twentieth century, Chaïm Perelman and Lucie Olbrechts-Tyteca's The New Rhetoric (1958) revived the rhetorical tradition, while the rise of artificial intelligence prompted computational approaches to argument, including abstract argumentation frameworks introduced by Phan Minh Dung in 1995.
