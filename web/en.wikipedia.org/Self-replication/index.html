Self-replication

Self-replication is any behaviour of a dynamical system that yields construction of an identical or similar copy of itself. Biological cells reproduce by cell division, and DNA replicates within them; in engineering and computer science, self-replication appears in self-reproducing machines, computer viruses and programs called quines that print their own source code.

Theory

John von Neumann gave the first rigorous treatment in the 1940s, designing a self-reproducing cellular automaton with a universal constructor that reads instructions both as data to copy and as a program to execute — a scheme strikingly similar to the role of DNA, discovered soon after. His work founded the study of artificial life and automata theory.

Machines and applications

Self-replicating machines have been proposed for space exploration, notably in NASA's 1980 study of self-replicating lunar factories, because a machine that can build copies of itself could industrialize a distant body from a single seed. The RepRap project applies a partial version of the idea: a 3D printer that prints many of its own parts. Critics raise safety concerns, dramatized in the "grey goo" scenario for hypothetical runaway molecular replicators.
