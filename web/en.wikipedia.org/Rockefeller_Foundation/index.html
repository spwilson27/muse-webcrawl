The Rockefeller Foundation is an American private foundation and philanthropic medical research organisation based in New York City. Chartered in 1913 by Standard Oil co-founder John D. Rockefeller, his son John D. Rockefeller Jr., and adviser Frederick Taylor Gates, it was one of the first great general-purpose foundations and remains among the most influential philanthropic institutions in the world, credited with foundational work in public health, agricultural science, and the social sciences.

Founding

John D. Rockefeller had experimented with large-scale organised philanthropy through the Rockefeller Institute for Medical Research (1901) and the General Education Board (1902). After a controversial attempt to obtain a federal charter failed in Congress, the Rockefeller Foundation was chartered by the State of New York on 14 May 1913, with an initial endowment of $100 million in Standard Oil shares. Its stated mission was "to promote the well-being of mankind throughout the world."

Public health

The Foundation's International Health Division became legendary for campaigns against hookworm in the American South and later worldwide, and for work on yellow fever — including funding the research that led Max Theiler to develop the 17D yellow fever vaccine, for which he won the Nobel Prize in 1951. Foundation officers helped establish schools of public health, including the Johns Hopkins School of Hygiene and Public Health (1916) and institutions in London, Toronto, and Beijing, and its China Medical Board built the Peking Union Medical College.

Agriculture and the Green Revolution

Beginning with the Mexican Agricultural Program in 1943, the Foundation funded plant breeders — most famously Norman Borlaug — whose high-yielding dwarf wheat varieties spread across Mexico, India, and Pakistan, laying the groundwork for the Green Revolution and earning Borlaug the Nobel Peace Prize in 1970. The Foundation later co-founded research centres such as IRRI (rice) in the Philippines and continued agricultural work in Africa through initiatives such as AGRA.

Social sciences, humanities, and population

The Foundation underwrote much of the early infrastructure of American social science, supporting the Social Science Research Council, the National Bureau of Economic Research, and university programmes in economics, anthropology, and international relations. Its humanities division aided scholars fleeing Nazi Europe. In the 1950s, John D. Rockefeller III spun off population work into the separate Population Council. The Foundation's mid-century support for eugenics-adjacent research and its funding of controversial studies remain subjects of historical criticism.

Modern era

In recent decades the Foundation has emphasised urban resilience (including the 100 Resilient Cities programme), food security, digital public infrastructure, pandemic preparedness, and climate change, committing $1 billion in 2020 toward a green recovery from COVID-19 and issuing the first "green" bond by a US foundation. Its endowment has fluctuated around several billion dollars, and it has awarded billions in grants over its history.
