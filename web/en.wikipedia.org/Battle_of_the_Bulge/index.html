The Battle of the Bulge, also known as the Ardennes Offensive, was the last major German offensive campaign on the Western Front during World War II. It was launched through the densely forested Ardennes region of Belgium and Luxembourg on 16 December 1944, taking the Allies by surprise. The battle's name comes from the wedge or "bulge" driven into the Allied lines, as shown in contemporary maps and news reports. It was the largest and bloodiest single battle fought by the United States in the war.

The offensive

Adolf Hitler's plan — codenamed Wacht am Rhein — aimed to drive through the Ardennes, cross the Meuse, and seize Antwerp, splitting the Allied armies and destroying four of them, in the hope of forcing a negotiated peace in the west. Germany committed some 400,000 men in the initial attack, achieved complete surprise in bad weather that grounded Allied aircraft, and advanced up to 100 kilometres in places.

Key episodes included the siege of Bastogne, a vital road junction held by the US 101st Airborne Division under Brigadier General Anthony McAuliffe — whose reply to a German surrender demand, "NUTS!", became famous — and the Malmedy massacre of American prisoners by Waffen-SS troops. German spearheads were stopped short of the Meuse near Dinant, short of fuel and battered once the weather cleared on 23 December and Allied airpower returned.

Counterattack and outcome

General George S. Patton's Third Army pivoted north and relieved Bastogne on 26 December. Through January the Allies squeezed out the bulge, restoring the original front by 25 January 1945. American casualties were roughly 90,000 and German losses 80,000–100,000, including much of Germany's remaining armoured reserve and aircraft; the Luftwaffe's losses in the New Year's Day Operation Bodenplatte compounded the failure. The defeat left Germany unable to resist the final Allied offensives into the Reich.
