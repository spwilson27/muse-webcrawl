Latin rights

Latin rights (Latin: ius Latii or ius latinum) were a set of legal rights held under Roman law by certain inhabitants of the Roman world who were not full Roman citizens. Originally the rights of the members of the Latin League, they came to denote an intermediate civic status between that of Roman citizen and of foreigner (peregrinus), and were widely used by Rome as an instrument for integrating provincial communities.

Content of the rights

The core Latin rights included commercium (the right to trade and hold property under Roman law and to make Roman contracts), connubium (the right to contract a legally recognised marriage with a Roman citizen), and migratio (the right to acquire Roman citizenship by moving to Rome). Later, under the Empire, the rights were systematised into grades — Latium minus and Latium maius — determining whether local magistrates alone, or magistrates together with town councillors (decurions) and their families, received Roman citizenship on holding office.

Use in the provinces

After the Social War (91–87 BC) all free inhabitants of Italy became Roman citizens, and Latin status was exported to the provinces. Grants of Latin rights to provincial towns — notably in Spain under Vespasian, who gave Latin rights to the whole peninsula — gave local elites a pathway to citizenship and encouraged the Romanisation of municipal life. Holders of the status were called Junian Latins in some contexts of manumission law. The distinction finally lost significance when the Constitutio Antoniniana of AD 212 granted citizenship to nearly all free inhabitants of the Empire.
