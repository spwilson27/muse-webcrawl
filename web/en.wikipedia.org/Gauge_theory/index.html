Gauge theory

In physics, a gauge theory is a type of field theory in which the Lagrangian, and hence the dynamics of the system itself, does not change (is invariant) under local transformations according to certain smooth families of operations (Lie groups). The term "gauge" refers to redundant degrees of freedom in the description: physical predictions must be independent of arbitrary local choices of phase or internal orientation.

History

The first gauge theory was Maxwell's electromagnetism, where the potentials can be changed by a gauge transformation without altering the fields. Hermann Weyl coined the term in the 1920s while attempting to unify gravity and electromagnetism. After quantum mechanics, the U(1) phase invariance of the wavefunction was recognised as the underlying symmetry of electromagnetism, and in 1954 Yang and Mills generalised the idea to non-abelian groups such as SU(2).

Yang–Mills theory and the Standard Model

Non-abelian Yang–Mills theories describe the strong and weak nuclear forces: quantum chromodynamics is an SU(3) gauge theory of quarks and gluons, and the electroweak theory unifies SU(2) × U(1). Together they form the Standard Model of particle physics, whose gauge invariance dictates the existence and interactions of the force-carrying gauge bosons. Quantisation required the Faddeev–Popov procedure and led to the discovery of asymptotic freedom.

Mathematical formulation

Mathematically, gauge fields are connections on principal bundles, and their curvature is the field strength. Gauge theory underlies modern geometry as well as physics, from instantons and monopoles to the gauge/gravity duality of string theory.
