Countershading

Countershading
Also known asThayer's law
TypeCamouflage (crypsis)
Described byAbbott Handerson Thayer (1896)
ExamplesSharks, deer, penguins, many fish

Countershading, or Thayer's law, is a method of camouflage in which an animal's coloration is darker on the upper side and lighter on the underside of the body. This shade gradient is thought to reduce the animal's visibility by counteracting the effects of self-shadowing: when lit from above, a uniformly coloured body appears lighter on top and darker below, betraying its three-dimensional shape; countershading reverses that gradient so the animal appears flat.

Examples

Countershading is widespread: most sharks, dolphins, and penguins are dark above and white below, making them hard to see from both above (against the dark depths) and below (against the bright surface). On land, deer, rabbits, and many caterpillars show the same pattern. Some animals, such as certain squid, achieve the effect actively with bioluminescent counter-illumination.

Applications

The principle was applied to military camouflage in both World Wars, notably in experiments with diffused lighting camouflage on ships, and Abbott Thayer's writings influenced early military camouflage design.
