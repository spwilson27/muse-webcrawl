Color grading

Color grading is the process of altering and enhancing the color of a motion picture, video image or still photograph, traditionally by photochemical means and now almost universally with digital tools. It encompasses both color correction — balancing exposure and color so shots match and appear natural — and the creative application of a distinctive look that supports the mood and storytelling of a production. The specialist who performs the work is called a colorist.

History

In the photochemical era, grading was called color timing: printer lights were adjusted during film printing to change the red, green and blue exposure of each shot. Techniques such as bleach bypass and push processing offered limited creative effects. The advent of telecine transfer and then the digital intermediate process in the late 1990s and 2000s made grading a fully digital discipline; O Brother, Where Art Thou? (2000) was an early landmark feature completed entirely as a digital intermediate.

Digital grading

Modern grading is performed in software such as DaVinci Resolve, Baselight and Adobe Premiere Pro, using control surfaces with trackballs and wheels. Colorists work with primary corrections (overall lift, gamma and gain), secondary corrections isolating specific colors or regions with keys and masks ("power windows"), and lookup tables (LUTs) that map colors between spaces or apply designed looks. Monitoring uses calibrated displays and scopes such as waveform monitors and vectorscopes. Workflows must manage color spaces carefully, increasingly under frameworks such as ACES, and deliver versions for cinema, broadcast and HDR streaming.

Creative use

Grading shapes genre and tone: desaturated, green-tinged palettes suggest dystopia; warm golden tones evoke nostalgia; the "orange and teal" look became pervasive in 2000s blockbusters because skin tones (orange) contrast maximally with complementary teal backgrounds.
