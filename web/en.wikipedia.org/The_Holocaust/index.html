The Holocaust was the genocide of European Jews carried out by Nazi Germany and its collaborators during the Second World War. Between 1941 and 1945, around six million Jews—two-thirds of Europe's Jewish population—were systematically murdered. Nazi persecution also targeted and murdered millions of others, including Roma, people with disabilities, Poles and Soviet prisoners of war.

Background and escalation

After Adolf Hitler came to power in 1933, the Nazi regime progressively excluded Jews from German society through the Nuremberg Laws (1935), the Kristallnacht pogrom (1938), expropriation and forced emigration. With the invasion of Poland in 1939 and of the Soviet Union in 1941, persecution escalated to mass murder: mobile killing units (Einsatzgruppen) shot more than a million Jews in occupied Soviet territory, while Jews across Europe were confined in ghettos under lethal conditions.

The "Final Solution"

In 1942 the regime coordinated the "Final Solution to the Jewish Question": the deportation of Jews to extermination camps—Auschwitz-Birkenau, Treblinka, Belzec, Sobibor, Chelmno and Majdanek—where most were killed in gas chambers on arrival. Others died in concentration camps from starvation, disease, forced labour and brutality, or on death marches as the camps were evacuated in 1945. Resistance included the Warsaw Ghetto Uprising of 1943 and revolts at Treblinka and Sobibor.

Aftermath

The Holocaust ended with Germany's defeat in May 1945. Leading Nazis were tried at Nuremberg for crimes against humanity; the genocide shaped the 1948 Genocide Convention, the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, and the establishment of Israel. Remembrance is maintained by institutions such as Yad Vashem and the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum, and 27 January is observed as International Holocaust Remembrance Day.
