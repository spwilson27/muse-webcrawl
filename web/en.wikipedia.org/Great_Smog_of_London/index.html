Great Smog of London

Date5–9 December 1952
LocationLondon, United Kingdom
TypeAir pollution disaster
Deaths~10,000–12,000 (estimated excess deaths)

The Great Smog of London, or Great Smog of 1952, was a severe air pollution event that affected the British capital in early December 1952. A period of unusually cold weather, combined with an anticyclone and windless conditions, allowed airborne pollutants — mostly from coal burning — to form a thick layer of smog over the city, lasting from Friday 5 December to Tuesday 9 December 1952.

The event

Londoners were familiar with fogs, but this "pea-souper" was unprecedented: visibility dropped to metres, transport ground to a halt, cinemas closed because audiences could not see the screen, and crime and accidents rose. The smoke-laden fog, laden with sulphur dioxide and particulates, seeped indoors. Contemporary reports blamed roughly 4,000 deaths, but later research estimated the total excess mortality at 10,000 to 12,000, with many thousands more suffering lasting respiratory illness. Cattle at Smithfield Market suffocated, and deaths from bronchitis and pneumonia soared.

Consequences

Initially the government downplayed the disaster, but public and parliamentary pressure led to the Beaver Committee and ultimately the Clean Air Act 1956, which introduced smoke control areas, restricted the burning of coal in cities and promoted cleaner fuels. The Act is considered a landmark in environmental law, and the Great Smog a turning point in public awareness of air pollution's health effects.
