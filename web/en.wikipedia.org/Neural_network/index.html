A neural network is a machine learning model composed of interconnected nodes ("artificial neurons") organized in layers, which learns to perform tasks by adjusting the strengths (weights) of its connections from examples. Loosely inspired by biological neural networks in animal brains, artificial neural networks (ANNs) are the core technology of modern deep learning, achieving state-of-the-art results in image recognition, speech recognition, natural language processing, and many other domains.

Basic model

Each artificial neuron computes a weighted sum of its inputs, adds a bias, and passes the result through a nonlinear activation function (such as the sigmoid, hyperbolic tangent, or rectified linear unit, ReLU). Neurons are arranged in an input layer, one or more hidden layers, and an output layer; networks with many hidden layers are called "deep." By the universal approximation theorem, even a single sufficiently wide hidden layer can approximate any continuous function, though depth confers efficiency in practice. A network's behavior is entirely encoded in its weights, which for modern models number from millions to hundreds of billions of parameters.

Learning

Networks are trained by minimizing a loss function measuring the difference between predictions and desired outputs. The backpropagation algorithm efficiently computes the gradient of the loss with respect to every weight by the chain rule, and gradient descent (and variants such as stochastic gradient descent and Adam) updates the weights step by step over many examples. Regularization methods — weight decay, dropout, data augmentation — combat overfitting so the network generalizes to new data.

Architectures

  • Feedforward networks (multilayer perceptrons) — the basic form, for classification and regression.
  • Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) — exploit spatial structure with shared-weight filters; dominant in computer vision from AlexNet (2012) onward.
  • Recurrent neural networks (RNNs, LSTMs) — maintain internal state for sequences such as text and speech.
  • Transformers (2017, "Attention Is All You Need") — based on self-attention; the architecture behind large language models such as GPT and BERT and most current AI systems.
  • Generative models — autoencoders, GANs, and diffusion models that learn to produce new data.

History

The field began with McCulloch and Pitts's logical neuron model (1943) and Frank Rosenblatt's perceptron (1958). Minsky and Papert's 1969 critique of perceptrons' limitations, together with funding cuts, contributed to an "AI winter." Backpropagation was popularized in 1986 by Rumelhart, Hinton, and Williams, and in the 1990s LSTMs and CNNs (LeNet) achieved practical successes. Deep learning took off after 2012, when AlexNet's GPU-trained CNN won the ImageNet competition by a wide margin — a breakthrough enabled by large datasets, GPU computing, and algorithmic refinements. Since then neural networks have powered AlphaGo, protein-structure prediction (AlphaFold), and the generative AI systems of the 2020s.
