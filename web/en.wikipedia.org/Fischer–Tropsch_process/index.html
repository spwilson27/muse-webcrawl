Fischer–Tropsch process

The Fischer–Tropsch process is a collection of chemical reactions that converts a mixture of carbon monoxide and hydrogen, known as synthesis gas or syngas, into liquid hydrocarbons. The process was developed by Franz Fischer and Hans Tropsch at the Kaiser Wilhelm Institute for Coal Research in Mülheim an der Ruhr, Germany, in the 1920s. A simplified overall reaction is:

(2n + 1) H2 + n CO → CnH2n+2 + n H2O

The products are mainly linear alkanes (paraffins) of varying chain length, together with alkenes, alcohols, and waxes, distributed roughly according to the Anderson–Schulz–Flory distribution. The reactions are catalysed by transition metals, principally iron and cobalt, at temperatures of about 150–350 °C and pressures of tens of atmospheres.

History

Fischer and Tropsch filed their key patents in the mid-1920s, and the process was taken up industrially in Nazi Germany, where coal-rich but oil-poor planners used it to produce synthetic fuels during the Second World War; by 1944 Germany was producing roughly 124,000 barrels per day of synthetic fuels from coal, much of it via Fischer–Tropsch and related hydrogenation processes. After the war, interest declined in most countries as cheap Middle Eastern oil became available, but South Africa's Sasol developed the process at large scale from the 1950s onward, initially because of apartheid-era sanctions and abundant coal.

Modern use

Modern applications include gas-to-liquids (GTL) plants, in which natural gas is first reformed to syngas and then converted to sulphur-free diesel, jet fuel, and lubricant base oils. Large examples include Shell's Pearl GTL plant in Qatar, among the largest industrial facilities in the world, and Sasol's operations in South Africa and abroad. Coal-to-liquids (CTL) and biomass-to-liquids (BTL) routes use the same synthesis step. Because the products are essentially free of sulphur and aromatics, Fischer–Tropsch diesel is valued as a clean-burning blending component, though the process has high capital costs and, when based on coal, a large carbon footprint unless coupled with carbon capture.

Chemistry

The mechanism is generally described as a surface polymerisation: carbon monoxide adsorbs and dissociates (or is hydrogenated) on the catalyst surface to form CHx building units, which couple into growing chains that terminate by hydrogenation or desorption. Catalyst choice strongly affects the product slate: cobalt catalysts favour long-chain paraffins and are preferred for gas-to-liquids, while iron catalysts tolerate lower-quality syngas, promote the water–gas shift reaction, and yield more olefins and oxygenates.
