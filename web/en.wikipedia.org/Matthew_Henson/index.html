Matthew Alexander Henson (8 August 1866 – 9 March 1955) was an African-American explorer who accompanied Robert Peary on most of his Arctic expeditions, including the 1908–1909 expedition that claimed to reach the geographic North Pole on 6 April 1909. Henson, a skilled dog-sled driver, craftsman, and speaker of the Inuit language, is considered by many historians to have been the first member of the party to reach the Pole's vicinity.

Early life

Henson was born in Maryland to freeborn Black sharecropper parents and was orphaned young. He went to sea at about age twelve as a cabin boy, educated himself aboard ship under Captain Childs, and travelled widely. He later worked in a Washington, D.C., clothing store, where he met Robert Peary in 1887 and was hired as his valet and assistant for a survey expedition to Nicaragua.

Arctic expeditions

Over the following two decades Henson joined Peary on seven Arctic voyages. He learned the Inughuit language, mastered dog-team handling, hunting, and sled construction, and became, by Peary's own admission, indispensable — Peary wrote that he could not get along without him. On the final dash of April 1909, the party consisted of Peary, Henson, and four Inughuit men — Ootah, Egigingwah, Seegloo, and Ooqueah. Their claim to have reached the Pole was celebrated but later disputed; nonetheless the expedition is generally credited with the first attainment of the geographic North Pole.

Later life and recognition

Returning to a segregated America, Henson received little of the acclaim given Peary. He published his memoir A Negro Explorer at the North Pole (1912) and worked for decades as a clerk in the New York Custom House. Recognition came late: the Explorers Club admitted him in 1937, Congress awarded him a medal in 1944, and President Truman honoured him in 1950. He died in 1955 and was buried in the Bronx; in 1988 his remains were reinterred at Arlington National Cemetery beside Peary.
