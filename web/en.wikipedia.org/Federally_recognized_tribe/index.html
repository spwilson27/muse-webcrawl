Federally recognized tribe

Federally recognized tribes have a government-to-government relationship with the United States.

A federally recognized tribe is an American Indian or Alaska Native tribal entity that is recognised as having a government-to-government relationship with the United States, with the responsibilities, powers, limitations, and obligations attached to that designation, and is eligible for funding and services from the Bureau of Indian Affairs (BIA) by virtue of its status as an Indian tribe.

As of recent years, there are 574 federally recognized tribes in the United States, including over 200 Alaska Native village entities. Recognition may be achieved through treaties, acts of Congress, administrative decisions, or the federal acknowledgment process administered by the Office of Federal Acknowledgment under regulations at 25 CFR Part 83.

Legal significance

Federal recognition confirms a tribe's inherent sovereignty, including rights of self-government, territorial jurisdiction over tribal lands in varying degrees, and eligibility for programs in areas such as health care (through the Indian Health Service), education, housing, and economic development. Recognized tribes may operate gaming under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act of 1988, subject to compacts with states. Some tribes are recognised only by individual states, and others are unrecognised, a distinction that can significantly affect their legal rights and access to services.
