Power law

In statistics, a power law is a functional relationship between two quantities, where a relative change in one quantity results in a proportional relative change in the other quantity, independent of the initial size of those quantities: one quantity varies as a power of another.

For instance, considering the area of a square in terms of the length of its side, if the length is doubled, the area is multiplied by a factor of four. Such relations are written as y = a xk, where k is the scaling exponent.

Empirical examples

Power-law relations have been reported in a wide variety of phenomena, including the sizes of craters on the Moon and of solar flares, the frequencies of words in most languages (Zipf's law), the magnitudes of earthquakes (the Gutenberg–Richter law), the forging patterns of human behaviour, the species-area relationship in ecology, and the Pareto distribution of incomes. The sizes of cities and the popularity of websites also often follow approximate power laws.

Scale invariance

A defining property of power laws is scale invariance: multiplying the argument by a constant factor rescales the function by a constant factor, so the shape of the relationship is unchanged. On a log–log plot, a power law appears as a straight line, which is the standard informal test used to detect one. However, rigorous statistical work has shown that many claimed power-law distributions are better described by alternatives such as the log-normal distribution, and that genuine power laws must be validated with care.

Generating mechanisms

Several mechanisms can generate power-law behaviour, including preferential attachment (the "rich get richer" process), multiplicative random processes, self-organized criticality, and optimisation. In network science, scale-free networks have degree distributions that follow a power law.
