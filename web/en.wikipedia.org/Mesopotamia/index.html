Mesopotamia is a historical region of Western Asia situated in the Tigris–Euphrates river system, in the northern part of the Fertile Crescent. It is widely regarded as one of the "cradles of civilisation": home to the first cities, the earliest known writing, and successive Sumerian, Akkadian, Babylonian and Assyrian civilisations.

Geography

The name means "the land between the rivers" — the Tigris and the Euphrates. The south (Babylonia, ancient Sumer and Akkad) was a flat alluvial plain where irrigation agriculture supported dense cities such as Uruk, Ur, Lagash and Babylon; the north (Assyria) was hillier, with rainfall farming and cities such as Nineveh and Assur.

History

By about 5300 BC farming villages dotted the region; by 3500 BC Uruk had become one of the world's first great cities and Sumerian scribes had developed cuneiform writing, at first for accounting. Sargon of Akkad (c. 2334 BC) created one of the first empires; Hammurabi of Babylon (18th century BC) issued his famous law code; the Neo-Assyrian Empire (911–609 BC) dominated the Near East; and Nebuchadnezzar II's Neo-Babylonian Empire fell to Cyrus the Great of Persia in 539 BC. Mesopotamia remained a rich province under Persian, Greek, Parthian and Sasanian rule until the Muslim conquests of the 7th century AD.

Contributions

Mesopotamian achievements include cuneiform literature (the Epic of Gilgamesh), codified law, the base-60 system still used for measuring time and angles, advances in astronomy and mathematics, ziggurat architecture, and organised irrigation and bureaucracy.

Religion and society

Each city honoured a patron god — Enlil at Nippur, Marduk at Babylon, Ashur at Assur — in great temple households that owned land and employed much of the population. Kingship was held to be divinely sanctioned, and divination, especially from omens and the stars, pervaded public life.
