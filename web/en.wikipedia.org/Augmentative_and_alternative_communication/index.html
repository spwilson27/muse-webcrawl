Augmentative and alternative communication

Augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) is an umbrella term encompassing the communication methods used to supplement or replace speech or writing for those with impairments in the production or comprehension of spoken or written language. AAC is used by those with a wide range of speech and language impairments, including congenital conditions such as cerebral palsy, intellectual impairment and autism, and acquired conditions such as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis and Parkinson's disease.

Forms of AAC

Unaided AAC uses no equipment: it includes gestures, facial expressions and manual sign languages. Aided AAC uses external tools, from low-tech picture boards and letter boards to high-tech speech-generating devices that produce digitised or synthesised speech output. Modern systems may be accessed by touch, switches, head pointers or eye-gaze tracking, enabling communication for people with severe motor impairments.

Users and outcomes

Estimates suggest that around 0.5% of the population could benefit from AAC. Research indicates that AAC use does not hinder the development of natural speech and may support language development in children. Successful implementation typically involves assessment by speech-language pathologists, selection of an appropriate vocabulary and access method, and training for both the user and their communication partners.

History

The modern field emerged in the 1970s and 1980s with advances in microelectronics and the founding of the International Society for Augmentative and Alternative Communication (ISAAC) in 1983. Stephen Hawking, who communicated through a cheek-operated speech synthesiser, became its most famous user.
