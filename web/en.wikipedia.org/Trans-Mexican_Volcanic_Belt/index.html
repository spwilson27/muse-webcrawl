The Trans-Mexican Volcanic Belt (Spanish: Eje Volcánico Transversal), also known as the Transvolcanic Belt and locally as the Sierra Nevada, is an active volcanic belt that covers central-southern Mexico. It stretches roughly 1,000 kilometres (620 mi) from west to east, from the Pacific coast near Puerto Vallarta and Colima across to the Gulf of Mexico near Veracruz, at about 19° north latitude.

The belt is one of Mexico's most prominent physiographic regions, and it hosts most of the country's population, including Mexico City, Puebla, Guadalajara, and Toluca, in its high intermontane basins.

Geology

The belt is an active volcanic arc formed by the subduction of the Cocos and Rivera plates beneath the North American Plate along the Middle America Trench. Unusually, the arc is not parallel to the trench but crosses it at an oblique angle, a geometry attributed to the flat-slab subduction of the Cocos Plate. Volcanism began in the Miocene and continues today.

Major volcanoes

  • Pico de Orizaba (5,636 m) — Mexico's highest peak, a dormant stratovolcano.
  • Popocatépetl (5,426 m) — continuously active since 1994, near Mexico City and Puebla.
  • Iztaccíhuatl (5,230 m) — dormant, adjacent to Popocatépetl.
  • Nevado de Toluca (4,680 m) — a stratovolcano with crater lakes.
  • Parícutin (2,808 m) — a cinder cone famously born in a cornfield in 1943.
  • Colima (3,820 m) — among Mexico's most active volcanoes.

Ecology

The belt's pine–oak forests form a distinct ecoregion, the Trans-Mexican Volcanic Belt pine–oak forests. The high forests of oyamel fir east of Mexico City host the overwintering colonies of the monarch butterfly, protected within the Monarch Butterfly Biosphere Reserve.
