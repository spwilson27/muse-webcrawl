Saharon Shelah

Saharon Shelah (Hebrew: שהרן שלח; born July 3, 1945) is an Israeli mathematician. He is a professor of mathematics at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem and Rutgers University in New Jersey. Shelah is among the most prolific mathematicians of the modern era, with more than a thousand research papers, many of them written with co-authors around the world. His work has transformed model theory and set theory.

Biography

Shelah was born in Jerusalem in 1945, the son of the Israeli poet Yehonatan Ratosh. He studied at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem, completing his doctorate in 1969 under the supervision of Michael Rabin, with a dissertation on stable theories. Shortly afterward he joined the faculty of the Hebrew University, where he has remained, while also holding a long-standing position at Rutgers University.

Mathematical work

Shelah's central achievement is classification theory, a programme within model theory that divides first-order theories into those that are "classifiable" — admitting a structure theory for their models — and those that are wild, encoding arbitrary combinatorial complexity. His book Classification Theory and the Number of Non-Isomorphic Models (1978, revised 1990) is a landmark of the field. He introduced key dividing lines such as stability, superstability, and the order property, and proved the Main Gap theorem with work extending over many years.

In set theory, Shelah created PCF theory (possible cofinalities), a powerful apparatus for studying the arithmetic of cardinal numbers in ZFC. Using PCF he proved, for example, that the continuum, if it is the ℵω+1-th cardinal in a certain sense, constrains the size of the power set of ℵω — a striking theorem of pure ZFC in a domain long thought to be wholly independent. He also developed the theory of proper forcing and made fundamental contributions to infinite combinatorics and abelian group theory.

Awards

Shelah received the Erdős Prize in 1977, the Israel Prize in 1998, the Bolyai Prize in 2000, and shared the Wolf Prize in Mathematics in 2001. He was awarded the EMET Prize in 2011 and the Leroy P. Steele Prize for Seminal Contribution to Research in 2013.
