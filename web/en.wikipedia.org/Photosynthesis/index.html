Photosynthesis

Photosynthesis is a biological process used by many cellular organisms to convert light energy into chemical energy, which is stored in organic compounds that can later be metabolised. In most cases, oxygen is released as a waste product. Photosynthesis is largely responsible for producing and maintaining the oxygen content of Earth's atmosphere, and it supplies most of the biological energy necessary for complex life on Earth.

Overview

The overall equation for oxygenic photosynthesis is: 6 CO2 + 6 H2O + light energy → C6H12O6 + 6 O2. In plants and algae the process occurs in chloroplasts, using the pigment chlorophyll, which absorbs mainly blue and red light and reflects green.

Light-dependent reactions

In the thylakoid membranes, light energy drives the splitting of water (photolysis), releasing oxygen and generating the energy carriers ATP and NADPH through an electron transport chain and chemiosmosis.

The Calvin cycle

In the stroma, the enzyme RuBisCO fixes carbon dioxide into organic molecules in the Calvin cycle, using the ATP and NADPH from the light reactions to produce three-carbon sugars. Variants such as C4 and CAM photosynthesis concentrate CO2 and reduce water loss in hot, dry environments.

History and significance

Jan Ingenhousz demonstrated in 1779 that plants restore "good air" only in sunlight; Melvin Calvin's group mapped the carbon pathway in the 1940s–50s. Cyanobacteria evolved oxygenic photosynthesis roughly 2.4 billion years ago, triggering the Great Oxidation Event.
