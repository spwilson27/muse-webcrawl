Motion sickness

[Image: Motion sickness affects travellers in vehicles, boats and simulators]

Motion sickness is a condition in which a disagreement exists between visually perceived movement and the vestibular system's sense of movement, or between senses more generally. Its symptoms include nausea, vomiting, cold sweating, headache, drowsiness, yawning and increased salivation. Depending on context it is called car sickness, seasickness, air sickness, space adaptation syndrome (in astronauts), or simulator/virtual-reality sickness.

Cause

The leading explanation is the sensory conflict theory: sickness arises when patterns of visual, vestibular and proprioceptive signals mismatch those expected from prior experience - for example, when reading in a car (vision reports stillness, the vestibular system reports motion). An evolutionary hypothesis holds that the brain interprets such unusual sensory conflicts as possible neurotoxin ingestion and triggers vomiting. Susceptibility peaks around ages 9-12, is higher in women, and is linked with migraine; it usually diminishes with repeated exposure, as sailors "get their sea legs."

Treatment and prevention

Preventive measures include looking at the horizon, facing the direction of travel, fresh air, driving rather than riding, and avoiding reading. Medications include antimuscarinics such as scopolamine (often worn as a patch) and antihistamines such as dimenhydrinate, meclizine and promethazine; ginger is also widely used with modest evidence of benefit.
