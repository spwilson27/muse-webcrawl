The common bile duct is a duct in the biliary system that carries bile from the liver and gallbladder to the duodenum. It is formed by the junction of the common hepatic duct (from the liver) and the cystic duct (from the gallbladder), and it is a structure of clinical importance in surgery and gastroenterology.

Anatomy

From its origin near the porta hepatis, the common bile duct descends in the free edge of the lesser omentum, passes behind the first part of the duodenum and through the head of the pancreas, and joins the main pancreatic duct to form the hepatopancreatic ampulla (ampulla of Vater). This opens into the second part of the duodenum at the major duodenal papilla, guarded by the sphincter of Oddi, which regulates the flow of bile and pancreatic juice. The duct is normally about 6–8 cm long with a diameter of around 6 mm, tending to widen with age and after gallbladder removal.

Function

The duct conveys bile — produced continuously by the liver and released from the gallbladder after meals — into the intestine, where bile salts emulsify dietary fat and aid absorption of fat-soluble vitamins.

Clinical significance

  • Choledocholithiasis — gallstones lodged in the duct, causing obstructive jaundice, pain, and complications such as cholangitis and pancreatitis; usually treated by ERCP.
  • Cholangitis — infection of the obstructed biliary tree, classically presenting with Charcot's triad of fever, jaundice, and right upper quadrant pain.
  • Choledochal cysts — congenital dilatations of the duct, associated with an increased risk of bile duct cancer.
  • Cholangiocarcinoma — cancer arising in the bile ducts, including hilar (Klatskin) tumours.
  • Surgical injury to the duct is a recognised risk of cholecystectomy (gallbladder removal).
