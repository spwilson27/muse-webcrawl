Psychosomatic medicine

Psychosomatic medicine is an interdisciplinary field of medicine and psychology concerned with the interplay of psychological, social, and biological factors in the cause, course, and treatment of physical illness. In modern practice the term covers the psychiatric care of the medically ill — a subspecialty of psychiatry also called consultation-liaison psychiatry — as well as research on how stress and emotion influence the body.

History and concepts

The word "psychosomatic" was introduced in the early nineteenth century and popularized in the 1930s and 1940s by figures such as Franz Alexander and Flanders Dunbar, who proposed that specific unconscious conflicts produced specific diseases — the so-called "holy seven" psychosomatic disorders, which included peptic ulcer, ulcerative colitis, asthma, and hypertension. Those specificity theories were largely abandoned, but later research established robust findings in areas such as the effects of chronic stress on immune and cardiovascular function, the role of depression in the prognosis of heart disease, and the placebo and nocebo effects.

Clinical practice

Psychosomatic medicine addresses conditions in which psychological factors are prominent, including somatic symptom disorder, functional disorders such as irritable bowel syndrome, chronic pain syndromes, and the psychiatric complications of medical illness and its treatment, such as delirium or depression after transplantation. Treatment combines medical management with psychotherapy, behavioural interventions, and medication. The field was formally recognized as a subspecialty of psychiatry in the United States in 2003; in 2017 its name was changed to consultation-liaison psychiatry to describe the role more clearly.
