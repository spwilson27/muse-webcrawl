Intermediate filaments (IFs) are cytoskeletal components found in the cells of vertebrates and many invertebrates. They have an average diameter of about 10 nanometres, which is between that of the thinner actin microfilaments (about 7 nm) and the thicker microtubules (about 25 nm) — hence the name "intermediate".

Intermediate filaments are composed of a large superfamily of related proteins sharing a central rod domain built from coiled-coil alpha-helices. Unlike actin filaments and microtubules, they are apolar, extremely stable, and do not serve as tracks for motor proteins; their principal role is mechanical, bearing tension and protecting cells against mechanical stress.

Structure and assembly

The basic building block is a dimer of two intertwined polypeptides forming a coiled coil. Two dimers align antiparallel to form a tetramer, tetramers associate laterally into unit-length filaments, and these anneal end-to-end and compact radially to form the mature 10 nm filament. Assembly does not require nucleotide hydrolysis, in contrast to actin and tubulin polymerization.

Types

Six classes are recognized. Types I and II are the acidic and basic keratins of epithelial cells; type III includes vimentin, desmin, peripherin and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP); type IV comprises the neurofilament proteins and alpha-internexin; type V are the nuclear lamins, which form the nuclear lamina beneath the inner nuclear membrane; and type VI includes nestin and related proteins.

Clinical significance

Mutations in intermediate filament genes cause a wide range of human diseases, including epidermolysis bullosa simplex (keratins 5 and 14), hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and muscular dystrophies (desmin), Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease (neurofilament light chain), and the premature-aging syndrome Hutchinson–Gilford progeria (lamin A). Tumour pathologists use intermediate filament expression profiles — such as cytokeratin patterns — to identify the tissue of origin of carcinomas.
