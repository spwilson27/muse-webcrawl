Mounted infantry

Mounted infantry are infantry soldiers who ride horses (or other animals, or later bicycles and vehicles) to the battlefield but dismount to fight on foot. They differ from cavalry, who are trained to fight from horseback, and from dragoons, who originally embodied this concept but in many armies evolved into conventional cavalry.

History

The concept dates to antiquity and the early modern period: commanders valued troops who could move at cavalry speed yet deliver disciplined infantry firepower. In the American Civil War, mounted infantry equipped with repeating rifles — such as Wilder's "Lightning Brigade" — proved highly effective. During the Second Boer War (1899–1902), both sides relied heavily on mounted infantry and mounted rifles to operate across the vast South African veld, and the British raised large numbers of Imperial Yeomanry and colonial mounted infantry units.

In the First World War, mounted infantry and mounted rifles served dismounted in the trenches as readily as on patrol; the Australian Light Horse, New Zealand Mounted Rifles and similar forces fought notably in the Sinai and Palestine campaign, including the charge at Beersheba in 1917 (executed as a mounted charge). With mechanisation, the role passed to motorised and mechanised infantry, who likewise ride to battle and fight on foot from armoured personnel carriers and infantry fighting vehicles.
