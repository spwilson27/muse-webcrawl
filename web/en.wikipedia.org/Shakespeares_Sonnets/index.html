Shakespeare's sonnets

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Shakespeare's sonnets are a collection of 154 poems written by William Shakespeare on themes of love, beauty, mortality, and the power of verse, most of them in the form of the Shakespearean sonnet: fourteen lines of iambic pentameter rhyming ABAB CDCD EFEF GG. They were published in a quarto of 1609 by Thomas Thorpe, followed by a narrative poem, "A Lover's Complaint."

Sequences and characters

The sequence is conventionally divided into sonnets addressed to a young man, the "Fair Youth" (sonnets 1–126) — opening with the procreation sonnets urging him to marry and have children — and sonnets concerning a "Dark Lady" (127–152), followed by two allegorical Cupid sonnets (153–154). A "Rival Poet" also appears. The dedication "to Mr. W.H., the only begetter of these ensuing sonnets" has never been conclusively explained; candidates for the Fair Youth include Henry Wriothesley, 3rd Earl of Southampton, and William Herbert, 3rd Earl of Pembroke, both patrons of Shakespeare.

Publication and authenticity

Francis Meres referred in 1598 to Shakespeare's "sugared sonnets among his private friends," and two (138 and 144) appeared in The Passionate Pilgrim (1599). Whether Thorpe's 1609 edition was authorised is unknown. The texts were corrupted and reordered by John Benson's 1640 edition, and the original order was restored by Edmond Malone in 1780. Famous sonnets include 18 ("Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?"), 29, 55, 73, 116 ("Let me not to the marriage of true minds"), 129, 130 ("My mistress' eyes are nothing like the sun"), and 146.

Themes and reception

The sonnets dwell on the ravages of time and the claim that poetry immortalises its subject; on desire, infidelity, and self-deception; and on the poet's own ageing. Long read as quasi-autobiography, they prompted Wordsworth's "with this key Shakespeare unlocked his heart" and Browning's sceptical riposte. They remain among the most quoted poems in English.
