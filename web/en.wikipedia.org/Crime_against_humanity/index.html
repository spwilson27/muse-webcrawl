Crime against humanity

Crimes against humanity are certain serious criminal acts committed as part of a large-scale attack against civilians. Unlike war crimes, crimes against humanity can be committed during both peace and war and against a state's own nationals as well as foreign nationals. Together with war crimes, genocide, and the crime of aggression, crimes against humanity are one of the core crimes under international criminal law and are prosecuted by international tribunals such as the International Criminal Court (ICC).

Origins and development

The phrase was used as early as the late 18th and early 19th centuries, and the concept gained legal force after the Second World War, when the Charter of the International Military Tribunal at Nuremberg defined crimes against humanity to include murder, extermination, enslavement, deportation, and other inhumane acts committed against any civilian population, as well as persecutions on political or racial grounds. The definition was later refined in the statutes of the tribunals for the former Yugoslavia and Rwanda, and codified in Article 7 of the Rome Statute of the ICC in 1998.

Definition under the Rome Statute

Article 7 of the Rome Statute lists acts including murder, extermination, enslavement, deportation or forcible transfer of population, imprisonment, torture, rape and other forms of sexual violence, persecution, enforced disappearance, apartheid, and other inhumane acts, when committed as part of a widespread or systematic attack directed against any civilian population, with knowledge of the attack.

Prosecutions

Defendants at Nuremberg and Tokyo were the first to be convicted of crimes against humanity. Later prosecutions took place before the International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia, the International Criminal Tribunal for Rwanda, and hybrid courts in Cambodia and Sierra Leone. The ICC, which began operating in 2002, has jurisdiction over crimes against humanity committed on the territory of, or by nationals of, its states parties.
