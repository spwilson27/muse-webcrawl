Companion planting

Companion planting in gardening and agriculture is the planting of different crops in proximity for any of a number of different reasons, including weed suppression, pest control, pollination, providing habitat for beneficial insects, maximising use of space, and otherwise increasing crop productivity. Companion planting is a form of polyculture.

Marigolds interplanted with vegetables

Mechanisms

Companion plants may benefit one another through several mechanisms:

  • Pest control — strongly scented plants such as marigolds, basil and onions can mask the odour of crop plants or repel insects; trap crops lure pests away from the main crop.
  • Nutrient provision — legumes fix atmospheric nitrogen in root nodules, enriching the soil for neighbouring plants.
  • Physical support and shade — tall plants such as maize can support climbing beans and shade heat-sensitive crops.
  • Beneficial insects — flowering companions attract pollinators and predators of pests such as ladybirds and hoverflies.
  • Weed suppression — dense ground-cover companions shade out weeds.

Traditional systems

The best-known traditional system is the "Three Sisters" of Indigenous North American agriculture, in which maize, climbing beans and squash are grown together: the maize provides a trellis, the beans fix nitrogen, and the squash shades the soil, suppressing weeds and conserving moisture. Similar intercropping traditions exist in Asia, Africa and Europe.

Scientific evidence

Research supports some companion-planting effects, such as trap cropping and the suppression of certain pests by intercropping, while other traditional pairings have not been confirmed experimentally. Companion planting remains a central technique of organic gardening.
