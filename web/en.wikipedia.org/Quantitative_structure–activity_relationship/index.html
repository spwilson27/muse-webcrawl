Quantitative structure–activity relationship

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Quantitative structure–activity relationship (QSAR) models are regression or classification models used in the chemical and biological sciences and in engineering. A QSAR relates a set of predictor variables (descriptors computed from molecular structure) to the potency of the response variable, typically the biological activity of a compound against some target, or a physicochemical property such as solubility or toxicity.

History

The field is commonly traced to the work of Corwin Hansch and Toshio Fujita in the 1960s, whose Hansch analysis correlated biological activity with hydrophobic (log P), electronic, and steric parameters of substituents. Later developments added three-dimensional (3D-QSAR) methods such as CoMFA, which compare molecular fields, and machine-learning approaches including random forests and support vector machines.

Methodology

A typical QSAR study assembles a dataset of compounds with measured activity, computes molecular descriptors (constitutional, topological, geometrical, and quantum-chemical), selects a subset of descriptors, fits a model, and validates it by cross-validation and external test sets. The OECD has published principles for the validation of QSAR models intended for regulatory use, requiring a defined endpoint, an unambiguous algorithm, a defined applicability domain, and measures of goodness of fit and predictivity.

Applications

QSAR models are widely used in drug discovery to prioritize compounds for synthesis, in cheminformatics, and in chemical risk assessment, where regulations such as REACH encourage their use to reduce animal testing.
