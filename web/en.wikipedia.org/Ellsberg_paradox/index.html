Ellsberg paradox

In decision theory, the Ellsberg paradox (or Ellsberg's paradox) is a paradox in which people's decisions are inconsistent with subjective expected utility theory. Daniel Ellsberg popularised the paradox in a 1961 paper, although earlier versions were noted by John Maynard Keynes. It is generally taken to be evidence of ambiguity aversion, in which a person is disinclined to favour any action that has outcomes with unknown probability, even if one outcome is guaranteed to improve their chances.

The experiment

In the classic setup, an urn contains 30 red balls and 60 balls that are either black or yellow in unknown proportion. Subjects typically prefer a bet on red over a bet on black (suggesting they believe black balls number fewer than 30), yet also prefer a bet on black-or-yellow over a bet on red-or-yellow (suggesting the opposite). No single assignment of probabilities can rationalise both choices, violating the sure-thing principle of expected utility.

Implications

The paradox helped motivate alternative models of choice under uncertainty, including maxmin expected utility and other approaches that distinguish risk, where probabilities are known, from ambiguity, where they are not.
