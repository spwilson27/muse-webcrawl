Collectivism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Collectivism is a moral stance, political philosophy, ideology, or social outlook that emphasizes the value of the group, collective, community, or nation over the individual. Collectivists focus on the interdependence of human beings within a collective and prioritize group goals, cohesion, and shared ownership or responsibility. It is commonly contrasted with individualism, which stresses individual autonomy, rights, and self-reliance.

Types and usage

The term is used in several related senses. In political philosophy, collectivism denotes systems in which the means of production are owned collectively — by the state, a commune, or society as a whole — as in forms of socialism and communism, but collectivist arrangements also appear in cooperatives, kibbutzim, and religious communities. In anthropology and cultural psychology, following researchers such as Geert Hofstede and Harry Triandis, collectivism describes cultures in which people are integrated into strong in-groups, identity is defined by group membership, and duty and harmony are emphasized; many East Asian, South Asian, African, and Latin American societies are described as more collectivist than Western societies.

History

Ideas resembling collectivism are ancient, appearing in tribal organization, Plato's communal arrangements for the guardian class in the Republic, and medieval commons. The term itself gained currency in the 19th century in debates over socialism; collectivist anarchists such as Mikhail Bakunin advocated federation of self-governing collectives in place of the state. In the 20th century, collectivization of agriculture in the Soviet Union under Stalin — a coercive state policy — gave the word strongly negative connotations for critics, while supporters of other collectivist traditions distinguish voluntary collective ownership from state compulsion.

Criticism and analysis

Critics, including economists of the Austrian school, argue that collectivism subordinates individual rights and can lead to authoritarianism; Friedrich Hayek's The Road to Serfdom (1944) is a prominent statement of this view. Defenders respond that some goods — public health, common infrastructure, or social welfare — are inherently collective, and that individual freedom itself depends on social institutions. Contemporary research typically treats individualism and collectivism as poles of a spectrum that can coexist within the same society or person depending on context.
