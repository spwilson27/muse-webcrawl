Flail

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A flail is an agricultural tool used for threshing, the process of separating grains from their husks. It consists of two long pieces of wood joined at the ends by a short strap of rawhide or chain: a longer handle or "helve" held by the thresher, and a shorter, freely swinging striking stick or "swingle" that beats the grain spread on the threshing floor. The same term is applied to a family of weapons derived from the tool, in which a striking head is attached to a handle by a flexible chain or hinge.

Agricultural flail

Threshing with a flail was one of the oldest and most widely distributed methods of processing cereals, practiced in Europe, Asia, and Africa for millennia. The thresher swung the swingle in a rotating motion so that it landed flat on the grain without crushing it, gradually working across the floor. The work was slow and laborious — a worker might thresh only a few bushels a day — and the flail was displaced during the 18th and 19th centuries by threshing machines and later the combine harvester, although it remained in use on small farms well into the 20th century.

Military flail

Peasant infantry readily adapted the agricultural flail as a weapon; Hussite armies of the 15th century used studded "war flails" to deadly effect against armored cavalry. Purpose-built military flails joined a spiked ball or bar to a haft by a chain, allowing the head to strike around a shield; the popular image of the one-handed "ball and chain" flail is largely a later invention, and surviving medieval examples are mostly two-handed weapons for infantry. Related flexible weapons include the Japanese nunchaku and kusarigama, and the Chinese meteor hammer and three-section staff.

Mine flail

In the 20th century the name was given to the mine flail, a vehicle — most famously the Sherman Crab of World War II — fitted with a rotating drum from which chains beat the ground ahead of the tank, detonating landmines in its path. Mine flails remain in use with modern military engineering units.
