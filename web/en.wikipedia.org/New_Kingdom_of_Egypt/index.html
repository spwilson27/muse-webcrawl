The New Kingdom, also called the Egyptian Empire, is the period in ancient Egyptian history between the sixteenth century BC and the eleventh century BC, covering the Eighteenth, Nineteenth and Twentieth Dynasties. It was Egypt's most prosperous and powerful era, when the kingdom became an empire stretching from Nubia in the south to Syria in the northeast.

Rise and empire

The New Kingdom began when Ahmose I expelled the Hyksos rulers and reunified Egypt. His successors, especially Thutmose I and Thutmose III — sometimes called the "Napoleon of Egypt" — campaigned deep into the Levant and Nubia. The female pharaoh Hatshepsut emphasised trade, famously sending an expedition to Punt, and built her mortuary temple at Deir el-Bahari. Pharaohs were buried in the Valley of the Kings near Thebes, while vast temple complexes grew at Karnak and Luxor.

The Amarna period

In the fourteenth century BC, Akhenaten upended Egyptian religion by promoting the sun-disk Aten above all gods, founding a new capital at Amarna and producing a distinctive, naturalistic art style. His reforms were reversed after his death; his successor was the young Tutankhamun, whose nearly intact tomb, found by Howard Carter in 1922, made him the most famous of all pharaohs.

Ramesside age and decline

The Nineteenth Dynasty's Ramesses II ("the Great") reigned 66 years, fought the Hittites at Kadesh (1274 BC) and concluded one of history's earliest known peace treaties, and covered Egypt with monuments, including Abu Simbel. In the Twentieth Dynasty, Ramesses III repelled the Sea Peoples, but strikes, tomb robbery, civil strife and economic decline followed; after Ramesses XI the kingdom split, ending the New Kingdom around 1070 BC.
