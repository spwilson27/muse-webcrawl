Argos

Country: Greece
Region: Peloponnese
Regional unit: Argolis
Population: about 22,000 (town)
Notable sites: Ancient theatre, Larissa castle, Heraion of Argos

Argos (Greek: Άργος) is a city in Argolis, in the Peloponnese of Greece, and one of the oldest continuously inhabited cities in the world, with a history of settlement reaching back some 7,000 years. In ancient Greece it was a powerful polis, a long-standing rival of Sparta, and the legendary home of the kings associated with the house of Atreus and the hero Diomedes.

History

Argos prospered in the Bronze Age as part of the Mycenaean world, and in Homer it is an epithet-laden kingdom linked with Agamemnon's realm. During the Archaic period Argos reached a height of power under the tyrant Pheidon (7th century BC), credited in tradition with reforming weights and measures, and won the Battle of Hysiae against Sparta in 669 BC. The city's fortunes reversed in 494 BC when the Spartan king Cleomenes I destroyed the Argive army at the Battle of Sepeia.

In the Classical period Argos was usually democratic and often allied with Athens against Sparta. It remained a significant city under the Hellenistic kingdoms and Rome; the geographer Pausanias described its monuments in the 2nd century AD. After periods of Byzantine, Frankish, Venetian, and Ottoman rule, Argos became part of the modern Greek state following the War of Independence.

Archaeology

The ancient city lies around the modern town. Landmarks include the theatre, one of the largest in Greece with some 20,000 seats, the Roman odeon and baths, the agora, and the citadel of Larissa above the city, crowned by a medieval castle. A few kilometres away stands the Heraion of Argos, the great sanctuary of the goddess Hera, the city's patron deity.

Mythology

In myth Argos takes its name from Argus, and the city is woven through the stories of Io, Danaus and the Danaids, Perseus (born to Danaë at Argos), and the Seven against Thebes. The many-eyed giant Argus Panoptes, slain by Hermes, also belongs to Argive tradition, as does the epithet "Argive" used by Homer for the Greeks collectively.
