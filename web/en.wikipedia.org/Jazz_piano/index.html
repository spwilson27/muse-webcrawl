Jazz piano

The piano has been central to jazz from ragtime and stride to modern improvisation.
Stylistic originsRagtime, blues, stride
Key figuresJelly Roll Morton, Art Tatum, Thelonious Monk, Bill Evans, Herbie Hancock
InstrumentsPiano, electric piano, organ
TechniquesComping, voicings, stride, improvisation

Jazz piano is the use of the piano in jazz, both as a solo instrument and as part of the rhythm section. Because the piano can supply melody, harmony, and rhythm simultaneously, pianists have shaped every era of jazz, from ragtime composers through the stride masters of Harlem to the harmonically adventurous modernists of the postwar period.

Early styles

Ragtime pianist-composers such as Scott Joplin laid the groundwork, while Jelly Roll Morton blended ragtime, blues, and improvisation into early jazz. In 1920s Harlem, stride pianists James P. Johnson, Fats Waller, and Willie "the Lion" Smith developed a virtuosic style built on a leaping left hand alternating bass notes and chords. Earl Hines added a horn-like, linear right hand, and Art Tatum's phenomenal technique set a standard that still awes pianists.

Bebop and beyond

In the bebop era, Bud Powell translated Charlie Parker's language to the keyboard, while Thelonious Monk forged an idiosyncratic, angular style as both pianist and composer. Bill Evans brought impressionistic voicings and interactive trio playing to prominence, and pianists such as Oscar Peterson, McCoy Tyner, Chick Corea, Keith Jarrett, and Herbie Hancock expanded the instrument's role in modal jazz, fusion, and free improvisation. Core techniques include "comping" (rhythmic chordal accompaniment), rootless voicings, and block chords.
