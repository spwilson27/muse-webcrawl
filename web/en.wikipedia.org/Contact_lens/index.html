Contact lens

A contact lens, or simply contact, is a thin lens placed directly on the surface of the eye. Contact lenses are considered medical devices and can be worn to correct vision or, for cosmetic or therapeutic reasons. In 2010, the worldwide market for contact lenses was estimated at US$6.1 billion, and an estimated 125 million people used contact lenses worldwide, including over 40 million in the United States.

History

Leonardo da Vinci sketched the concept of altering corneal power by immersing the eye in water in 1508, and René Descartes proposed a corneal lens tube in 1636. Practical lenses began with Adolf Gaston Eugen Fick's glass scleral lenses in 1888. Plastic PMMA corneal lenses followed in the late 1940s; Otto Wichterle and Drahoslav Lím developed hydrogel soft lenses in 1961, launched commercially in 1971. Silicone hydrogel materials, introduced in the late 1990s, greatly increased oxygen permeability and enabled safe extended wear.

Types

Corrective contact lenses correct refractive errors such as myopia, hyperopia, astigmatism (toric lenses), and presbyopia (multifocal designs). Lenses vary by wear schedule: daily disposables, two-weekly and monthly replacements, rigid gas-permeable lenses, and extended-wear designs approved for overnight use. Cosmetic lenses change the apparent colour of the iris; therapeutic lenses include bandage lenses used after injury or surgery.

Care and complications

Reusable lenses require cleaning and disinfection with multipurpose solution or hydrogen peroxide systems; failure to follow hygiene guidance risks microbial keratitis, including Acanthamoeba infection. Other complications include corneal hypoxia, giant papillary conjunctivitis, dry eye symptoms, and corneal neovascularisation. Proper fitting by an eye care professional and adherence to replacement schedules minimise risk.
