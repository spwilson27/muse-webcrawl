Motion of no confidence

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia · "Vote of no confidence" redirects here.

A motion of no confidence, also called a vote of no confidence, is a statement or vote about whether a person in a position of responsibility — most often a government in a parliamentary system — is still deemed fit to hold that position. If the motion passes, the government must typically resign or call an election; in some systems the head of government alone is removed.

Parliamentary systems

In Westminster-style systems, the government holds office only while it commands the confidence of the lower house. Defeat on an explicit no-confidence motion — or sometimes on a major bill or budget — obliges the prime minister to resign or seek a dissolution. Famous examples include the 1979 vote that brought down James Callaghan's Labour government in the United Kingdom by a single vote (311 to 310), leading to Margaret Thatcher's election victory.

Constructive votes

Some constitutions require a constructive vote of no confidence: parliament can remove a chancellor or prime minister only by simultaneously electing a successor with an absolute majority. Germany's Basic Law (Article 67) pioneered the device after the instability of the Weimar Republic; it has succeeded only once, in 1982, when Helmut Kohl replaced Helmut Schmidt. Spain uses a similar rule — in 2018 Mariano Rajoy was removed this way and replaced by Pedro Sánchez — as does Poland.

Other contexts

No-confidence votes also occur in presidential or hybrid systems for individual ministers, in corporate boards, trade unions and student bodies, and in some countries (such as Sweden) a declaration of no confidence in a single minister compels only that minister's resignation.
