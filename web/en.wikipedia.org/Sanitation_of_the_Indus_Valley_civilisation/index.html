Sanitation of the Indus Valley Civilisation

The cities of the Indus Valley Civilisation (c. 3300–1300 BCE) possessed some of the most advanced sanitation systems of the ancient world. At Mohenjo-daro, Harappa, Lothal and other sites, planned streets, wells, bathing platforms and covered drainage networks served a large urban population some two thousand years before comparable municipal systems appeared elsewhere.

Water supply

Mohenjo-daro alone contained hundreds of brick-lined wells — by some estimates around 700 — roughly one for every third house, an extraordinary density for a Bronze Age city. Wells were built of wedge-shaped bricks and many remained in use for centuries. Houses typically included a bathing platform, floored with tightly fitted bricks and sloped so that waste water drained away.

Drainage

Household bathrooms and latrines connected through terracotta or brick chutes to street drains. The main drains, built of baked brick set in mortar, ran along the principal streets, covered with bricks or limestone slabs that could be lifted for cleaning. Smaller channels fed into larger conduits, and soak pits and sumps collected solid waste. At Lothal, drains were also built of kiln-fired brick with careful gradients, and at Banawali and Kalibangan similar systems have been excavated. Some scholars describe the Mohenjo-daro network as the earliest known comprehensive urban sewerage system.

The Great Bath

The most celebrated hydraulic structure is the Great Bath at Mohenjo-daro: a watertight brick tank about 12 × 7 metres and 2.4 metres deep, sealed with bitumen, approached by staircases and surrounded by rooms. It is generally interpreted as a place for ritual bathing, reflecting the cultural importance of water purity that persisted in later South Asian traditions.

Significance

The uniformity of bricks, drains and street layouts across widely separated cities suggests strong civic organisation. After the decline of the Indus cities around 1900 BCE, this level of urban sanitation was not matched in the region for many centuries. The Indus systems are frequently cited in modern public-health history as evidence that large-scale sanitation is among the earliest functions of city government.
