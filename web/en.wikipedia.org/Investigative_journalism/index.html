Investigative journalism

Investigative journalism is a form of journalism in which reporters deeply investigate a single topic of interest, such as serious crimes, racial injustice, political corruption, or corporate wrongdoing. An investigative journalist may spend months or years researching and preparing a report. Practitioners sometimes use the terms "watchdog reporting" or "accountability reporting".

History

Precursors include the "muckrakers" of the Progressive Era in the United States, such as Ida Tarbell, Upton Sinclair, and Lincoln Steffens, who exposed monopolies, food industry abuses, and municipal corruption. In the 20th century, celebrated investigations included the reporting of Bob Woodward and Carl Bernstein on the Watergate scandal, which led to President Richard Nixon's resignation in 1974.

Methods

Investigative work relies on primary research: court and public records, data analysis, leaked documents, confidential sources, and interviews, often corroborating information others wish to keep secret. Modern cross-border collaborations — such as the International Consortium of Investigative Journalists' Panama Papers (2016) — pool reporters and documents across many news organizations.

Risks and challenges

Investigative reporters may face legal threats, surveillance, loss of access, and, in many countries, violence. The work is resource-intensive, and declining newsroom budgets have led to growth of nonprofit investigative outlets.
