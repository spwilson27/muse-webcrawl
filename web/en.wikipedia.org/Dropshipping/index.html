Dropshipping

Dropshipping is a retail fulfilment method in which a store does not keep the products it sells in stock. Instead, when a store sells a product, it purchases the item from a third party — typically a wholesaler or manufacturer — which ships it directly to the customer. As a result, the seller never handles the product directly.

Business model

The dropshipper markets products, often through an online storefront built on platforms such as Shopify, and forwards orders to the supplier, which handles storage, packing, and delivery. The retailer profits from the difference between the retail price and the wholesale price. Because no inventory is held, start-up costs are low, and the model became popular with small e-commerce businesses in the 2010s, frequently using suppliers listed on marketplaces such as AliExpress.

Advantages and disadvantages

Advantages include minimal capital requirements, low overheads, location independence, and the ability to offer a wide product range. Disadvantages include thin margins, little control over shipping speed and packaging, liability for supplier errors, and intense competition because the same products are offered by many sellers. Long delivery times and inconsistent product quality are common consumer complaints.

Legality and criticism

Dropshipping itself is legal in most jurisdictions, but some practitioners have been criticised for misleading advertising, selling counterfeit goods, infringing trademarks, or failing to comply with consumer-protection and tax rules. Regulators advise consumers that their legal rights apply against the seller, not the overseas supplier.
