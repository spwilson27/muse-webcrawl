Capital budgeting

Capital budgeting
FieldCorporate finance
PurposeEvaluating and selecting long-term investments
Key methodsNet present value (NPV), internal rate of return (IRR), payback period, profitability index

Capital budgeting, also called investment appraisal, is the process by which a business evaluates potential major investments or expenditures — such as new plants, machinery, products or research programmes — to decide which projects are worth funding. Because capital projects commit large sums for many years, firms analyse the forecast cash flows of each project against the cost of capital to maximise shareholder value.

Methods

The net present value (NPV) method discounts all expected future cash flows to the present at a required rate of return and subtracts the initial outlay; a positive NPV indicates the project creates value, and NPV is generally regarded as the theoretically soundest criterion. The internal rate of return (IRR) is the discount rate that makes NPV zero; a project is accepted if its IRR exceeds the hurdle rate, though IRR can mislead when comparing mutually exclusive projects or when cash flows change sign more than once. Simpler rules include the payback period — how quickly the initial outlay is recovered — and its discounted variant, which ignore cash flows after payback. The profitability index expresses NPV per unit of investment and is useful under capital rationing.

Process and complications

A typical process involves generating proposals, estimating incremental after-tax cash flows (excluding sunk costs and financing charges, but including opportunity costs and working-capital changes), assessing risk through sensitivity and scenario analysis, and post-auditing completed projects. Riskier projects are evaluated at higher discount rates, often the firm's weighted average cost of capital adjusted for project risk. Real options analysis extends capital budgeting by valuing managerial flexibility to expand, defer or abandon a project.
