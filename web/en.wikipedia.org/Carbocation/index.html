Carbocation

A carbocation is an ion with a positively charged carbon atom. Carbocations are important reactive intermediates in organic chemistry, arising in reactions such as SN1 substitution and E1 elimination, electrophilic addition, and many rearrangements. The positively charged carbon typically has only six valence electrons and a planar, sp²-hybridised geometry with an empty p orbital.

Structure and stability

Carbocation stability increases with substitution: tertiary carbocations are more stable than secondary, which are more stable than primary, which are more stable than methyl. Stability is increased by hyperconjugation and inductive electron donation from neighbouring alkyl groups, and greatly increased by resonance in allylic and benzylic systems. Aromatic carbocations such as the cyclopropenyl cation and the tropylium ion are especially stabilised.

Rearrangements

Carbocations frequently undergo rearrangement: a hydride or alkyl group shifts with its bonding electrons (Wagner–Meerwein rearrangement) to convert a less stable cation into a more stable one. Such shifts explain many otherwise surprising reaction products and are central to terpene biosynthesis in nature.

History

Carbocations were first proposed in the early 20th century to explain the behaviour of triphenylmethyl salts and rearrangement reactions. George A. Olah's work with superacids in the 1960s allowed long-lived carbocations to be observed directly by NMR spectroscopy, for which he received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1994.
