Indentured servitude in British America

Indentured servitude in British America was a labour system under which a person agreed by contract (an indenture) to work for an employer for a fixed term, typically four to seven years, in exchange for passage to the colonies and maintenance during the term. It was a principal source of labour in the seventeenth- and early eighteenth-century colonies, especially in the Chesapeake; historians estimate that a large share — in some colonies a majority — of English immigrants arrived as indentured servants.

Terms and conditions

Servants' contracts could be sold by ship captains to planters and other employers. During the term, servants could not marry without permission, could be punished corporally, and had terms extended for offences such as running away or, for women, pregnancy. At freedom they were owed "freedom dues" — clothing, tools, corn and sometimes land — though the value and reliability of these varied.

Decline

From the late seventeenth century, enslaved African labour increasingly displaced indentured servitude in the plantation colonies, as falling servant migration, improving conditions in England and the economics of lifetime hereditary slavery changed employers' calculations. Indentured migration continued on a smaller scale into the eighteenth century, including transported convicts and "redemptioners" from continental Europe.
