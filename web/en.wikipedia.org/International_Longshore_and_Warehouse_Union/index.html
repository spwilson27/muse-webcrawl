The International Longshore and Warehouse Union (ILWU) is a labor union which primarily represents dock workers on the West Coast of the United States, Hawaii, and British Columbia, Canada. It also represents workers in warehousing, agriculture, tourism and other industries through divisions and affiliated unions. The union was established in 1937 after the 1934 West Coast longshore strike, which included the events of "Bloody Thursday" in San Francisco.

History

Longshore workers on the Pacific Coast had been represented by a district of the International Longshoremen's Association (ILA). Under the leadership of Harry Bridges, the district won union recognition and coastwide bargaining after the 1934 strike and general strike in San Francisco. In 1937 the Pacific district broke from the ILA and affiliated with the new Congress of Industrial Organizations (CIO) as the ILWU. Bridges led the union for four decades, and the ILWU negotiated landmark agreements such as the 1960 Mechanization and Modernization Agreement, which accepted containerisation in exchange for income and job guarantees. The ILWU was expelled from the CIO in 1950 during anti-communist purges, remaining independent for decades before affiliating with the AFL–CIO in 1988; it disaffiliated again in 2013.

Structure and influence

The union is known for its internal democracy, with elected local officers and a powerful coast committee negotiating with the Pacific Maritime Association of shipping lines and terminal operators. Work stoppages, such as the 2002 lockout closed by a Taft–Hartley injunction, have underscored the economic significance of West Coast ports.
