Friedel–Crafts Reaction

The Friedel–Crafts reactions are a set of reactions developed by Charles Friedel and James Crafts in 1877 to attach substituents to an aromatic ring. The two principal types are Friedel–Crafts alkylation and Friedel–Crafts acylation, both proceeding by electrophilic aromatic substitution and typically catalysed by a strong Lewis acid such as aluminium chloride.

Alkylation

In Friedel–Crafts alkylation, an alkyl halide reacts with an aromatic ring in the presence of a Lewis acid catalyst, replacing a ring hydrogen with an alkyl group. The reaction is important industrially, for example in producing ethylbenzene from benzene and ethene. Drawbacks include carbocation rearrangements, polyalkylation (since the product is more reactive than the starting material), and failure with strongly deactivated rings.

Acylation

In Friedel–Crafts acylation, an acyl chloride or acid anhydride installs an acyl group on the ring, giving an aryl ketone. The product is deactivated, so multiple acylation is avoided and no rearrangement occurs. A subsequent reduction of the ketone (for example Clemmensen or Wolff–Kishner reduction) makes acylation-then-reduction a common strategy for installing alkyl groups that cannot be added cleanly by direct alkylation.

Limitations

Both reactions fail on strongly deactivated aromatic rings, such as nitrobenzene, and with amine substituents that complex the catalyst. Modern variants use solid acid catalysts and greener conditions for industrial processes.
