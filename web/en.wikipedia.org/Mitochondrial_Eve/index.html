Mitochondrial Eve

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In human genetics, the Mitochondrial Eve (also mt-Eve or mt-MRCA) is the most recent woman from whom all living humans are descended in an unbroken line purely through their mothers. In other words, she is the most recent common matrilineal ancestor of everyone alive today. She is estimated to have lived roughly 100,000–230,000 years ago, most likely in East Africa.

Concept

Because mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) is passed from mother to child essentially unchanged except by mutation, every living person's mtDNA descends from a single ancestral molecule. Tracing mutations backward, lineages coalesce until only one remains: the mtDNA of Mitochondrial Eve. She was not the only woman alive in her time, nor the first anatomically modern human woman — she is simply the one whose purely female line of descent happens to have survived unbroken, while the matrilines of her contemporaries eventually ended (for example, where a woman had only sons or no children).

The 1987 study

The concept became famous through a 1987 paper in Nature by Rebecca Cann, Mark Stoneking and Allan Wilson, who analysed mtDNA from 147 people across several continents and concluded that all sampled lineages converged on a single African ancestor around 140,000–200,000 years ago. The study provided strong support for the recent out-of-Africa model of human evolution. Later whole-mitogenome studies refined the dates and identified the deepest branches (haplogroup L) among southern African populations.

Common misconceptions

Mitochondrial Eve was not the sole female ancestor of modern humans: most of the human genome descends from thousands of her contemporaries. Nor was she necessarily paired with Y-chromosomal Adam, the analogous most recent common patrilineal ancestor, who may have lived at a quite different time. The identity of "Eve" also shifts over time as lineages die out — a future generation's mt-MRCA will be a descendant of today's.
