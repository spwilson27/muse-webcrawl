Uluṙu-Kata Tjuṯa National Park is a protected area in the Northern Territory of Australia. The park is home to both Uluru and Kata Tjuṯa, two of Australia's most famous geological formations, and lies some 1,431 kilometres south of Darwin and 440 kilometres southwest of Alice Springs by road. It is listed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

History

The area was first protected as the South-Western Reserve in 1920 and proclaimed the Ayers Rock–Mount Olga National Park in 1958. In 1985, under the Aboriginal Land Rights Act, title was granted to the Uluru-Kata Tjuta Aboriginal Land Trust and the land leased back to the Director of National Parks for 99 years, with the park jointly managed by a board with an Aṉangu majority. The park adopted its present dual name in 1995.

Flora, fauna and culture

Despite the arid climate, the park supports more than 400 plant species, 21 native mammal species and a rich reptile fauna, including species reintroduced after local extinction. The landscape is central to Tjukurpa, the Aṉangu law, and many sites have restricted access for cultural reasons. Walks at Uluru and the Valley of the Winds at Kata Tjuṯa are popular with visitors, while photography of some sacred sites is prohibited.
