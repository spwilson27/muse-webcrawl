On the Crown

On the Crown (Περὶ τοῦ στεφάνου)
Author: Demosthenes
Delivered: 330 BC, Athens
Type: Forensic/political oration

On the Crown (Ancient Greek: Περὶ τοῦ Στεφάνου, Perì toû stephánou) is the most celebrated oration of the Athenian statesman and orator Demosthenes, delivered in 330 BC. Widely regarded as a masterpiece of Greek prose rhetoric, it is Demosthenes' political testament — a defence of his entire career and of Athens' resistance to Philip II of Macedon.

Background

In 336 BC the politician Ctesiphon proposed that Athens honour Demosthenes with a golden crown for his services to the city. Aeschines, Demosthenes' longtime rival, indicted Ctesiphon for proposing an illegal decree, arguing that Demosthenes had not yet passed the required audit of his offices and that his policies had harmed Athens. The trial, delayed for years, finally took place in 330 BC, after Alexander the Great had destroyed Thebes and Macedonian supremacy was unchallengeable.

Content

Demosthenes surveys his whole public life: his opposition to Philip, the embassies, the alliance with Thebes, and the defeat at Chaeronea in 338 BC. He argues that even though Athens lost, fighting for freedom was the only honourable course — as at Marathon and Salamis — and that fortune, not his policy, caused the outcome. He attacks Aeschines' record and character in scathing terms, invoking the famous oath "by those who fell at Marathon." Aeschines failed to win one-fifth of the jurors' votes, was fined, and left Athens for Rhodes.

Legacy

The speech was studied throughout antiquity as the supreme example of the grand style; Cicero translated and imitated it, and it remained a school text into the Byzantine era and beyond.
