Nonsense literature

Nonsense literature is a broad categorization of literature that balances elements that make sense with elements that do not, or subverts conventions of language and logic to playful or satirical effect. The effect of nonsense is often caused by an excess of meaning, rather than a lack of it.

History

Nursery rhymes such as "Hey Diddle Diddle" are early examples of the form. The genre flourished in 19th-century Britain with Edward Lear, whose A Book of Nonsense (1846) popularized the limerick, and Lewis Carroll, whose poems "Jabberwocky" (in Through the Looking-Glass, 1871) and "The Hunting of the Snark" (1876) remain canonical works of literary nonsense.

Characteristics

Nonsense verse often invents words (portmanteaus), uses rigorous rhyme and metre to contain absurd content, and features impossible creatures, journeys, and logic. Scholars such as Elizabeth Sewell and Wim Tigges have analysed how nonsense creates a self-contained world governed by its own rules.

Later developments

The tradition influenced Edward Gorey, Spike Milligan, Dr. Seuss, and the surrealists and Dadaists, who prized irrational juxtaposition. Nonsense techniques also appear in the work of James Joyce and in modern children's literature.
