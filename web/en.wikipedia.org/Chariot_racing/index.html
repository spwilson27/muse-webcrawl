Chariot racing

Chariot racing (Greek: ἁρματοδρομία, harmatodromia; Latin: ludi circenses) was one of the most popular sports in ancient Greece and Rome. Four-horse chariot racing, in the quadriga, was a feature of the ancient Olympic Games from 680 BC, while two-horse biga racing was added later. In Rome, races at the Circus Maximus drew crowds of well over 100,000 spectators and were the centerpiece of public festivals.

Greece

Chariot racing is depicted as early as the funeral games for Patroclus in Homer's Iliad. In the Olympics, it was the owner of the team, not the driver, who was crowned victor, allowing women who owned teams — such as the Spartan princess Cynisca — to win Olympic victories. Greek races ran around turning posts in a hippodrome, typically twelve laps for the four-horse event.

Rome

Roman racing was organized around four professional factions — the Reds, Blues, Greens, and Whites — whose colors inspired passionate and sometimes violent loyalties among fans. Charioteers such as Gaius Appuleius Diocles, who won 1,462 of 4,257 races in the 2nd century AD, became wealthy celebrities; Diocles' lifetime winnings are calculated to have been enough to provide grain for the entire city of Rome for a year. Races usually ran seven laps of the Circus Maximus, counted with wooden eggs and dolphin-shaped markers. Crashes, called naufragia ("shipwrecks"), were frequent at the turning posts (metae).

Byzantine era

Chariot racing continued in Constantinople's Hippodrome, where the Blues and Greens factions became powerful political associations; their rivalry erupted in the Nika Riots of 532 AD, which nearly overthrew Emperor Justinian I and left tens of thousands dead. The sport declined through the early Middle Ages.
