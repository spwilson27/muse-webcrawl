Richard II (6 January 1367 – c. 14 February 1400), also known as Richard of Bordeaux, was King of England from 1377 until his deposition in 1399. The younger son of Edward, the Black Prince, he succeeded his grandfather Edward III at the age of ten. His reign saw the Peasants' Revolt of 1381, bitter struggles with the baronial opposition of the Lords Appellant, and his eventual overthrow by his cousin Henry Bolingbroke, who became Henry IV. Richard II was the last king of the main line of the House of Plantagenet.

Early reign and the Peasants' Revolt

During Richard's minority, government was exercised by councils strongly influenced by his uncle John of Gaunt. In 1381, grievances over poll taxes and serfdom erupted in the Peasants' Revolt led by Wat Tyler. The fourteen-year-old king met the rebels at Mile End and Smithfield; Tyler was killed, and Richard's personal courage in facing the crowd was widely remarked, though the concessions he had promised were revoked once the revolt collapsed.

Conflict with the Lords Appellant

As Richard grew older he favored a circle of courtiers, including Robert de Vere and Michael de la Pole, and pursued peace with France, angering magnates who profited from war. In 1386–88 the Lords Appellant — among them the king's uncle Gloucester and the Earl of Arundel — took control of government, defeated de Vere's forces at Radcot Bridge, and in the Merciless Parliament of 1388 executed or exiled several of Richard's closest advisers. Richard reasserted his authority gradually from 1389.

"Tyranny" and deposition

After the death of his queen, Anne of Bohemia, in 1394, Richard's rule grew increasingly arbitrary. In 1397 he arrested Gloucester (who died, likely murdered, at Calais), Arundel (executed), and Warwick. He banished Henry Bolingbroke and Thomas Mowbray in 1398, and on John of Gaunt's death in 1399 he seized the Lancastrian estates rather than allowing Bolingbroke to inherit. While Richard campaigned in Ireland, Bolingbroke landed in England, gathered support, and forced Richard's surrender at Flint Castle. Richard was compelled to abdicate; the crown passed to Henry IV, and Richard died the following year at Pontefract Castle, probably by starvation.

Character and legacy

Richard was a patron of art and architecture: the rebuilding of Westminster Hall with its celebrated hammerbeam roof, and the Wilton Diptych, date from his reign. Historians have debated his temperament and mental stability; his exalted view of kingship influenced later theories of royal authority. William Shakespeare's play Richard II (c. 1595) fixed his image as a poetic, self-dramatizing king who lost his realm through misgovernment.
