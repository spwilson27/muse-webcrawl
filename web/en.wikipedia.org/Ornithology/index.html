Ornithology

Field observation is central to ornithological research.

Ornithology is the branch of zoology devoted to the study of birds. It encompasses their anatomy, physiology, behaviour, ecology, evolution, classification, distribution, and conservation. Birds are among the best-studied of all animal groups, thanks both to their visibility and to the contributions of a large community of amateur birdwatchers.

History

Aristotle recorded observations of bird migration and anatomy, though the scientific study of birds took shape in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries with works such as Conrad Gessner's Historia Avium and the classification of Francis Willughby and John Ray. In the nineteenth century, bird specimens collected worldwide fed the development of taxonomy and evolutionary theory — Darwin's finches of the Galápagos being the most famous example. John James Audubon's The Birds of America (1827–1838) set new standards for bird art. In the twentieth century, figures such as Konrad Lorenz, Niko Tinbergen, and David Lack turned ornithology toward behaviour and ecology.

Methods

Ornithologists employ field observation, ringing (banding) to track individual birds, mist-netting, museum collections, bioacoustics, radar, satellite telemetry, and molecular phylogenetics. Large citizen-science projects such as eBird and annual Christmas Bird Counts generate data on a continental scale.

Applications

Bird studies inform conservation, environmental monitoring — birds being sensitive indicators of ecosystem health — and the management of hazards such as bird strikes on aircraft.
