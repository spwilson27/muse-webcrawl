Simulation hypothesis

The simulation hypothesis proposes that what humans experience as reality is an artificial simulation — for example, a computer simulation run by a technologically advanced civilisation. The idea has antecedents in philosophy, from Zhuangzi's butterfly dream and Plato's cave to Descartes's evil demon, and in modern form in the "brain in a vat" thought experiment.

Bostrom's simulation argument

In a widely discussed 2003 paper, philosopher Nick Bostrom argued that at least one of three propositions is very likely true: (1) almost all civilisations at our level go extinct before becoming "posthuman"; (2) posthuman civilisations are almost never interested in running significant numbers of simulations of their evolutionary history ("ancestor simulations"); or (3) we are almost certainly living in a computer simulation. The argument reasons that if posthuman civilisations run many simulations, simulated observers would vastly outnumber real ones. Bostrom emphasised that the argument does not by itself say which disjunct is true.

Discussion and criticism

The hypothesis has been debated by physicists, computer scientists and philosophers. Some physicists have proposed looking for potential signatures of simulation, such as lattice-like discretisation in cosmic rays; others argue that computational limits, the problem of nested simulations, or the lack of a plausible motive undermine the idea. Public figures including Elon Musk have endorsed the idea as probable, while critics note it is currently untestable and inherits classic sceptical problems. Related ideas include simulated consciousness, digital physics, and the fictional treatment popularised by the film The Matrix (1999).
