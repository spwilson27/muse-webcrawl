Internment of Japanese Canadians

From 1942 to 1949, Canada forcibly relocated and incarcerated over 22,000 Japanese Canadians — comprising over 90% of the total Japanese Canadian population — from British Columbia's Pacific coast. Following the attack on Pearl Harbor on December 7, 1941, the federal government invoked the War Measures Act and ordered the removal of all people of Japanese ancestry from a 100-mile-wide zone along the coast.

Some were sent to internment camps in the interior of British Columbia, others to prisoner-of-war camps in Ontario, road camps, or sugar beet farms on the Prairies. The government also seized and sold Japanese Canadian property — homes, farms, fishing boats, and businesses — without consent, using the proceeds to pay for the internment itself.

Background

Anti-Asian sentiment in British Columbia dated to the late 19th century. Japanese immigrants faced discriminatory laws, including disenfranchisement, and violence such as the 1907 anti-Asian riots in Vancouver. Despite this, by 1941 a thriving community existed, concentrated in fishing, farming, and small business.

Internment

In early 1942, under Order-in-Council P.C. 1486, Japanese Canadians were given 24 hours' notice and processed through Hastings Park in Vancouver. Men were separated from their families and sent to road camps; families were housed in livestock barns before transfer to camps such as Tashme, New Denver, Slocan, and Greenwood. Conditions were harsh, with uninsulated shacks, no electricity, and limited schooling.

Deportation and dispersal

In 1945 the government forced internees to choose between moving east of the Rockies or "repatriation" to Japan — a country many had never seen. Nearly 4,000 were deported before the policy was abandoned in 1947. Restrictions on movement and the franchise lasted until April 1, 1949, four years after the war ended.

Redress

On September 22, 1988, Prime Minister Brian Mulroney delivered a formal apology in the House of Commons and announced a redress agreement: $21,000 to each surviving internee, a community fund, and the creation of the Canadian Race Relations Foundation.
