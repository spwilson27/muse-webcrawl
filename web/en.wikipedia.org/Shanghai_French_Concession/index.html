The Shanghai French Concession (French: Concession française de Changhaï; Chinese: 上海法租界) was a foreign concession in Shanghai, China, from 1849 until 1943. Established by the French consul Charles de Montigny under the arrangements following the Treaty of Whampoa (1844) and the opening of Shanghai by the Treaty of Nanjing, the concession was administered separately from the neighbouring Shanghai International Settlement, remaining under direct French consular and municipal control.

Beginning as a modest strip of land between the Chinese walled city and the British settlement along the Huangpu River, the concession expanded westward in stages — notably in 1900 and 1914 — until it covered much of what is now Xuhui District and part of Huangpu District. Its tree-lined avenues, garden villas, cafés, and churches gave it a distinctly Parisian character, and it became home to a large population of Chinese residents, White Russian émigrés, and French and other foreign nationals.

History

Founding

France obtained the right to a concession in April 1849, when the Daotai of Shanghai, Lin Gui, proclaimed the grant of land to the French consul. Unlike the British and American settlements, which merged in 1863 to form the International Settlement, the French kept their own administration, police (including the Garde Municipale with many Vietnamese and Chinese officers), courts, and municipal council.

Expansion and character

The concession grew through successive grants, reaching its maximum extent after the 1914 expansion, which pushed its boundary west to the Catholic centre of Xujiahui (Zikawei), with its cathedral, observatory, and orphanages run by French Jesuits. Grand avenues were laid out — Avenue Joffre (now Huaihai Road), Avenue Pétain, Avenue Foch, Route Ferguson — and planted with London plane trees, which remain the district's signature. The concession housed the city's most fashionable shops, dance halls, and residences, as well as notorious gambling houses and opium dens on its fringes.

Politics and society

The concession was the cradle of the Chinese Communist movement: the First Congress of the Chinese Communist Party met in July 1921 in a shikumen house on Rue Wantz (now Xingye Road) in the concession. Because of French extraterritorial jurisdiction, the district also sheltered political dissidents, gangsters such as the Green Gang boss Du Yuesheng, who worked closely with the French authorities, and waves of Russian refugees after 1917.

Retrocession

In 1943, during the Second World War, the Vichy French government agreed to relinquish the concession to the Japanese-sponsored Reorganized National Government of China; the formal transfer occurred on 30 July 1943, and the postwar French Republic confirmed the retrocession in the Sino-French treaty of 1946. The concession's institutions — its municipal services, schools, and hospitals — were absorbed into the Chinese administration of Shanghai.

Legacy

Today the former concession is among Shanghai's most desirable and characterful neighbourhoods, known for its plane-tree avenues, shikumen lanes, Art Deco apartment blocks, and historic villas. Major landmarks within its boundaries include Fuxing Park (laid out by the French in 1909), the former residence of Sun Yat-sen, the Shanghai Conservatory of Music, and numerous consulates and historic hotels. The district roughly corresponds to the southern part of Huangpu District and the eastern part of Xuhui District.
