The Alfonsine Tables (Spanish: Tablas Alfonsíes, Latin: Tabulae Alphonsinae), sometimes spelled Alphonsine Tables, provide data for computing the position of the Sun, Moon and planets relative to the fixed stars. The tables are named after Alfonso X of Castile, who sponsored their creation. They were compiled in Toledo, Spain, and contain astronomical data starting on January 1, 1252, the date of the coronation of the king.

Production

Alfonso X assembled a team of scholars, known as the Toledo School of Translators, who among other tasks were tasked with producing new tables that updated the Tables of Toledo. The new tables were based on earlier astronomical works and observations by Arab astronomers, to which they added observations by astronomers Alfonso had gathered in Toledo, among them Yehuda ben Moshe and Isaac ibn Sid. The work was carried out between about 1252 and 1270, with the epoch set at the beginning of Alfonso's reign.

The original Spanish version is lost, but a Latin version produced in Paris in the early 14th century, associated with John of Saxony and John of Lignères, became the standard form in which the tables circulated across Europe.

Methodology

The methods used for the tables followed the geocentric model of Claudius Ptolemy's Almagest. Each planet's motion was modelled with deferents and epicycles. An important parameter the Alfonsine tables made use of was the length of the tropical year, attributed to Thabit ibn Qurra, according to which the equinoxes precess in a variable fashion known as trepidation. The mean tropical year length used, 365 days, 5 hours, 49 minutes and 16 seconds, was very close to the value used in the later Gregorian calendar.

Influence

The Alfonsine Tables were the most popular astronomical tables in Europe from the 13th century until the 16th century, used by astronomers including Nicolaus Copernicus, who owned a copy during his student years. Georg von Peuerbach's Tabulae Eclipsium (c. 1459) was based on them. Erasmus Reinhold's Prutenic Tables (1551) were based on Copernican theory but retained many Alfonsine parameters. The tables were first printed in Venice in 1483 by Erhard Ratdolt, and numerous editions followed.

A famous anecdote relates that Alfonso, upon hearing an explanation of the complexity of the Ptolemaic system, remarked that if God had consulted him at the Creation, he would have recommended something simpler. Historians consider the attribution dubious, but the remark became proverbial.
