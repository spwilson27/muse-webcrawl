Environmental sustainability

Environmental sustainability is the responsibility to conserve natural resources and protect global ecosystems to support health and wellbeing, now and in the future. It is one of the principal dimensions of sustainability, alongside social and economic sustainability. Because so many decisions that impact the environment are not felt immediately, a key element of environmental sustainability is its forward-looking nature.

Definition

The concept is closely related to the definition of sustainable development given by the Brundtland Commission in 1987: development that "meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs." Environmental sustainability implies meeting current human needs without undermining the capacity of ecosystems to regenerate, maintaining biological diversity, and keeping the consumption of resources and the production of wastes within the limits the planet can support.

Key areas

  • Climate stability — limiting greenhouse gas emissions to keep global warming within safe bounds.
  • Biodiversity — halting species loss and protecting habitats and ecosystem services.
  • Resource management — sustainable use of water, soil, forests, fisheries and minerals.
  • Pollution control — reducing air, water and soil contamination, including plastics and chemical waste.
  • Energy transition — shifting from fossil fuels to renewable energy sources.

Frameworks and goals

International frameworks include the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (2015), the Paris Agreement on climate change, and the concept of planetary boundaries, which identifies nine Earth-system processes that should remain within safe limits. Tools used to measure progress include ecological footprint accounting, life-cycle assessment, and environmental impact assessment.

Challenges

Barriers include population growth, consumption patterns in affluent economies, rebound effects that erode efficiency gains, and conflicts between short-term economic interests and long-term ecological stability. Many scholars argue that incremental improvements are insufficient without systemic changes in energy, food, transport and manufacturing systems.
