Anonymous work

Many early books, ballads and pamphlets were published anonymously

An anonymous work is a work, such as a book, poem, or piece of music, whose creator is not identified. Anonymity differs from pseudonymity, in which an author uses an assumed name. In many cultures and periods anonymity was the norm: medieval epics, folk tales, religious texts and much early literature circulated without named authors, and attribution was sometimes added only later by tradition.

Reasons for anonymity

Authors may remain anonymous to avoid persecution or prosecution (as with political pamphlets and dissident writing), to let a work be judged on its own merits, for modesty or religious humility, for safety, or because the identity of the creator was simply never recorded. Famous examples include Beowulf, the novel Primary Colors (initially published as "by Anonymous", later revealed as Joe Klein), and much of the literature of antiquity.

Legal treatment

Copyright law makes special provision for anonymous and pseudonymous works. Under the Berne Convention and the laws of many countries, the term of protection for such works is calculated from publication rather than the author's death — for example, 95 years from publication or 120 years from creation in the United States for works made for hire and anonymous works — unless the author's identity becomes known. Publishers may exercise rights on behalf of unknown authors.
