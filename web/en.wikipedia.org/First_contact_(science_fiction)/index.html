First contact (science fiction)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

First contact is a common theme in science fiction about the first meeting between humans and extraterrestrial life, or of any sentient species' first encounter with another. The theme allows writers to explore subjects including xenophobia, transcendentalism, linguistics, and diplomacy, and to examine how humanity might react to contact with a culture utterly different from its own.

Overview

First contact stories range from catastrophic invasions to benign, transformative encounters. H. G. Wells's The War of the Worlds (1898) established the hostile-contact archetype, imagining Martians colonizing Earth much as European powers had colonized other peoples. Later writers explored peaceful or ambiguous contact: Murray Leinster's story "First Contact" (1945) gave the theme its name, presenting a stand-off between human and alien ships solved by an exchange of vessels. Other major treatments include Stanisław Lem's Solaris (1961), in which the alien proves essentially unknowable; Arthur C. Clarke's Childhood's End (1953) and Rendezvous with Rama (1973); and the films Close Encounters of the Third Kind (1977), Contact (1997), and Arrival (2016), the latter centered on the problem of communicating with beings whose language structures their experience of time.

Variations

The theme has many sub-variations: contact by radio signal rather than in person; covert contact already long established; contact initiated by humans on another species' world, raising ethical questions modeled on colonial first contacts between human cultures; and "uplift" scenarios in which a more advanced species deliberately raises another to sentience. Anthropological science fiction, such as the work of Ursula K. Le Guin, often uses first contact as a tool for examining human societies themselves.

Relation to real-world protocols

The fictional theme parallels real scientific discussion in SETI research, where "post-detection protocols" have been drafted for the announcement and handling of a genuine signal, and where the messaging extraterrestrial intelligence (METI) debate asks whether humanity should actively transmit. The common scientific assumption that contact would occur by signal across interstellar distances, rather than by visitation, is reflected in stories built around decoding a transmission.
