Mythology

The gods of Olympus, a central subject of Greek mythology

Mythology is the study of myths — collections of stories, traditions and beliefs belonging to a particular culture or religious tradition. A myth is a traditional story, especially one concerning the early history of a people or explaining a natural or social phenomenon, typically involving supernatural beings or events. The term "mythology" may also refer to the body of myths of a specific culture, such as Greek mythology, Norse mythology or Egyptian mythology.

Myths are often endorsed by rulers and priests and closely linked to religion or spirituality. Many societies group their myths, legends and history together, considering myths and legends to be true accounts of their remote past.

Etymology

The word myth comes from the Greek mythos (μῦθος), meaning "story" or "word". In early Greek usage it simply meant a tale; only later, in contrast with logos (reasoned account), did it acquire the sense of a fictional or unverifiable story. "Mythology" combines mythos with -logia, "the study or account of".

Functions of myth

Scholars have proposed many functions for myth: explaining natural phenomena (aetiological myths), legitimating social institutions and kingship (charter myths), encoding moral values, and expressing psychological truths. The anthropologist Bronisław Malinowski argued that myth acts as a "charter" for social customs; Mircea Eliade stressed myth's role in connecting believers with a sacred time of origins (illud tempus); Joseph Campbell popularized the idea of the "monomyth" or hero's journey shared across cultures.

Major mythological traditions

  • Greek and Roman mythology — the Olympian gods, heroes such as Heracles and Odysseus, recorded in Hesiod, Homer and Ovid
  • Norse mythology — Odin, Thor and Ragnarök, preserved in the Eddas
  • Egyptian mythology — Ra, Osiris, Isis and the myth of death and rebirth
  • Mesopotamian mythology — the Epic of Gilgamesh and the Enuma Elish creation epic
  • Hindu mythology — the Vedas, the Mahabharata and Ramayana
  • Chinese mythology — Pangu, Nüwa and the Jade Emperor

Comparative mythology

Comparative mythology studies the similarities among myths of different cultures, seeking common structures and origins, including Proto-Indo-European religion. Recurring motifs include the creation of the world from chaos, a great flood, the dying-and-rising god, the cosmic world tree, and the trickster.
