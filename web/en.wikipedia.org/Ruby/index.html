A ruby is a pinkish-red to blood-red coloured gemstone, a variety of the mineral corundum (aluminium oxide). Ruby is one of the traditional cardinal gems, alongside amethyst, sapphire, emerald and diamond. Its red colour is caused mainly by the presence of the element chromium. The name derives from ruber, Latin for red.

Properties

Rubies have a hardness of 9.0 on the Mohs scale, exceeded among natural gems only by moissanite and diamond. All natural rubies have imperfections, including colour impurities and inclusions of rutile needles known as "silk"; gemmologists use these to distinguish natural rubies from synthetics and substitutes. Some rubies show a three- or six-point asterism, or "star", when cut as cabochons.

Sources and value

Historically mined in the Mogok Valley of Myanmar (Burma), whose "pigeon blood" stones set the price standard, rubies also come from Thailand, Cambodia, Vietnam, India, Afghanistan, and more recently Mozambique and other East African deposits. A ruby's value depends primarily on colour, then on clarity, cut and carat weight. In 2015 the Sunrise Ruby, a 25.59-carat Burmese stone, sold at auction for over US$30 million, a world record per carat for a coloured gemstone.

Applications

Beyond jewellery, synthetic ruby was the gain medium of the first laser (1960), and ruby bearings are used in watches and precision instruments for their hardness and low friction.
