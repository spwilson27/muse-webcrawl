Helmholtz free energy

A thermodynamic system at constant temperature and volume

In thermodynamics, the Helmholtz free energy (or Helmholtz energy) is a thermodynamic potential that measures the useful work obtainable from a closed thermodynamic system at a constant temperature (isothermal) and volume (isochoric). It is named after the German physicist Hermann von Helmholtz.

Definition

The Helmholtz energy is defined as

A ≡ U − TS

where A is the Helmholtz free energy (SI unit: joule), U is the internal energy of the system, T is the absolute temperature, and S is the entropy of the system. The change in Helmholtz energy during a process equals the maximum amount of work the system can perform in a thermodynamic process in which temperature is held constant; at constant temperature and volume, the Helmholtz energy is minimized at equilibrium.

Mathematical development

From the first law of thermodynamics and the definition of entropy, the differential of A for a reversible process is dA = −S dT − P dV + Σ μi dNi, so that temperature, pressure, and chemical potentials can be recovered as partial derivatives of A. The Helmholtz energy is the Legendre transform of the internal energy with respect to entropy.

Statistical mechanics

In statistical mechanics, the Helmholtz energy is related to the canonical partition function Z by A = −kBT ln Z, a relation that makes it the natural potential for systems held at constant temperature and volume.
