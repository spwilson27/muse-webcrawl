Giovanni Boccaccio (16 June 1313 – 21 December 1375) was an Italian writer, poet and humanist, best known for The Decameron, a collection of one hundred tales that is a landmark of Italian prose and of world literature. With Dante Alighieri and Petrarch, he is regarded as one of the "three crowns" of Italian literature.

Life

Born in Certaldo, the illegitimate son of a Florentine merchant, Boccaccio was sent to Naples to learn banking and law, but abandoned both for literature. He witnessed the Black Death in Florence in 1348, an experience that frames his masterpiece. He later served Florence as a diplomat, became a close friend and admirer of Petrarch, and devoted his later years to scholarship, including lectures on Dante in 1373–74, before retiring to Certaldo.

The Decameron

Written in the early 1350s, The Decameron is framed as the stories told by seven young women and three young men sheltering from the plague in a villa outside Florence: ten narrators tell ten tales on each of ten days. The tales range from bawdy comedy to tragedy and celebrate wit, resourcefulness and love, offering a vivid panorama of medieval Italian society. The work influenced Chaucer, Shakespeare and countless later storytellers, and helped establish prose fiction in Europe.

Other works

Boccaccio also wrote the first notable psychological romance, the Elegia di Madonna Fiammetta; De mulieribus claris (On Famous Women), a collection of biographies of notable women; and the Genealogia deorum gentilium, a vast encyclopaedia of classical mythology that served humanists for centuries.
