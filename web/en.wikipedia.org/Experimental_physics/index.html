Experimental physics

Experimental physics is the category of disciplines and sub-disciplines in the field of physics concerned with the observation of physical phenomena and with experiments. Its methods vary greatly from problem to problem, from simple observations and experiments, such as Galileo's work on falling bodies and Cavendish's experiment measuring the density of the Earth, to vastly complex facilities such as the Large Hadron Collider. Experimental physics stands in complementary relation to theoretical physics, which develops the models that experiments test.

Overview

Experimental physics regroups all the disciplines of physics involving the acquisition of data, data-acquisition methods, and the detailed conceptualization (beyond simple thought experiments) and realizations of laboratory experiments. It is often concerned with empirical testing of theoretical predictions and the precise measurement of physical constants. Famous historical experiments include the Michelson–Morley experiment, the Millikan oil-drop experiment, the Davisson–Germer experiment demonstrating electron diffraction, and the Wu experiment that revealed parity violation.

Methods and practice

An experiment typically proceeds through design and construction of apparatus, controlled measurement, and statistical and systematic analysis of errors. Modern experiments frequently involve large international collaborations, sophisticated instrumentation — particle detectors, lasers, cryogenics, telescopes, and interferometers — and extensive computing. Verification requires replication, blind analysis, and open scrutiny of results, as illustrated by celebrated retractions and null results in the history of the field.

Current frontiers

Contemporary experimental physics includes particle physics at colliders and neutrino observatories; gravitational-wave astronomy following the LIGO detections beginning in 2015; precision tests of fundamental symmetries; condensed-matter experiments on superconductivity and topological materials; and atomic, molecular, and optical physics enabling quantum information science. Experiments such as those confirming the Higgs boson in 2012 exemplify the interplay of experiment and theory.
