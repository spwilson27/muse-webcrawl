In probability theory and statistics, Bayes' theorem (also Bayes' law or Bayes' rule), named after Thomas Bayes, describes the probability of an event based on prior knowledge of conditions that might be related to the event. It gives a way to update beliefs — the posterior probability — in light of new evidence.

Statement

P(A|B) = P(B|A) · P(A) / P(B)

Here P(A|B) is the posterior, the probability of event A given that B has occurred; P(B|A) is the likelihood; P(A) is the prior probability of A; and P(B) is the marginal probability of the evidence. The theorem follows directly from the definition of conditional probability.

Applications

Bayes' theorem underlies Bayesian probability and Bayesian inference throughout science and engineering: medical diagnosis (interpreting test results in light of disease prevalence, where it explains why even accurate tests yield many false positives for rare conditions), spam filtering, machine learning classifiers, forensic reasoning, and the Bayesian approach to statistical estimation and hypothesis testing. The classic prosecutor's fallacy — confusing P(evidence | innocence) with P(innocence | evidence) — is a failure to apply the theorem correctly.

History

Bayes's essay solving the "inverse probability" problem was published after his death in 1763, edited by his friend Richard Price. Pierre-Simon Laplace independently developed and popularised the rule, applying it to celestial mechanics and judicial probabilities. Use of Bayesian methods declined with the rise of frequentist statistics in the early 20th century, then revived from the mid-century onward, greatly accelerated by modern computing.
