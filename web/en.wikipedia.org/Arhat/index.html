Arhat

An arhat (Sanskrit: अर्हत्, "one who is worthy"; Pali: arahant) is, in Buddhism, a person who has attained enlightenment and liberation from the cycle of rebirth (saṃsāra), having eradicated the defilements (kilesa) and realized nirvana. The arhat is the goal of practice in Theravada Buddhism and the fourth and highest of the noble persons (ariya-puggala).

In Theravada Buddhism

The Pali Canon describes the arahant as one who has destroyed the ten fetters and the āsavas (taints), completed the Noble Eightfold Path, and will not be reborn. The Buddha himself is described as an arahant in the liturgy ("iti pi so bhagavā arahaṃ" — "thus indeed is the Blessed One an arahant"). Disciples such as Sāriputta and Moggallāna are revered as model arahants. A formal distinction is drawn between the enlightenment of a Buddha, who discovers the path independently, and that of an arahant disciple, who follows the path taught by a Buddha.

In Mahayana Buddhism

In Mahayana texts the arhat (Chinese: luohan; Japanese: rakan) is honoured but viewed as having attained a lesser realization than the bodhisattva, who postpones final nirvana to liberate all beings, or the fully awakened Buddha. Sets of sixteen, eighteen, or five hundred luohans are popular subjects in East Asian Buddhist art and devotion.

In Jainism

The term arihant is also central to Jainism, where it denotes a soul that has conquered inner passions such as anger, pride, deceit, and greed, attained omniscience (kevala jnana), and teaches the path to liberation before finally becoming a siddha.
