Humphry Repton

Humphry Repton (21 April 1752 – 24 March 1818) was an English landscape designer. Often regarded as the successor to Capability Brown, he was the last great English landscape designer of the eighteenth century, and is credited with coining the term "landscape gardening". His work marked a shift from Brown's sweeping naturalism toward more formal arrangements near the house, foreshadowing the gardenesque style of the Victorian era.

Life and career

Repton was born at Bury St Edmunds, Suffolk, the son of a collector of excise. After an unsuccessful career in trade in Norwich and the Netherlands, and the loss of much of his fortune, he turned to landscape design in 1788 at the age of 36, proclaiming himself Brown's successor. His first paid commission was Catton Park in Norfolk.

Over his career Repton worked on more than 400 commissions for the landed gentry of England, including Harewood House, Woburn Abbey, Blaise Castle, Longleat, and the Royal Pavilion at Brighton (unexecuted). For many clients he produced his famous "Red Books" — bound volumes of watercolour views with hinged overlays ("slides") showing the grounds before and after his proposed improvements.

In 1811 Repton was injured in a carriage accident and thereafter used a wheelchair. He died in 1818 and was buried at Aylsham, Norfolk.

Approach

Unlike Brown, Repton reintroduced terraces, gravel walks, flower gardens, and fences near the house, believing a house should sit within a designed foreground before opening to parkland. He emphasized "appropriateness" and utility, and wrote extensively, including Sketches and Hints on Landscape Gardening (1795), Observations on the Theory and Practice of Landscape Gardening (1803), and Fragments on the Theory and Practice of Landscape Gardening (1816). His son John Adey Repton became an architect, and he collaborated at times with John Nash.
