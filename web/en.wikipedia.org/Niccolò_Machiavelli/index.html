Niccolò di Bernardo dei Machiavelli (3 May 1469 – 21 June 1527) was an Italian diplomat, author, philosopher, and historian who lived during the Renaissance. He is best known for his political treatise The Prince (Il Principe), written in about 1513 and published posthumously in 1532, which brought him a reputation as an atheist and an amoral cynic, and gave rise to the pejorative adjective "Machiavellian".

Machiavelli served as a senior official and diplomat of the Florentine Republic from 1498 to 1512, undertaking missions to the courts of France, the Empire, and the Papacy, and observing Cesare Borgia's campaigns. He organised a citizen militia for Florence. When the Medici returned to power in 1512 he was dismissed, arrested, and tortured on suspicion of conspiracy, then retired to his estate at Sant'Andrea in Percussina, where he wrote his major works.

Works and thought

In The Prince, Machiavelli analysed how rulers acquire and hold power, arguing that effective truth (verità effettuale) matters more than imagined ideals, and that a prince must learn "how not to be good", using cruelty and deception when necessity (necessità) requires, while managing fortune (fortuna) through ability (virtù). His Discourses on the First Ten of Titus Livy present a more republican Machiavelli, praising the Roman republic, civic arms, and productive conflict between the people and the elite. He also wrote The Art of War, the comic play The Mandrake, and a History of Florence.

Influence

Machiavelli is regarded as a founder of modern political science and of political realism. His works were placed on the Catholic Church's Index of Forbidden Books in 1559, and later thinkers from Bacon and Hobbes to Rousseau, Gramsci, and modern republican theorists have debated whether he was a teacher of evil, a patriot, or a scientist of politics.
