Hedeby (Old Norse: Heiðabýr) was an important Danish Viking Age trading settlement near the southern end of the Jutland Peninsula, now in Schleswig-Holstein, Germany. It developed around 770 AD and flourished as a major centre of trade linking the Baltic and North Seas, thanks to its position at the head of the Schlei inlet close to the narrow base of Jutland, where goods could be portaged a short distance to the Eider river and the North Sea.

History

Hedeby appears in Frankish annals in 804, when the Danish king Godfred moved merchants from the Slavic town of Reric to his own port. Its semi-circular rampart, harbour works and dense workshops housed a population estimated at around 1,500–2,000 at its height — making it one of the largest settlements in Viking Age Scandinavia. Goods traded there included slaves, furs, amber, glass beads, pottery and quernstones from the Rhineland.

The town was attacked several times and was destroyed by fire in 1050 by the Norwegian king Harald Hardrada. In 1066 a Slavic raid delivered the final blow; the surviving inhabitants moved across the Schlei to the new town of Schleswig, and Hedeby was never rebuilt.

Archaeology

The site preserves exceptionally rich remains, including waterfront timbers, a ship burial and the wreck of a Viking warship found in the harbour. The Viking Museum Hedeby displays finds including runestones, jewellery and craftworking evidence. In 2018 the archaeological border landscape of Hedeby and the Danevirke was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
