Beringia

Beringia is defined today as the loose biogeographical region surrounding the Bering Strait, the Chukchi Sea, and the Bering Sea. During the Last Glacial Maximum, roughly 26,500 to 19,000 years ago, sea levels were as much as 120 metres lower than today, exposing a vast land bridge — the Bering land bridge — that connected Siberia to Alaska and reached up to 1,000 kilometres wide from north to south at its greatest extent.

The land bridge

The exposed continental shelf formed a dry, cold plain covered in steppe-tundra vegetation. Rather than a narrow corridor, Beringia was a broad subcontinent in its own right, supporting herds of grazing megafauna such as woolly mammoths, bison, and caribou, along with their predators. Many plant and animal species are shared between northeastern Asia and northwestern North America as a result of this connection.

Peopling of the Americas

Beringia is central to theories of the initial human settlement of the Americas. Genetic and archaeological evidence suggests that ancestral Native American populations became isolated in Beringia for thousands of years — the "Beringian standstill" hypothesis — before spreading southward as the ice sheets retreated, through an ice-free corridor and/or along the Pacific coast (the "kelp highway" hypothesis).

Reflooding

As global temperatures rose at the end of the Pleistocene, melting ice sheets raised sea levels and the land bridge was submerged beneath the Bering Strait by about 11,000 years ago. Only the Diomede Islands, St. Lawrence Island, and a few other high points remain above water today.
