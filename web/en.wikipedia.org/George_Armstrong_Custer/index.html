George Armstrong Custer (December 5, 1839 – June 25, 1876) was a United States Army officer and cavalry commander in the American Civil War and the American Indian Wars. Custer graduated from West Point in 1861 — last in his class — but rose to prominence as a highly aggressive cavalry commander, achieving the brevet rank of major general of volunteers at age 25.

Custer in 1865, photographed by Mathew Brady's studio.

Civil War

Known as the "Boy General", Custer distinguished himself at Gettysburg, where his Michigan Brigade helped repel J.E.B. Stuart's cavalry, and in Sheridan's Shenandoah and Appomattox campaigns. His troopers were credited with blocking Lee's retreat; Custer was present at the surrender at Appomattox Court House. After the war he reverted to his regular-army rank and became lieutenant colonel of the new 7th Cavalry.

Indian Wars and death

Custer campaigned against the Cheyenne and Lakota, including the controversial 1868 attack on Black Kettle's village at the Washita River. During the Great Sioux War, on 25 June 1876, he led five companies of the 7th Cavalry in an attack on a large Lakota, Northern Cheyenne and Arapaho encampment on the Little Bighorn River. His entire immediate command — some 210 men — was killed in the Battle of the Little Bighorn, remembered as "Custer's Last Stand".

Legacy

Custer's widow Elizabeth ("Libbie") Custer spent decades burnishing his heroic image through books and lectures. Modern assessments are divided: his Civil War daring is widely acknowledged, while his frontier record — Washita, treaty violations in the Black Hills expedition of 1874 and the recklessness at Little Bighorn — is heavily criticised, particularly by Native American writers. He remains one of the most recognisable figures of the American West.
