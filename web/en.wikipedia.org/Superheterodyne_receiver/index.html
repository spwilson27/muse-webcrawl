Superheterodyne receiver

A superheterodyne receiver, often shortened to superhet, is a type of radio receiver that uses frequency mixing to convert a received signal to a fixed intermediate frequency (IF) which can be processed more conveniently than the original carrier frequency. It was invented by the American engineer Edwin Howard Armstrong in 1918 during World War I, and virtually all modern radio receivers use the principle.

Principle of operation

The incoming radio signal from the antenna is mixed with a signal from a local oscillator in a nonlinear device called a mixer. The mixing produces sum and difference frequencies; the difference, the intermediate frequency, is selected by a fixed-tuned bandpass filter. Because the IF is fixed, the filter and amplifier stages can be optimized once, giving the receiver high selectivity and sensitivity across its tuning range. The IF signal is then demodulated to recover the original information.

Advantages and drawbacks

Compared with earlier designs such as the tuned radio frequency (TRF) receiver, the superheterodyne offers much better selectivity and stable gain. Its principal drawback is the image frequency problem: a second, unwanted signal offset from the desired one by twice the IF can also be converted to the IF, requiring preselection filtering at the front end. Double-conversion superheterodynes use two IF stages to ease both image rejection and selectivity requirements.

History

Armstrong patented the circuit in 1919 after developing it in France for detecting weak, short pulses from enemy equipment. The design was commercialized by RCA in the 1920s and by the 1930s had become the standard architecture for broadcast receivers, a position it still holds in most radio, television, and communications equipment.
