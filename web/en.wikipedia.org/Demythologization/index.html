Demythologization

Demythologization (German: Entmythologisierung) is a theological programme associated above all with the German New Testament scholar Rudolf Bultmann (1884–1976), who proposed that the mythological elements of the New Testament — its first-century picture of a three-storied universe, angels and demons, miraculous interventions, and an imminent end of the world — should not be discarded but interpreted in terms of human existence, so that the gospel's message (the kerygma) could be understood by modern people. Bultmann set out the programme in his 1941 essay "New Testament and Mythology", which provoked one of the major theological controversies of the 20th century.

Bultmann's argument

Bultmann held that modern people, who use electric light and medicine and understand the world through science, cannot honestly believe the mythic worldview presupposed by the New Testament, and that preaching should not demand such belief as a sacrifice of the intellect. Myth, in his definition, speaks of divine and otherworldly realities in worldly, objectifying terms. Demythologizing therefore does not eliminate the myths but interprets them existentially, drawing on Martin Heidegger's analysis of human existence: stories of the cross and resurrection, for example, express the possibility of a new, authentic self-understanding opened by faith. Critics — including Karl Barth, Dietrich Bonhoeffer in some respects, and many conservative theologians — objected that the programme reduced Christian faith to philosophy or dissolved essential doctrines, while others, such as Karl Jaspers, thought Bultmann had not gone far enough.

Wider use

Outside theology, the term is used more loosely for efforts to strip myth, legend, or ideology from a subject in order to reveal an underlying historical or rational core — for instance in discussions of national founding myths or of the history of science.
