Modern art

Modern art comprises the artistic work produced roughly from the 1860s to the 1970s, and denotes the styles and philosophies of that era. The term is usually associated with art in which the traditions of the past were set aside in a spirit of experimentation. Modern artists experimented with new ways of seeing and with fresh ideas about the nature of materials and the functions of art, tending away from narrative — characteristic of the traditional arts — toward abstraction.

Origins

Art historians usually date modern art's beginnings to the later nineteenth century: Édouard Manet's provocative paintings of the 1860s, the Impressionists' break with the official Salon in the 1870s, and the successive innovations of Post-Impressionists such as Van Gogh, Cézanne, Gauguin and Seurat. Rejecting academic illusionism, these artists treated painting as an autonomous arrangement of colour and form.

Major movements

The early twentieth century produced a rapid succession of movements: Fauvism's unleashed colour (1905); Cubism, developed by Picasso and Braque from 1907, which fractured objects into geometric facets; the abstraction of Kandinsky and Malevich; Futurism, Expressionism and De Stijl; Dada's anti-art provocations during the First World War; and Surrealism's exploration of the unconscious in the 1920s. The Bauhaus linked art with design and architecture, while sculptors such as Brâncuși reduced form to essentials.

After the Second World War the centre of the avant-garde shifted to New York, where Abstract Expressionism — Pollock's dripped canvases, Rothko's colour fields — dominated. Reactions followed: Pop art embraced mass-media imagery, Minimalism reduced art to simple industrial forms, and Conceptual art subordinated the object to the idea. By the 1970s the pluralism that followed is often labelled postmodernism.

Legacy

Modern art permanently expanded the definition of art: photography, found objects, performance and installation all entered the museum. Institutions such as the Museum of Modern Art in New York (founded 1929) codified its canon, which has since been criticised and broadened to include artists beyond Europe and North America. Its experiments continue to shape contemporary practice.
