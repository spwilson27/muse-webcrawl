Borrowed chord

A borrowed chord (also called chord borrowing, modal exchange, or modal mixture) is a chord borrowed from the parallel key (parallel major or minor scale) and used in a piece whose primary key differs. For example, in a piece in C major, using a chord from C minor — such as iv (F minor), ii° (D diminished), or ♭VI (A♭ major) — is a borrowed chord. The technique has been used since the Renaissance and became especially common in the Romantic era and in popular music.

Common examples

The most frequently borrowed chord in major keys is the minor subdominant, iv, often resolving to I with a descending chromatic line. The Picardy third is a special case in which a piece in a minor key ends on a major tonic chord borrowed from the parallel major. In rock and pop music, chords such as ♭III, ♭VI, and ♭VII borrowed from the parallel minor are characteristic of genres from classic rock to film scoring.

Analysis

Borrowed chords are typically analyzed with the Roman numeral of the source key, adjusted with accidentals (e.g., ♭VI in major). They allow composers to expand harmonic color while keeping a strong sense of the home tonic, since the tonic itself usually remains unchanged.
