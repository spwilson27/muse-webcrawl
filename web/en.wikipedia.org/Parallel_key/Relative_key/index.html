Relative key

In music theory, a relative key is the major or minor key that shares the same key signature as a given key. Every major key has a relative minor whose tonic is a minor third below the major tonic, and every natural minor key has a relative major a minor third above. Thus C major and A minor are relatives (neither has sharps or flats), as are G major and E minor (one sharp), and D minor and F major (one flat).

Distinction from parallel keys

Relative keys should not be confused with parallel keys. Parallel keys share the same tonic but have different key signatures — for example, C major and C minor. Modulation between relative keys is among the smoothest in tonal music because no pitch content changes at all in natural forms; only the tonal center shifts. Composers frequently exploit this relationship, and pieces in minor keys commonly move to the relative major for secondary themes in sonata form.

Finding the relative minor

To find the relative minor of a major key, count down three semitones (a minor third) from the major tonic, or locate the sixth scale degree of the major scale. The circle of fifths displays major keys on its outer ring and their relative minors on the inner ring, aligned radially.
