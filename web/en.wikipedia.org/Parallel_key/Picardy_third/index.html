Picardy third

A Picardy third (also spelled Picardy Tierce, from the French tierce de Picardie) is a major chord used as the final tonic chord of a musical section that is in a minor key. It is achieved by raising the third of the expected minor tonic chord to form a major triad. For example, a piece in D minor concluding on a D major chord (D–F♯–A) instead of D minor ends with a Picardy third.

History

The practice was near-universal in Renaissance and Baroque music. Because the major third produced purer acoustic consonance in the final sonority and because minor-mode pieces were conventionally understood to require a "perfect" ending, composers from the fifteenth through the eighteenth centuries routinely ended minor-key works with major chords. J.S. Bach used Picardy thirds extensively in chorales and keyboard works, and the device lends its name to the region of Picardy in northern France, though the reason for the association is uncertain — it may be a corruption of an older term.

Usage

By the Romantic era, ending a minor-key piece with a minor chord had become normal, and the Picardy third became a deliberate expressive choice, suggesting consolation, hope, or transfiguration. In modern analysis the chord is an example of a borrowed chord from the parallel major key, and it remains common in hymns, folk music, and popular songs.
