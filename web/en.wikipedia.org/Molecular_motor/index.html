Molecular motor

Kinesin "walking" along a microtubule, hauling cargo

Molecular motors are natural or artificial molecular machines that consume energy to produce directed mechanical work at the nanometre scale. In biology they are protein complexes that convert the chemical energy of ATP hydrolysis (or an ion gradient) into motion, driving processes such as intracellular transport, muscle contraction, cell division, and the rotation of bacterial flagella.

Biological motors

The best-known families are the cytoskeletal motors. Myosins move along actin filaments; myosin II powers muscle contraction and cytokinesis. Kinesins mostly walk toward the plus end of microtubules, carrying vesicles and organelles, while dyneins move toward the minus end and power the beating of cilia and flagella. Kinesin's two "heads" step hand-over-hand in 8-nanometre strides, one ATP per step. Other biological motors include ATP synthase, whose rotating central shaft synthesises ATP as protons flow through it; the bacterial flagellar motor, a true rotary engine spinning a helical propeller; and nucleic-acid motors such as helicases and polymerases that track along DNA and RNA.

Artificial molecular motors

Chemists have built synthetic analogues, recognised by the 2016 Nobel Prize in Chemistry to Jean-Pierre Sauvage, Fraser Stoddart, and Ben Feringa. Feringa's light-driven rotary motor (1999) uses overcrowded alkenes that rotate unidirectionally under illumination; related systems have powered molecular shuttles, switches, and a four-wheel-drive "nanocar".
