The Skaergaard intrusion is a layered gabbro complex in East Greenland, near the head of Kangerlussuaq fjord. Formed about 55 million years ago during the opening of the North Atlantic Ocean, it is the world's most famous example of a layered intrusion and the type locality for understanding how magma chambers crystallise.

Geology

The intrusion was emplaced as a single pulse of tholeiitic basaltic magma into gneiss and overlying basalts, crystallising slowly beneath a roof of its own volcanic cover. Systematic study by L. R. Wager and W. A. Deer, published in 1939, established the classic model of fractional crystallisation: crystals formed in the magma sank or grew on the chamber floor, producing a layered sequence of cumulate rocks — from olivine-rich gabbros at the base upward through increasingly iron-rich ferrogabbros, culminating in the "Sandwich Horizon" where the last, most evolved liquid crystallised.

The intrusion is divided into a Layered Series (Lower, Middle and Upper Zones), a Marginal Border Series against the walls, and an Upper Border Series beneath the roof. Its well-preserved igneous layering — including modal layering, trough bands and cross-bedding-like structures — made it the benchmark against which other layered intrusions, such as the Bushveld Complex, are compared. Modern work has refined Wager's model, debating the roles of convection, in-situ crystallisation at the margins, and compaction of the crystal pile.

Significance

Wager and Deer's work at Skaergaard fundamentally shaped igneous petrology, introducing concepts of cumulates and cryptic layering (systematic compositional change in minerals through the sequence). The intrusion also hosts minor platinum-group element and gold mineralisation in the Platinova reef.
