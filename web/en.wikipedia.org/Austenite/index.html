Austenite

Austenite, also known as gamma-phase iron (γ-Fe), is a metallic, non-magnetic allotrope of iron or a solid solution of iron with an alloying element. In plain-carbon steel, austenite exists above the critical eutectoid temperature of 727 °C; other alloys of steel have different eutectoid temperatures. The austenite allotrope is named after Sir William Chandler Roberts-Austen, an English metallurgist known for his research on the physical properties of metals and their alloys.

Austenite is the face-centred cubic phase at the top of the iron–carbon diagram.

Structure and properties

Austenite has a face-centred cubic (FCC) crystal structure, which can dissolve considerably more carbon — up to about 2% by weight at 1,147 °C — than the body-centred cubic ferrite phase. It is ductile, non-magnetic and stable only at high temperatures in pure iron, but alloying elements such as nickel and manganese can stabilise it to room temperature, as in the common austenitic stainless steels (such as grade 304).

Transformations

On cooling, austenite decomposes at the eutectoid temperature into pearlite, a mixture of ferrite and cementite. Faster cooling produces bainite or, by quenching, the hard diffusionless phase martensite — the basis of hardening steel. Some austenite may be retained untransformed in quenched steels. The transformations of austenite govern most heat treatments of steel, including annealing, normalising and quenching and tempering.
