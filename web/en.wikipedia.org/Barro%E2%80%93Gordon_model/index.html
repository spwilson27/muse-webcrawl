Barro–Gordon model

The Barro–Gordon model, developed by economists Robert Barro and Robert J. Gordon in 1983, is a macroeconomic model of monetary policy based on the theory of time inconsistency. It shows how a policymaker with discretion — the ability to change policy after expectations are formed — has an incentive to create surprise inflation in order to reduce unemployment below its natural rate, even though rational agents anticipate this incentive.

Setup and result

In the model, the policymaker minimizes a loss function that penalizes both inflation and deviations of unemployment from a target below the natural rate. Because the public forms rational expectations, the policymaker cannot systematically fool them: in equilibrium, inflation is positive — an "inflation bias" — while unemployment remains at its natural rate, a worse outcome than a credible commitment to low inflation.

Implications

The model provided a central theoretical argument for rules-based monetary policy, central bank independence, inflation targeting, and the appointment of "conservative" central bankers — institutional arrangements that let society commit to low inflation. It builds on the earlier time-inconsistency analysis of Kydland and Prescott (1977).
