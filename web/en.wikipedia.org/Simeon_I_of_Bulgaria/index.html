Simeon I the Great

Born: c. 864/865

Simeon I the Great (Bulgarian: Симеон I Велики, c. 864/865 – 27 May 927) ruled the First Bulgarian Empire from 893 to 927. His successful wars against the Byzantine Empire, the Magyars and the Serbs led Bulgaria to its greatest territorial extent, and his reign is celebrated as the Golden Age of medieval Bulgarian culture.

Early life and accession

Simeon was the third son of Knyaz Boris I, the Christianiser of Bulgaria. Educated in Constantinople — at the University of Magnaura — where he mastered Greek rhetoric and theology, he was intended for a church career. When his elder brother Vladimir attempted to restore paganism, Boris deposed him in 893 and installed Simeon as ruler; the capital was moved from Pliska to Preslav, and Slavonic was established as the language of church and state.

Wars with Byzantium

Commercial disputes sparked war with Byzantium in 894. After victories and setbacks — including devastating Magyar incursions instigated by Constantinople — Simeon crushed the Magyars in alliance with the Pechenegs and annihilated a Byzantine army at Bulgarophygon (896). In 913 he marched on Constantinople and was ceremonially received; the details of his coronation there remain disputed. Following the regency's repudiation of the settlement, Simeon won one of the greatest victories of the Middle Ages at Achelous (917) and spent his last decade campaigning in Greece and Serbia, adopting the title "Emperor (Tsar) of the Bulgarians and the Romans". He died in 927 while preparing another assault on Constantinople.

Golden Age

Simeon's court at Preslav patronised translations of Byzantine theology, law and chronicles into Old Church Slavonic, and works such as John the Exarch's Shestodnev and Constantine of Preslav's writings; the Preslav and Ohrid literary schools flourished. Bulgaria under Simeon stretched from the Adriatic to the Black Sea, making him the most powerful ruler in south-eastern Europe.
