Geographic tongue

Geographic tongue, also known as benign migratory glossitis, is a harmless inflammatory condition of the mucous membrane of the tongue, usually of the dorsal surface. It is characterized by patches of depapillation — the loss of the small finger-like projections (papillae) on the tongue's surface — forming smooth, red areas with slightly raised, whitish borders that resemble the outlines of a map. The patches change location, pattern, and size over time, hence the name "migratory".

Signs and symptoms

In most cases geographic tongue causes no symptoms at all and is discovered incidentally during an oral examination. Some people report increased sensitivity or a burning sensation when eating spicy, salty, or acidic foods, or hot beverages. The condition occurs in up to roughly 2–3% of the general population and may appear at any age, often beginning in childhood; it tends to wax and wane over the course of life.

Causes

The cause is not well understood. The condition appears to run in families, suggesting a genetic component, and it has been associated with psoriasis and with fissured tongue, which frequently coexists. Associations with allergies, hormonal factors, and nutritional deficiencies have been reported but not consistently confirmed; it is not infectious and is not caused by cancer.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is usually made on the characteristic clinical appearance alone, and biopsy is rarely required. Because the condition is benign and usually asymptomatic, treatment is generally unnecessary; reassurance is the mainstay. For people with discomfort, topical corticosteroids, antihistamine rinses, or avoidance of irritating foods may help.
