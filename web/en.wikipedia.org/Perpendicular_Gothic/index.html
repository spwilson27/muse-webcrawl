Perpendicular Gothic

Perpendicular Gothic (also called the Rectilinear style, or simply Perpendicular) is the third and final phase of English Gothic architecture, flourishing from roughly the 1370s to the 1540s. It is unique to England — no exact equivalent developed in continental Europe — and is characterised by an emphasis on vertical lines, large windows and intricate stone tracery.

Characteristics

The style takes its name from the strong vertical mullions of its windows, which rise uninterrupted through the whole height of the window, and from the grid-like panels of tracery that cover wall surfaces. Arches become flatter (the four-centred "Tudor" arch), towers are taller, and roofs are celebrated in the spectacular fan vaults of Gloucester Cathedral's cloisters and King's College Chapel, Cambridge. Interiors are flooded with light through vast expanses of stained glass set in relatively slender walls.

Examples

Early examples include the choir of Gloucester Cathedral (begun about 1337). Famous later buildings in the style are King's College Chapel, Cambridge; St George's Chapel, Windsor; Henry VII's Chapel at Westminster Abbey; the Divinity School and Magdalen Tower at Oxford; and the great parish churches of the wool towns, such as St Mary Redcliffe, Bristol. The style was phased out as Renaissance classicism arrived in the mid-sixteenth century, but was widely imitated in the Gothic Revival.
