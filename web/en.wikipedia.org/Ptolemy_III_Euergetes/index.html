Ptolemy III Euergetes (Greek: Πτολεμαῖος Εὐεργέτης, "Ptolemy the Benefactor"; c. 284 – late 222 BC) was the third king of the Ptolemaic dynasty of Egypt, reigning from 246 to 222 BC. Under him the Ptolemaic kingdom reached the height of its military power and territorial extent.

Reign

Ptolemy succeeded his father Ptolemy II Philadelphus in 246 BC and married Berenice II of Cyrene, reuniting Cyrenaica with Egypt. Soon after his accession he invaded the Seleucid Empire in the Third Syrian War (246–241 BC) to avenge the murder of his sister Berenice Syra and her infant son. His armies advanced deep into Mesopotamia, reportedly as far as Babylon, before a revolt in Egypt forced his return; the settlement nevertheless left Egypt in control of much of the Syrian and Anatolian coast and brought enormous war booty home. Ptolemy also campaigned in the south, and inscriptions credit him with restoring Egyptian cult statues captured by the Persians centuries earlier — one source of his epithet "Euergetes" (Benefactor).

Domestic rule and legacy

At home Ptolemy III patronized the Library of Alexandria and the cult of Sarapis, and in 238 BC the priestly synod at Canopus issued the Decree of Canopus, which attempted a calendar reform by adding a leap day every four years — a scheme not adopted until the Augustan era. He sponsored temple building at Edfu and elsewhere and maintained the fleet that made Egypt the dominant naval power of the eastern Mediterranean. He died in late 222 BC and was succeeded by his son Ptolemy IV Philopator, under whom the kingdom's long decline began.
