Walden Pond is a pond in Concord, Massachusetts, famous as the setting of Henry David Thoreau's book Walden (1854). It is a kettle hole formed by a retreating glacier about 10,000–12,000 years ago, and at 102 feet (31 m) deep it is the deepest natural body of fresh water in Massachusetts.

Thoreau's stay

From July 1845 to September 1847, Thoreau lived in a one-room cabin he built on land owned by his friend Ralph Waldo Emerson, on the pond's north-west shore. His experiment in deliberate, simple living produced Walden; or, Life in the Woods, a classic of American literature and a foundational text for environmentalism and voluntary simplicity. The cabin site, marked out in stones, was located in 1945 by archaeologist Roland Robbins.

Later history

In the late 19th and early 20th centuries the pond was a popular recreation spot with an amusement grove; swimming remains popular today. The Emerson and Thoreau families helped preserve the woods, and the property was deeded to the Commonwealth in 1922. Walden Pond State Reservation (about 335 acres) is managed by the Massachusetts Department of Conservation and Recreation, draws hundreds of thousands of visitors annually, and was designated a National Historic Landmark.

Ecology

Because it has no surface inlet or outlet and is fed by groundwater, Walden has been intensively studied by limnologists; records of its clarity and biology extend back to Thoreau's own soundings and observations, making it a reference site for the ecological effects of climate and development.
