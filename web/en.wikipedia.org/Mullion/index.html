Mullion


Type: Architectural element
Used in: Windows, curtain walls, screens

A mullion is a vertical element that forms a division between units of a window or screen, or is used decoratively. When dividing adjacent window units, its primary purpose is structural — supporting the glazing — but it also serves an aesthetic role, especially in Gothic and traditional architecture.

History

Stone mullions were a feature of Romanesque and Gothic architecture, where they divided tall window openings into lights filled with tracery and stained glass. In domestic architecture, mullioned windows of stone, wood, or later metal became characteristic of Tudor and vernacular buildings in Britain. With modern curtain-wall construction, mullions are aluminium or steel sections that frame glass panels on high-rise facades.

Distinction from muntins

In North American usage, a mullion is the larger structural member separating window units or door-and-window combinations, whereas a muntin (glazing bar) is the smaller strip holding individual panes within a sash. In British usage the terms overlap more loosely, though "glazing bar" is the usual term for the smaller element.

Materials and modern use

Mullions may be stone, timber, steel, aluminium, or uPVC. In contemporary facades, mullion spacing and depth strongly affect a building's appearance and its daylighting and thermal performance.
