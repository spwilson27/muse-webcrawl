Germanic weak verb

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In the Germanic languages, a weak verb (German: schwaches Verb) is a verb that marks the past tense by adding a dental suffix — in English, -ed or -d — rather than by changing the stem vowel (ablaut), as strong verbs do. For example, English walk/walked and love/loved are weak, while sing/sang is strong. The weak conjugation is one of the defining innovations of the Germanic branch of Indo-European and remains the default, productive pattern: nearly all newly coined verbs in modern Germanic languages are weak.

Origin

The dental preterite is unique to Germanic and has no direct analogue elsewhere in Indo-European. Its origin is debated. A leading theory derives the suffix from the verb "to do" (Proto-Germanic *dōną), so that the past tense was originally a periphrastic formation, comparable to English "did walk." Another theory connects it with the Proto-Indo-European past-tense formations in *-t-, the same suffix seen in Latin verbal adjectives. Whatever its source, the weak preterite was fully established by the time of the earliest written records, such as Gothic.

Classes in Proto-Germanic

Proto-Germanic is reconstructed with four classes of weak verbs, distinguished by the suffix vowel that appeared between the stem and the personal endings:

Class I contained many causative verbs derived from strong verbs (compare English fall/fell, lie/lay, rise/raise), and its verbs were subject to i-umlaut in languages such as Old English and Old High German.

In the modern languages

In English, weak verbs are traditionally divided into regular verbs, which form the past with the spelling -ed realised as /t/, /d/, or /ɪd/, and a small group of irregular weak verbs (such as keep/kept, teach/taught, bring/brought) that show vowel changes but still carry the dental suffix. In German, regular weak verbs take the prefix ge- and the suffix -t in the past participle (sagen — sagte — gesagt, "to say"). Over centuries, many originally strong verbs have drifted into the weak conjugation, a process that continues today.
