The Food Corporation of India (FCI) is a statutory corporation owned by the Government of India under the Ministry of Consumer Affairs, Food and Public Distribution. Set up under the Food Corporations Act 1964, it began operations on 14 January 1965 with its first district office at Thanjavur, Tamil Nadu. The FCI is the main agency responsible for the procurement, storage, movement, and distribution of foodgrains in India, and it plays a central role in the country's food security system.

Objectives

The FCI was created with three principal objectives: to provide farmers remunerative prices through effective price support operations; to distribute foodgrains throughout the country through the public distribution system; and to maintain a satisfactory level of operational and buffer stocks of foodgrains to ensure national food security.

Operations

The corporation procures wheat and rice (paddy) from farmers at the minimum support price (MSP) announced by the government, largely through state government agencies acting on its behalf. The procured grain is stored in a nationwide network of depots, silos, and covered-and-plinth storage, and is moved across the country — chiefly by rail — from surplus states such as Punjab, Haryana, and Madhya Pradesh to deficit states.

Grain held in the central pool is issued to state governments for distribution through the Targeted Public Distribution System (TPDS) and welfare schemes, including the midday meal scheme and the provisions of the National Food Security Act 2013, under which subsidized grain is supplied to roughly two-thirds of India's population. Excess stocks may be sold in the open market under the Open Market Sale Scheme.

Organization

The FCI operates through a headquarters in New Delhi, zonal offices (North, South, East, West, and North-East), regional offices in the states, and several hundred district offices and depots. It is one of the largest supply-chain operations in Asia, handling tens of millions of tonnes of grain each year.

Criticism and reform

The FCI has frequently been criticized for high operational costs, storage losses, and carrying buffer stocks well above prescribed norms, which add to the government's food subsidy bill. Committees, including the High Level Committee on Reorienting the Role and Restructuring of FCI (Shanta Kumar Committee, 2015), have recommended reforms such as greater outsourcing of procurement and storage, wider use of negotiable warehouse receipts, and a gradual shift toward cash transfers in some areas.
