Consumer theory is the study of how people allocate their money income to the purchase of goods and services. Given their income and the prices of goods, consumers aim to maximize their utility — their satisfaction — subject to their budget constraint. Consumer theory is the foundation of demand analysis in microeconomics: the demand curves used throughout economics are derived from its model of choice.

The basic model

In the standard model, a consumer ranks bundles of goods by preference, summarized by a utility function, and chooses the most preferred bundle that is affordable. Graphically, the optimum occurs where an indifference curve — representing bundles of equal utility — is tangent to the budget line. The resulting quantities demanded depend on prices and income, yielding Marshallian demand functions.

Price changes

A change in a good's price has two components: the substitution effect, as consumers switch toward the relatively cheaper good, and the income effect, as the price change alters real purchasing power. The Slutsky equation decomposes the total effect into these parts. For normal goods the two effects reinforce each other; for inferior goods they oppose, and for the rare Giffen good the income effect dominates so that demand rises with price.

Revealed preference and extensions

Paul Samuelson's revealed-preference approach reconstructs consumer theory from observable choices without assuming a utility function directly. Later work has extended the theory to choices over time (intertemporal choice), under uncertainty (expected utility), involving household production and characteristics of goods (Lancaster's model), and to systematic deviations from rationality studied by behavioural economics.
