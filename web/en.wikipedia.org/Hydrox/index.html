Hydrox

Deep diving with hydrogen-containing breathing gases demands specialised procedures.

Hydrox, a gas mixture of hydrogen and oxygen, is occasionally used as a breathing gas in very deep diving. It allows divers to descend to depths of several hundred metres, far beyond the limits of air or nitrox, by exploiting the low density and high diffusivity of hydrogen and by avoiding nitrogen narcosis.

History

The use of hydrogen in diving dates to the 1940s, when the Swedish engineer Arne Zetterström experimented with hydrogen–oxygen mixtures for the Swedish Navy; he died in 1945 during an ascent procedure failure. Interest revived in the 1960s and 1970s in France, where the diving company Comex, under Henri Germain Delauze, conducted extensive research. In 1988–1992 Comex's Hydra programme culminated in diver Théo Mavrostomos reaching a simulated depth of 701 metres in a hyperbaric chamber using a hydrogen–helium–oxygen mixture (hydreliox), while hydrox itself was used in open-water dives to around 240 metres.

Advantages and disadvantages

Hydrogen has the lowest density of any gas, so breathing resistance at depth is minimal and the work of breathing stays manageable. Hydrogen also has a mild narcotic effect that can counteract high-pressure nervous syndrome (HPNS), the tremors and neurological disturbances seen in very deep helium diving. However, hydrogen forms an explosive mixture with oxygen across a wide range of concentrations, so the oxygen fraction must be kept very low (typically around 4 percent or less at depth)—safe at high pressure where the partial pressure of oxygen suffices, but hypoxic near the surface, requiring gas switching during descent and ascent. Hydrogen can also cause its own form of narcosis at extreme pressures.

Related mixtures

Hydrox is related to hydreliox (hydrogen, helium, and oxygen) and to the more common deep-diving gases heliox and trimix. Because of its handling hazards and the success of hydreliox for record depths, hydrox remains a niche mixture used mainly in experimental and commercial saturation contexts.
