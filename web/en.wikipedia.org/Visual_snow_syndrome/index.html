Visual snow syndrome

Visual snow syndrome
SpecialtyNeurology, ophthalmology
SymptomsStatic-like dots across the visual field, palinopsia, photophobia, nyctalopia, enhanced entoptic phenomena
Usual onsetOften young adulthood
CausesUnknown; hyperexcitability of the visual cortex proposed

Visual snow syndrome (VSS) is an uncommon neurological condition in which the affected individuals see persistent flickering white, black, or transparent dots across the whole visual field, resembling the static of an untuned television. The symptom is continuous, usually present in both eyes, and persists with the eyes closed. The condition was defined as a syndrome distinct from persistent migraine aura in studies published in the 2010s.

Signs and symptoms

Diagnostic criteria proposed by researchers require visual snow for at least three months plus at least two additional visual symptoms: palinopsia (afterimages or trailing), enhanced entoptic phenomena (floaters, blue-field entoptic phenomenon, spontaneous photopsia), photophobia, and impaired night vision (nyctalopia). Migraine and tinnitus are common comorbidities.

Causes and mechanisms

The cause is unknown. Proposed mechanisms include hyperexcitability of neurons in the lingual gyrus and visual cortex, and dysfunction of thalamocortical rhythms. Imaging studies have reported metabolic changes in visual association areas. The syndrome must be distinguished from hallucinogen persisting perception disorder (HPPD), which it can resemble.

Treatment

There is no established cure. Management typically includes ruling out secondary causes with ophthalmological and neurological examination, reassurance, migraine management, and, in some reported cases, medications such as lamotrigine or acetazolamide with limited evidence. Patient advocacy and awareness grew through organizations such as Eye on Vision Foundation and the Visual Snow Initiative in the late 2010s.
