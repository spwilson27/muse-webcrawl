David Galula

David Galula (10 January 1919 – 11 May 1967) was a French military officer and scholar who became one of the most influential theorists of counterinsurgency warfare. His 1964 book Counterinsurgency Warfare: Theory and Practice, written after service in China, Greece, Indochina and Algeria, was largely ignored for decades before being rediscovered by Western militaries during the wars in Iraq and Afghanistan.

Early life and career

Galula was born in Sfax, Tunisia, then part of the French protectorate of Tunisia. He graduated from the military academy of Saint-Cyr in 1939 and served in North Africa during the Second World War. After the war he was posted to China, where he observed the Chinese Civil War and the victory of Mao Zedong's Communists, an experience that shaped his conviction that insurgencies are won or lost politically among the population rather than on the battlefield.

Algeria

From 1956 to 1958 Galula commanded a company in the Djebel Aïssa Mimoun area of Kabylia during the Algerian War. Applying his emerging theory, he combined military operations with political organisation, census-taking, schooling and the progressive isolation of insurgents from the population, and his sector became notably quieter than surrounding areas. He later served on the staff of the French national defence headquarters in Paris.

Later life and legacy

Galula left the army in 1962 and spent time at Harvard University's Center for International Affairs, where he wrote Counterinsurgency Warfare. The book distils his experience into laws and principles emphasising that the population is the prize, that political power is the objective, and that counterinsurgency is overwhelmingly political rather than military in character. He died of lung cancer in 1967. The book was republished in 2006 and strongly influenced the United States Army's counterinsurgency field manual FM 3-24.
