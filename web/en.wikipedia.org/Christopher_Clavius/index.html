Christopher Clavius

Born25 March 1538, Bamberg
Died6 February 1612, Rome
FieldsMathematics, astronomy
Known forGregorian calendar reform

Christopher Clavius (25 March 1538 – 6 February 1612) was a German Jesuit mathematician and astronomer, best remembered as the principal architect of the Gregorian calendar. He spent most of his career teaching at the Collegio Romano in Rome and was sometimes called "the Euclid of the sixteenth century".

Calendar reform

By the 1570s the Julian calendar had drifted about ten days from the solar year, shifting the date of Easter. Clavius served on the commission appointed by Pope Gregory XIII and wrote the definitive exposition and defence of the reformed calendar introduced by the papal bull Inter gravissimas in 1582. His system dropped ten days in October 1582 and refined the leap-year rules so that century years are leap years only when divisible by 400.

Scientific work

Clavius wrote widely used textbooks on arithmetic, geometry, astronomy and the sphere, and his edition of Euclid's Elements influenced teaching for decades. Though he defended the geocentric system and opposed Galileo's telescopic findings in principle, he acknowledged the observations made by Jesuit astronomers with the telescope, and he and Galileo maintained a respectful correspondence.

Legacy

The large lunar crater Clavius is named after him, as is the asteroid 20237 Clavius. His reform of mathematical education in the Jesuit curriculum, set out in the Ratio Studiorum, shaped science teaching across Catholic Europe.
