Lens (anatomy)

The lens, or crystalline lens, is a transparent biconvex structure in the eye that, together with the cornea, refracts light so that it is focused on the retina. By changing its shape, the lens adjusts the eye's focal length so that images at different distances can be brought into focus; this process is called accommodation.

Structure

The lens is enclosed in a clear elastic capsule and suspended behind the iris by the zonular fibres (zonule of Zinn), which connect it to the ciliary body. It consists of an outer cortex and a denser central nucleus, built up of elongated lens fibre cells packed with transparent proteins called crystallins. The human lens is roughly 10 mm in diameter and about 4 mm thick, and it grows throughout life as new fibres are added at its equator. It contains no blood vessels or nerves, receiving nutrients from the aqueous humour.

Function and accommodation

The lens provides roughly one third of the eye's total refractive power (about 15–20 dioptres in the relaxed eye), the cornea providing the remainder. When the ciliary muscle contracts, tension on the zonular fibres is released, the elastic lens rounds up, its power increases, and near objects come into focus. Relaxation of the muscle flattens the lens for distance vision.

Clinical significance

With age the lens stiffens, reducing accommodation and causing presbyopia. Opacification of the lens is a cataract, the commonest cause of blindness worldwide; it is treated by removing the lens and usually replacing it with an artificial intraocular lens. Absence of the lens is called aphakia, and displacement of the lens ectopia lentis.
