Model organism

Model organism
ExamplesE. coli, baker's yeast, fruit fly, C. elegans, zebrafish, mouse
Chosen forShort life cycle, easy breeding, small genome, genetic tractability

A model organism is a non-human species that is extensively studied to understand biological phenomena, on the grounds that discoveries in the model will illuminate the workings of other organisms — especially humans. Model organisms are chosen for practical advantages such as small size, rapid reproduction, ease of laboratory maintenance, and genomes that can be readily manipulated.

Major models

The bacterium Escherichia coli founded molecular biology. The yeasts Saccharomyces cerevisiae (baker's yeast) and Schizosaccharomyces pombe model the eukaryotic cell cycle. The fruit fly Drosophila melanogaster, used by T. H. Morgan's school from 1910, remains central to genetics and development; the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans, with its exactly mapped cell lineage, underpins developmental biology and neuroscience. The plant Arabidopsis thaliana is the standard flowering-plant model. Among vertebrates, the zebrafish offers transparent embryos for developmental studies, and the house mouse (Mus musculus) — whose genome is about 99% homologous in gene terms with ours — dominates biomedical research, including knockout models of human disease.

Value and limits

Because core cellular machinery is evolutionarily conserved, findings transfer surprisingly well: genes for cell division in yeast have direct counterparts in human cancers. Models also allow experiments impossible in humans. Yet differences matter — drugs that succeed in mice often fail in people — so researchers increasingly combine models, use organoids derived from human cells, and debate the ethics of animal experimentation, particularly for mammals.
