Chapter 13, Title 11, United States Code

Chapter 13 is the wage earner's plan under the U.S. Bankruptcy Code.
EnactedBankruptcy Reform Act of 1978
Codified11 U.S.C. §§ 1301–1330
Administered byU.S. Bankruptcy Courts; standing trustees
PredecessorChapter XIII of the Bankruptcy Act of 1898

Chapter 13 of Title 11 of the United States Code governs the bankruptcy of individuals with regular income in the United States. Often called the wage earner's plan, it enables an individual debtor to develop a court-confirmed plan to repay all or part of their debts over a period of three to five years, under the supervision of a standing trustee, while generally keeping their property.

History

Chapter 13 descends from Chapter XIII of the Bankruptcy Act of 1898, introduced by the Chandler Act of 1938. The modern chapter was enacted as part of the Bankruptcy Reform Act of 1978, which created the modern Bankruptcy Code. The Bankruptcy Abuse Prevention and Consumer Protection Act of 2005 (BAPCPA) substantially revised the chapter, adding a means test that determines whether a debtor's plan must run three years or the full five-year "applicable commitment period."

Eligibility

Only individuals (not corporations or partnerships) with regular income may file under Chapter 13. The debtor's debts must fall below statutory ceilings for secured and unsecured debt, which are adjusted periodically for inflation; for cases filed after the temporary increase lapsed, the limits are approximately $465,275 in unsecured debt and $1,395,875 in secured debt (figures adjusted triennially). A debtor who had a prior bankruptcy case dismissed within the preceding 180 days for willful failure to abide by court orders may be barred from refiling.

The repayment plan

The debtor proposes a plan under which future earnings are submitted to the control of a standing trustee, who distributes payments to creditors. The plan must:

  • Pay priority claims (such as recent taxes and domestic support obligations) in full;
  • Pay unsecured creditors at least as much as they would receive in a Chapter 7 liquidation (the "best interests of creditors" test);
  • Be proposed in good faith and be feasible; and
  • If the trustee or an unsecured creditor objects, either pay unsecured claims in full or devote all of the debtor's projected disposable income over the applicable commitment period to the plan.

Debtors above the state median income generally must use the five-year period and compute disposable income using the means-test formula borrowed from Chapter 7.

Automatic stay and co-debtor stay

Filing a Chapter 13 petition triggers the automatic stay of 11 U.S.C. § 362, halting most collection activity, foreclosure, and repossession. Uniquely, § 1301 provides a co-debtor stay that also protects individuals who are liable with the debtor on consumer debts. The stay and the plan together allow debtors to cure mortgage arrears over time while keeping their homes — a central advantage over Chapter 7.

Discharge

Upon completing plan payments, the debtor receives a discharge under § 1328(a) that is somewhat broader than the Chapter 7 discharge, though BAPCPA narrowed the historical "super discharge." Certain debts — including most taxes, domestic support, student loans (absent undue hardship), and debts for death or personal injury caused by drunk driving — are not dischargeable. A limited "hardship discharge" is available under § 1328(b) if a debtor cannot complete the plan through no fault of their own.

Comparison with other chapters

Unlike Chapter 7, which liquidates non-exempt assets, Chapter 13 lets debtors retain property in exchange for committing future income. Chapter 11, primarily for businesses and high-debt individuals, involves creditor voting on plans and greater expense; Chapter 12 is a similar but tailored regime for family farmers and fishermen.
