Alzheimer's disease

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is a neurodegenerative disease that usually starts slowly and progressively worsens. It is the cause of 60–70 percent of cases of dementia. The most common early symptom is difficulty remembering recent events; as the disease advances, symptoms include problems with language, disorientation, mood swings, loss of motivation, and behavioural changes, until bodily functions are lost.

Causes and pathology

The disease is characterized by the accumulation of amyloid-beta plaques outside neurons and tau protein tangles inside them, accompanied by loss of synapses and neurons, particularly in the hippocampus and cerebral cortex. The greatest risk factor is age; most cases are sporadic, while rare early-onset familial forms are caused by mutations in the APP, PSEN1, or PSEN2 genes. The APOE ε4 allele substantially increases risk. The condition was first described by the German psychiatrist Alois Alzheimer in 1906, based on the case of Auguste Deter.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is based on medical history, cognitive testing, and increasingly on biomarkers — amyloid and tau PET imaging and cerebrospinal fluid or blood tests. There is no cure. Cholinesterase inhibitors and memantine provide modest symptomatic benefit, and anti-amyloid monoclonal antibodies such as lecanemab and donanemab, approved in the 2020s, can modestly slow decline in early disease, though with significant risks and controversy over their benefit. Care is largely supportive, and the disease places a heavy burden on families and health systems worldwide, affecting tens of millions of people.
