Pas-de-Calais is a department in northern France, in the Hauts-de-France region. It takes its name from the Pas de Calais, the French name for the Strait of Dover, the narrowest point of the English Channel, which separates the department from England by some 34 kilometres of water. Its prefecture is Arras, and its largest city is Calais.

Geography

Pas-de-Calais borders the departments of Nord and Somme, and has a long coastline on the Channel and the North Sea, marketed as the Côte d'Opale (Opal Coast). The chalk cliffs of Cap Blanc-Nez and Cap Gris-Nez face the white cliffs of Dover across the strait. Major towns include Calais, Boulogne-sur-Mer, Arras, Lens, Béthune and Saint-Omer.

History

The department was created during the French Revolution in 1790 from parts of the former provinces of Artois and Picardy. In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries its eastern districts formed part of the Nord-Pas de Calais coal basin, and towns such as Lens and Béthune grew around coal mining, which ended with the closure of the last pits in 1990. Pas-de-Calais was heavily fought over in both world wars: the battles of Arras and Vimy Ridge took place there in the First World War, and the region lay under German occupation in the Second. The Canadian National Vimy Memorial stands on Vimy Ridge near Arras.

Economy and transport

Calais is one of France's busiest passenger ports, with ferry services to Dover, and the French terminal of the Channel Tunnel is located at Coquelles near Calais, giving the department a central role in trade and travel between Britain and continental Europe. Boulogne-sur-Mer is a major fishing port and fish-processing centre. Tourism is concentrated on the Opal Coast resorts such as Le Touquet.
