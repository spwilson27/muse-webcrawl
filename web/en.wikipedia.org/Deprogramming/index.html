Deprogramming

Deprogramming refers to measures intended to persuade a person to abandon membership in a group — typically a new religious movement labelled a "cult" — that the person's family or others regard as harmful. The term emerged in the United States in the early 1970s and is most closely associated with Ted Patrick, often called the "father of deprogramming". Unlike voluntary "exit counselling", classic deprogramming was frequently involuntary: the member was abducted or detained, sometimes under a court conservatorship, and subjected to days of intensive argument, information and confrontation intended to break the group's alleged "mind control".

History

Ted Patrick, a former community organizer from San Diego, began removing young people from groups such as the Children of God in 1971 at the request of their parents, developing a confrontational method based on the premise that members had been brainwashed and could be "snapped out" by relentless questioning. Through the 1970s hundreds of deprogrammings took place in the United States, often organized through networks associated with the Cult Awareness Network. Parents sometimes obtained temporary conservatorships over adult children to give the practice legal cover.

Controversy

Deprogramming was fiercely controversial. Supporters argued it rescued victims of coercive persuasion; critics — including many sociologists of religion and civil liberties advocates — contended that the brainwashing model was scientifically unsound and that kidnapping people for their beliefs violated religious freedom. Several deprogrammers, including Patrick himself, were convicted of kidnapping or unlawful imprisonment. A pivotal moment came in 1995, when a jury awarded damages to Jason Scott, a Pentecostal church member deprogrammed by Rick Ross with referrals from the Cult Awareness Network; the resulting judgment bankrupted the network, whose name and assets were later acquired by persons associated with Scientology. After the Scott case, involuntary deprogramming largely disappeared in the United States, replaced by voluntary exit counselling and mediation.

Methods

Typical sessions involved isolating the member in a hotel room or house, presenting documents and testimony critical of the group, questioning doctrine and leadership, and depriving the person of contact with the group until they agreed to leave. Outcomes varied: some subjects renounced the group, others returned to it at the first opportunity, and some later sued their deprogrammers or families.
