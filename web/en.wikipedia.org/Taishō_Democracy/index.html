Taishō Democracy

Taishō Democracy (Japanese: 大正デモクラシー, Taishō demokurashii) refers to a period of liberal and democratic political development in Japan during the reign of the Emperor Taishō (1912–1926) and, by extension, into the early Shōwa period until the early 1930s. It was marked by the growth of party politics, expanding suffrage, mass movements, and a vibrant intellectual and cultural life.

Background

During the Meiji period (1868–1912), Japan had been governed under an oligarchic system in which unelected elder statesmen, the genrō, dominated policy. By the early twentieth century, an expanding urban middle class, an increasingly assertive press, and organised labour began to challenge this arrangement, demanding that governments be formed by parties commanding a majority in the elected lower house of the Imperial Diet.

Key developments

The period saw the rise of party cabinets, beginning with the premiership of Hara Takashi in 1918, the first commoner to hold the office, following the Rice Riots of 1918. The two great parties, the Rikken Seiyūkai and the Rikken Minseitō, alternated in power. In 1925 the Diet passed the Universal Manhood Suffrage Law, granting the vote to all men aged 25 and over and quadrupling the electorate, though the same year saw the repressive Peace Preservation Law aimed at socialist and communist movements.

Culture and society

Taishō Democracy coincided with flourishing liberal thought, exemplified by the political scientist Yoshino Sakuzō, who articulated the doctrine of minponshugi — government for and by the people, within the imperial framework. Urban culture blossomed: cafés, cinemas, department stores, and the figure of the "modern girl" (moga) symbolised a cosmopolitan new Japan.

Decline

The movement faltered amid the economic shocks of the late 1920s, the Great Depression, and growing military autonomy. The assassination of Prime Minister Inukai Tsuyoshi in the May 15 Incident of 1932 ended party cabinets, and by the mid-1930s Japan had moved decisively toward militarism and ultranationalism.
