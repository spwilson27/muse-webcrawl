Bullet loan

A bullet loan is a loan in which the entire principal is repaid in a single lump sum at the end of the loan term, rather than being amortized through regular installments of principal and interest. During the term the borrower typically makes interest-only payments (or, in a zero-coupon arrangement, no payments at all), and the full principal balance falls due as a single "bullet" payment at maturity.

Characteristics

Bullet loans have lower periodic payments than amortizing loans of the same size and term, which can help borrowers whose income or asset sale is expected later. The trade-off is substantial refinancing and repayment risk: the borrower must have the lump sum available, sell the underlying asset, or refinance at maturity — a risk that materializes if credit markets tighten or asset values fall.

Uses

Bullet structures are common in commercial real estate lending, where loans are often structured as balloon loans (amortized over a longer period but maturing earlier), in bridge loans and construction loans, and in corporate bond issues, where the standard bond is effectively a bullet loan: coupons are paid periodically and the face value is repaid at maturity.

Comparison with amortization

Unlike an amortizing loan, in which the balance declines steadily and total interest cost is lower, a bullet loan keeps the full principal outstanding for the entire term, increasing total interest paid and concentrating credit risk at maturity.
