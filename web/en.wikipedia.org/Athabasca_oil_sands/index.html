Athabasca oil sands

The Athabasca oil sands, also known as the Athabasca tar sands, are large deposits of bitumen or extremely heavy crude oil in northeastern Alberta, Canada — roughly centred on the boomtown of Fort McMurray. Along with the neighbouring Cold Lake and Peace River deposits, they contain one of the world's largest proven reserves of oil — about 165 billion barrels recoverable — making Canada the holder of the third-largest proven oil reserves in the world, after Venezuela and Saudi Arabia.

Geology

The deposits lie in the McMurray Formation, laid down in an ancient river estuary about 120 million years ago. Oil generated in deeper source rocks migrated and was biodegraded into bitumen mixed with sand, clay and water. Only about 20% of the resource is shallow enough to surface-mine; the rest is produced in situ, mainly by steam-assisted gravity drainage (SAGD), which injects steam to liquefy bitumen so it can be pumped to the surface.

History

Indigenous peoples used bitumen to waterproof canoes, and fur traders reported the seeps in the 18th century. Commercial production began in 1967 with the Great Canadian Oil Sands (now Suncor) plant, followed by Syncrude in 1978. Rising oil prices and improved technology drove rapid expansion in the 2000s, transforming Fort McMurray and making the oil sands Canada's largest source of crude.

Environmental issues

Oil sands production is energy- and water-intensive and has higher greenhouse-gas emissions per barrel than conventional oil. Surface mining disturbs boreal forest and creates tailings ponds; reclamation is legally required but slow. These impacts, and proposed export pipelines such as Keystone XL, have made the oil sands a focus of international environmental controversy and Indigenous rights debates.
