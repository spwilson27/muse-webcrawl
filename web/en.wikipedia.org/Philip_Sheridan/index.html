Philip Sheridan

Philip Henry Sheridan (March 6, 1831 – August 5, 1888) was a career United States Army officer and a Union general in the American Civil War. His career was noted for his rapid rise to major general and his close association with Lieutenant General Ulysses S. Grant, who transferred Sheridan from the Western Theater to command the Army of the Shenandoah in the East. He became one of the most famous Union generals of the war, and later served as Commanding General of the U.S. Army.

Early life and career

Sheridan was born to Irish immigrant parents and grew up in Somerset, Ohio. He graduated from West Point in 1853 (after a year's suspension for a fight with a fellow cadet) and served on the frontier in Texas and the Pacific Northwest before the Civil War.

Civil War

Rising quickly from captain to major general of volunteers, Sheridan distinguished himself as an infantry division commander at Perryville, Stones River (where his stand bought time for the Union army), Chickamauga and Chattanooga. Grant gave him command of the Cavalry Corps of the Army of the Potomac in 1864; his troopers fought J. E. B. Stuart's cavalry, mortally wounding Stuart at Yellow Tavern.

In August 1864 Sheridan took command in the Shenandoah Valley. After initial caution he won victories at Third Winchester and Fisher's Hill, then crushed Jubal Early's army at Cedar Creek (October 19, 1864) — a battle made famous by his dramatic ride from Winchester to rally his retreating men, celebrated in the poem "Sheridan's Ride". His systematic devastation of the Valley's farms ("the Burning") destroyed its capacity to supply the Confederacy. In April 1865 his cavalry cut off Lee's retreat at Appomattox, forcing the surrender.

Postwar career

Sheridan served as military governor of Texas and Louisiana during Reconstruction until removed by President Andrew Johnson for aggressively enforcing freedmen's rights. He then commanded on the Great Plains during the Indian Wars, directing harsh winter campaigns against the Cheyenne, Comanche and Kiowa — a record that remains controversial, including the remark attributed to him, "The only good Indians I ever saw were dead." He succeeded Sherman as Commanding General in 1883 and was promoted to General of the Army in 1888, shortly before his death.
