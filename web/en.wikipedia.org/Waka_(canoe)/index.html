Waka (canoe)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Waka
TypeCanoe (single or double hull)
OriginPolynesia / New Zealand
UsesVoyaging, war, fishing, transport, ceremony, sport

Waka are Māori watercraft, usually canoes ranging in size from small, unadorned watercraft (waka tīwai) used for fishing and river transport, to large decorated war canoes (waka taua) up to 40 metres (130 ft) long. The word also denotes the great ocean-voyaging canoes in which the ancestors of the Māori migrated from Hawaiki to New Zealand.

Voyaging waka

Māori oral traditions name many migration canoes — including Tainui, Te Arawa, Mataatua, Kurahaupō, Tākitimu, Aotea, Horouta and Tokomaru — from which iwi trace their descent. These double-hulled or outrigger voyaging canoes navigated the Pacific using stars, swell patterns, winds and bird flight. Modern voyaging societies have revived traditional navigation, building replica double-hulled waka hourua such as Hōkūleʻa (Hawaiʻi) and Haunui and Te Aurere (New Zealand).

Types

  • Waka taua — large war canoes with carved prow (tauihu) and sternpost (taurapa), carrying up to 80 paddlers or more.
  • Waka tētē — medium-sized canoes for transport and fishing.
  • Waka tīwai — simple dugout canoes.
  • Waka ama — outrigger canoes, now popular as a sport (waka ama racing).
  • Mōkihi — rafts of flax or raupō used on rivers in the South Island.

Construction

Hulls were traditionally hollowed from a single log of tōtara or other durable timber using stone adzes and fire, then lashed with flax fibre (muka) and adorned with carving (whakairo) and feather work. Famous surviving waka taua include Ngā Toki Matawhaorua at Waitangi, carved in 1940 for the centenary of the Treaty of Waitangi.

Modern use

Waka remain central to Māori ceremonial life, appearing at coronations of the Māori monarch, tangihanga, and national commemorations. Waka ama (outrigger canoe racing) is one of New Zealand's fastest-growing water sports, and waka hourua voyages continue to reconnect Pacific peoples.
