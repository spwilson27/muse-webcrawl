Zitouna Mosque

The Zitouna Mosque (Arabic: جامع الزيتونة, Jāmiʿ al-Zaytūna, "Mosque of the Olive Tree"), also known as the Al-Zaytuna Mosque, is the principal and oldest mosque in the medina of Tunis, Tunisia. Founded around 698–732 AD and rebuilt in its present form in the 9th century under the Aghlabids, it covers some 5,000 square metres in the heart of the old city. For over a millennium its attached teaching institution, the University of Ez-Zitouna, was one of the great centres of Islamic learning in the Maghreb.

History

Tradition holds that the mosque was founded on the site where an olive tree grew, after the Arab conquest of Tunis, by Hassan ibn al-Nu'man around 698 or by Ubayd Allah ibn al-Habhab around 732. The building was comprehensively rebuilt in 856–863 by the Aghlabid emir Abu Ibrahim Ahmad, financed in part by the Abbasid caliph al-Musta'in. Subsequent dynasties — Fatimids, Zirids, Hafsids, Ottomans, and Husaynid beys — added galleries, a library, ablution facilities, and decoration; the distinctive square minaret, 44 metres high, was completed in 1894 in Almohad style.

Architecture

The mosque is a hypostyle hall whose prayer room is carried on about 160 columns and capitals reused from the ruins of ancient Carthage. Its vast courtyard is surrounded by arcaded galleries, and the prayer hall is crowned by early example of a dome over the bay before the mihrab. The building served as a defensive as well as religious anchor of the medina, its outer walls abutting the souks.

The university

Teaching at Zitouna began in the mosque's early centuries and matured into a structured university, among the oldest in the Islamic world, teaching Maliki law, theology, Arabic, and the sciences. Its graduates included the historian Ibn Khaldun's teachers, the reformer Tahar Haddad, and the poet Aboul-Qacem Echebbi; the institution also played a role in Tunisian nationalism. Under President Bourguiba, the university was secularized and absorbed into the state education system in the 1960s; a re-founded University of Ez-Zitouna exists today in Tunis.
