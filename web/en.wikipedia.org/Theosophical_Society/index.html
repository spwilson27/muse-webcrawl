The Theosophical Society is an organization formed in the United States in 1875 by Helena Blavatsky, Henry Steel Olcott, William Quan Judge, and others, to advance the spiritual principles and search for truth known as Theosophy. The society's stated aims were to form a nucleus of the universal brotherhood of humanity without distinction of race, creed, sex, caste or colour; to encourage the comparative study of religion, philosophy and science; and to investigate unexplained laws of nature and the powers latent in humanity.

History

The society was founded in New York City on 17 November 1875. In 1879 Blavatsky and Olcott travelled to India, and in 1882 the international headquarters were established at Adyar, near Madras (now Chennai), where they remain today. Blavatsky's major works, Isis Unveiled (1877) and The Secret Doctrine (1888), laid out the movement's synthesis of Eastern and Western esotericism, drawing on Hinduism, Buddhism, Neoplatonism, Gnosticism and Hermeticism.

Its doctrines include reincarnation, karma, the existence of spiritually advanced beings known as Mahatmas or Masters, and the idea of a divine wisdom (theosophia) underlying all religions. After Blavatsky's death in 1891, leadership disputes led to schisms, notably the secession of the American section under William Quan Judge in 1895. Under Annie Besant, who became president in 1907, the society promoted the young Jiddu Krishnamurti as a coming "World Teacher"; Krishnamurti dissolved the order created around him in 1929 and later left the society.

Influence

The Theosophical Society played a significant role in introducing South and East Asian religious ideas to the West, encouraging Buddhist and Hindu revival movements in Asia, and influencing modern art, literature and the New Age movement. Its publishing houses issued numerous editions and translations of Eastern scriptures.
