Criterion of multiple attestation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The criterion of multiple attestation, also called the criterion of independent attestation, is a tool used by biblical scholars in the historical study of Jesus and early Christianity. According to the criterion, a saying or action attributed to Jesus is more likely to be authentic if it appears in multiple independent sources, or in multiple literary forms, within the early Christian tradition.

Application

In practice, scholars compare material across sources believed to be independent of one another, such as the Gospel of Mark, the hypothetical sayings source Q, the special material in Matthew (M) and Luke (L), the Gospel of John, the letters of Paul, and non-canonical works such as the Gospel of Thomas. A motif found, for example, in both Mark and Q is held to have circulated before either document was written, strengthening the case that it is early tradition.

Criticism

The criterion has important limitations. Widespread attestation may show only that a tradition was popular or useful to the early church, not that it goes back to Jesus himself; conversely, genuinely historical material preserved in only one source would be wrongly discounted. Establishing the independence of sources is also difficult, since the relationships among the Gospels remain debated. For these reasons the criterion is generally used alongside others — such as the criterion of embarrassment and the criterion of dissimilarity — rather than on its own.
