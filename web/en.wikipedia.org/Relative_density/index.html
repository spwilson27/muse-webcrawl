Relative density

Relative density, also called specific gravity, is the ratio of the density of a substance to the density of a given reference material. Specific gravity usually refers to the ratio for liquids with respect to water at its densest (at 4 °C, where water's density is very nearly 1 g/cm³). It is a dimensionless quantity.

Definition

Relative density RD = ρsubstance / ρreference. A relative density less than 1 means the substance is less dense than the reference — a liquid with specific gravity below 1 will float on water, other things being equal; greater than 1 means it sinks.

Measurement

Specific gravity of liquids is commonly measured with a hydrometer, which floats higher in denser liquids, or with a pycnometer or digital density meter. For gases the reference is usually air at a specified temperature and pressure. Brewers and winemakers track specific gravity of wort and must to estimate sugar content and alcohol production; the petroleum industry uses the related API gravity scale.

Temperature and pressure dependence

Because densities change with temperature (and, for gases, strongly with pressure), a quoted specific gravity should specify the temperatures of both the sample and the reference, e.g. SG 20/4, meaning the substance at 20 °C relative to water at 4 °C.

Uses

Relative density is used in mineral identification and gemology (gemstones have characteristic specific gravities), urine analysis in medicine, battery state-of-charge testing via electrolyte gravity, soil mechanics (specific gravity of soil solids), and quality control in food and chemical industries.
