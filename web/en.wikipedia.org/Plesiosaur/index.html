Plesiosaur

Fossil rangeLate Triassic – Late Cretaceous, ~203–66 Ma
OrderPlesiosauria
GroupsPlesiosauroidea (long-necked), Pliosauroidea (short-necked)
A long-necked plesiosaur of the genus Elasmosaurus.

The plesiosaurs (order Plesiosauria) were a group of marine reptiles that flourished during the Mesozoic Era, from the latest Triassic, about 203 million years ago, until the end of the Cretaceous, 66 million years ago. They are characterised by a broad, turtle-like body, a relatively small head, and four large paddle-shaped flippers, with which they "flew" through the water in a manner resembling sea turtles and penguins. Although often popularly grouped with the dinosaurs, plesiosaurs belonged to a separate reptile lineage, the Sauropterygia.

Forms

Two main body plans evolved. The plesiosauroids had long necks, small heads, and long tails was not typical — rather short tails; extreme forms such as Elasmosaurus had up to 76 neck vertebrae and reached 14 metres in length, probably feeding on small fish and cephalopods. The pliosauroids, such as Liopleurodon and Kronosaurus, had short necks and enormous heads with conical teeth, acting as apex predators of fish, ichthyosaurs, and other plesiosaurs. Some pliosaurs exceeded 10 metres in length with skulls over 2 metres long.

Life history

Plesiosaurs were air-breathing and almost entirely marine, though some remains occur in estuarine deposits. Fossil evidence — an adult Polycotylus preserved with a single large foetus — shows that at least some plesiosaurs gave birth to live young rather than laying eggs, and may have cared for their offspring. Gastroliths (stomach stones) are frequently found with skeletons, possibly aiding buoyancy control or digestion.

Discovery

The first plesiosaur, Plesiosaurus dolichodeirus, was found by the fossil collector Mary Anning at Lyme Regis in 1823 and described by William Conybeare; its snake-like neck was so unlike any living animal that its authenticity was questioned. Plesiosaur fossils have since been found on every continent, including Antarctica. Popular culture has repeatedly linked plesiosaurs to alleged lake monsters, most famously the Loch Ness Monster, though the group has been extinct for 66 million years.
