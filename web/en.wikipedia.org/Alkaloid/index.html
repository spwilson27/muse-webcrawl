Alkaloid

ClassNatural products, secondary metabolites
Defining featureBasic nitrogen atom(s)
ExamplesMorphine, caffeine, nicotine, quinine, cocaine

Alkaloids are a broad class of naturally occurring organic compounds that contain at least one nitrogen atom. Some synthetic compounds of similar structure may also be termed alkaloids. In addition to carbon, hydrogen and nitrogen, alkaloids may also contain oxygen or sulfur, and more rarely other elements such as phosphorus, chlorine, and bromine.

Alkaloids are produced by a large variety of organisms including bacteria, fungi, plants, and animals. They can be purified from crude extracts of these organisms by acid-base extraction, or solvent extractions followed by silica-gel column chromatography. Alkaloids have a wide range of pharmacological activities including antimalarial, antiasthma, anticancer, cholinomimetic, vasodilatory, analgesic, antibacterial, and antihyperglycemic activities. Many have found use in traditional or modern medicine, or as starting points for drug discovery.

History

The study of alkaloids began in the early 19th century, when Friedrich Sertürner isolated morphine from opium in 1804 — the first alkaloid to be isolated and crystallized. The name "alkaloid" (from "alkali-like") was introduced in 1819 by the German pharmacist Carl Friedrich Wilhelm Meissner. In the following decades, many important alkaloids were isolated, including quinine (1820), caffeine (1821), nicotine (1828), atropine (1833), and cocaine (1860). The first total synthesis of an alkaloid, coniine, was achieved by Albert Ladenburg in 1886.

Classification

Alkaloids are often divided into groups according to their chemical structure (for example, indole, quinoline, isoquinoline, tropane, and steroidal alkaloids), their biochemical precursor (such as ornithine-, lysine-, tyrosine-, or tryptophan-derived alkaloids), or their pharmacological activity. "True alkaloids" derive from amino acids and have nitrogen in a heterocyclic ring; "protoalkaloids" derive from amino acids but lack the ring; "pseudoalkaloids" have nitrogen-containing structures built from non-amino-acid precursors such as terpenes or purines.

Distribution and function

Alkaloids are especially common in flowering plants, where they occur in families such as the Papaveraceae (poppies), Ranunculaceae, Solanaceae (nightshades), and Papilionaceae (legumes). Their bitter taste and toxicity are thought to serve as defences against herbivores. In animals, alkaloids occur in some frogs (such as the poison dart frogs) and in the venoms and secretions of various species.

Applications

Many alkaloids are used as medicines and recreational drugs. Morphine and codeine are important analgesics; quinine was a standard antimalarial; vinblastine and vincristine are used in chemotherapy; caffeine and nicotine are widely consumed stimulants. Some alkaloids, such as cocaine and morphine derivatives, are strictly controlled substances because of their potential for abuse.
