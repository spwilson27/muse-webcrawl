Air brake

An air brake is a type of power braking system for vehicles in which compressed air presses on a piston to apply the brake pads or shoes. Air brakes are used extensively on large road vehicles (trucks and buses) and on trains, where they replaced hand brakes and steam brakes.

Railway air brakes

The railway air brake was developed by George Westinghouse, whose first straight-air patent dates to 1869 and whose automatic air brake of 1872 made the system fail-safe: air pressure in a continuous brake pipe running the length of the train holds the brakes off, and a reduction in pressure — whether commanded by the driver or caused by a break in the train — causes each vehicle's triple valve to admit air from a local reservoir into its brake cylinder, applying the brakes. The Burlington brake trials of 1886–87 demonstrated the system's effectiveness, and laws such as the United States Railroad Safety Appliance Act of 1893 made automatic air brakes and couplers mandatory, drastically reducing railway accidents.

Modern systems include graduated release, distributor valves, and electronically controlled pneumatic (ECP) brakes, which apply the brakes simultaneously along very long freight trains.

Road vehicle air brakes

On trucks and buses, an engine-driven compressor fills reservoirs; pressing the brake pedal meters air to the brake chambers, while spring brakes provide parking and emergency braking that engages automatically if air pressure falls. Most heavy vehicles use S-cam drum brakes or air disc brakes. Air brakes can tolerate the leakage and repeated applications that would overheat or exhaust a hydraulic system, and they allow trailers to be coupled and uncoupled easily via gladhand connectors.
