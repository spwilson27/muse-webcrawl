Constituent assembly

A constituent assembly is a body of representatives elected or appointed for the specific purpose of drafting, and in some cases adopting, a new constitution for a state. Unlike an ordinary legislature, a constituent assembly exercises pouvoir constituant — the constituent power of the people — and is often considered sovereign for the task, though its work may still require ratification by referendum or by existing institutions.

History and examples

The concept took modern form in the American and French revolutions: the Philadelphia Convention of 1787 drafted the United States Constitution, and France's National Constituent Assembly (1789–1791) transformed the Estates-General and proclaimed the Declaration of the Rights of Man. Notable later examples include the Frankfurt Parliament of 1848, the Weimar National Assembly of 1919, the Constituent Assembly of India (1946–1949), chaired in its drafting work by B. R. Ambedkar, the Italian Constituent Assembly of 1946, and the Parliamentary Council that wrote West Germany's Basic Law in 1949.

In recent decades constituent assemblies have featured in transitions to democracy and post-conflict settlements: South Africa's Constitutional Assembly (1994–1996), Nepal's constituent assemblies (2008–2015), Tunisia's National Constituent Assembly (2011–2014), and Chile's Constitutional Convention (2021–2022), whose draft was rejected in a referendum. Venezuela's assemblies of 1999 and 2017 illustrate the controversy such bodies can provoke when they displace existing legislatures. Assemblies differ in whether they are separately elected for the purpose, converted from a sitting legislature, or mixed with appointed members, and in how directly citizens participate through public consultation.
