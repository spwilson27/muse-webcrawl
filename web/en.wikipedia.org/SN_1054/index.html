SN 1054

SN 1054 is a supernova that was first observed on 4 July 1054 and remained visible for over two years. Recorded by Chinese, Japanese, and possibly Arab and Native American observers as a brilliant "guest star" in the constellation Taurus, it was bright enough to be seen in daylight for 23 days. Its expanding remnant is the Crab Nebula, at whose centre lies the Crab Pulsar.

Historical records

Chinese court astronomers of the Song dynasty recorded a guest star southeast of Tianguan (ζ Tauri) in 1054 that shone in daylight for 23 days and remained visible at night for 642 days. Japanese records in the Meigetsuki also note it. Possible depictions have been claimed in Ancestral Puebloan petroglyphs in the American Southwest, though these interpretations are debated. No unambiguous European record survives, possibly because of cloudy weather or cultural factors.

The remnant

The ejected material forms the Crab Nebula, catalogued by Charles Messier as M1 — the first object in his catalogue — after John Bevis observed it in 1731. In 1942, Jan Oort and Nicholas Mayall established the link between the nebula and the 1054 event by matching its expansion age. In 1968 the Crab Pulsar, a neutron star spinning about 30 times per second, was discovered at its heart, confirming that SN 1054 was a core-collapse supernova of a massive star.
