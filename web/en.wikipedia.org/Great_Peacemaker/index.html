Great Peacemaker

The Great Peacemaker (in Mohawk: Skennenrahawi, sometimes written Deganawida or Dekanawida) was a Huron prophet and spiritual leader who, together with the Onondaga chief Hiawatha and the Mother of Nations Jikonsaseh, founded the Iroquois Confederacy (Haudenosaunee). He promulgated the Great Law of Peace (Kaianere'kó:wa), the oral constitution under which the five (later six) nations of the confederacy were united.

Traditional account

According to Haudenosaunee oral tradition, the Peacemaker was born among the Huron people north of Lake Ontario. A vision led him to carry a message of peace — the Good Message (or Good News of Peace and Power) — to the five warring nations south of the lake: the Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga and Seneca. Legend holds that he crossed Lake Ontario in a canoe made of white stone, demonstrating his spiritual power. With Hiawatha, an Onondaga (in some accounts Mohawk) orator grieving the loss of his family, he persuaded the nations one by one to accept the Great Law. The most resistant figure was the Onondaga sorcerer-chief Tadodaho, whose twisted body and snakes in his hair symbolised discord; Hiawatha combed the snakes from Tadodaho's hair, and he became the confederacy's central firekeeper.

The Great Law of Peace

The Great Law established a confederacy governed by fifty sachems (hoyané) nominated by clan mothers, with decisions made by consensus among the nations. Its symbols include the Tree of Peace — a great white pine under which the nations buried their weapons — and the eagle perched atop it, watching for approaching danger. The confederacy later admitted the Tuscarora, becoming the Six Nations. Scholars have debated the founding date, with proposals ranging from a solar eclipse in 1142 to the fifteenth century.

Legacy

The Great Law of Peace is recognised as one of the oldest participatory constitutions in the world and remains the governing law of the Haudenosaunee. In 1988 the United States Congress passed a resolution acknowledging the influence of the Iroquois Confederacy's system of government on the development of the United States Constitution — a claim of influence that remains debated among historians.
