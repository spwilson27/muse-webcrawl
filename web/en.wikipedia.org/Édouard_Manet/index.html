Édouard Manet

Édouard Manet (1832–1883), a pivotal figure in the transition from Realism to Impressionism.

Édouard Manet (23 January 1832 – 30 April 1883) was a French modernist painter. He was one of the first 19th-century artists to paint modern life, and a pivotal figure in the transition from Realism to Impressionism.

Early works and scandal

Born into a wealthy Parisian family, Manet rejected a naval career to study art, training in the studio of Thomas Couture and copying Old Masters at the Louvre. His Le Déjeuner sur l'herbe (1863), exhibited at the Salon des Refusés, and Olympia (1863, exhibited 1865) provoked scandal with their frank treatment of modern subjects and their flat, confrontational style, even as younger painters hailed him as a leader.

Later career

Manet painted scenes of contemporary Paris — cafés, bullfights, railway stations, and portraits of friends such as Émile Zola and Berthe Morisot. Though associated with the Impressionists, he refused to exhibit with them, seeking official recognition at the Salon. His late masterpiece, A Bar at the Folies-Bergère (1882), was painted when he was already gravely ill; he died the following year, aged 51.

Legacy

Manet's bold brushwork, compressed space and modern subject matter profoundly influenced the Impressionists and the development of modern art.
