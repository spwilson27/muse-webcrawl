The term First World originally described, within the Three-World model of the Cold War era, the bloc of capitalist countries aligned with the United States and NATO, as opposed to the Second World of communist states aligned with the Soviet Union, and the Third World of non-aligned and neutral countries. The model was coined by French demographer Alfred Sauvy in 1952, by analogy with the three estates of pre-revolutionary France.

Membership

The First World comprised the United States, Canada, Western Europe, Australia, New Zealand and Japan, together with allies such as Turkey, South Korea and (at various times) other anti-communist states. Neutral Western countries such as Switzerland, Sweden, Austria and Ireland were sometimes counted as First World by economics despite their lack of NATO membership.

After the Cold War

With the dissolution of the Soviet Union in 1991, the political meaning of the three worlds collapsed. "First World" survives in popular usage as a rough synonym for wealthy, industrialised, developed countries — a usage reflected in the ironic phrase "first world problems" — while development discourse generally prefers terms such as "developed country", "Global North", or the World Bank's income classifications as more precise and less dated.
