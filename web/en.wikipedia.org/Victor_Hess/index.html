Victor Franz Hess (24 June 1883 – 17 December 1964) was an Austrian-American physicist who shared the 1936 Nobel Prize in Physics with Carl David Anderson "for his discovery of cosmic radiation".

Early life and education

Hess was born at Schloss Waldstein in Peggau, Styria, the son of a royal forester. He studied physics at the University of Graz, earning his doctorate in 1910, and then worked at the Institute for Radium Research in Vienna under Stefan Meyer.

Discovery of cosmic rays

Scientists of the time were puzzled that electroscopes slowly discharged even when shielded, suggesting a penetrating radiation from the ground. In 1911–1912 Hess carried electrometers aloft in a series of hydrogen balloon flights, reaching altitudes above 5,000 metres. Contrary to expectations, the ionization first decreased with altitude but then increased well above the level at the surface, and it did not diminish during a near-total solar eclipse. Hess concluded that the radiation entered the atmosphere from outer space — the phenomenon Robert Millikan later named "cosmic rays". His results were published in 1912 and earned him the Lieben Prize in 1919.

Later career

Hess became professor at Graz, then Innsbruck, where he established a cosmic-ray observatory on the Hafelekar mountain. A Catholic opponent of National Socialism and married to a Jewish wife, he was dismissed after the Anschluss of 1938 and emigrated to the United States, joining Fordham University in New York. He later worked on radium safety standards and, with William T. McNiff, devised a method of detecting radium poisoning by measuring exhaled air. He became a U.S. citizen in 1944 and died in Mount Vernon, New York, in 1964.
