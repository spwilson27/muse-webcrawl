Modern Paganism, also known as contemporary Paganism or Neopaganism, is an umbrella term for a diverse range of new religious movements that draw inspiration from the pre-Christian, pre-modern religions of Europe, North Africa, and the Near East. Modern Pagans typically revere nature, celebrate seasonal cycles, and practise polytheistic or pantheistic worship of deities drawn from historical pagan pantheons.

Origins

The movement's intellectual roots lie in Renaissance interest in classical religion, 18th- and 19th-century Romanticism, folklore studies, and occultism. In the 20th century these streams crystallised into organised traditions. In Britain, Gerald Gardner publicised Wicca in the 1950s, claiming initiation into a surviving witch-cult — a claim historians regard as doubtful, though Wicca's rituals demonstrably combine ceremonial magic, folk practice, and literary sources. Druidry developed through orders such as the Order of Bards, Ovates and Druids; Heathenry (Germanic neopaganism) organised from the 1970s in Iceland, Britain, and the United States, where the Ásatrúarfélagið won Icelandic state recognition in 1973.

Beliefs and practices

There is no shared creed. Common threads include reverence for nature as sacred, celebration of the turning seasons — in Wicca and Druidry, the eight festivals of the Wheel of the Year (Samhain, Yule, Imbolc, Ostara, Beltane, Litha, Lughnasadh, Mabon) — and ritual practice that may include magic, divination, and the invocation of gods and goddesses. Traditions range from reconstructionist movements such as Hellenism, Kemetism (Egyptian), and Slavic Rodnovery, which attempt to rebuild historical practice from scholarship, to eclectic forms blending many sources.

Demographics

Precise numbers are elusive because adherents are few, dispersed, and often uncounted; estimates in the United States range from several hundred thousand to over a million, with Wicca consistently among the largest traditions. Censuses in the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, and New Zealand record tens of thousands of self-identified Pagans. Modern Paganism's growth since the 1960s has been linked to environmentalism, feminism (notably goddess-oriented and Dianic traditions), and the counterculture.

Criticism and issues

Scholars debate the historical continuity claimed by some groups, and some Germanic and Slavic traditions have struggled with appropriation by far-right and folkish movements, which mainstream Heathen organisations publicly reject. As the movement has matured it has gained legal recognition in several countries for marriages, chaplaincy, and burial grounds.
