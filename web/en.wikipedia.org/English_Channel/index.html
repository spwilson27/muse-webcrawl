The English Channel, also known simply as the Channel (French: la Manche, "the Sleeve"), is an arm of the Atlantic Ocean that separates Southern England from northern France. It links the North Sea and the Atlantic, is about 560 km (350 mi) long and varies in width from 240 km (150 mi) at its widest to just 34 km (21 mi) at the Strait of Dover, its eastern end.

Geography and shipping

The Channel is one of the busiest shipping lanes in the world, with more than 500 vessels a day passing through, governed by a traffic separation scheme established in 1967. Its shores include the ports of Southampton, Portsmouth, Dover, Calais, Boulogne, Le Havre and Cherbourg, and the Channel Islands (Jersey, Guernsey, Alderney, Sark) lie in the Gulf of Saint-Malo off the French coast. The Channel Tunnel, opened in 1994, provides a fixed rail link between Folkestone and Coquelles near Calais.

History

Formed as rising sea levels flooded a former river valley at the end of the last ice age, separating Britain from the continent around 6500 BC, the Channel has been a historic invasion route and defensive moat: the Spanish Armada (1588), Napoleonic invasion plans, and Operation Sea Lion all foundered there, while the Allied D-Day landings crossed it in 1944. Matthew Webb made the first unaided swim across in 1875, and Channel swimming remains a celebrated endurance feat administered under Channel Swimming Association rules.
