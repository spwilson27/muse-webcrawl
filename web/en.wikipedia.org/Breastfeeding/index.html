Breastfeeding

Breastfeeding, also known as nursing, is the process of feeding a human child's milk directly from the mother's breasts. Human breast milk is the healthiest form of milk for babies; it provides nutrition, immune factors and other bioactive components adapted to the infant. Health authorities including the World Health Organization recommend exclusive breastfeeding for the first six months of life and continued breastfeeding, with appropriate complementary foods, up to two years of age or beyond.

Physiology

Milk production (lactation) is driven by the hormone prolactin, while the let-down reflex, by which milk is ejected from the alveoli into the ducts, is triggered by oxytocin released when the infant suckles. The first milk, colostrum, is thick, yellowish and rich in antibodies, particularly secretory IgA. Mature milk changes in composition over the course of a feed and as the child grows.

Benefits

Breastfeeding is associated with reduced rates of gastrointestinal and respiratory infections, otitis media and sudden infant death syndrome in infants, and possibly with lower risks of later obesity and diabetes. For mothers, it is associated with reduced risks of breast and ovarian cancer, type 2 diabetes and postpartum haemorrhage, and it promotes post-pregnancy weight loss and birth spacing through lactational amenorrhea.

Practice and challenges

Successful breastfeeding depends on correct attachment (latch) of the infant to the breast. Common difficulties include sore nipples, engorgement, blocked ducts and mastitis. Most medications are compatible with breastfeeding, but some conditions — including certain infections and infant galactosemia — may prevent it. Donor human milk and infant formula are alternatives when a mother's own milk is unavailable.

History and society

For most of history, breastfeeding — by the mother or a wet nurse — was the only safe way to feed an infant. Rates declined sharply in many industrialised countries in the mid-20th century with the marketing of formula, prompting international regulation through the WHO International Code of Marketing of Breast-milk Substitutes (1981) and the Baby Friendly Hospital Initiative. Laws and norms governing breastfeeding in public and mothers' rights to express milk at work vary widely by country.
