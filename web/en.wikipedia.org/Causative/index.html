Causative

In linguistics, a causative (abbreviated CAUS) is a valency-increasing operation that indicates that a subject either causes someone or something else to do or be something, or causes a change in state of a non-volitional event. Normally, it brings in a new argument (the causer), pushing the original subject into an oblique role, while keeping the original object unchanged.

Types

Causatives may be lexical, where causation is expressed by a separate verb form or an unrelated word, as in English kill ("cause to die") versus die; morphological, where a verb is modified with an affix, as in Japanese or Turkish; or periphrastic (analytic), where an auxiliary verb such as English make, have, or get, or French faire, is used.

Semantics

Linguists distinguish direct causation, where the causer acts directly on the causee, from indirect causation, where the causer arranges for an event to occur. Languages differ in whether a single causative form covers both types or separate forms exist.

Examples across languages

Japanese uses the suffix -(s)aseru; Turkish uses suffixes such as -dir; Quechua uses -chi; and many Bantu languages use a causative extension of the verb stem. Semitic languages such as Arabic and Hebrew have causative verb stems within their root-and-pattern morphology.
