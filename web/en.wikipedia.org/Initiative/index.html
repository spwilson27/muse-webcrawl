Initiative

In political science, an initiative (also known as a popular initiative or citizens' initiative) is a means by which a petition signed by a specified number of voters can force a government to choose either to enact a law or hold a public vote in the legislature, or, in the case of an indirect initiative, first pass the measure to the legislature for consideration. It is a form of direct democracy.

An initiative lets citizens propose laws directly, bypassing or prompting the legislature.

Types

In a direct initiative, a measure that gathers enough signatures is placed directly on the ballot for voters to decide. In an indirect initiative, the proposal is first submitted to the legislature, which may enact it, reject it (sending it to the ballot), or propose an alternative. Initiatives may enact statutes or amend constitutions, and are distinct from referendums, in which a legislature refers a measure to the voters, and from recalls, which remove officials.

Use around the world

Switzerland is the most prominent user: at the federal level, 100,000 signatures can propose a constitutional amendment, which is decided by a double majority of voters and cantons. In the United States the initiative exists in about half the states — including California, Oregon, Colorado and Arizona — a legacy of the Progressive Era, and has been used for measures on taxation (California's Proposition 13), term limits, drug policy and electoral reform. Critics point to the influence of paid signature gathering and well-funded campaigns; supporters value the initiative as a check on unresponsive legislatures.
