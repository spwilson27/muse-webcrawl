The Literary Digest was an influential American weekly magazine founded in 1890 and published by Funk & Wagnalls. It is best remembered as a pioneer of the public opinion poll, and for its famously incorrect prediction of the outcome of the 1936 United States presidential election.

History

The magazine began in 1890 as a digest of articles culled from other periodicals, summarizing current opinion on politics, science, religion, and literature. Under editors such as William Seaver Woods, it grew into one of the most widely read general-interest weeklies in America, reaching a circulation of roughly one million copies by the 1920s. Its brisk, condensed format anticipated later news magazines such as Time.

The 1936 straw poll

Beginning with the 1916 election, the Digest conducted large-scale "straw polls," mailing ballots to millions of names drawn from telephone directories, automobile registration lists, and subscriber rolls. These polls correctly predicted the winners of the elections of 1920, 1924, 1928, and 1932.

In 1936 the magazine mailed about ten million ballots and received more than 2.4 million responses, predicting that Republican Alf Landon would defeat President Franklin D. Roosevelt by 57% to 43%. Roosevelt instead won in a landslide, carrying every state except Maine and Vermont. The poll's sample was badly skewed: telephone and car owners were wealthier than the general electorate, and responders differed systematically from non-responders. The debacle destroyed the magazine's credibility. George Gallup, using a much smaller but representative quota sample, correctly forecast Roosevelt's victory and further embarrassed the Digest by predicting its own result in advance.

Decline and end

Damaged by the 1936 failure and squeezed by competitors, the Literary Digest was sold in 1937 and merged into Review of Reviews in 1938, ending its run. The episode remains a standard cautionary example in statistics teaching about sampling bias and non-response bias.
