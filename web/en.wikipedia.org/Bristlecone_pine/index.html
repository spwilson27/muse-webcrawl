Bristlecone pine

The bristlecone pines are a group of three closely related species in the genus Pinus — the Great Basin bristlecone pine (Pinus longaeva), the Rocky Mountain bristlecone pine (Pinus aristata), and the foxtail pine (Pinus balfouriana) — native to high mountain regions of the western United States. They are named for the dark purple female cones, each scale of which bears a small prickle or bristle. The Great Basin species includes the longest-lived individually verified non-clonal organisms on Earth.

Longevity

Bristlecone pines grow at elevations of 2,700–3,700 metres (8,900–12,100 ft) on poor dolomitic soils, in conditions of cold, wind, and low rainfall that would kill most trees. Their strategy of extreme frugality — dense, resinous wood, a very slow growth rate, and the ability to keep most of the tree dead while a narrow strip of living bark sustains a few branches — lets them persist for millennia. A Pinus longaeva named Methuselah, in the White Mountains of California, has been dated at more than 4,800 years old; its exact location is kept secret. An even older specimen, Prometheus, was cut down in 1964 for research and later counted at roughly 4,900 years.

Dendrochronology

Because dead bristlecone wood decays extremely slowly in the dry mountain air, overlapping ring sequences from living and dead trees have produced a continuous tree-ring chronology extending back some 9,000 years. This record, pioneered by Edmund Schulman and refined at the University of Arizona's Laboratory of Tree-Ring Research, was used to calibrate the radiocarbon dating scale, revealing systematic fluctuations in atmospheric carbon-14.
