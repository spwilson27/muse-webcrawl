Prism (optics)

An optical prism is a transparent optical element with flat, polished surfaces that are designed to refract light. At least one surface must be angled — elements with two parallel surfaces do not have a significant optical effect. The most familiar type is the triangular prism, made of glass or other transparent material.

Types

Dispersive prisms spread light into its spectral components; Isaac Newton famously used a pair of prisms to demonstrate that white light is composed of a spectrum of colours. Reflective prisms, such as the Porro prism used in binoculars, exploit total internal reflection to invert or rotate images. Polarising prisms, like the Nicol prism, split light by polarisation.

How prisms work

Light changes speed as it moves from one medium to another, causing it to bend (refract). The degree of bending depends on wavelength — shorter (blue) wavelengths bend more than longer (red) ones — a phenomenon called dispersion, which separates white light into a rainbow spectrum.

Applications

Prisms are used in spectroscopes, binoculars, periscopes, cameras, and ophthalmic instruments. In architecture, anidolic lighting systems use prisms to redirect daylight into buildings.
