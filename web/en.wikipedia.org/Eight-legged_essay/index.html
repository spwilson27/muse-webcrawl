Eight-legged essay

Eight-legged essay
Chinese八股文 (bāgǔwén)
Literally"eight-bone text"
PeriodMing and Qing dynasties (standardised c. 1487; abolished 1901)
ContextImperial civil service examinations

The eight-legged essay (Chinese: 八股文; pinyin: bāgǔwén) was a highly rigid literary composition style prescribed for the imperial examinations of Ming- and Qing-dynasty China. Candidates for the civil service were required to write essays in this format on themes drawn from the Confucian Four Books, interpreting them strictly according to the commentaries of Zhu Xi and writing, in effect, in the voice of the ancient sages.

Structure

The essay's name derives from its eight prescribed sections or "legs": opening (破題, "breaking open the topic"),承題 (receiving the topic), 起講 (beginning the exposition), 入手, 起股, 中股, 後股 (the successive parallel-prose "legs"), and 束股 (conclusion). The middle sections were written in parallel prose — paired passages of equal length and matched structure — and the whole was subject to strict limits on length, forbidden words and deviations from orthodoxy.

History and criticism

The form crystallised under the Chenghua Emperor in the late 15th century and dominated the examinations for more than four centuries. From the 19th century it was attacked as the symbol of a sterile, backward-looking education that stifled original thought; reformers such as Kang Youwei and Liang Qichao campaigned for its abolition, and it was scrapped in 1901, a few years before the examination system itself ended in 1905. In modern Chinese, "baguwen" remains a byword for rigid, doctrinaire and empty writing.
