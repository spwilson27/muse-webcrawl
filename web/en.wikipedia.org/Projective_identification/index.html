Projective identification

Projective identification is a term introduced by Melanie Klein in 1946 to describe a psychological process in psychoanalytic theory. In projective identification, one person (the projector) unconsciously projects unwanted aspects of the self onto another person (the recipient), and interacts with them in ways that pressure the recipient to experience and behave in accordance with the projection. Unlike simple projection, in which the recipient is merely misperceived, projective identification involves an interpersonal dimension: the recipient is induced to feel and enact the projected state.

Development of the concept

Klein originally described projective identification as an infantile phantasy of splitting off parts of the self and placing them into the mother, linked to her account of the paranoid-schizoid position. Wilfred Bion extended the idea by treating it as a form of communication: the infant projects unbearable feelings into the caregiver, who — through "containment" — processes and returns them in tolerable form, a model later applied to the psychoanalytic relationship itself. Thomas Ogden emphasized the interplay of projection, pressure on the recipient, and reinternalization by the projector of a modified version of what was projected.

Clinical use

Clinicians use the concept to understand countertransference — the feelings evoked in the therapist during treatment — as a source of information about the patient's inner world. The idea has also been applied to couples and family therapy, group dynamics, and organizational life, where members may be recruited into roles carrying disowned emotions of the group. Critics have noted the term's breadth and occasional vagueness, and empirical psychologists regard it as a clinical construct rather than a laboratory-validated mechanism.
