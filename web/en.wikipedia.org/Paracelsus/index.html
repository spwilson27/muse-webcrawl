Paracelsus (born Theophrastus von Hohenheim; c. 1493 – 24 September 1541) was a Swiss physician, alchemist, lay theologian and philosopher of the German Renaissance. A pioneer in several aspects of the medical revolution of the Renaissance, he emphasised the value of observation over received authority, promoted chemical medicines, and is credited as the founder of toxicology.

Life

Paracelsus was born at Einsiedeln, the son of a physician and chemist who directed his early education in botany, mining and natural philosophy. He took his medical doctorate at the University of Ferrara around 1515 and then travelled for years through Europe, the Balkans and possibly the Near East, serving as a military surgeon and learning from barber-surgeons, miners and midwives. In 1527 he became city physician and lecturer at Basel, where he scandalised the establishment by lecturing in German instead of Latin and publicly burning the works of Galen and Avicenna; a legal dispute drove him from the city in 1528, and he spent much of his remaining life as an itinerant physician, dying in Salzburg in 1541.

Ideas

Paracelsus rejected the humoral medicine of Galen and instead held that the body was governed by chemical processes. He proposed the tria prima — salt, sulphur and mercury — as fundamental principles of matter, and taught that diseases had specific external causes and specific chemical remedies, founding the iatrochemical tradition. His famous dictum sola dosis facit venenum ("the dose makes the poison") founded the science of toxicology, and his Von der Bergsucht ("On the Miners' Sickness") was a pioneering work of occupational medicine. He gave zinc its name and used opium (laudanum), mercury and other mineral drugs in treatment. His name "Paracelsus" meant "equal to or greater than Celsus", the Roman medical writer — a measure of his self-confidence and his quarrel with tradition.

Legacy

Much of Paracelsus's work was published only posthumously, and his blend of medicine, alchemy, astrology and theology influenced both early modern science and later occult traditions. Historians of medicine regard him as a pivotal figure between medieval and modern medicine, and he is remembered in the Paracelsus Medical University in Salzburg and in the element name promethium's early proposed name and various awards for pharmacology.
