Definiteness

In linguistics, definiteness is a semantic feature of noun phrases that distinguishes between referents that are identifiable in a given discourse context and those that are not. A definite noun phrase, such as English "the book", signals that the speaker assumes the hearer can identify the particular entity being referred to, while an indefinite noun phrase, such as "a book", introduces a referent that is not presented as identifiable. Definiteness is closely related to, but distinct from, specificity and familiarity.

Marking across languages

Languages mark definiteness in many different ways. English and most other Germanic and Romance languages use articles: the definite article "the" and the indefinite article "a/an". In Scandinavian languages the definite article may appear as a suffix on the noun, as in Swedish huset, "the house". Some languages, such as Russian and Japanese, have no articles at all and rely on context, word order, or demonstratives instead. Others mark the distinction on verbs or through case-like particles; Turkish, for example, uses the accusative case to mark definite direct objects, and Persian uses the particle rā for definite objects. In many languages, proper names, pronouns, and possessives are inherently definite.

Uses of the definite article

Even within one language, definite marking serves several distinct functions. It can be anaphoric, referring back to something already mentioned ("a man walked in; the man sat down"); deictic, pointing to something in the immediate situation; or based on shared general knowledge, as with unique referents like "the sun". So-called weak definites, such as "read the newspaper" or "go to the hospital", do not pick out a uniquely identifiable individual in the ordinary way and have been a focus of theoretical debate.

Theoretical accounts

Philosophers of language have offered competing analyses of definite descriptions. Bertrand Russell's theory treated "the F" as a quantificational expression asserting existence and uniqueness, whereas Peter Strawson argued that uniqueness is a presupposition rather than an assertion. In formal semantics, definiteness is often modelled using familiarity conditions, maximality, or uniqueness within a contextually restricted domain. Definiteness effects — such as the unacceptability of definite noun phrases in existential "there" sentences in English ("there is a/*the book on the table") — are a further classic topic of study.
