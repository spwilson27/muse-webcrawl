Vyakarana

Vyākaraṇa (Sanskrit: व्याकरण, "explanation, analysis", literally "separation and exposition") is one of the six Vedangas, the auxiliary disciplines of Vedic study, devoted to Sanskrit grammar and linguistics. Its purpose within the Vedic tradition was to safeguard the correct recitation and interpretation of the sacred texts, and it developed into one of the most sophisticated grammatical traditions of the ancient world.

Pāṇini and the Aṣṭādhyāyī

The crowning achievement of vyākaraṇa is the Aṣṭādhyāyī ("Eight Chapters") of Pāṇini, composed around the 5th–4th century BCE. Its nearly 4,000 aphoristic rules (sūtras) form a complete generative grammar of Sanskrit, using an algebraic metalanguage with markers, rule ordering, and recursion that modern scholars have compared to a formal system or computer program. Pāṇini drew on earlier grammarians such as Śākaṭāyana and codified the language of the cultured elite (śiṣṭa).

The commentarial tradition

Kātyāyana's Vārttika (c. 3rd century BCE) critically examined Pāṇini's rules, and Patañjali's Mahābhāṣya ("Great Commentary", c. 2nd century BCE) defended and elaborated them. Together these three — Pāṇini, Kātyāyana, and Patañjali — are the munitraya, the "triad of sages", whose authority defined the discipline. A vast later literature, including the Kāśikā commentary and works by Bhartṛhari, who wrote the Vākyapadīya on the philosophy of language, kept the tradition central to Indian intellectual life for over two millennia.

Influence

Introduced to the West in the early nineteenth century, Pāṇinian grammar influenced the founders of comparative philology and, later, structural and generative linguistics; its rule formalism is often cited as an ancestor of modern formal grammar and of the Backus–Naur notation used in computing.
