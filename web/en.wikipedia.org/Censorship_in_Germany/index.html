Censorship in Germany

Censorship in Germany has taken many forms across the country's history, from the Carlsbad Decrees of 1819 and Imperial press laws, through the comprehensive propaganda and censorship apparatus of Nazi Germany and the state censorship of East Germany, to the constitutionally limited media regulation of the Federal Republic.

19th and early 20th centuries

The Carlsbad Decrees (1819) imposed press censorship and surveillance of universities across the German Confederation. The German Empire's Reich Press Law of 1874 formally abolished preventive censorship, though prosecutions for lèse-majesté and other offences continued. The Weimar Constitution of 1919 guaranteed free expression ("Censorship does not take place"), with exceptions exploited for film and "trash and dirt" laws.

Nazi Germany and East Germany

Under the Nazis (1933–1945), the Reich Ministry of Public Enlightenment and Propaganda under Joseph Goebbels controlled all media, publishing, film, and the arts; book burnings in May 1933 symbolised the regime. In the German Democratic Republic (1949–1990), all media were state-controlled and publishing required licences, although formally the GDR constitution also disclaimed censorship.

Federal Republic

Article 5 of the Basic Law guarantees freedom of the press and states "There shall be no censorship." Limitations include laws against incitement and Holocaust denial, protection of minors through media indexing, and — in the digital era — the Network Enforcement Act (NetzDG, 2017), which obliges large platforms to remove unlawful content quickly and has been debated as a form of delegated regulation.
