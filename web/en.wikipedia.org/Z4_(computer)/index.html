Z4 (computer)

The Z4 was a digital computer designed by the German engineer Konrad Zuse and built by his company, Zuse Apparatebau. It is widely considered to be one of the world's first commercial digital computers, delivered to ETH Zürich in 1950.

History

Work on the Z4 began in Berlin during the Second World War as a successor to the Z3. The machine survived the war by being moved to Göttingen and later to the Allgäu region. After the war Zuse founded Zuse KG and completed the machine, leasing it to ETH Zürich, where it remained in service until 1955.

Design

Like its predecessors, the Z4 was an electromechanical machine built from telephone relays, but it was far more capable: it had a memory of 64 words of 32 bits, a punched-tape reader, and could perform floating-point arithmetic, square roots, and conditional instructions. Plans existed for conditional branching, which Zuse had already implemented in part.

Legacy

The Z4 is preserved today at the Deutsches Museum in Munich. It influenced early European computing and demonstrated that a commercially viable general-purpose computer could be built outside the United States and Britain.
