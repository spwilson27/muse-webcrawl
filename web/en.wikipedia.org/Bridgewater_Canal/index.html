The Bridgewater Canal connects Runcorn, Manchester, and Leigh in North West England. Commissioned by Francis Egerton, 3rd Duke of Bridgewater, to carry coal from his mines at Worsley to Manchester, its first section opened in 1761. It is often described as the first true canal in England — built independently of a natural river — and its success ignited the country's "Canal Mania" and helped fuel the Industrial Revolution.


The canal at Barton upon Irwell.

Construction

Engineer James Brindley designed the canal as a contour canal with no locks on the original level. Its most famous feature was the Barton Aqueduct, which carried the canal high over the River Irwell — a spectacle dubbed one of the wonders of the age. Coal from the Duke's underground mines at Worsley Delph was loaded directly into boats through navigable underground levels.

Impact

The canal halved the price of coal in Manchester at a stroke, and tolls made it enormously profitable for its owners. Extensions followed to the Mersey at Runcorn (1776) and to Leigh, connecting with the Leeds and Liverpool Canal.

Present day

The canal passed to the Manchester Ship Canal Company and is still owned by its successors. Carrying no commercial traffic today, it is a popular leisure waterway, notable for the orange-brown colour of its water, stained by iron from the old coal mines.
