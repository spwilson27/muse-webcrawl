Interval (music)

In music theory, an interval is a difference in pitch between two sounds. An interval may be described as horizontal, linear, or melodic if it refers to successively sounding tones, such as two adjacent pitches in a melody, and vertical or harmonic if it pertains to simultaneously sounding tones, such as in a chord.

Size and quality

The size of an interval is measured by the number of letter names it spans (second, third, fourth, fifth, sixth, seventh, octave). Its quality refines this: intervals may be perfect (unison, fourth, fifth, octave), major or minor (seconds, thirds, sixths, sevenths), or altered by semitone adjustments as augmented or diminished. For example, C to G is a perfect fifth (seven semitones), C to E a major third (four semitones), and C to E♭ a minor third (three semitones). In equal temperament, the size of an interval in cents is 1200 times the base-2 logarithm of its frequency ratio; a semitone is 100 cents.

Consonance and dissonance

Intervals are traditionally classed as consonant (octave, fifth, major and minor thirds and sixths) or dissonant (seconds, sevenths and all augmented and diminished intervals), a classification shaped by both acoustics — simple frequency ratios produce smoother sounds — and musical style.

Inversion and compound intervals

Inverting an interval moves one note by an octave: a major third becomes a minor sixth, a perfect fifth a perfect fourth, and sizes sum to nine. Intervals wider than an octave are called compound intervals (e.g., a compound major third is a major tenth).
