Hikikomori

Hikikomori (Japanese: ひきこもり or 引きこもり, lit. "pulling inward, being confined") are reclusive adolescents or adults in Japan who withdraw from society and seek extreme degrees of isolation and confinement. The Japanese Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare defines hikikomori as individuals who refuse to leave their parents' house, and isolate themselves in their room and reduce social contact for a period exceeding six months. Estimates of the number of hikikomori in Japan range from several hundred thousand to over a million, including a growing population of middle-aged recluses (the so-called "8050 problem", in which people in their 50s are supported by parents in their 80s).

Causes and characteristics

The phenomenon was popularised by psychiatrist Tamaki Saitō's 1998 book. Proposed contributing factors include academic and workplace pressure, bullying, family dynamics, economic insecurity, and the pressures of Japanese social expectations; hikikomori often maintain contact with the outside world only through the internet, video games and convenience-store runs at night. Withdrawal may overlap with depression, social anxiety, autism spectrum conditions or avoidant personality traits, though hikikomori is not itself a formal diagnosis. Similar patterns of severe social withdrawal have been reported in South Korea, Taiwan, and increasingly in Western countries.

Responses

Responses include government surveys and support centres, "rental sister" and outreach services, supported-employment programs, and non-profit organisations. Cultural depictions include the novel and anime Welcome to the N.H.K.
