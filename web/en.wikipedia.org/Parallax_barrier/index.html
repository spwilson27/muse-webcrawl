Parallax barrier

A parallax barrier is a device placed in front of an image source, such as a liquid crystal display, to allow it to show a stereoscopic or multiscopic image without the viewer needing to wear 3D glasses. It consists of a layer of material with a series of precision slits, which allow each eye to see a different set of pixels, creating a sense of depth through parallax in the same way as stereopsis.

Principle

The barrier is a sheet containing vertical slits spaced so that, from the intended viewing position, the left eye sees only the columns of the display carrying the left-eye image and the right eye sees only the right-eye columns. Because each eye receives a different image, the brain fuses them into a three-dimensional picture. The barrier reduces brightness and horizontal resolution, since each eye sees only part of the panel's pixels, and the effect works only within certain viewing zones.

History

The principle was described independently by Auguste Berthier in 1896 and Frederic E. Ives, who built working devices in the first years of the 20th century. Related techniques were used in integral photography and in lenticular printing. Electronically switchable barriers appeared in the 2000s, using an LCD layer that can turn the barrier on (3D mode) or off (full-resolution 2D mode).

Applications

Parallax-barrier displays were built into products such as the Sharp Actius RD3D laptop (2003), the LG Optimus 3D and HTC Evo 3D smartphones (2011), and most famously the Nintendo 3DS handheld console (2011), whose autostereoscopic top screen made glasses-free 3D a mass-market feature. The later New Nintendo 3DS added eye tracking to stabilise the viewing zone ("super-stable 3D"). Barriers have also been used in medical imaging, automotive dashboards, and 3D televisions.

Limitations

Drawbacks include reduced brightness, halved or worse effective resolution per eye, crosstalk between views, and restricted viewing positions. Competing glasses-free technologies include lenticular lenses, directional backlights, and light-field displays.
