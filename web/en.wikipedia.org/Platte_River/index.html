Platte River

The Platte River is a major river in the State of Nebraska and one of the most significant tributary systems in the Missouri River drainage. Its watershed includes much of the eastern slope of the Rocky Mountains in Colorado and Wyoming and the central Great Plains. The Platte is formed in western Nebraska by the confluence of the North Platte and South Platte rivers and flows eastward about 310 miles (500 km) to join the Missouri River near Plattsmouth, south of Omaha.

Course and character

The Platte is a classic braided river: shallow, wide, and filled with shifting sandbars and small islands. The remark attributed to early travellers—that it was "a mile wide and an inch deep" and "too thick to drink, too thin to plow"—captures its character. Flows vary greatly with season and are now heavily influenced by irrigation withdrawals and reservoirs on its tributaries.

History

The Platte valley formed the great highway of westward expansion in the 19th century: the Oregon, California, and Mormon trails, and later the Pony Express and the first transcontinental railroad, all followed its course across Nebraska. Fort Kearny and other posts protected the emigrant routes.

Ecology

The central Platte in Nebraska is a critical stopover habitat for migratory birds. Each spring, hundreds of thousands of sandhill cranes and large numbers of waterfowl rest and feed in the river's shallow channels and adjacent wet meadows during their northward migration. The river also provides habitat for the endangered whooping crane, the interior least tern, and the piping plover, and its diversion for agriculture has been the subject of interstate compacts and a cooperative recovery program among Nebraska, Colorado, Wyoming, and the federal government.
