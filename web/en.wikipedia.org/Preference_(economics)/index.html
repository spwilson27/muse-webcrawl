Preference (economics)

In economics, preferences are the orderings that an agent uses to rank alternative bundles of goods or outcomes according to the desirability (utility) they provide. Preferences are a foundational primitive of consumer theory and microeconomics: given their preferences and constraints, agents are modeled as choosing the most preferred feasible alternative.

Formal representation

A preference relation ≽ ("is at least as good as") is defined over a consumption set. From it derive strict preference (≻) and indifference (~). Preferences may be represented graphically with indifference curves, which collect all bundles the consumer regards as equally good.

Axioms

Standard theory assumes preferences are complete (any two bundles can be compared), transitive (if A ≽ B and B ≽ C then A ≽ C), and often continuous and monotonic (more of a good is better). Convexity of preferences yields diminishing marginal rates of substitution and well-behaved demand. Relaxing completeness or transitivity leads to generalized models studied in decision theory.

Utility functions

When preferences satisfy completeness, transitivity, and continuity, the Debreu representation theorem guarantees a continuous utility function that represents them: bundles with higher utility are strictly preferred. Utility in this framework is ordinal — only rankings matter, not the size of utility differences — though cardinal measures appear in expected-utility theory for choices under risk (von Neumann–Morgenstern) and in welfare economics.

Revealed preference

Paul Samuelson's revealed preference approach (1938 onward) infers preferences from observed choices rather than assuming introspective rankings, imposing consistency conditions such as the Weak and Strong Axioms of Revealed Preference. The approach lets economists test the theory of demand against market data.

Criticism and extensions

Behavioral economics documents systematic violations of the standard axioms — framing effects, loss aversion, hyperbolic discounting, and reference dependence — prompting models such as prospect theory (Kahneman and Tversky). Preferences may also be endogenous, shaped by advertising, habit, and social norms, a point emphasized by critics of the assumption of fixed, exogenous tastes.
