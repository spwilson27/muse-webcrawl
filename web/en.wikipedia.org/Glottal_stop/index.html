Glottal stop


The vocal folds producing a glottal stop.

The glottal stop (or glottal plosive) is a type of consonantal sound produced by obstructing airflow in the vocal tract by closing the glottis. Its symbol in the International Phonetic Alphabet is ⟨ʔ⟩, resembling a question mark without the dot.

Articulation

To produce the sound, the vocal folds are pressed together so that no air passes; when they are released, a brief cough-like catch is heard. In English it is the sound in the middle of the exclamation "uh-oh".

Occurrence

The glottal stop is a phoneme in many languages, including Arabic (hamza), Hawaiian (ʻokina), Tagalog, and numerous Indigenous languages of the Americas and Australia. In English it is usually allophonic: it appears as t-glottalisation in accents such as Cockney (where "butter" may be pronounced "bu'er"), Estuary English and many American dialects before syllabic nasals, as in "button".

Writing

Several writing systems have a dedicated letter for it: Arabic hamza (ء), Hebrew aleph (א) in some uses, and the Hawaiian ʻokina (ʻ), which is considered a consonant letter. In transliteration of Semitic languages an apostrophe-like character (ʾ) is used.
