Gas giant

A large planet composed mainly of hydrogen and helium, lacking a well-defined solid surface.

A gas giant is a giant planet composed mainly of hydrogen and helium. The term was coined in 1952 by science-fiction writer James Blish. Jupiter and Saturn are the Solar System's gas giants; the term is sometimes also applied more broadly to include Uranus and Neptune, though planetary scientists increasingly classify those two as ice giants because their interiors are dominated by heavier volatile substances such as water, ammonia and methane.

Gas giants have no solid surface: their atmospheres gradually thicken with depth until the hydrogen is compressed into a liquid, and at still greater pressures into an electrically conducting fluid called metallic hydrogen, which is thought to generate their powerful magnetic fields. Most models give them a dense core of rock and ice of roughly 5–20 Earth masses, formed early by core accretion before the planet rapidly captured gas from the protoplanetary disc.

Structure

Jupiter's interior is estimated to consist of a dilute core overlain by a deep layer of metallic hydrogen extending to about 78 per cent of the planet's radius, capped by liquid molecular hydrogen and the visible cloud decks of ammonia, ammonium hydrosulfide and water. Both Jupiter and Saturn radiate substantially more heat than they receive from the Sun, a legacy of their formation and slow contraction; for Saturn, the settling of helium droplets through the metallic hydrogen layer ("helium rain") is thought to supply additional energy.

Exoplanetary gas giants

Thousands of gas-giant exoplanets have been detected. Many are "hot Jupiters"—giants orbiting extremely close to their stars—whose existence challenged earlier formation theories and led to models of planetary migration. Cold, wide-orbit giants more like Jupiter remain a major target for direct-imaging surveys.
