Kingdom of Northumbria

Northumbria (Old English: Norþanhymbra Rīce) was an early medieval Anglo-Saxon kingdom in what is now Northern England and south-east Scotland, formed in 654 AD by the union of the kingdoms of Bernicia and Deira. At its height it stretched from the Humber and Mersey in the south to the Firth of Forth in the north.

Golden age

Powerful seventh-century kings — Edwin, Oswald, and Oswiu — made Northumbria the dominant kingdom of Britain. Edwin's conversion in 627 brought Christianity from Canterbury, while Oswald invited Aidan from Iona to found Lindisfarne, launching the Irish mission. The Synod of Whitby (664) aligned the Northumbrian church with Rome. Northumbrian monasteries at Wearmouth-Jarrow and Lindisfarne produced the Venerable Bede's Ecclesiastical History and the Lindisfarne Gospels, masterpieces of Insular art, during a cultural golden age.

Decline and Viking age

From the late 8th century the kingdom was plagued by dynastic violence; the Viking raid on Lindisfarne in 793 opened a new era. In 867 the Great Heathen Army captured York and conquered Deira, establishing the Scandinavian kingdom of Jórvík, while a remnant Anglian Northumbria survived around Bamburgh. The northern lands were fought over by Scots, Norse, and the rising kingdom of Wessex until Northumbria was finally absorbed into England in the 10th–11th centuries; its last earl was deposed in 1072.

Legacy

Northumbria gave its name to the modern region of Northumbria and to Northumberland; its dialect influenced northern English and Scots, and its saints — Cuthbert, Aidan, Oswald — remain central to northern English identity.
