Pulque

Pulque is a traditional Mexican alcoholic beverage made from the fermented sap of the maguey (agave) plant. Milky white, slightly viscous, and mildly effervescent, it typically contains about 4–6% alcohol and continues to ferment after production, giving it a short shelf life. Pulque has been produced in central Mexico for well over a thousand years and held religious significance in pre-Hispanic cultures.

Production

When a mature maguey is about to flower, its central bud is cut out, leaving a cavity into which the plant's sap, called aguamiel ("honey water"), collects. A harvester known as a tlachiquero scrapes the cavity and collects the sap daily using a long gourd. The sap is transferred to fermentation vessels and seeded with a starter culture, fermenting within hours through the action of bacteria and yeasts such as Zymomonas mobilis and Lactobacillus species, which also produce its characteristic viscosity.

History

Pulque (octli in Nahuatl) was sacred in Aztec and earlier Mesoamerican societies, associated with the goddess Mayahuel and the Centzon Totochtin, the "four hundred rabbits" of drunkenness. Consumption was ritually restricted, and public drunkenness was heavily punished except among the elderly and on feast days. After the Spanish conquest, production commercialized, and great pulque haciendas made fortunes in the colonial and nineteenth-century periods. In the twentieth century, pulque declined under competition from beer and campaigns portraying it as unhygienic, though it has seen a revival among younger drinkers in Mexico City pulquerías.

Varieties and serving

Plain pulque (pulque blanco) may be flavored as curados, blended with fruits, nuts, or other ingredients such as pineapple, guava, celery, or walnut. It is traditionally drunk in dedicated taverns called pulquerías and consumed soon after fermentation, as it spoils quickly.
