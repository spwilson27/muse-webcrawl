Gothic rock

Gothic rock (also called goth rock or simply goth) is a style of rock music that emerged from post-punk in the United Kingdom in the late 1970s. The first post-punk bands which shifted towards dark music with gothic overtones include Siouxsie and the Banshees, Joy Division, Bauhaus, and the Cure.

The genre itself was defined as a separate movement from post-punk in part by its darker music accompanied by introspective and romantic lyrics. Gothic rock gave rise to a broader goth subculture that included clubs, fashion, and publications in the 1980s.

Characteristics

Gothic rock typically combines driving basslines (often played high and melodic), tribal or drum-machine rhythms, atmospheric and chorus-laden guitars, synthesizers, and baritone or dramatic vocals. Lyrically it draws on Gothic literature, horror films, religion, and romantic melancholy. Taking cues from the Doors, the Velvet Underground, David Bowie and Iggy Pop, early goth artists favored a dark, introspective atmosphere over punk's aggression.

History

Bauhaus's 1979 single "Bela Lugosi's Dead" is often cited as the genre's founding record. The Batcave club in London (opened 1982) became the movement's fashion and social hub. The second wave of the mid-1980s, led by The Sisters of Mercy, Fields of the Nephilim and The Mission, moved gothic rock toward arena scale. Later developments included ethereal wave (Cocteau Twins, Dead Can Dance on 4AD), deathrock in the United States, and influence on industrial, darkwave and later alternative metal and emo acts. Goth festivals such as the Wave-Gotik-Treffen in Leipzig continue to anchor the subculture.
