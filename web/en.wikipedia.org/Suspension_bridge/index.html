Suspension bridge

A suspension bridge is a type of bridge in which the deck is hung below suspension cables on vertical suspenders. The main cables pass over towers and are anchored at each end, carrying the deck's load in tension. Suspension bridges achieve the longest spans of any bridge type; the longest, Türkiye's 1915 Çanakkale Bridge, has a main span of 2,023 metres.

Structure

The main cables, today spun from thousands of high-strength steel wires, follow a parabolic curve under the deck's load. Vertical suspenders hang the stiffened deck from the cables; towers carry the cables' vertical load in compression to their foundations, while massive anchorages resist the cables' horizontal pull. Deck stiffening girders or trusses distribute concentrated loads and limit movement.

History

Simple suspension bridges of rope and bamboo were built for centuries in the Himalayas, China and the Andes. The modern form began with James Finley's chain bridges in early 19th-century America and Thomas Telford's Menai Bridge (1826). John A. Roebling pioneered wire-cable construction, culminating in the Brooklyn Bridge (1883) with steel cables. Twentieth-century bridges such as the George Washington and Golden Gate bridges extended spans greatly; the 1940 collapse of the Tacoma Narrows Bridge in wind ("Galloping Gertie") transformed the aerodynamic design of decks.

Advantages and limits

Suspension bridges can span wide straits with few piers, carry decks high above navigation, and are relatively resilient in earthquakes. They are, however, expensive and their flexibility makes them less suited to the heaviest railway loads than cable-stayed or truss designs.
