Weather Bureau

Weather Bureau
United States government agency
Founded: 1870 (as part of the Signal Corps); 1891 (as the Weather Bureau)
Abolished: 1970 (renamed National Weather Service)
Parent: U.S. Department of Agriculture (1891–1940); Department of Commerce (1940–1970)

The Weather Bureau was a United States federal agency responsible for weather observation and forecasting. It is the direct predecessor of the modern National Weather Service. The Bureau operated the nation's official weather observation network, issued storm warnings and forecasts, and conducted research into meteorology for more than three-quarters of a century.

History

Systematic weather observation in the United States began in 1870, when Congress authorized the Army Signal Corps to take weather observations at military stations and warn of storms on the Great Lakes and Atlantic coast. In 1891 the service was transferred to the Department of Agriculture and renamed the Weather Bureau. Its first chief was Mark W. Harrington.

Under Willis L. Moore, chief from 1895 to 1913, the Bureau expanded its telegraph-based observation network, began daily weather maps, and developed hurricane warnings. The Bureau moved to the Department of Commerce in 1940 and was briefly renamed the U.S. Weather Bureau; in 1965 it was placed within the new Environmental Science Services Administration (ESSA). In 1970, under the newly formed National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA), the Bureau was renamed the National Weather Service.

Functions

The Bureau maintained hundreds of first-order stations taking surface observations, launched radiosonde balloons for upper-air data, operated river and flood forecasting services, and issued aviation, agricultural, and marine forecasts. Its county warning system, staffed by local forecasters, became the backbone of American severe-weather warning.
