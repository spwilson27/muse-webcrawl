Michigan Avenue is a major north–south street in Chicago, Illinois, that runs from the Chicago River southward through the South Side. North of the river, its continuation through the Near North Side — a stretch of upscale shops, restaurants, hotels, and landmarks between the river and Oak Street — is famous as the Magnificent Mile. The avenue is one of Chicago's best-known streets, comparable to Fifth Avenue in New York City.

History

Michigan Avenue takes its name from Lake Michigan, which it once bordered before landfills extended the shoreline eastward. In the 19th century the avenue south of the river was a fashionable residential boulevard of mansions; the opening of the Michigan Avenue Bridge (now DuSable Bridge) in 1920 — a centrepiece of Daniel Burnham's 1909 Plan of Chicago — joined the two halves and transformed the northern section into a grand commercial boulevard. The Wrigley Building (1921–24) and the neo-Gothic Tribune Tower (1925) anchored its new prestige, and the post-1947 development promoted by Arthur Rubloff coined the "Magnificent Mile" name.

The Magnificent Mile and landmarks

The Mile contains flagship department stores and luxury boutiques, historic hotels, and major buildings including the John Hancock Center (875 North Michigan Avenue, now "875 North Michigan", completed 1969), the Fourth Presbyterian Church, and the Chicago Water Tower and Pumping Station (1869) — among the few structures to survive the Great Chicago Fire of 1871 and now symbols of the city. South of the river, the Michigan Avenue Historic District faces Grant Park and Millennium Park, with the Art Institute of Chicago across the street; the avenue continues south past cultural institutions and historic districts to Chicago's far South Side.
