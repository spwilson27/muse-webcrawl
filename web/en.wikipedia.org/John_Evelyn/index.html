John Evelyn

John Evelyn (31 October 1620 – 27 February 1706) was an English writer, landowner and gardener, best known for the diary he kept for most of his adult life — a major source for the social and political history of 17th-century England, covering the Civil War, the Restoration, the Great Plague and the Great Fire of London. He was a founding fellow of the Royal Society and the author of Sylva (1664), the first book published by that body.

Life

Evelyn was born at Wotton House in Surrey into a family enriched by gunpowder manufacture. Educated at Balliol College, Oxford, he avoided deep involvement in the Civil War, travelling in France and Italy in the 1640s, where he studied art, architecture and gardening. In 1647 he married Mary Browne, daughter of the English ambassador in Paris. Returning to England, he settled at Sayes Court in Deptford, creating a celebrated garden, and after the Restoration held various public posts, serving on commissions for sick and wounded seamen during the Dutch wars and for the rebuilding of St Paul's Cathedral, on whose foundation stone ceremony he was present.

Diary and writings

Evelyn's diary, kept from 1640 to 1706 and published only in 1818, rivals Pepys's as a chronicle of the age — more decorous and reflective, and spanning a far longer period. Sylva (1664), prompted by naval concerns over timber supplies, urged landowners to plant trees and remains a classic of forestry literature. Other works include Sculptura (1662), the first English book on engraving, translations of French gardening manuals, and the unpublished Elysium Britannicum, an encyclopaedia of horticulture. He corresponded widely with figures such as Christopher Wren, Robert Boyle and Samuel Pepys.

Death and legacy

Evelyn inherited Wotton in 1699 and died in London in 1706. Sayes Court's grounds were later partly preserved; his reputation rests on the diary and on Sylva, whose call for reforestation is often cited as an early statement of sustainable forestry.
