Resultative

In linguistics, a resultative is a form that expresses that something comes about through the action of the verb's referent, describing a state resulting from the action. Resultatives include resultative verb compounds in languages such as Chinese, Japanese, and Korean, and resultative secondary predicates in languages such as English and German.

Examples

In English, adjectives and prepositional phrases can serve as resultative phrases: in "She hammered the metal flat", "He froze the pond solid", and "They painted the barn red", the bolded phrase denotes the state that the object comes to be in as a result of the action. A debated subtype is the "weak" resultative, as in "The dog barked the chickens awake", in which the object is not an ordinary object of the verb.

Analysis

Resultatives have been a central topic in the study of the syntax–semantics interface and of argument structure. Analysts have proposed constraints on resultatives — for example, that the result phrase is predicated of an object (the "direct object restriction") — and have disagreed about whether resultatives are formed in the syntax, in the lexicon, or by constructional templates, as in construction grammar.

Across languages

Languages differ considerably in the resultatives they permit: Germanic languages and Chinese allow productive resultative formation, while Romance languages generally restrict it to a few types, often requiring a reflexive or a different construction.
