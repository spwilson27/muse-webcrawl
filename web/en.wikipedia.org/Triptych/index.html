Triptych


A medieval altarpiece triptych

A triptych is a work of art—usually a panel painting—divided into three sections. The central panel is typically the largest, flanked by two smaller hinged wings that can be folded shut. The term comes from the Greek triptychos, "consisting of three layers", originally applied to ancient three-leaved writing tablets.

History

Triptychs became a standard format for Christian altarpieces in the Middle Ages and Renaissance, especially in Northern European painting. Examples include Rogier van der Weyden's altarpieces and Hieronymus Bosch's The Garden of Earthly Delights, whose outer panels, when closed, show the creation of the world in grisaille. The format protected the inner images and allowed different displays for feast days and ordinary days.

Modern use

The three-panel format has been revived by modern and contemporary artists, including Francis Bacon, whose triptychs of distorted figures are among his best-known works. Triptychs also appear in photography and popular culture whenever an image is presented in three related parts.
