Roman religion was the set of religious beliefs and practices of ancient Rome, centred on maintaining right relations between the human community and the gods (pax deorum, "peace of the gods"). It was a polytheistic, ritual-focused civic religion: correct performance of sacrifice, prayer, vows and festivals mattered more than personal belief or morality, and public cult was inseparable from the Roman state.

Deities and cult

The Romans worshipped a large pantheon. Jupiter Optimus Maximus, Juno and Minerva formed the Capitoline Triad, honoured in the great temple on the Capitoline Hill. Mars, Venus, Apollo, Diana, Ceres, Vesta and many others had their own temples, priesthoods and festivals recorded in the Roman calendar. Household religion centred on the lares and penates (guardian spirits of home and store-room) and the genius of the head of household.

Rome freely adopted foreign gods — from the Greek pantheon, whose deities were identified with Roman ones, to eastern cults such as those of Cybele, Isis and Mithras. The state regulated public cult through priestly colleges: the pontifices, headed by the pontifex maximus; the augurs, who read the will of the gods from birds and other signs; and the Vestal Virgins, who tended Vesta's sacred fire.

Ritual and the state

Magistrates such as the consuls performed public sacrifices on behalf of the state, and no major undertaking — a battle, an election, the founding of a colony — began without taking the auspices. Religion legitimised political authority; from Augustus onwards the cult of the emperor and his family provided a focus of loyalty across the empire.

Decline

Christianity spread through the empire in the 1st–3rd centuries AD and, after Constantine's conversion, received imperial favour. In 380 AD the Edict of Thessalonica made Nicene Christianity the state religion, and under Theodosius I traditional sacrifices were banned and temples closed, bringing public Roman religion to an end by the close of the 4th century.
