Traditionalism (Spain)


Carlist requetés, heirs of Spanish Traditionalism

Traditionalism (Spanish: Tradicionalismo) is a Spanish political doctrine that emerged in the 19th century, advocating the restoration of the political, social and religious institutions of Spain's traditional Catholic monarchy against liberalism, centralism and secularisation. It is most closely associated with Carlism, the movement that supported the rival claim to the Spanish throne of Don Carlos and his descendants.

Traditionalists defended the Catholic Church's central place in public life, the historic regional fueros (charters and self-governing privileges), a decentralised and corporative social order, and a monarchy limited by tradition and intermediary bodies rather than by liberal constitutionalism. Its motto was Dios, Patria, Fueros, Rey — God, Fatherland, Fueros, King.

Traditionalist ideas were developed by thinkers such as Juan Donoso Cortés and, in the later 19th century, by Juan Vázquez de Mella, the leading theorist of the Carlist Traditionalist Communion. Traditionalists fought in the three Carlist Wars of the 19th century and, as the requetés, formed a key component of Franco's Nationalist forces in the Spanish Civil War (1936–1939). Under Franco the movement was forcibly merged into the single party, FET y de las JONS, and its distinct identity faded; small Traditionalist organisations survive in Spain today.
