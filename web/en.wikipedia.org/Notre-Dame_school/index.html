Notre-Dame school

Periodc. 1160 – 1250
LocationNotre-Dame Cathedral, Paris
Key figuresLéonin, Pérotin
Known forOrganum, early polyphony, rhythmic modes

The Notre-Dame school is the name given by music historians to the composers associated with the Cathedral of Notre-Dame in Paris in the late 12th and early 13th centuries, who developed the first substantial repertory of polyphonic music in the Western tradition. The school is known through the writings of the theorist known as Anonymous IV and through large manuscript collections such as the Magnus liber organi ("Great Book of Organum").

Composers and style

The two named masters are Léonin (Leoninus), credited with compiling the Magnus liber of two-voice organum, and his successor Pérotin (Perotinus), who composed organa for three and four voices such as Viderunt omnes and Sederunt principes. Their music sets a slow-moving chant tenor against florid upper voices (organum purum) or rhythmic passages in discant style governed by the six rhythmic modes — the first systematic rhythmic notation in Western music. The school also cultivated the conductus and early forms of the motet.

Significance

Notre-Dame polyphony marks a turning point: music became a composed, notated art attributed to individual masters, and the techniques of measured rhythm and multi-voice writing laid the foundations for later medieval and Renaissance music.
