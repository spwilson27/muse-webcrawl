The "Ride of the Valkyries" (German: Walkürenritt or Ritt der Walküren) is the popular name for the prelude to Act 3 of Die Walküre, the second of the four music dramas that constitute Richard Wagner's monumental cycle Der Ring des Nibelungen (The Ring of the Nibelung). The main theme, which first appears in the prelude, was first written down by Wagner on 23 July 1851; a preliminary draft of the Ride was composed in 1854 as part of the opera's score, and the full orchestration was completed in early 1856.

Together with the "Bridal Chorus" from Lohengrin, the Ride of the Valkyries is one of Wagner's best-known pieces. It is about eight minutes long in the opera, though the concert version is often shortened to around five minutes. The music depicts the Valkyries — warrior maidens of Norse mythology who gather the souls of slain heroes — riding through a stormy night sky on their flying horses, calling to one another as they assemble on a mountaintop.

In the opera

In Die Walküre, the Ride heralds the gathering of the Valkyries on the summit of a rocky mountain. Eight of them arrive, bearing dead heroes on their saddles, and await their sister Brünnhilde, who flees with the pregnant Sieglinde in defiance of Wotan's commands. The music, driven by a galloping rhythm in the strings and brilliant fanfares in the brass, accompanies their war cries ("Hojotoho!") as they laugh and call across the storm.

Musical character

The piece is in B minor and is built on an insistent dotted rhythm suggesting galloping hooves. Wagner layers arpeggiated figures in the strings and woodwinds beneath heroic brass motifs. The leitmotif of the Ride recurs throughout the Ring cycle and is associated with the Valkyries and heroic endeavour. Its sheer orchestral weight made it a favourite excerpt in concerts, and Wagner himself conducted it separately despite having reservations about extracting music from his dramas.

In popular culture

The Ride of the Valkyries has been used countless times in film, television and advertising. Its most famous cinematic appearance is in Francis Ford Coppola's Apocalypse Now (1979), where it is blasted from helicopter loudspeakers during an air assault — a use that cemented its association with helicopters and aerial attack. Earlier, D. W. Griffith used it in The Birth of a Nation (1915), and it accompanied newsreels during the Second World War. It has been parodied in cartoons such as What's Opera, Doc? (1957), in which Elmer Fudd pursues Bugs Bunny to Wagnerian strains.
