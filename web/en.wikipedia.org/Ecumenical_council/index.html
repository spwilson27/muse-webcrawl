Ecumenical council

An ecumenical council, also called a general council, is a meeting of bishops and other church authorities to consider and rule on questions of Christian doctrine, administration, discipline, and other matters in which those entitled to vote are convoked from the whole world (oikoumene) and which secures the approbation of the whole Church.

An ecumenical council gathers bishops from across the Christian world.

The first seven councils

The first seven ecumenical councils, held between 325 and 787, are recognised by both the Eastern Orthodox Church and the Catholic Church, as well as by many Protestant churches. They are:

  • First Council of Nicaea (325) — condemned Arianism and produced the original Nicene Creed.
  • First Council of Constantinople (381) — expanded the Creed and condemned Apollinarism.
  • Council of Ephesus (431) — proclaimed Mary as Theotokos and condemned Nestorianism.
  • Council of Chalcedon (451) — defined Christ as one person in two natures.
  • Second Council of Constantinople (553) — addressed the Three Chapters controversy.
  • Third Council of Constantinople (680–681) — condemned monothelitism.
  • Second Council of Nicaea (787) — restored the veneration of icons.

Later councils

The Catholic Church recognises a total of twenty-one ecumenical councils, the most recent being the Second Vatican Council (1962–1965). The Council of Trent (1545–1563) was central to the Counter-Reformation. Eastern Orthodox churches generally recognise only the first seven, though some theologians speak of later synods, such as the councils of Constantinople of 879–880 and 1341–1351, as having ecumenical authority.

Authority and reception

The authority of a council depends on its reception by the churches. Disputes over which councils are truly ecumenical were a major factor in the schisms following Ephesus and Chalcedon, and in the East–West Schism of 1054.
