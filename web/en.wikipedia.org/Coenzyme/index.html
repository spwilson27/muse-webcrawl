Coenzyme

A coenzyme is an organic non-protein compound that binds with an enzyme to catalyse a reaction. Coenzymes are often broadly called cofactors, but chemically they are a subset: cofactors also include inorganic ions such as magnesium and zinc. Coenzymes are small molecules that cannot by themselves catalyse a reaction but help enzymes to do so; many are derived from vitamins or are made from nucleotides such as adenosine triphosphate (ATP).

Function

Coenzymes typically act as carriers of chemical groups, electrons, or hydrogen atoms between enzymes. For example, NAD+ and NADP+ carry electrons and hydrogen in redox reactions, coenzyme A carries acyl groups, tetrahydrofolate carries one-carbon units, and ATP transfers phosphate groups. Unlike substrates, many coenzymes are regenerated within the cell and are needed only in small, catalytic amounts. A coenzyme that is tightly or permanently bound to its enzyme is often called a prosthetic group; the protein part of such an enzyme without its cofactor is the apoenzyme, and together they form the holoenzyme.

Vitamin-derived coenzymes

Many coenzymes are derived from B vitamins: thiamine (B1) gives thiamine pyrophosphate, riboflavin (B2) gives FAD and FMN, niacin (B3) gives NAD+, pantothenic acid (B5) gives coenzyme A, and folate (B9) gives tetrahydrofolate. This is one reason these vitamins are essential nutrients: organisms cannot synthesize the coenzymes without them.

History

The first coenzyme to be identified was NAD+, discovered by Arthur Harden and William Young in 1906 during studies of alcoholic fermentation, when they showed that a heat-stable, dialysable fraction ("coferment") was required alongside enzymes for fermentation to proceed.
