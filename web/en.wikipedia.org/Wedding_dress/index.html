Wedding dress

A wedding dress (or bridal gown) is the dress worn by the bride during a wedding ceremony. The colour, style and ceremonial importance of the gown can depend on the religion and culture of the wedding participants. In many Western cultures, the wedding dress is commonly white, a tradition popularized by Queen Victoria, who wore a white lace gown when she married Prince Albert in 1840.

History

Before the Victorian era, a Western bride might marry in any colour; black was especially popular in Scandinavia. The white dress was not intended to symbolize virginity but rather wealth and status: white garments were difficult to clean and could be worn only once. Mary, Queen of Scots, wore a white wedding gown in 1559 when she married her first husband, Francis, the Dauphin of France, because white was her favourite colour, although the colour was then the official colour of mourning for French queens.

During the economic struggles of the Great Depression and the Second World War, many brides married in their best dress or in a suit. After the war, elaborate white gowns designed by houses such as Dior became the standard for prosperous brides.

Styles

Common silhouettes include the A-line, ball gown, mermaid (or trumpet), sheath and empire waist. Necklines range from strapless sweetheart styles to high illusion necklines. Gowns are often made from satin, lace, chiffon, organza or tulle, and are traditionally accompanied by a veil and a bouquet.

Non-Western traditions

In China and India, brides traditionally wear red, a colour associated with luck, prosperity and happiness. Japanese brides may wear a white kimono (shiromuku) for the ceremony and change into a coloured kimono for the reception. Korean brides wear the colourful hanbok. In many Middle Eastern and South Asian ceremonies, the bride's hands and feet are decorated with henna (mehndi).
