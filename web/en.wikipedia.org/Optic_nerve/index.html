Optic nerve

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The optic nerve, also known as the second cranial nerve (CN II), is a paired nerve that transmits visual information from the retina to the brain. Each optic nerve contains roughly 1.2 million axons of retinal ganglion cells. Strictly speaking it is not a peripheral nerve but a tract of the central nervous system, myelinated by oligodendrocytes, and it develops as an outgrowth of the diencephalon.

Structure

The nerve leaves the orbit through the optic canal and travels backwards to the optic chiasm, where fibres from the nasal retina cross to the opposite side; the signals continue via the optic tracts to the lateral geniculate nucleus of the thalamus and then to the visual cortex. Its point of exit from the eyeball creates the blind spot of the retina, where there are no photoreceptors.

Clinical significance

Disorders of the optic nerve cause characteristic visual field defects. Optic neuritis, often associated with multiple sclerosis, causes painful loss of central vision; glaucoma damages the nerve head; pituitary tumours pressing on the chiasm produce bitemporal hemianopia; and swelling of the optic disc (papilloedema) is a sign of raised intracranial pressure.
