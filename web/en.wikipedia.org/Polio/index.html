Polio

Poliomyelitis, commonly shortened to polio, is an infectious disease caused by the poliovirus. Approximately 75% of cases are asymptomatic; mild symptoms which can occur include sore throat and fever; in a proportion of cases more severe symptoms develop such as headache, neck stiffness, and paresthesia. These symptoms usually pass within one or two weeks. In about 0.5% of cases a less common symptom is permanent paralysis of the muscles, usually in the legs; in extreme cases this can extend to paralysis of the diaphragm.

Cause and transmission

Polio is caused by poliovirus types 1, 2 and 3, enteroviruses spread mainly by the faecal–oral route. The virus infects the throat and intestines and can invade the central nervous system, destroying motor neurons.

Prevention

Two vaccines are used worldwide: the inactivated polio vaccine (IPV) developed by Jonas Salk (introduced 1955) and the oral polio vaccine (OPV) developed by Albert Sabin (introduced 1961). The Global Polio Eradication Initiative, launched in 1988, reduced cases by over 99%; wild poliovirus type 2 was declared eradicated in 2015 and type 3 in 2019, leaving type 1 endemic only in Afghanistan and Pakistan.

Treatment

There is no cure; treatment is supportive, including pain relief, antibiotics for secondary infections, and ventilatory support (historically the iron lung) for respiratory paralysis. Post-polio syndrome can affect survivors decades later.
