Deglazing (cooking)

Deglazing is a cooking technique for removing and dissolving browned food residue from a pan in order to flavour a sauce, gravy or soup. The browned residue, known in French as sucs, forms on the bottom of a pan after meat or vegetables have been seared or roasted, and is rich in flavour compounds produced by the Maillard reaction and caramelisation.

Method

After the main ingredient is removed from the pan, most excess fat is poured off. A liquid — commonly wine, stock, beer, spirits such as cognac, or even water — is added to the hot pan. The heat causes the liquid to simmer and loosen the residue, and the cook scrapes the pan bottom with a wooden spoon or spatula to dissolve it. The resulting liquid is then reduced, seasoned and often finished with butter or cream to form a pan sauce.

Uses

Deglazing is fundamental to many classic French sauces and is used in dishes such as coq au vin, steak Diane and various gravies. When spirits are used and ignited, the technique overlaps with flambéing. The choice of liquid influences the final flavour: acidic liquids such as wine also help to cut the richness of a fatty dish, while stock gives a more neutral base.
