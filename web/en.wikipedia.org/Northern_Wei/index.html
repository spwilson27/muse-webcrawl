Northern Wei

The Northern Wei (386–535 CE) was an imperial dynasty of China ruled by the Tuoba clan of the Xianbei people. Founded by Tuoba Gui (Emperor Daowu), it unified northern China in 439, ending the chaotic Sixteen Kingdoms period, and was the first and longest-lived of the Northern dynasties during China's Northern and Southern dynasties period.

History

The Tuoba, a branch of the Xianbei, emerged from the steppe and established their state in the north. By 439 Emperor Taiwu had conquered the remaining rival states of the north. The dynasty is noted for its patronage of Buddhism — the great cave temples of Yungang near the capital Pingcheng and of Longmen near Luoyang date from this era — and for periods of persecution of Buddhism as well, notably under Taiwu.

Sinicization

Emperor Xiaowen (reigned 471–499) pursued a sweeping programme of sinicization: in 494 he moved the capital from Pingcheng to Luoyang, ordered the court to adopt Han Chinese dress, language and surnames — the imperial clan changed its name from Tuoba to Yuan — and promoted intermarriage and Chinese administrative practice. The equal-field land system (juntian) introduced under his reign influenced Chinese land policy for centuries.

Decline and division

The reforms alienated the semi-nomadic garrisons of the northern frontier, whose rebellions (the Revolt of the Six Garrisons, from 523) plunged the dynasty into civil war. In 534–535 the empire split into the rival Eastern Wei and Western Wei, controlled by the warlords Gao Huan and Yuwen Tai respectively.
