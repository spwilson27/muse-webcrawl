Mob rule

Mob rule or ochlocracy (from Greek ochlos, "mob") is a form of government or social control in which political decisions and public order are dictated by an unruly crowd rather than by law or constituted authority. The term is pejorative and is often contrasted with the rule of law and constitutional democracy.

Concept and history

Polybius, in his theory of anacyclosis, described ochlocracy as the corrupt degeneration of democracy, in which popular government decays into domination by mob sentiment, eventually inviting a tyrant to restore order. Similar warnings appear in Plato and Aristotle. Thinkers of the Enlightenment and the American Founding Fathers drew on this tradition: James Madison argued in The Federalist that representative institutions and an extended republic could refine public passions and prevent factional mobs from seizing direct control.

Usage

"Mob rule" is used both descriptively — for episodes such as lynchings, pogroms, or riots in which crowds impose their will by intimidation — and rhetorically, as a criticism of majoritarian politics or direct democracy measures that opponents believe bypass deliberation and minority rights. Political scientists note that the term is frequently contested: what one side calls mob rule, another may call popular uprising or mass protest, depending on the legitimacy they grant the crowd's grievances and methods.
