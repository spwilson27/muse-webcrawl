The staff of Hermes, more commonly known as the caduceus (Latin: cādūceus, from Greek κηρύκειον kērū́keion, "herald's staff"), is the staff carried by Hermes in Greek mythology and by Mercury in Roman mythology. It is a short staff entwined by two serpents, sometimes surmounted by wings.

Mythology

In Greek myth, Hermes received the staff from Apollo, in exchange for the lyre which Hermes had invented from a tortoise shell. According to one account, Hermes used the staff to separate two fighting snakes, which then wrapped themselves around it in harmony, giving the staff its characteristic appearance. The serpents were sometimes said to be gifts marking the staff's role in reconciling opposites.

Symbolism

The caduceus is associated with Hermes' roles as herald and messenger of the gods, guide of the dead, and protector of merchants, shepherds, liars, thieves and travellers. In Roman times it became a symbol of commerce and negotiation, since Mercury was the god of trade. The winged top represents Hermes' speed and role as a messenger between the divine and mortal worlds.

Confusion with the Rod of Asclepius

The caduceus is frequently confused with the Rod of Asclepius, the symbol of the Greek god of medicine, which features a single serpent entwined around a rough staff without wings. Despite this, the caduceus has been widely adopted by medical organisations, particularly in North America, while the Rod of Asclepius is the emblem used by the World Health Organization.

Modern usage

The caduceus appears in heraldry, in the insignia of customs services and tax authorities in several countries, and as a Unicode symbol (☤, U+2624). Its association with commerce survives in its use by chambers of commerce and commercial publishers.
