Cholera toxin

OrganismVibrio cholerae
TypeAB5 enterotoxin
TargetIntestinal epithelial cells
EffectMassive fluid secretion

Cholera toxin is an enterotoxin secreted by the bacterium Vibrio cholerae, the causative agent of cholera. It is responsible for the profuse watery diarrhoea ("rice-water stool") characteristic of the disease, which can cause life-threatening dehydration within hours.

Structure

The toxin is an AB5 multimeric protein: a single enzymatically active A subunit sits atop a ring of five B subunits. The B subunits bind to GM1 ganglioside receptors on the surface of intestinal epithelial cells, allowing the A subunit to enter the cell.

Mechanism of action

Inside the cell, the A subunit transfers an ADP-ribose group onto the Gs alpha subunit of a heterotrimeric G protein, locking it in its active state. This permanently activates adenylate cyclase, raising intracellular cyclic AMP levels. The resulting activation of CFTR chloride channels and inhibition of sodium absorption drives a massive outpouring of water and electrolytes into the intestinal lumen.

Uses in research

Because of its specific binding and transport properties, cholera toxin and its B subunit are widely used as neuronal tracers and as tools in cell biology.
