Scratch Orchestra

The Scratch Orchestra was a British experimental music ensemble founded in 1969 by the composers Cornelius Cardew, Michael Parsons and Howard Skempton. Open to anyone regardless of musical training, it embodied Cardew's belief that music-making should be a democratic, participatory activity, and it became a landmark in the history of British experimental and improvised music.

Foundation and methods

The orchestra grew out of Cardew's experimental composition class at Morley College in London. Its draft constitution, published in the Musical Times in June 1969, established an ensemble in which trained musicians and complete beginners ("scratch" performers) worked together. Activities were organised into categories that included improvisation rites, scratch compositions written by members, performances of experimental classics (by composers such as John Cage, Christian Wolff and La Monte Young), and research projects. Notation often took the form of verbal instructions or graphic scores rather than conventional staves, so that no formal training was needed to take part.

The orchestra's membership at times approached fifty. It gave concerts in venues ranging from concert halls to village greens, toured the United Kingdom, and famously travelled by train to perform in communities around the country. Recordings include The Great Learning (1971), Cardew's large-scale work based on texts of Confucius, written for the orchestra's mixed abilities.

Politics and dissolution

In the early 1970s several leading members, Cardew among them, moved toward Maoist politics, and ideological disputes split the group: a faction sought to subordinate musical activity to political struggle, while others defended the orchestra's original pluralism. The Scratch Orchestra effectively disbanded in the mid-1970s, though its influence on British improvised music, community music and participatory performance has been lasting. Retrospective histories and archive releases, and revivals of its repertoire, have continued into the twenty-first century.
