Bede

Bede (Old English: Bēda; c. 673 – 26 May 735), also known as the Venerable Bede (Latin: Beda Venerabilis), was an English Benedictine monk at the twin monastery of St Peter's at Wearmouth and St Paul's at Jarrow in the kingdom of Northumbria. He is best known as the author of the Ecclesiastical History of the English People (Historia ecclesiastica gentis Anglorum, completed in 731), which earned him the title "the father of English history". In 1899 Pope Leo XIII declared him a Doctor of the Church; he is the only native of Great Britain so designated.

Life

Almost everything known of Bede's life comes from the brief autobiographical note at the end of the Ecclesiastical History. Given to the monastery at the age of seven as an oblate, he spent the rest of his life there — "applying myself entirely to the study of Scripture" — becoming a deacon at nineteen and a priest at thirty. He survived a plague that devastated Jarrow in the 680s. His pupils included Cuthbert, whose letter describing Bede's death — dictating a translation of the Gospel of John into Old English to the last — is a famous document in its own right. Bede remarked that he knew Latin, Greek and some Hebrew, an unusual breadth for his age.

Works

Bede wrote over sixty works in Latin: biblical commentaries, homilies, saints' lives (including prose and verse lives of St Cuthbert), hymns, and scientific treatises. His works on chronology, especially De temporum ratione (The Reckoning of Time, 725), popularised the practice of dating years from the birth of Christ — Anno Domini dating — throughout Western Europe, and clarified the calculation of the date of Easter. He also discussed the tides, correctly connecting them to the moon, and accepted the spherical shape of the Earth.

The Ecclesiastical History

The Historia ecclesiastica gentis Anglorum, dedicated to King Ceolwulf of Northumbria, narrates the conversion of the English kingdoms to Christianity from the mission of St Augustine in 597 to Bede's own day. Drawing on documents, oral tradition and the works of earlier writers such as Gildas, Bede named many of his sources and distinguished report from record with unusual care for his period. It remains the single most important source for early Anglo-Saxon history, and preserves such episodes as the Synod of Whitby (664) and the story of Caedmon, the first named English poet.

Veneration

Bede's remains were translated to Durham Cathedral in the 11th century, where his shrine stands in the Galilee Chapel. His title "Venerable", attached to his name since the early Middle Ages, was formally confirmed when he was canonised by recognition. He is commemorated on 25 May in the Roman Catholic, Anglican and Orthodox calendars.
