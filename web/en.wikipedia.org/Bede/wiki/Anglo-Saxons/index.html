Anglo-Saxons

Periodc. 450 – 1066
RegionEngland, southern Scotland
LanguagesOld English, Latin
ReligionsGermanic paganism, then Christianity

The Anglo-Saxons were a cultural group that inhabited England from the 5th century after the end of Roman Britain. They comprised Germanic settlers — principally Angles, Saxons and Jutes — who migrated from the North Sea coastlands of Europe, together with Britons who adopted their culture, and they spoke dialects that developed into Old English.

History

Following the Roman withdrawal around 410, settlers established kingdoms including Northumbria, Mercia, East Anglia, Kent and Wessex, traditionally grouped as the Heptarchy. The conversion to Christianity, begun by Augustine's mission of 597, produced the scholarship of Bede and Alcuin and a rich literary culture, including the epic Beowulf and the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle. From the late 8th century, Viking raids and settlement created the Danelaw, until Alfred the Great and his successors reconquered Danish territory and forged a unified kingdom of England in the 10th century.

End and legacy

Anglo-Saxon England ended with the Norman Conquest of 1066, when William of Normandy defeated Harold Godwinson at Hastings. Yet Anglo-Saxon institutions — shires, hundreds, the common-law tradition and much of the English language's core vocabulary — endured beneath Norman rule. See also Alcuin and Anno Domini.
