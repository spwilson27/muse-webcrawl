Northumbria was an early medieval English kingdom in what is now northern England and south-east Scotland, formed from the union of the Anglian territories of Bernicia and Deira under King Æthelfrith around 604 and unified formally in 654. It took its name from its position north of the river Humber.

Golden age

In the 7th and early 8th centuries Northumbria was the leading power in Britain and a centre of learning and art. Kings Edwin, Oswald and Oswiu extended its overlordship, while monasteries at Lindisfarne, Wearmouth–Jarrow and York produced Bede, the Lindisfarne Gospels and the scholar Alcuin. The Synod of Whitby (664) aligned the Northumbrian church with Roman practice.

Decline and end

Internal strife and Viking assault weakened the kingdom from the late 8th century; in 867 the Great Heathen Army captured York and southern Northumbria became part of the Danelaw as the Norse kingdom of Jórvík. The northern rump survived under earls at Bamburgh until the kingdom's final absorption into England in 954, and the name survives in the modern region of North East England.
