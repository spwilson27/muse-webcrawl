Anno Domini

Devised byDionysius Exiguus, 525
EpochTraditional year of the Incarnation of Jesus
CounterpartBC ("Before Christ"); also styled CE/BCE
Popularised byBede, 8th century

The terms Anno Domini (AD, Latin for "in the year of the Lord") and Before Christ (BC) label years in the Julian and Gregorian calendars. The AD era begins with the year traditionally reckoned as the conception or birth of Jesus of Nazareth; years before it are counted backwards as BC.

Origins

The system was devised in 525 by the Scythian monk Dionysius Exiguus as part of his Easter tables, replacing the Diocletian era. Dionysius placed the Incarnation in the year later numbered 1, but apparently miscalculated: most scholars date the birth of Jesus to between 6 and 4 BC. There is no year zero — 1 BC is followed directly by AD 1.

Spread

The era spread through the works of the Northumbrian scholar Bede, whose Ecclesiastical History of the English People (731) dated events AD and BC-style, and it gradually became the standard year-numbering of Western Christendom. In modern academic use the religiously neutral equivalents CE ("Common Era") and BCE are often substituted, with identical numbering. See also Alcuin, Anglo-Saxons and Northumbria.
