Alcuin of York (c. 735 – 19 May 804) was an English scholar, clergyman, poet and teacher from York, Northumbria. He was the foremost intellectual of the Carolingian Renaissance, invited by Charlemagne to lead the palace school at Aachen and to direct the revival of learning across the Frankish empire.

Life

Educated at York's cathedral school, Alcuin became its master and built its library into one of the finest in Europe. Meeting Charlemagne at Parma in 781, he accepted the king's invitation to court. At Aachen he taught the royal family and a generation of Frankish churchmen, advised on education and liturgy, and corresponded with scholars across Europe — some 300 of his letters survive. In 796 he became abbot of St Martin's Abbey at Tours, where he developed the clear script that evolved into Carolingian minuscule and produced revised texts of the Bible.

Works and legacy

Alcuin wrote textbooks on grammar, rhetoric, dialectic and arithmetic (including the famous river-crossing problems in the Propositiones ad acuendos iuvenes), theological treatises, saints' lives and poetry. His reforms standardised handwriting and education across the empire and preserved much classical learning. See also Carolingian Renaissance and Northumbria.
