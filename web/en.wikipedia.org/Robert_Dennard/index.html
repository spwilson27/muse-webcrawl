Robert Heath Dennard (5 September 1932 – 23 April 2024) was an American electrical engineer and inventor. Working at IBM, he invented dynamic random-access memory (DRAM) in 1966, for which he was granted a patent in 1968; the single-transistor, single-capacitor memory cell he devised remains the basis of virtually all DRAM used in computers today.

Dennard scaling

In a landmark 1974 paper with colleagues, Dennard described how MOSFET transistors behave when scaled down: if transistor dimensions and supply voltage shrink by the same factor, power density stays constant while switching speed improves. This principle, known as Dennard scaling (or MOSFET scaling), underpinned decades of progress predicted alongside Moore's law, as chips became both denser and faster without increasing power per unit area. The breakdown of Dennard scaling in the mid-2000s, owing to leakage currents, led to the "power wall" and the industry's shift to multicore processors.

Life and honours

Dennard was raised in rural Texas, studied electrical engineering at Southern Methodist University (BS 1954, MS 1956), and earned his PhD from the Carnegie Institute of Technology in 1958 before joining IBM's research division. He received the National Medal of Technology in 2009, the IEEE Medal of Honor, the Charles Stark Draper Prize, and the Kyoto Prize in 2013.
