Shakers

The Shakers, formally the United Society of Believers in Christ's Second Appearing, are a millenarian Christian sect founded in England in the mid-18th century and established in America in 1774 under the leadership of Ann Lee ("Mother Ann"), whom followers regarded as the female manifestation of Christ's second coming.

Beliefs and practices

Shakers practiced celibacy, communal living, pacifism, and equality of the sexes and races. Their name derives from the ecstatic movements of their worship, which included marching, dancing, and shaking. Communities organized into "families" lived in sex-segregated dormitories and worked communally in farming and crafts.

Craftsmanship and decline

Shaker furniture, boxes, architecture, and inventions — including the circular saw blade design credited to Tabitha Babbitt — are celebrated for functional simplicity under the maxim "Hands to work, and hearts to God" and the principle that beauty rests in utility. Membership peaked around 6,000 in the mid-19th century; because celibacy required growth by conversion and adoption, numbers declined through the 20th century. A small community remains active at Sabbathday Lake, Maine.
