Countervalue

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In nuclear strategy, countervalue is the targeting of an opponent's assets that are of value but not actually a military threat, such as cities and civilian populations. Counterforce, by contrast, is the targeting of an opponent's military forces and facilities. The terms were elaborated in the 1960s as analysts distinguished between strikes meant to destroy an enemy's ability to wage war and strikes meant to impose unacceptable costs on its society.

Doctrine

A countervalue strategy threatens what the adversary's leadership values — its population, industrial base, and economy — in order to deter attack. It underlies the concept of mutually assured destruction (MAD): if both sides retain survivable forces able to devastate the other's cities after absorbing a first strike, neither can rationally initiate nuclear war. United States policy under Secretary of Defense Robert McNamara in the early 1960s publicly emphasised counterforce "no-cities" targeting, but the operational war plans (the SIOP) always retained massive countervalue options.

Cold War and beyond

Soviet doctrine, while officially disavowing the deliberate targeting of civilians, planned for comprehensive strikes against military, industrial, and urban targets. Arms control theorists debated the morality and stability of countervalue threats: critics argued that threatening mass civilian destruction is both immoral and, in a crisis, incredible, since carrying it out after deterrence had failed would serve no purpose; defenders replied that the threat need only be believed in advance to deter.

China's declared minimum-deterrence posture has traditionally relied on a small survivable force capable of countervalue retaliation against a handful of cities. India similarly frames its deterrent around massive retaliation. France's force de frappe was built around the threat of "tearing an arm" from a stronger adversary by striking its cities.
