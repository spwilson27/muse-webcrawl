Precambrian

The Precambrian (or Pre-Cambrian, sometimes abbreviated pЄ, or Cryptozoic) is the earliest part of Earth's history, set before the current Phanerozoic Eon. The Precambrian is so named because it preceded the Cambrian, the first period of the Phanerozoic Eon, which is named after Cambria, the Latinised name for Wales, where rocks from this age were first studied. The Precambrian accounts for 88% of the Earth's geologic time, spanning from the formation of the Earth about 4.54 billion years ago to the beginning of the Cambrian Period, 538.8 million years ago.

Overview

The Precambrian is divided into three eons: the Hadean (4.54–4 billion years ago), the Archean (4–2.5 billion years ago) and the Proterozoic (2.5 billion–538.8 million years ago). Relatively little is known about the Precambrian compared with the Phanerozoic, because Precambrian rocks have often been heavily metamorphosed, deeply buried, or destroyed by plate tectonics; the fossil record is also sparse because most organisms lacked hard parts.

Life

Life originated during the early Archean, with the oldest widely accepted evidence of bacterial life dating to roughly 3.5–3.8 billion years ago. Photosynthesis by cyanobacteria gradually oxygenated the atmosphere during the Great Oxidation Event around 2.4 billion years ago, transforming the planet's chemistry and making aerobic life possible. Eukaryotic cells — with nuclei and organelles acquired through endosymbiosis — appeared by about 2 billion years ago. Multicellular life became conspicuous only near the end of the Proterozoic, with the soft-bodied Ediacaran biota appearing after about 635 million years ago.

Climate and continents

The Precambrian saw the growth of the first continental cratons, the assembly and breakup of supercontinents such as Rodinia, and extreme climate episodes including the Huronian glaciation and the "Snowball Earth" glaciations of the Cryogenian Period, when ice may have reached the equator.
