Royal icing

Royal icing is a hard white icing made by beating together egg whites (or meringue powder) and finely ground icing (confectioners') sugar, sometimes with a little lemon juice or glycerine. It dries to a smooth, hard, matte finish, and is the traditional medium for decorating wedding cakes, Christmas cakes, gingerbread houses and biscuits (cookies).

Properties and use

Because it sets rock-hard, royal icing is used where structure and fine detail are required: piped lace, run-out collars, string work, extension work and the assembly of gingerbread houses. Consistency is adjusted for the task — stiff peaks for piping flowers and borders, a thinner "flood" consistency for filling outlined shapes on biscuits ("flooding"). The name is traditionally explained by its use on the wedding cake of Queen Victoria and Prince Albert in 1840, an elaborately white-iced cake that popularised both royal icing and the white wedding cake. Elaborate Victorian exhibitions of piped royal-icing work, by confectioners such as the French pâtissier Antonin Carême's successors in London, made it a showcase skill of professional sugarcraft.

Safety and alternatives

Because raw egg white carries a small risk of Salmonella, modern recipes commonly substitute pasteurised egg white or meringue powder. Royal icing should not be used on cakes intended to be cut with difficulty-free serving unless softened slightly with glycerine, which prevents it setting completely hard.
