Armenian language

Armenian (endonym: հայերեն, hayeren) is an Indo-European language and the only member of its independent branch of that family. It is the official language of Armenia and is spoken by the Armenian diaspora worldwide, with several million speakers. Armenian is written in its own alphabet, created in 405 AD by the monk Mesrop Mashtots.

History

Armenian's earliest written form, Classical Armenian (Grabar), dates from the 5th century AD, when the Bible was translated into Armenian following the invention of the alphabet — the beginning of a rich literary tradition often called a golden age. Middle Armenian developed from the 11th to 17th centuries. Modern Armenian has two standardised literary forms, Eastern Armenian (spoken in Armenia and Iran) and Western Armenian (historically spoken in Anatolia and now in the diaspora), which differ in pronunciation and some grammar and spelling conventions; a spelling reform in Soviet Armenia in the 1920s further separated the two orthographies.

Linguistic features

Armenian has a distinctive consonant system with three-way contrasts in stops and affricates, no grammatical gender, seven noun cases, and a rich system of agglutinative verb conjugation. Its vocabulary contains many loanwords from Iranian languages, reflecting centuries of contact, as well as borrowings from Greek, Syriac, Turkish, Russian, and French. Because of extensive sound changes, the Indo-European origins of many Armenian words were long obscured, and the language's place in the family was firmly established only through the work of Heinrich Hübschmann in the 1870s.
