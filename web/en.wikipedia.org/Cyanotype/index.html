Cyanotype

The cyanotype (from Ancient Greek kyanos, "dark blue", and typos, "mark, impression") is a slow-reacting, economical photographic printing formulation sensitive to a limited near-ultraviolet and blue light spectrum. It produces a monochrome, blue-coloured print on a range of supports, and is often used for art and reprography in the form of blueprints.

History

The cyanotype process was developed by the English scientist and astronomer Sir John Herschel in 1842, shortly after photography was announced in 1839. Herschel was searching for a means of copying his notes and diagrams. The botanist Anna Atkins used the process to produce the first photographically illustrated book, Photographs of British Algae: Cyanotype Impressions (1843), pre-dating William Henry Fox Talbot's The Pencil of Nature.

Process

The process uses two chemicals in solution: ammonium iron(III) citrate and potassium ferricyanide. A support such as paper or cloth is coated with the mixed solution and dried in the dark. Objects or a negative are placed on the sensitized material and exposed to ultraviolet light; the exposed areas reduce ferric iron to ferrous iron, which reacts with the ferricyanide to form insoluble ferric ferrocyanide — the pigment Prussian blue. Unexposed salts wash away in water, leaving a white image on a blue ground.

Uses

Cyanotype became the standard method for reproducing technical drawings, giving the word "blueprint" its meaning, and remained in use into the twentieth century. It is still popular with artists and photographers as an alternative process, prized for its simplicity, low cost, and deep blue tones. Large-format engineering drawings, botanical photograms, and fine-art prints are common applications.

Toning and variations

Finished cyanotypes can be toned with substances such as tannic acid or tea to shift the blue toward brown, black, or purple. Modern variants replace the classic formula with faster or single-solution chemistry, and digital negatives have widened the tonal control available to printers.
