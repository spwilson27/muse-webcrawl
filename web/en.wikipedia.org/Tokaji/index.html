Tokaji (Hungarian: tokaji, "of Tokaj") is the name of the wines from the Tokaj wine region in north-eastern Hungary and adjacent parts of Slovakia. The region is famous above all for Tokaji Aszú, a sweet wine made from grapes affected by noble rot (Botrytis cinerea), which for centuries was prized at the courts of Europe — Louis XIV of France is said to have called it "the king of wines, the wine of kings". Tokaj was one of the world's first delimited wine appellations, classified by royal decree in the 1730s, and its cultural landscape is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Terroir and grapes

Vineyards lie on volcanic soils in the foothills of the Zemplén Mountains, where autumn mists from the Tisza and Bodrog rivers encourage noble rot, followed by dry afternoons that concentrate the shrivelled aszú berries. The principal grape is Furmint, valued for high acidity and thick skins, alongside Hárslevelű and Sárgamuskotály (Yellow Muscat); permitted varieties also include Zéta, Kövérszőlő and Kabar.

Aszú production

Aszú berries are picked individually and collected in hods called puttony. Traditionally, a number of puttonyos (3 to 6) of aszú paste were added to a gönci cask of base wine or must to ferment, the number indicating the wine's sweetness; since 2013 the classification has been simplified, with Aszú defined by a minimum residual sugar of 120 g/L and aged at least three years, including 18 months in barrel. The cellars of the region, carved into volcanic rock and lined with the cellar mould Cladosporium cellare, maintain the cool, humid conditions for ageing. The rare Eszencia, made from the free-run juice of aszú berries, is among the sweetest and longest-lived wines in the world.

Other styles and history

Tokaj also produces dry Furmint, Szamorodni (made from whole bunches, dry or sweet), late-harvest wines and Fordítás and Máslás. The region's golden age ran from the 17th to the 19th century, when Tokaji was exported across Europe; phylloxera, two world wars, the division of the region by the Treaty of Trianon and communist-era collectivisation damaged its reputation, but investment after 1990 restored quality and international standing. A long-running dispute with Slovakia over use of the Tokaj name was settled under EU wine law, with a small Slovak Tokaj region recognised.
