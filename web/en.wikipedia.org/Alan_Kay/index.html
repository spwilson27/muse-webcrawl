Alan Curtis Kay (born 17 May 1940) is an American computer scientist best known for his pioneering work on object-oriented programming and graphical user interfaces, and for conceiving the Dynabook, a vision of a portable personal computer for children that anticipated laptops and tablets.

Career

Kay earned his doctorate at the University of Utah in 1969, where he encountered Ivan Sutherland's Sketchpad and Douglas Engelbart's work. At Xerox PARC in the 1970s, he led the Learning Research Group, which developed Smalltalk — one of the first and most influential object-oriented programming languages — and contributed to the Alto personal computer with its bitmapped display, overlapping windows, icons and Ethernet networking. He later worked at Atari, Apple (as an Apple Fellow), Walt Disney Imagineering, and HP Labs, and founded the Viewpoints Research Institute.

Ideas

Kay coined the aphorism "The best way to predict the future is to invent it." He popularised the term "object-oriented programming" and defined it around messaging, encapsulation and late binding — later remarking that in inventing the term he "didn't have C++ in mind." He has been a persistent critic of how personal computing developed and an advocate of computers as a medium for learning, influenced by Seymour Papert's constructionism.

Honours

Kay received the ACM Turing Award in 2003 "for pioneering many of the ideas at the root of contemporary object-oriented programming languages" and for contributions to personal computing, as well as the Kyoto Prize in 2004.
