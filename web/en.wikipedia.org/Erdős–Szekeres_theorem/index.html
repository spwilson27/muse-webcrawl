Erdős–Szekeres theorem

Among any sufficiently long sequence of distinct numbers there is a long increasing or decreasing subsequence

In mathematics, the Erdős–Szekeres theorem asserts that, given r and s, any sequence of distinct real numbers of length at least (r − 1)(s − 1) + 1 contains a monotonically increasing subsequence of length r or a monotonically decreasing subsequence of length s. The theorem was proved by Paul Erdős and George Szekeres in a 1935 paper.

Proof

One elegant proof assigns to each term of the sequence a pair (a, b), where a is the length of the longest increasing subsequence ending at that term and b is the length of the longest decreasing subsequence ending there. Distinct terms receive distinct pairs, because if one term precedes and is smaller than another their a values differ, and if it is larger their b values differ. If no increasing subsequence has length r and no decreasing subsequence has length s, there are only (r − 1)(s − 1) possible pairs, so the sequence can have at most that many terms.

Interpretation and variants

The result is an early and prototypical example of Ramsey theory: complete disorder is impossible, and large enough structures inevitably contain ordered substructures. For the same pair of mathematicians, the Erdős–Szekeres conjecture concerning convex polygons in point sets — the "happy ending problem" — asks for the minimum number of points in general position guaranteeing a convex n-gon.

Applications

Monotone subsequence results underpin the Dilworth and Mirsky theorems on partially ordered sets, patience sorting, and the analysis of algorithms related to longest increasing subsequences. Variants of the theorem consider sequences in higher dimensions, permutations, and geometric point sets.
