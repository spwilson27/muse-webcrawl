Moscow Actionism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Moscow Actionism (Russian: Московский акционизм) is a radical performance-art movement that emerged in Moscow in the early 1990s, in the aftermath of the collapse of the Soviet Union. The term was coined by critic Viktor Misiano to describe a wave of provocative public actions by artists who treated the streets and media spaces of the new Russia as their medium. It is related to, but distinct from, the earlier Moscow Conceptualism of the 1970s and 1980s.

Characteristics

Actionist performances were deliberately scandalous, transgressive and media-oriented: the artists sought not only to perform an act but to generate its documentation and press coverage. Key figures include Oleg Kulik, who performed as a dog chained and barking at gallery visitors and passers-by (from 1994); Alexander Brener, who in 1997 sprayed a green dollar sign on a Malevich painting in Amsterdam's Stedelijk Museum; Avdei Ter-Oganyan; Anatoly Osmolovsky; and the groups E.T.I. (Expropriation of the Territory of Art) and Neceziudik.

Notable actions

In 1991 the E.T.I. group lay down in Red Square to spell out a three-letter Russian obscenity with their bodies in front of Lenin's Mausoleum — an act that coincided with a new law against foul language in public places and became an emblem of the era.

Legacy

Moscow Actionism is generally considered to have peaked in the 1990s, giving way in the 2000s and 2010s to a new generation of activist art collectives such as Voina and Pussy Riot, whose trial in 2012 drew international attention.
