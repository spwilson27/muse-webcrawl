Bone china

Bone china is a type of soft-paste porcelain that is composed of bone ash, feldspathic material, and kaolin. It has been defined as "ware with a translucent body" containing a minimum of 30 per cent of phosphate derived from animal bone and calculated calcium phosphate. Developed by the English potter Josiah Spode in the 1790s, bone china is known for its high levels of whiteness and translucency, and its high mechanical strength and chip resistance, which allows it to be produced in thinner cross-sections than other types of porcelain.

Fine bone china tableware is prized for its whiteness, translucency and strength.

History

European potters spent centuries trying to replicate Chinese hard-paste porcelain. Experiments with bone ash in England began around 1748 (Thomas Frye at the Bow porcelain factory took out a patent using bone ash), but it was Josiah Spode II in Stoke-on-Trent who, in the 1790s, perfected and commercialised the standard recipe—about 50 per cent bone ash, 25 per cent china clay (kaolin), and 25 per cent china stone. The resulting ware dominated the English fine-china market, and "bone china" became a British speciality, made by firms such as Minton, Royal Crown Derby, Wedgwood, and Royal Doulton.

Production

Bone ash is produced by calcining cattle bones at around 1,000 °C to remove organic matter, yielding largely tricalcium phosphate and hydroxyapatite. The body is formed by slip casting, jiggering, or pressing, fired in a biscuit firing at up to about 1,280 °C, then glazed and glost-fired at a lower temperature; decoration may be applied by transfer printing, lithography, or hand-painting, often with gilding. Bone china's translucency comes from the glassy phase formed during firing.

Properties and use

Despite being thin and delicate in appearance, bone china has the highest mechanical strength and impact resistance of the porcelains, making it suitable for fine tableware in hotels, embassies, and royal households. British standards (and trade usage) require a minimum bone ash content; the term "fine bone china" typically indicates a higher proportion. Because it contains animal products, some consumers prefer alternatives such as hard-paste porcelain.
