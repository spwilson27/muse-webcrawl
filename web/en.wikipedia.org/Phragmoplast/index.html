Phragmoplast

The phragmoplast is a plant cell-specific structure that serves as a scaffold for cell plate assembly and subsequent formation of a new cell wall separating two daughter nuclei during cytokinesis. It consists mainly of microtubules, microfilaments, and elements of the endoplasmic reticulum, which assemble in two opposing sets perpendicular to the plane of the future cell plate.

The phragmoplast guides vesicles to the midline, where they fuse to build the cell plate.

Formation

During late anaphase and telophase of mitosis, the phragmoplast assembles from the remains of the spindle midzone. Golgi-derived vesicles carrying cell wall materials are transported along the microtubules to the equatorial plane, where they fuse to form the cell plate. The phragmoplast expands centrifugally — outward toward the parent cell wall — as the cell plate grows, until the two meet and fuse.

Function

The phragmoplast defines the position of the new cell wall. Its position is anticipated earlier in the cell cycle by the preprophase band, a ring of microtubules that marks the future division site. Once the growing cell plate reaches the parental wall, it fuses at the site previously occupied by the preprophase band.

Comparison with animal cytokinesis

Unlike animal cells, which divide by a contractile ring pinching the cell in two, plant cells possess rigid walls and must construct a new partition from the inside out. The phragmoplast is therefore a defining feature of cytokinesis in land plants and some green algae.
