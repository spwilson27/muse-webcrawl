The Tarim Basin is a large endorheic basin in Xinjiang, northwestern China, one of the largest drainage basins in the world with no outlet to the ocean. Ringed by the Tian Shan mountains to the north, the Pamirs to the west, and the Kunlun and Altun ranges to the south, its interior is occupied largely by the Taklamakan Desert, one of the world's largest sandy deserts.

Geography

Rivers fed by mountain snowmelt, above all the Tarim River, flow into the basin and historically ended in marshes and lakes such as Lop Nur, a shifting salt lake once used for Chinese nuclear tests. Along the desert margins, oasis cities — Kashgar, Khotan, Aksu, Turfan, and Kuqa — sustained agriculture and trade.

History

The basin was a crucial corridor of the Silk Road, whose northern and southern routes skirted the Taklamakan. Its inhabitants spoke Indo-European Tocharian languages as well as Iranian Saka languages, and Buddhism flourished in oasis kingdoms before the spread of Islam. The desiccated Tarim mummies, some dating to around 2000 BCE, and the manuscripts recovered by Aurel Stein and other explorers attest to the region's ancient cosmopolitan cultures. Today the population is largely Uyghur, and the basin contains major oil and gas fields.
