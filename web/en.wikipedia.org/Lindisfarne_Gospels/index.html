The Lindisfarne Gospels is an illuminated Latin gospel book produced around 700–725 AD in the monastery of Lindisfarne, off the coast of Northumbria in northern England. Now held by the British Library, it is one of the finest surviving works of Insular (Hiberno-Saxon) art, combining Mediterranean, Anglo-Saxon and Celtic decorative traditions. According to a colophon added in the 10th century, it was made in honour of God and St Cuthbert, traditionally by Eadfrith, bishop of Lindisfarne (698–721), and bound with a jewelled cover by Billfrith the anchorite.

Contents and decoration

The manuscript contains the four Gospels following Jerome's Vulgate, preceded by canon tables, prefatory letters and chapter lists. Each Gospel opens with a miniature of the evangelist, a "carpet page" of dense interlace ornament arranged around a cross, and a spectacular decorated incipit page in which the opening words dissolve into pattern. The script is a majestic Insular half-uncial, written on vellum made from roughly 150 calf skins.

The Old English gloss

Between 950 and 970, Aldred, provost of the community of St Cuthbert then at Chester-le-Street, added a word-for-word gloss in the Northumbrian dialect of Old English between the lines of the Latin text. It is the earliest surviving translation of the Gospels into English, and Aldred's colophon names the book's makers.

History

The book travelled with the community of St Cuthbert as it fled Viking raids in 875, eventually reaching Durham, whose cathedral held it for centuries. Its jewelled binding was lost during its wanderings (or, in another tradition, earlier). At the Dissolution of the Monasteries it passed into private hands and was acquired by Sir Robert Cotton; the Cotton library later formed part of the founding collections of the British Museum and then the British Library. Campaigns in the North East of England periodically call for the manuscript to be displayed, and occasionally permanently housed, in Durham.
