Rossby wave

Rossby waves, also called planetary waves, are large-scale meanders in high-altitude winds, such as the jet streams, and in ocean currents. They arise naturally in rotating fluids because of the variation of the Coriolis effect with latitude (the beta effect) and were first identified and explained by the Swedish-American meteorologist Carl-Gustaf Rossby in 1939.

Atmospheric Rossby waves

In the atmosphere, Rossby waves appear as the great north–south undulations of the mid-latitude westerlies, forming the ridges and troughs seen on upper-air weather charts. They typically have wavelengths of several thousand kilometres and drift slowly, usually westward relative to the mean flow. Because the pattern of ridges and troughs steers surface cyclones and anticyclones, Rossby waves govern much of day-to-day weather variability in the mid-latitudes. When the waves become nearly stationary and amplified, they can produce persistent "blocking" patterns associated with prolonged heat waves, cold spells, droughts, or flooding.

Oceanic Rossby waves

The oceans support Rossby waves as well. Barotropic and baroclinic oceanic Rossby waves propagate westward across ocean basins at speeds of a few kilometres per day, carrying signals of wind changes across entire oceans over months to years. They play an essential role in phenomena such as El Niño–Southern Oscillation, in western boundary current dynamics, and in the adjustment of the ocean to changing winds.

Physics

Rossby waves exist because a displaced fluid parcel experiences a restoring mechanism through conservation of potential vorticity: moving a parcel poleward increases the planetary vorticity it experiences, inducing relative vorticity that pushes it back, causing oscillation. The phase speed of a Rossby wave relative to the mean flow is westward and proportional to the latitudinal gradient of Coriolis parameter (β) divided by the square of the total wavenumber, so shorter waves travel more slowly. Rossby waves also occur in planetary atmospheres beyond Earth — they shape the circulation of Venus, Jupiter, and other planets — and in astrophysical and laboratory rotating fluids.
