Rebec

The rebec (sometimes rebecha, rebeckha, or in older English ribibe) is a bowed stringed instrument of the medieval and early Renaissance periods. It has a pear- or teardrop-shaped body carved from a single piece of wood, typically two or three (occasionally up to five) strings, and is played with a bow, held either under the chin, against the shoulder or chest, or resting on the lap.

History

The rebec derives from the Arabic rabāb, transmitted to Europe through both Byzantium and Islamic Iberia around the 10th century. It became one of the most popular instruments of the later Middle Ages, used by minstrels and jongleurs for dance music and song accompaniment, and it is frequently depicted in medieval art, often played by angels in religious paintings. From the 15th century the rebec gradually declined in courtly use as the viol and violin families rose, though it survived in folk traditions in parts of Europe. Its descendants and relatives include the Greek and Balkan lyra and various folk fiddles.

Construction and sound

The body, neck, and pegbox are carved from one block of wood, with a thin soundboard glued on top and small soundholes cut into it. Strings were of gut, tuned usually in fifths. The rebec's tone is bright, nasal, and penetrating, well suited to outdoor and dance performance, and surviving and reconstructed instruments are used today in early-music performance.
