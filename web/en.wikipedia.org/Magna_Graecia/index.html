Magna Graecia

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Magna Graecia (Latin for "Greater Greece"; Greek: Megalē Hellas) was the name given by the Romans to the coastal regions of southern Italy — in modern Calabria, Apulia, Basilicata, Campania and Sicily — that were extensively colonised by Greek settlers from the eighth century BCE onward. The colonies became wealthy, populous city-states that transmitted Greek language, coinage, architecture and philosophy to the peoples of Italy, including the rising power of Rome.

Colonisation

The earliest foundation was Cumae (c. 750 BCE), established by settlers from Euboea, followed by cities including Syracuse (733 BCE), Tarentum (706 BCE, by Sparta), Rhegium, Croton, Sybaris, Metapontum and Naples. Colonies were independent poleis, often rivalling their mother cities in wealth; Sybaris became proverbial for luxury. The region produced major figures of Greek culture: Pythagoras settled at Croton, Parmenides and Zeno founded the Eleatic school of philosophy, and Archimedes was a Syracusan.

Decline and Roman conquest

From the fourth century BCE the Greek cities, weakened by wars among themselves, against Carthage in Sicily, and against Italic peoples such as the Samnites and Lucanians, increasingly sought Roman help. Tarentum's appeal to Pyrrhus of Epirus (280–275 BCE) ended in Roman victory, and by 272 BCE all of Magna Graecia had passed under Roman control, although Greek language and culture persisted in the south for centuries — a Griko-speaking minority survives in Calabria and Apulia to this day.

Archaeological heritage

The region preserves some of the best Greek antiquities anywhere: the three Doric temples of Paestum, the temples of Agrigento's Valley of the Temples, the theatre of Syracuse, and the painted tombs and bronzes (including the Riace warriors) of Reggio's museum.
