Optical character recognition

Optical character recognition (OCR) is the electronic or mechanical conversion of images of typed, handwritten, or printed text into machine-encoded text, whether from a scanned document, a photograph, or subtitle text superimposed on an image. It is widely used to digitize printed records so they can be edited, searched, stored compactly, and processed by software such as text-to-speech readers and machine translation systems.

History

Early OCR devices were designed as reading aids for the blind: Emanuel Goldberg's machines of the 1910s–1920s and the Optophone, which converted print into tones, were pioneering examples. Commercial OCR spread in the 1950s and 1960s with machines such as the RCA and IBM readers used for mail sorting and banking, and standardized fonts like OCR-A and OCR-B were created to make recognition reliable. Ray Kurzweil's 1976 omni-font reading machine brought the technology to wider prominence.

Process

Modern OCR pipelines typically include preprocessing (deskewing, binarization, and noise removal), layout analysis to find text regions, segmentation into lines and characters or direct line recognition, classification using pattern matching or neural networks, and post-processing with dictionaries and language models to correct errors. Contemporary systems, such as the open-source Tesseract engine and commercial cloud services, achieve very high accuracy on clean printed text; handwriting recognition remains harder and is often treated as a separate problem (ICR).

Applications

OCR underpins large-scale digitization projects such as Google Books and digital libraries, automated data entry from forms and invoices, license-plate recognition, passport reading at borders, and accessibility tools for visually impaired readers.
