Hyperkalemia

Hyperkalemia is an elevated level of potassium (K+) in the blood. Normal potassium levels are between 3.5 and 5.0 mmol/L; levels above 5.5 mmol/L are generally considered hyperkalemic, and severe hyperkalemia (above 6.5 mmol/L) is a medical emergency because of the risk of potentially fatal abnormal heart rhythms (arrhythmias).

Causes

The most common cause is kidney failure, in which the kidneys cannot excrete potassium adequately. Other causes include medications such as ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, potassium-sparing diuretics, and NSAIDs; adrenal insufficiency (Addison's disease); and the release of potassium from damaged cells in burns, trauma, or hemolysis. Apparent hyperkalemia ("pseudohyperkalemia") may result from hemolysis of the blood sample itself.

Signs and diagnosis

Hyperkalemia often causes few symptoms until it is severe, when palpitations, muscle weakness, or paralysis may occur. Characteristic electrocardiogram changes include peaked T waves, a widened QRS complex, and loss of P waves, progressing in the worst case to a sinusoidal pattern and cardiac arrest.

Treatment

Management aims to stabilize the heart (with intravenous calcium), shift potassium into cells (with insulin and glucose, beta-agonists, or sodium bicarbonate), and remove potassium from the body (with diuretics, potassium-binding resins such as patiromer or sodium zirconium cyclosilicate, or dialysis).
