Industrial Revolution

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Industrial Revolution, sometimes called the First Industrial Revolution, was the transition from an agrarian and handicraft economy to one dominated by industry and machine manufacturing. Beginning in Britain in the mid-eighteenth century, it spread to continental Europe and the United States, transforming economies, cities, and everyday life, and marking a turning point in global history: for the first time, sustained growth in both population and per-capita income became possible.

Causes

Historians cite Britain's abundant coal, colonial trade and capital accumulation, an agricultural revolution that freed labour and fed growing towns, high wages that encouraged labour-saving invention, patent law, and a culture of practical science. Cheap coal near navigable water was especially favourable to steam power.

Key developments

Textile machinery — the spinning jenny (1764), water frame, and spinning mule — mechanised cotton production in factories. James Watt's improved steam engine (patented 1769) became a universal prime mover for mills, mines, locomotives, and steamships. Abraham Darby's coke smelting and later puddling and the Bessemer process made iron and steel cheap. Steam railways from the 1820s–30s and canals integrated national markets.

Social effects

Industrialisation drew millions into factory towns such as Manchester, where long hours, child labour, and overcrowded housing provoked reform movements, trade unions, and new political ideologies. Living standards, after an early period of stagnation debated by historians, rose substantially in the later nineteenth century. Production of iron depended on minerals such as hematite ore.

Spread and later phases

Belgium, France, the German states, and the northern United States industrialised during the nineteenth century, followed by Japan after the Meiji Restoration. A "Second Industrial Revolution" (c. 1870–1914) added steel, electricity, chemicals, and mass production.
