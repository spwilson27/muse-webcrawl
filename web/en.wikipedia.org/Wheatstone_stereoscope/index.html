Wheatstone stereoscope

The Wheatstone stereoscope is an optical instrument invented by Charles Wheatstone and described in 1838. It used mirrors to present separate drawings to the left and right eyes so that the viewer perceived a single three-dimensional image. The device provided an influential demonstration of binocular disparity before photography became widely available.

Principle

Two slightly different pictures, drawn from viewpoints corresponding to the two eyes, were placed at the sides of the apparatus. Mirrors angled toward the viewer reflected each image into the appropriate eye. The brain fused the pair into a scene with apparent depth, showing that stereoscopic vision depends on the difference between retinal images.

Significance

Wheatstone's work helped found the experimental study of depth perception and preceded later lenticular and photographic stereoscopes, including the popular Brewster form. The mirror stereoscope remains a landmark in vision science because it isolated binocular cues from motion and prior knowledge.
