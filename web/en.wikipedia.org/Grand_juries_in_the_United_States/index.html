Grand juries in the United States

A grand jury decides whether to bring criminal charges

Grand juries in the United States are panels of citizens empowered by law to conduct legal proceedings, investigate potential criminal conduct, and determine whether criminal charges should be brought. The Fifth Amendment to the U.S. Constitution requires that capital or otherwise infamous federal crimes be charged by grand jury indictment; this requirement, unusually, has not been incorporated against the states by the Supreme Court, so state practice varies.

Function and procedure

A federal grand jury consists of 16 to 23 members; at least 12 votes are required to return an indictment ("true bill"), otherwise the jury returns a "no bill". Proceedings are secret, the prosecutor presents the evidence, the accused has no right to appear or present a defence case, and hearsay is permitted. Grand juries also wield subpoena power over documents and witnesses, and some states use civil or investigative grand juries to probe public corruption and issue reports.

Criticism

Critics argue that grand juries almost always indict when prosecutors ask — a New York chief judge famously remarked that a grand jury would "indict a ham sandwich" — and give prosecutors a one-sided, secret forum. Defenders respond that the institution screens weak cases and interposes citizens between the state and the accused. High-profile decisions not to indict police officers have periodically fuelled reform debates.
