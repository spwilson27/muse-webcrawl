Hu Shih

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hu Shih (Chinese: 胡適; 17 December 1891 – 24 February 1962), born Hu Hongxing, was a Chinese philosopher, essayist and diplomat. A leading figure of the New Culture Movement, he championed the use of written vernacular Chinese (baihua) over classical Chinese and promoted pragmatism, liberalism and scientific method in Chinese intellectual life.

Born: 17 December 1891, Shanghai, Qing Empire
Died: 24 February 1962 (aged 70), Taipei, Taiwan
Education: Cornell University; Columbia University (PhD)
Known for: Baihua movement; New Culture Movement; pragmatism

Life and work

Hu studied in the United States on a Boxer Indemnity Scholarship, first at Cornell and then at Columbia under the pragmatist philosopher John Dewey. His 1917 article “A Preliminary Discussion of Literary Reform”, published in New Youth while he was still a student, launched the literary revolution that made vernacular Chinese the standard written language. Returning to Peking University, he applied evidential scholarship to the history of Chinese philosophy and literature, famously redating and re-authenticating classical texts and promoting research on vernacular novels such as Dream of the Red Chamber.

Later career

Hu served as the Republic of China’s ambassador to the United States (1938–1942), mobilising American support during the Second Sino-Japanese War, and later as president of Peking University (1946–1948). After 1949 he lived mostly in the United States and Taiwan, where he became president of Academia Sinica in 1957 and remained a symbol of liberal thought until his death in Taipei.
