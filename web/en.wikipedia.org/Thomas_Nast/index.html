Thomas Nast (September 27, 1840 – December 7, 1902) was a German-born American caricaturist and editorial cartoonist, regarded as the "Father of the American Cartoon." Working for Harper's Weekly from 1862 to 1886, he created the modern popular image of Santa Claus and the political symbols of the Democratic donkey and Republican elephant.

Career

Nast's Civil War illustrations earned praise from Abraham Lincoln, who called him "our best recruiting sergeant." In the early 1870s his relentless cartoon campaign against New York's Tammany Hall machine and its leader William "Boss" Tweed contributed to Tweed's downfall; Tweed reportedly said he did not care what the papers wrote, since his constituents could not read, "but they can see pictures."

Legacy

Nast popularized Uncle Sam, Columbia, and the Tammany tiger, and his Christmas drawings defined the visual Santa Claus for generations. His legacy is debated for its use of ethnic caricature, particularly of Irish immigrants, even as his influence on political cartooning remains unrivalled.
