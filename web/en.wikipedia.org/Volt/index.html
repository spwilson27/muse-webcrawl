Volt

The volt (symbol: V) is the unit of electric potential, electric potential difference (voltage), and electromotive force in the International System of Units (SI). It is named after the Italian physicist Alessandro Volta (1745–1827), inventor of the voltaic pile, the first chemical battery.

Definition

One volt is defined as the potential difference between two points of a conducting wire carrying a constant current of one ampere when the power dissipated between the points is one watt. Equivalently, it is the potential difference that will impart one joule of energy per coulomb of charge. In SI base units, 1 V = 1 kg·m²·s⁻³·A⁻¹.

Since 2019, the volt has been defined in terms of fixed fundamental constants through the Josephson effect: the Josephson constant, KJ = 2e/h, has an exact value because the elementary charge e and the Planck constant h are fixed. Josephson junction arrays provide the practical standard for the volt worldwide.

Common voltages

Familiar sources include the AA battery (1.5 V), the lithium-ion cell (about 3.7 V nominal), the automobile battery (12 V), and household mains electricity (about 120 V in North America and 230 V in most of Europe). High-voltage transmission lines operate at hundreds of thousands of volts to reduce resistive losses over long distances.

History

The volt was established as a practical unit at the first International Electrical Congress in Paris in 1881. It was originally defined in terms of the electromotive force of standard cells, notably the Weston cell, before being redefined on absolute and later quantum-electrical bases.
