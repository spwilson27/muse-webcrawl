Knight of faith

Abraham, Kierkegaard's exemplar of the knight of faith.

The knight of faith is a central concept in the philosophy of Søren Kierkegaard, developed in his 1843 work Fear and Trembling, published under the pseudonym Johannes de silentio. The knight of faith is an individual who, having renounced everything in "infinite resignation", nevertheless believes "by virtue of the absurd" that what was renounced will be restored in this life — a double movement that Kierkegaard contrasts with the single movement of the knight of infinite resignation.

Abraham

Kierkegaard's model is the biblical patriarch Abraham, commanded by God to sacrifice his son Isaac on Mount Moriah. In obeying, Abraham performed a "teleological suspension of the ethical": he acted against the universal moral law that a father must not kill his son, in absolute relation to God, while simultaneously believing that he would receive Isaac back. Because this faith cannot be mediated or explained in universal terms, the knight of faith is necessarily silent and outwardly indistinguishable from an ordinary person — Kierkegaard imagines him looking like a tax collector, enjoying his dinner and his pipe, wholly at home in the finite world he has renounced and regained.

Interpretation

The concept has been read as a defence of religious faith against Hegelian rationalism, as a psychological portrait of trust, and, critically, as a dangerous sanctification of fanaticism. It remains one of the most discussed ideas in existential philosophy.
