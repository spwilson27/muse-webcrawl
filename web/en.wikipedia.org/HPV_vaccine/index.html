Human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccines are vaccines that prevent infection by certain types of human papillomavirus. Available HPV vaccines protect against either two, four, or nine types of HPV. All HPV vaccines protect against at least HPV types 16 and 18, which together cause the greatest risk of cervical cancer. HPV vaccines are used to prevent HPV infection and therefore cervical cancer.

Medical uses

The vaccines are recommended for routine immunization of adolescents, typically before potential exposure to HPV, generally at ages 11 or 12 in the United States, though vaccination may begin at age 9 and is recommended for catch-up through age 26 and, based on shared clinical decision-making, for some adults aged 27 to 45. In 2022 the World Health Organization updated its recommendations to a one- or two-dose schedule for girls and young women. The vaccine is given by injection into muscle, usually in two or three doses depending on age at first dose.

Effectiveness

The vaccines provide near-complete protection against persistent infection with the HPV types they cover when given before exposure. Population studies in countries with high coverage have shown large declines in HPV infections, genital warts and cervical precancers, and a Swedish nationwide study published in 2020 found vaccinated women had a substantially lower risk of invasive cervical cancer. The vaccines do not treat existing infection or disease, and cervical screening remains recommended.

Safety

HPV vaccines are considered safe. The most common adverse effects are pain, redness and swelling at the injection site, and brief fainting is occasionally reported in adolescents. Large reviews by the WHO Global Advisory Committee on Vaccine Safety have found no link between HPV vaccination and autoimmune conditions, complex regional pain syndrome or postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome beyond coincidence.

History

The link between HPV and cervical cancer was established by Harald zur Hausen's research group in the 1980s. Virus-like particle technology developed by Ian Frazer and Jian Zhou at the University of Queensland, and others, enabled the vaccines. The quadrivalent vaccine Gardasil (Merck) was licensed in 2006; the bivalent Cervarix (GSK) followed; and the nine-valent Gardasil 9, covering five additional oncogenic types, was licensed in 2014.
