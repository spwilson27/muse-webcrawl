Contract cheating

Contract cheating is a form of academic dishonesty in which students pay others to complete their coursework. The term was coined in a 2006 study by Robert Clarke and Thomas Lancaster, who proposed using it as a more inclusive way to discuss the issue of ghostwriting and students paying third parties, including "essay mills", to complete university assignments.

Forms

Contract cheating can take many forms, including purchasing essays from essay-writing services, hiring individuals to sit examinations on a student's behalf (impersonation), and obtaining work from friends, family members, or other students, whether paid or unpaid. Commercial services marketing bespoke coursework to students are often called "essay mills".

Prevalence

A 2018 systematic review of survey samples estimated that around 15.7% of students had engaged in some form of contract cheating at some point, with rates appearing to rise over time. Because the behaviour is covert, accurate measurement is difficult, and self-reported figures are likely to be underestimates.

Responses and legislation

Several jurisdictions have legislated against the provision and advertising of contract cheating services. Australia amended its Tertiary Education Quality and Standards Agency Act in 2020 to criminalise providing or advertising academic cheating services, and the United Kingdom's Skills and Post-16 Education Act 2022 made it an offence to provide, arrange, or advertise cheating services to students in England. Educators have responded with assessment redesign, including more personalised, staged, and oral components. See also academic dishonesty and plagiarism.
