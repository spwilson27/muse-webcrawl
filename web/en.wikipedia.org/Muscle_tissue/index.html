Muscle tissue

Muscle tissue is one of the four basic types of animal tissue, specialised for contraction. Its cells, called muscle fibres or myocytes, contain ordered arrays of the contractile proteins actin and myosin, which slide past one another to shorten the cell and generate force. Muscle produces body movement, maintains posture, stabilises joints, moves substances through the body and generates heat.

Types

There are three types of muscle tissue in vertebrates:

  • Skeletal muscle — attached to bones by tendons; its long, multinucleated fibres show visible cross-striations, and contraction is under voluntary control by the somatic nervous system.
  • Cardiac muscle — found only in the heart wall; branched, striated cells joined by intercalated discs containing gap junctions, allowing coordinated involuntary contraction.
  • Smooth muscle — found in the walls of hollow organs such as the gut, blood vessels and uterus; spindle-shaped, non-striated cells contracting involuntarily, slowly and rhythmically under autonomic and hormonal control.

Structure and mechanism

In striated muscle, actin and myosin are organised into repeating units called sarcomeres, bounded by Z discs. Contraction follows the sliding filament theory: calcium ions released within the fibre bind troponin, exposing myosin-binding sites on actin; myosin heads then cycle through attachment, power stroke and detachment, powered by ATP. Stimulation arrives via motor neurons at the neuromuscular junction, where acetylcholine triggers an action potential.

Development and repair

Muscle develops from the mesoderm of the embryo. Skeletal muscle retains a population of satellite cells that repair damage and mediate growth, whereas cardiac muscle has very limited regenerative capacity — a central problem in recovery from heart attacks.
