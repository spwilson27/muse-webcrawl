A contract is an agreement that specifies certain legally enforceable rights and obligations pertaining to two or more parties. A contract typically involves consent to transfer of goods, services, money, or a promise to transfer any of those at a future date. In the event of a breach of contract, the injured party may seek judicial remedies such as damages or equitable relief.

In common law jurisdictions, three key elements must be present for the formation of a contract: (1) offer and acceptance, (2) consideration, and (3) the intention to create legal relations. Civil law systems structure the doctrine differently but likewise enforce seriously intended agreements.

Formation

An offer is a definite promise, capable of acceptance, to be bound on specified terms. Acceptance must be unqualified and communicated; a counter-offer extinguishes the original offer. Consideration — something of value exchanged by each party — is required in common law, though deeds and promissory estoppel provide exceptions. Agreements may be written, oral, or implied from conduct; some categories, such as sales of land, must be in writing under statutes of frauds.

Validity and defences

Contracts may be void, voidable, or unenforceable because of incapacity, duress, undue influence, misrepresentation, mistake, illegality, or unconscionability. Ambiguous terms are construed according to interpretation rules, including construction against the drafter in standard-form contracts.

Performance, breach, and remedies

Parties must perform as promised; a material breach discharges the injured party and entitles it to damages, ordinarily measured by the expectation interest. Equitable remedies include specific performance and injunctions, available where damages are inadequate, and restitution for unjust enrichment when a contract fails. Modern commercial law, including the Uniform Commercial Code in the United States and the CISG internationally, codifies many of these rules for sales of goods.
