Postcolonial studies

Postcolonial studies (also known as postcolonial theory) is the academic study of the cultural legacy of colonialism and imperialism, focusing on the human consequences of the control and exploitation of colonized people and their lands. It is a critical theory approach to literature and the social sciences that draws on fields including anthropology, sociology, history, political science, philosophy, and literary studies.

Overview

Postcolonial studies examines how European colonial powers shaped the cultures, identities, and political structures of colonized societies, and how those societies responded during and after formal decolonization. The field emerged in the late 1970s and 1980s, drawing impetus from the wave of independence movements that followed the Second World War, when dozens of nations in Africa, Asia, and the Caribbean achieved sovereignty.

Scholars in the field analyze texts, institutions, and cultural practices to uncover the assumptions and power relations embedded in colonial discourse, and to recover the voices and agency of colonized peoples.

Key concepts

Central concepts include Orientalism, the Western construction of the East as exotic and inferior; the Other, the colonized subject defined in opposition to the European self; hybridity and mimicry, describing how colonized cultures blend and imitate colonial forms; the subaltern, those excluded from structures of power and representation; and Eurocentrism, the privileging of European perspectives in accounts of world history.

Major figures

Foundational works include Edward Said's Orientalism (1978), which argued that Western scholarship about the Middle East and Asia served imperial interests; Frantz Fanon's The Wretched of the Earth (1961), a foundational analysis of colonial violence and liberation; and the writings of Homi K. Bhabha and Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak, whose essay "Can the Subaltern Speak?" (1988) questioned whether marginalized groups can represent themselves within dominant discourses. Aimé Césaire's Discourse on Colonialism (1950) is an important precursor.

Methods and themes

Postcolonial scholarship engages in close reading of colonial-era literature, counter-readings of canonical texts (such as reading The Tempest or Jane Eyre from the colonized perspective), archival research, and analysis of language, education, cartography, and museum practices. Recurring themes include nationalism, diaspora, race, gender, language politics, and the environmental consequences of empire.

Criticism

Critics have argued that postcolonial studies can be overly textual and detached from material conditions, that the prefix "post" risks implying colonialism has ended, and that the field sometimes homogenizes very different colonial experiences. Marxist critics in particular have charged the field with neglecting class and political economy.
