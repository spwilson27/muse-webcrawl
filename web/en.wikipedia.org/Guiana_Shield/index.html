Guiana Shield

The Guiana Shield is one of the three cratons of the South American Plate, a 1.7-billion-year-old Precambrian geological formation in north-eastern South America. It underlies Guyana, Suriname and French Guiana, together known as the Guianas, as well as parts of Venezuela, Colombia and northern Brazil.

Geology

The shield consists of ancient metamorphic and igneous rocks among the oldest on the continent. Its most distinctive landforms are the tepuis, flat-topped sandstone mountains that rise abruptly from the rainforest and savanna, of which Mount Roraima is the best known. Angel Falls, the world's tallest waterfall, drops from Auyán-tepui in Venezuela.

Ecology

The region is covered largely by undisturbed tropical rainforest and has exceptionally high biodiversity and endemism, partly because the tepui summits have been isolated for millions of years. The shield's forests are an important carbon store, and the Guianas retain some of the highest forest cover of any region on Earth.

Resources

The shield is rich in minerals, including gold, diamonds and bauxite, and its rivers have significant hydroelectric potential. Mining, both legal and illegal, is a growing source of deforestation and mercury pollution in the region.
