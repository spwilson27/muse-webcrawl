GABA

Gamma-aminobutyric acid, or γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA), is the chief inhibitory neurotransmitter in the developmentally mature mammalian central nervous system. Its principal role is to reduce neuronal excitability throughout the nervous system. GABA is an amino acid, produced in neurons from glutamate by the enzyme glutamate decarboxylase (GAD) with vitamin B6 as a cofactor.

Function

GABA acts at two classes of receptor. GABAA receptors are ligand-gated chloride channels whose activation hyperpolarises the neuron, reducing the likelihood of an action potential. GABAB receptors are G protein-coupled receptors that act through second messengers and potassium channels. GABAergic interneurons shape the timing and synchrony of cortical activity; dysfunction of GABA signalling is implicated in anxiety disorders, epilepsy, insomnia, and other conditions.

Pharmacology

Many widely used drugs act on the GABA system. Benzodiazepines, barbiturates, and certain anaesthetics enhance GABAA receptor function; baclofen is a GABAB agonist; vigabatrin and tiagabine raise GABA levels by blocking its breakdown or reuptake. Ethanol's effects on the brain also involve GABAergic enhancement. GABA itself crosses the blood–brain barrier poorly, so the effects of oral GABA supplements on the brain remain uncertain, though it also functions in peripheral tissues such as the pancreas, where it is produced by beta cells.

Distribution and development

GABA was first identified in the brain in 1950. In the developing brain, GABA is initially excitatory because immature neurons have high internal chloride concentrations — a switch driven by changing expression of the NKCC1 and KCC2 chloride transporters. Outside the nervous system, GABA is found in plants, where it accumulates in response to stress, and it is produced by some bacteria during fermentation.
