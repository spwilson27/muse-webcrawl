Shetland bus

Active: 1941–1945
Route: Shetland & Bergen, Norway
Operator: Royal Norwegian Navy / SOE
Craft: Norwegian fishing boats

The Shetland bus was the nickname given to a clandestine special operations group that made a regular link between the Shetland Islands in Scotland and German-occupied Norway during the Second World War. From mid-1941 until the surrender of Germany, it carried agents of the Special Operations Executive (SOE) and supplies to Norway, and brought refugees and resistance fighters back to Britain.

History

The operation began when Norwegian fishing boats, fleeing the German occupation, arrived in Shetland. British and Norwegian authorities organised the crossings using the boats' local knowledge of the Norwegian coast. The base eventually settled at Scalloway in Shetland, with an administrative headquarters at Lunna House.

The boats and crews

The early crossings were made by ordinary fishing cutters such as the Arthur, Aksel and Bergholm, crewed by Norwegian volunteers. The slow, lightly armed boats were vulnerable to German aircraft and patrols, and several were lost with all hands. In 1943 the United States Navy supplied three submarine chasers — Hessa, Hitra and Vigra — which made the winter crossings far safer and faster.

Legacy

The Shetland bus is commemorated by a memorial in Scalloway, and the men who sailed the route, many of whom never returned, remain celebrated in both Norway and Shetland. A famous book and film, The Shetland Bus by David Howarth, recorded the story of the operation.
