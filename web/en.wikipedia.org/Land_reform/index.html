Land reform


Land reform redistributes agricultural land from large estates to smallholders and tenants.

Land reform, also known as agrarian reform, involves the changing of laws, regulations, or customs regarding land ownership. It typically consists of a government-initiated or government-supported redistribution of agricultural land, most often from large landowners — landlords, estates, or agribusiness holdings — to the peasants, tenants, and landless labourers who actually work the land. Land reform may also include the abolition of feudal dues, the collectivisation or decollectivisation of farms, and changes in tenancy law.

Rationale

Advocates argue that highly concentrated land ownership produces rural poverty, political domination by landed elites, and inefficient agriculture, because tenants who can be evicted at will have little incentive to invest in the land. Critics respond that breaking up large farms can destroy economies of scale, and that poorly designed reforms — with insecure titles or no credit — leave beneficiaries worse off. The empirical record is mixed: the reforms in Japan, South Korea, and Taiwan after the Second World War are widely credited with supporting both equity and rapid industrialisation, while several reforms elsewhere stalled or were reversed.

History

Redistributive land policies are ancient: the Roman Gracchi brothers proposed limits on public land holdings in the 2nd century BC, and land-to-the-tiller ideas recurred in many agrarian societies. Major modern reforms include the Mexican reform following the 1910 revolution, enshrined in Article 27 of the 1917 constitution and the ejido system; the reforms imposed in occupied Japan (1946–1950), which transferred land from landlords to some three million tenant households; and the Chinese land reform of 1946–1953, which preceded collectivisation. In Eastern Europe after 1989, the dominant direction was reversed: restitution and privatisation of formerly collective land.

Forms

Land reforms vary along several dimensions: whether compensation is paid to former owners and at what level; whether beneficiaries receive full private title, use rights, or membership in a cooperative; ceilings on holdings; and whether the measure is market-assisted (willing buyer, willing seller, as in later South African and Brazilian programmes) or expropriatory. Tenancy reform — regulating rents and securing tenure without changing ownership — is a weaker but often politically easier variant.

Notable cases

Zimbabwe's fast-track land reform programme after 2000, which seized commercial farms with little compensation, is frequently cited as a cautionary example because of its effects on food production and the wider economy. Scotland's Land Reform (Scotland) Act 2003, by contrast, mainly created access rights and a community right to buy. In India, land reform was a state subject after independence; results varied, with Kerala and West Bengal (Operation Barga) recording tenancy reforms, while in much of the country ceiling laws were widely evaded.
