Fluoride

Fluorite (calcium fluoride), a principal natural source of fluoride.

Fluoride is an inorganic, monatomic anion of fluorine, with the chemical formula F−, whose salts are typically white or colourless. Fluoride salts typically have distinctive bitter tastes, and are odourless. Its salts and minerals are important chemical reagents and industrial chemicals, mainly used in the production of hydrogen fluoride for fluorocarbons. Fluoride is classified as a weak base since it only partially associates in solution, but concentrated fluoride is corrosive and can attack the skin.

Fluoride is the simplest fluorine anion. In terms of charge and size, the fluoride ion resembles the hydroxide ion. Fluoride ions occur on earth in several minerals, particularly fluorite, but are present only in trace quantities in bodies of water in nature.

Occurrence

Fluoride occurs naturally in minerals such as fluorite (CaF2), cryolite and fluorapatite, and in low concentrations in groundwater, seawater, tea, and some foods. Volcanic emissions are a major natural source of atmospheric fluoride. In some regions, notably parts of India, China and East Africa's Rift Valley, natural groundwater fluoride levels are high enough to cause widespread fluorosis.

Uses

  • Dental health — fluoride in toothpaste, mouth rinses and professionally applied gels strengthens tooth enamel and reduces dental caries. Many countries also adjust fluoride levels in drinking water (water fluoridation), a practice endorsed by major health bodies but periodically debated.
  • Industry — fluoride compounds are used in aluminium smelting (as cryolite flux), steelmaking, glass etching, uranium enrichment (via UF6), and the manufacture of fluoropolymers such as PTFE.
  • Chemistry — fluoride salts are reagents in organic synthesis, and the fluoride ion is used in fluoride-ion batteries and ion-selective electrodes.

Health effects

At low concentrations fluoride reduces tooth decay by promoting remineralisation and forming acid-resistant fluorapatite in enamel. Excessive intake during childhood can cause dental fluorosis — mottling of the teeth — and chronic high exposure can cause skeletal fluorosis, a painful stiffening of bones and joints. Health authorities set guideline values for drinking water (the WHO guideline is 1.5 mg/L) to balance caries prevention against fluorosis risk.
