Lactic acid bacteria
OrderLactobacillales
Key generaLactobacillus, Lactococcus, Streptococcus, Leuconostoc, Pediococcus

Lactic acid bacteria (LAB) are a group of gram-positive, low-GC, acid-tolerant, generally non-sporulating bacteria that produce lactic acid as the major end product of carbohydrate fermentation. They are among the most important microorganisms in food production and human health.

Characteristics

LAB are aerotolerant anaerobes that lack a functional respiratory chain and gain energy by substrate-level phosphorylation. Homofermentative species convert sugars almost entirely to lactic acid; heterofermentative species also produce carbon dioxide, ethanol and acetic acid.

Habitat and uses

They occur naturally on plants, in milk and in the gastrointestinal and mucosal surfaces of animals. Humans have used them for millennia to make yogurt, cheese, sourdough bread, sauerkraut, kimchi, pickles, cured sausages, wine (malolactic fermentation) and silage. The acid they produce preserves food by inhibiting spoilage organisms and pathogens.

Health

Certain strains are used as probiotics. LAB are generally recognised as safe, though rare infections occur in severely immunocompromised patients, and some species cause dental caries or spoilage of beer and wine.

Taxonomy

The group corresponds largely to the order Lactobacillales in the phylum Bacillota. In 2020 the sprawling genus Lactobacillus was split into 25 genera, so many familiar species received new names (for example Lacticaseibacillus casei).
