Deep inelastic scattering

Deep inelastic scattering is the name given to the process used to probe the insides of hadrons (particularly nucleons such as the proton and neutron) using leptons — electrons, muons, or neutrinos — as projectiles. "Deep" refers to the large momentum transfer involved, which gives a very short de Broglie wavelength and hence fine spatial resolution; "inelastic" means the target hadron is broken up rather than recoiling intact.

History

Experiments at the Stanford Linear Accelerator Center (SLAC) in the late 1960s, led by Jerome Friedman, Henry Kendall, and Richard Taylor, scattered high-energy electrons off protons and observed far more large-angle scattering than expected if the proton's charge were smoothly distributed. Interpreting the results with Bjorken scaling, Richard Feynman proposed the parton model, in which the lepton scatters from point-like constituents inside the nucleon. These constituents were identified with quarks, and the results gave crucial early evidence for the quark model; Friedman, Kendall, and Taylor received the 1990 Nobel Prize in Physics.

Kinematics and structure functions

The process is described by invariants including the momentum transfer Q² and the Bjorken scaling variable x, interpreted in the parton model as the fraction of the nucleon's momentum carried by the struck parton. The cross section is expressed through structure functions F1(x, Q²) and F2(x, Q²), whose near-independence of Q² (scaling) signals point-like constituents; small deviations from scaling are predicted by quantum chromodynamics through gluon radiation and are encoded in the scale evolution of parton distribution functions.

Later experiments

Deep inelastic scattering has been performed with muon beams at CERN, neutrino beams at Fermilab and CERN, and — at the HERA collider at DESY (1992–2007) — with electrons and positrons colliding directly with protons, extending measurements to very high Q² and very small x. The planned Electron–Ion Collider continues this programme.
