Muscle cuirass

A bronze muscle cuirass

The muscle cuirass or heroic cuirass is a type of cuirass — body armour protecting the torso — moulded to mimic an idealised human physique, with sculpted pectoral muscles, nipples, abdominal muscles, and navel. Worn in classical antiquity by Greek and Roman soldiers and commanders, it combined protection with the display of heroic bodily ideals.

History

Anatomically shaped bronze cuirasses appeared in Greece by the Archaic period; famous early examples include the cuirass from Argos (c. 720 BC). The type flourished in the 5th and 4th centuries BC and was widely adopted by Italic peoples and the Romans. In Roman art, emperors and generals are frequently depicted in richly decorated muscle cuirasses — as on the statue of Augustus of Prima Porta — even though practical armour of mail or plate was more common in battle by that era.

Construction

The cuirass consisted of two bronze plates, front and back, shaped over a form and fastened at the sides and shoulders with hinges and straps. Leather or linen pteruges (hanging strips) often protected the shoulders and upper legs.
