Forensic science

FieldsPathology, toxicology, DNA analysis, trace evidence, digital forensics
ApplicationsCriminal investigation, civil litigation, disaster victim identification

Forensic science, also known as criminalistics, is the application of science to criminal and civil laws. Forensic scientists collect, preserve, and analyze physical evidence found at crime scenes or associated with suspects and victims, and may provide expert testimony in court. The word forensic comes from the Latin forēnsis, meaning "of or before the forum".

History

Early forensic methods appeared in China, where the Xi Yuan Ji Lu ("Collected Cases of Injustice Rectified", 1248) described distinguishing drowning from strangulation. In the 19th century, Alphonse Bertillon developed anthropometric identification, Francis Galton systematised fingerprint classification, and Edmond Locard founded a police laboratory in Lyon, formulating the exchange principle: "every contact leaves a trace". The discovery of DNA profiling by Alec Jeffreys in 1984 transformed identification evidence.

Subfields

  • Forensic pathology — determination of cause and manner of death.
  • Forensic toxicology — detection of drugs and poisons, including formaldehyde and other chemicals in tissue.
  • Trace evidence — fibres, glass, paint, soil and gunshot residue.
  • Latent prints and questioned documents — fingerprints and handwriting.
  • Digital forensics — recovery and analysis of data from computers and phones.
  • Forensic anthropology and odontology — identification from skeletal remains and teeth.

Reliability and reform

A landmark 2009 report by the U.S. National Academy of Sciences found that many traditional forensic techniques lacked rigorous scientific validation, and subsequent reviews have led to reforms in accreditation, proficiency testing and the avoidance of overstated conclusions in court.
