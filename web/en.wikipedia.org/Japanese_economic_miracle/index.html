Japanese economic miracle

The Japanese economic miracle refers to Japan's record period of rapid economic growth between the end of World War II and the end of the Cold War. During the high-growth period from 1955 to 1973, Japan's real GNP grew at an average rate of nearly 10 percent per year, transforming the war-devastated country into the world's second-largest economy by 1968.

Background

Japan emerged from the war with its industrial base in ruins, but with a literate, disciplined workforce and a backlog of modern technology to absorb. Reforms under the Allied occupation — land reform, zaibatsu dissolution, labour legislation — reshaped the economy, and procurement demand from the Korean War provided an early stimulus.

The high-growth era

Under the Income Doubling Plan of Prime Minister Hayato Ikeda, announced in 1960, investment in heavy industry, shipbuilding, steel, automobiles and consumer electronics surged. The Ministry of International Trade and Industry (MITI) coordinated industrial policy, while high household savings channelled capital through the banking system to export-oriented firms. The 1964 Tokyo Olympics and the launch of the Shinkansen symbolized the era's confidence.

Slowdown and legacy

Growth moderated after the 1973 oil crisis but remained strong by international standards. The miracle ended with the asset price bubble of the late 1980s and its collapse in the early 1990s, ushering in the "Lost Decades". The period nonetheless established Japan's reputation for manufacturing quality and export-led growth, providing a model studied by other East Asian economies.
