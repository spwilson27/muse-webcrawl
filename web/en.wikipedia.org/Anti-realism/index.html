Anti-realism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Realism and anti-realism contrasted

In analytic philosophy, anti-realism is a position which encompasses many varieties, each characterisable in terms of what it denies about a given subject area, such as the external world, the past or future, other minds, mathematical objects, or moral judgements. The term was introduced by the British philosopher Michael Dummett, building on the work of Gottlob Frege, Ludwig Wittgenstein and the Vienna Circle.

Dummett characterised anti-realism about a statement as the rejection of the principle of bivalence — the claim that the statement is determinately true or false independently of our means of knowing — in favour of a theory of meaning based on assertibility: to understand a statement is to know what would count as verifying it.

Varieties

  • Semantic anti-realism (Dummett): meaning should be explained by verification or assertibility conditions rather than possibly transcendent truth conditions.
  • Scientific anti-realism: constructive empiricism (Bas van Fraassen) holds that science should aim at empirical adequacy, not truth about unobservable entities.
  • Mathematical anti-realism: intuitionism denies that mathematical statements have truth values independent of proof.
  • Moral anti-realism: denies the existence of objective moral facts or properties.
  • Metaphysical anti-realism: associated with Kantian idealism and later with Hilary Putnam's "internal realism".

Debates

Anti-realists argue that realists cannot explain how we could grasp recognition-transcendent truth. Realists respond with "no miracles" arguments — especially in science, where the predictive success of theories about electrons or genes would be miraculous if those theories were not approximately true.
