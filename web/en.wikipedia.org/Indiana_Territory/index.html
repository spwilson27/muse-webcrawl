The Indiana Territory was an organized incorporated territory of the United States that existed from July 4, 1800, to December 11, 1816, when the southeastern portion of the territory was admitted to the Union as the state of Indiana. It was created by an act of Congress signed by President John Adams, dividing the Northwest Territory in preparation for Ohio statehood.

Extent

At its creation, the Indiana Territory was vast, encompassing present-day Indiana, Illinois, Wisconsin, Michigan, and part of Minnesota. The Michigan Territory was split off in 1805 and the Illinois Territory in 1809, leaving roughly the boundaries of modern Indiana, plus small adjustments.

Government

William Henry Harrison, later the ninth President of the United States, served as the territory's first governor from 1801 to 1812. As governor, Harrison negotiated a series of treaties with Native American nations that transferred millions of acres to the United States, provoking resistance led by the Shawnee brothers Tecumseh and Tenskwatawa. Tensions culminated in the Battle of Tippecanoe (1811) and the territory's involvement in the War of 1812.

Slavery question

Although the Northwest Ordinance of 1787 prohibited slavery, territorial leaders repeatedly petitioned Congress to suspend the ban and permitted long-term indentures of enslaved people. An anti-slavery movement gained strength, and the 1816 Indiana constitution prohibited slavery outright.

Statehood

The capital moved from Vincennes to Corydon in 1813. An enabling act passed Congress in April 1816, a constitution was drafted at Corydon in June, and Indiana entered the Union as the 19th state on December 11, 1816, with Jonathan Jennings as its first governor.
