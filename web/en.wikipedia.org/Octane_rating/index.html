Octane rating

Image placeholder: Fuel pump showing octane grades
Octane grades displayed at a service station

An octane rating, or octane number, is a standard measure of a fuel's ability to withstand compression in an internal combustion engine without undergoing pre-ignition — the spontaneous combustion of the air–fuel mixture that causes engine knocking. The higher the octane number, the more compression the fuel can withstand before detonating.

Measurement

The rating is defined relative to a mixture of two reference fuels: n-heptane, assigned 0, which knocks readily, and iso-octane (2,2,4-trimethylpentane), assigned 100, which resists knocking. A fuel rated 90 octane behaves, in knock resistance, like a mixture of 90% iso-octane and 10% heptane in a standardized test engine.

  • Research Octane Number (RON) — measured under milder test conditions; used in most of the world.
  • Motor Octane Number (MON) — measured at higher speed and temperature, usually 8–10 points lower than RON.
  • Anti-Knock Index (AKI) — the average of RON and MON, (R+M)/2, posted on pumps in the United States and Canada; typical U.S. grades are 87 (regular), 88–90 (midgrade), and 91–94 (premium).

Effects and use

Using a fuel with a higher octane rating than an engine requires provides no general benefit in power or economy; high-compression and turbocharged engines designed for premium fuel can, however, lose performance or suffer damage on lower-octane fuel, though modern engine knock sensors usually prevent damage by retarding ignition timing. Octane rating does not measure energy content — it is purely a measure of knock resistance.

Additives and history

Octane ratings were historically raised with tetraethyl lead, whose phase-out on health and environmental grounds (and to protect catalytic converters) drove the shift to unleaded gasoline with ratings achieved through refining and additives such as MTBE (largely replaced) and ethanol, which has a high blending octane. Aviation gasoline uses separate lean/rich rating conventions, and diesel fuel is rated by cetane number instead.
