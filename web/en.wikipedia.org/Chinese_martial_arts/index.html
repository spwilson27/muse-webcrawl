Chinese martial arts, commonly referred to with umbrella terms kung fu (Chinese: 功夫; pinyin: gōngfu) and wushu (武術; wǔshù), are numerous fighting styles that have developed over the centuries in Greater China. These fighting styles are often classified according to common traits, identified as "families", "sects", or "schools" of martial arts.

History

Chinese martial arts trace their legendary origins to semi-mythical figures such as the Yellow Emperor, and to the Shaolin Monastery, where tradition credits the monk Bodhidharma with exercises that became Shaolin kung fu. Historically, martial skills developed for warfare, hunting, and self-defense, and were shaped by Chinese philosophy, medicine, and religion, including Confucianism, Taoism, and Buddhism. During the Ming and Qing dynasties, styles proliferated among military units, militias, and secret societies.

Classification

Styles are commonly divided into external styles emphasizing muscular power and speed (such as Shaolin kung fu, hung gar, and wing chun) and internal styles emphasizing breath, posture, and qi cultivation (such as tai chi, baguazhang, and xingyiquan). A geographic division distinguishes northern styles — known for extended kicks and acrobatics — from southern styles, which favor low stances and hand techniques.

Training

Traditional training includes stances, forms (taolu) — choreographed sequences of movements — weapons practice (staff, spear, broadsword, straight sword), conditioning, and applications with partners. Health-oriented exercises such as qigong are closely associated. In the People's Republic of China, standardized sport wushu developed taolu competition and full-contact sanda (sanshou) sparring.

In popular culture

Chinese martial arts spread worldwide through migration and cinema. Hong Kong action films of the 1970s, starring Bruce Lee and later Jackie Chan and Jet Li, made kung fu a global phenomenon, and the arts remain a major subject of film, television, and video games.
