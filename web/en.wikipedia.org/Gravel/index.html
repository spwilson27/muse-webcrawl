Gravel is a loose aggregation of rock fragments. It is classified by particle size range and includes granules (2–4 mm), pebbles (4–64 mm), cobbles (64–256 mm) and boulders (larger than 256 mm) under the Udden–Wentworth scale used by geologists. Gravel forms naturally where rock is weathered and transported by water or ice, accumulating in river beds, alluvial fans, beaches and glacial deposits.

Production and types

Commercial gravel is produced either by screening natural deposits (bank gravel, river gravel and pea gravel, whose rounded stones are smoothed by water) or by mechanically crushing rock such as granite, limestone or trap rock, producing angular crushed stone that locks together in construction. Worldwide, gravel and sand together form the most extracted solid material group, with tens of billions of tonnes used annually.

Uses

Gravel is a principal ingredient of concrete and asphalt, the base material for roads, railways (as track ballast, usually in coarser crushed form) and building foundations. It is used for drainage layers and French drains because water flows freely through it, and in landscaping for paths, driveways and decorative ground cover. Gravel roads remain common in rural areas worldwide. Geologically, consolidated gravel forms the sedimentary rocks conglomerate (rounded clasts) and breccia (angular clasts).
