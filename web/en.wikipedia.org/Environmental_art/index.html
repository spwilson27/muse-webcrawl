Environmental art

Environmental art is a range of artistic practices encompassing both historical approaches to nature in art and more recent ecological and politically motivated types of works. Environmental art has evolved away from formal concerns, for example monumental earthworks using earth as a sculptural material, towards a deeper consideration of systems, processes and phenomena — including ecological relationships and social contexts.

History

The movement emerged in the late 1960s and early 1970s, largely in the United States, alongside land art and conceptual art. Robert Smithson's Spiral Jetty (1970), a 460-metre coil of basalt and earth in Utah's Great Salt Lake, Michael Heizer's monumental desert works, and Walter De Maria's The Lightning Field (1977) redefined sculpture as landscape-scale intervention. In Britain, Richard Long and Andy Goldsworthy made ephemeral works from walking and natural materials, documented in photographs.

Ecological art

From the 1980s, "eco-art" turned from making work in the environment to working for it: Alan Sonfist's Time Landscape replanted native forest in Manhattan; Agnes Denes planted and harvested a wheatfield on a Lower Manhattan landfill (Wheatfield — A Confrontation, 1982); Joseph Beuys organised the planting of 7,000 oaks; and Mel Chin's Revival Field used plants to extract heavy metals from contaminated soil. Such works involve collaboration with scientists and communities and aim at remediation and awareness.

Themes and criticism

Environmental art addresses climate change, biodiversity loss, pollution and humanity's relationship with the non-human world. It has been criticised where large earthworks scar fragile landscapes, or where documentation and art-market value overshadow ecological benefit — tensions the field continues to debate.
