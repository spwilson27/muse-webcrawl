Gutenberg discontinuity

The Gutenberg discontinuity is the boundary between the Earth's mantle and its outer core, located at a depth of about 2,900 kilometres (1,800 miles) below the surface. It is named after the German-American seismologist Beno Gutenberg (1889–1960), who determined its depth in the early 20th century using seismic wave observations.

Discovery and properties

Seismic waves from earthquakes change abruptly at the discontinuity. Primary (P) waves decrease sharply in velocity, while secondary (S) waves — which cannot travel through liquid — disappear entirely. This behaviour led scientists to conclude that the outer core is liquid, composed mainly of molten iron and nickel, while the mantle above is solid rock. The region directly above the boundary, a layer roughly 150–300 km thick called the D″ layer, shows complex structure, including partial melt patches (ultra-low velocity zones) and large low-shear-velocity provinces beneath Africa and the Pacific.

The temperature at the core–mantle boundary is estimated at roughly 4,000 °C (7,200 °F), and heat flowing across it drives convection in the outer core, powering the geodynamo that generates the Earth's magnetic field, as well as mantle plumes rising from the boundary region.
