A sport utility vehicle (SUV) is a car classification that combines elements of road-going passenger cars with features of off-road vehicles, such as raised ground clearance and (often) four-wheel drive. Typically built in a two-box design with an upright, spacious cabin, SUVs became one of the world's dominant vehicle segments in the early twenty-first century.

History

The concept descends from military and rural utility vehicles such as the wartime Jeep, the Land Rover (1948), the Toyota Land Cruiser, the Jeep Wagoneer (1963) and the Range Rover (1970). The segment expanded rapidly in North America in the 1980s and 1990s with models such as the Jeep Cherokee, Ford Explorer and Toyota RAV4; compact unibody "crossover" SUVs later displaced body-on-frame models in most markets. By the 2010s and 2020s SUVs accounted for roughly half of global new-car sales, including a fast-growing share of electric vehicles.

Characteristics

SUVs generally offer high seating positions, large cargo capacity and available all-wheel drive, and are marketed on safety, versatility and commanding road presence. They range from subcompact crossovers to full-size, three-row, body-on-frame models.

Criticism

Critics cite the segment's higher fuel consumption and CO2 emissions relative to smaller cars — the International Energy Agency identified SUV growth as a major driver of global oil demand and emissions in the 2010s — as well as greater danger to pedestrians and occupants of smaller vehicles because of mass and high front ends, and poorer dynamic safety (rollover risk) in early designs. Emissions rules that classified SUVs as light trucks, and consumer preference, encouraged their spread, prompting regulators in several countries to tighten standards.
